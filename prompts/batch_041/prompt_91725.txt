I run a monthly discussion group at the public library — eight or nine adults, no economists among them, though two are engineers and one taught high-school chemistry, so they are comfortable with the basic logic of a controlled experiment and won't be frightened off by the word variable. Next month's topic is why some countries are rich and others poor, and I've been put down to lead it. I've been chewing on this since an argument with my brother-in-law, who holds that it is all geography and nothing else, and what I want the group to leave with is not the usual three-way horse race — geography, culture, institutions — but something harder: the suspicion that the interesting question is not which of those three lines up best with wealth, but whether the evidence we could ever have is capable of separating them, and what sort of evidence would be. So please don't write the talk for me; plan it. I need the order in which the ideas have to arrive and why each has to wait for the one before it — in particular, whether the group has to feel the pull of the three candidates before they can be shown the trouble with them, or whether the trouble should come first and the candidates be introduced as failed answers to it. I need the one case that should carry the whole session, what that case must have going for it and what it must not have, and where in the sequence it belongs. I need the misconceptions I should expect, each pinned to the exact point in the sequence where I head it off, and the single question I can leave them with that will show me in a week's time whether they actually got it or merely nodded.

Here is my half-formed plan, so you can tell me where it is wrong. My carrying case was going to be two towns a few miles apart on either side of a national border — same language, same soil, same church, different governments — one of them several times richer than the other. My worry is that the group will say "well, the government, obviously" and believe they have learned something, when the real lesson is about what such a comparison can and cannot rule out; I can't tell whether that means the example is wrong or merely misplaced. I would also like to get across a distinction I have only half worked out, and I'd like you to sharpen it or kill it: that a factor can be a genuine cause in the story of one particular country without being a cause of the pattern across countries, so that "why is this one rich?" and "why are rich ones rich?" may not have the same answer. The misconceptions I am braced for are that a correlation plus a plausible story is already an explanation; that history is simply a longer correlation table, so tracing things back further settles them; that a factor which cannot be measured precisely, like culture, therefore cannot be doing any work; and that a single country which got rich after a policy change, or stayed poor after a flood of aid, is decisive rather than merely suggestive. Tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level, and what to say in place of mangling it.
