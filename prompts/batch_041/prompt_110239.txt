In a seminar discussion about whether a machine could ever be in a position to fully verify or understand itself, a colleague closed the matter with a line I have now heard in several variants: Gödel showed that no system can fully capture itself, that there are always truths it cannot reach. I do not doubt that something in the neighborhood is right, but I suspect the sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and that some of those jobs are done well while others are not. What I want is not an explanation of the incompleteness theorems but a map of the claims this one sentence could be making.

Please lay those claims out as a menu, ordered roughly from what is almost trivially true to what is genuinely radical, and for each one say whether it is an accurate statement of what the theorems establish, a loose but defensible gloss, or simply false. As you do this, keep separate the following: the claim about provability within one particular formal system versus the claim about truth, knowledge, or understanding in general; the readings that respect the theorems' scope conditions (consistency, effective axiomatizability, enough arithmetic to encode syntax) versus the readings that quietly drop them; what the first theorem gives on its own versus what the second theorem adds; and the reading on which the theorems are supposed to show that human mathematical insight outruns any mechanical procedure, the argument usually associated with J. R. Lucas and Roger Penrose. For the vaguer formulations, I would like to know exactly which premise has to be supplied to turn "there are truths this system cannot prove" into "there are truths that cannot be known."

Finally, tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually support the conclusion my colleague drew, that a machine can never fully verify or understand itself, and what further assumptions that reading would need in order to reach it. I am not after history or citations; I want the argumentative map.
