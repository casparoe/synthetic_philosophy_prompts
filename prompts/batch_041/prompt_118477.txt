In a seminar I'm taking on chance and rational credence, we spent a session on the principle that ties your degrees of belief to objective chance, and I've been unable to settle a worry that came out of it. Stated in the way that seems most natural to me, the principle says that if you know the objective chance of some proposition A is p, and none of your other evidence is relevant to A over and above the chance (no crystal ball, no inside tip, no information that already settles how the chance plays out), then your credence in A ought to be p. I want to be able to defend that, because it looks like the plainest requirement of rationality imaginable: what else would you go on, if you knew the chances and nothing else of use? The difficulty arises, though, once we take seriously the picture on which chances are not free-standing features of the world but rather the probabilities that figure in the best systematization of the whole history of the world, the system that best balances strength and simplicity. On that picture the future can reach back into the present chance facts, and there will be futures that undermine the present chances: A has a present chance of 0.5, and yet had A occurred, the best system would have assigned A a chance of 0.9 given the present state, so that the proposition stating the present chance, that A's chance is 0.5, can be true only in worlds where A does not occur.

A friend in the seminar thinks this is fatal rather than awkward. His argument is that an agent who knows the chance of A is 0.5 knows a proposition that, together with what she knows about the past, rules A out; so the principle would have her put credence 0.5 on something her own evidence excludes, and any rule with that consequence has to go. What he proposes instead is a rule of the following shape: your credence in A conditional on the past together with the complete theory of chance should equal the chance of A conditional on the theory alone, which, in the undermining case, yields a value different from the chance itself. I can't tell whether that is a genuine repair, an evasion dressed up as precision, or a cure for a disease the original principle never had, and I suspect the answer turns on questions I haven't got straight about what it is to know a theory of chance and about whether the trouble lies with the principle or with the account of chance it is being paired with.

Please write me an essay that adjudicates this. I would like it to say precisely what the original principle claims and what the revised rule claims, and to show whether and where the two actually diverge in the undermining case rather than merely sounding different; to assess my friend's argument that the original principle is refuted by that case, saying what has to be true of the agent's evidence for the argument to go through; and to say whether the problem belongs to the principle or to the account of chance, including whether it survives if chances are instead taken to be primitive features of the world that do not supervene on the total history, so that no future could undermine them. Then reach a verdict: either the original principle stands as stated, or the revision is correct, or some third position, with the reasons that support it and the strongest objection it faces.
