I'm putting together a 90-minute session for my undergrad seminar on infinity -- 14 students, mixed math and philosophy, and I want them to actually feel the actual-vs-potential infinity thing in their guts rather than nod along to the hotel story they've all seen memed to death. Here's the rough shape I have in mind, so design me a proper runnable version (or tell me where it breaks and give me a better one): I turn the room into a hotel. Index cards taped along the wall are the "rooms," labelled 1, 2, 3, ... as far as the eye can see, with a big roll of blanks for "overflow," and four students run the front desk with a ledger where every occupied room has to be written down. I play the arriving guest. Round one: every card is already taken (I pre-place occupancy tokens), I walk in and ask for a room, they panic, and eventually somebody suggests moving the guest in 1 to 2, 2 to 3, and so on, and giving me room 1. Round two: I show up with an infinite bus, an infinite line of new guests. Round three: infinitely many buses, each carrying infinitely many guests. All by the book, all fine.

Now here's the twist I want you to help me stage, because this is the whole point: I plant one student as an inspector / auditor whose job is to refuse to sign off on any room assignment she can't see completed. Every time the desk proposes a shift she asks "which guest moves, and when are you finished?" -- and since at every finite stage there's a guest still without a room (or so she'll insist), she won't sign the ledger. I want the class to genuinely feel the deadlock between "there's a bijection, so a room exists for everyone" and "nobody can ever carry the assignment out." So: rules, roles, materials, exactly how the desk keeps the ledger, how many rounds and in what order, when I should reveal that the auditor isn't just being difficult and that her position has a name and a pedigree (the old Aristotelian thought that infinity is always a process, never a finished thing -- and the finitist who refuses to treat a completed infinite totality as a legitimate object at all), and a debrief that lands the real points: that we *define* two collections as the same size by the existence of a matching, not by one being a proper part of the other; that this is exactly what it means for a set to be infinite in the Dedekind sense; and that you can always take in a newcomer at the front but never at the back, and what that asymmetry between counting and ordering actually tells you.

Two more things I need from you. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I already expect someone to say "just build a bigger hotel," someone else to get personally annoyed at the auditor, and the desk to physically give up moving tokens and treat that as a practical snag rather than a conceptual one, but tell me the others and how to handle each. Second, be honest in the debrief notes about what this activity can't reproduce: it can't get them anywhere near uncountability, it can't give the finitist's positive picture, only her objection, and the finite tokens are a simulation so "you can't finish the move" is partly an artefact of the props -- I need to know where to flag that so I don't oversell what they've felt. Also, I was chatting with a colleague who thinks the auditor role is too gimmicky and will just read as me having rigged a stooge -- if you agree, tell me how to run it without a plant.
