I'm a graduate student in a political economy seminar, and our last session left me with a conceptual puzzle about futarchy that I can state but cannot really resolve. The proposal, as it was presented to us, is that a polity would fix a single measurable welfare metric — say, an index combining real consumption, life expectancy, and a penalty for inequality — and then let prediction markets decide which policies to adopt: for any proposed policy, traders would bet on the value of the metric under that policy versus its value under the status quo, and the policy would pass if the market's estimate of the difference were positive. The idea is supposed to separate values from beliefs: the metric encodes what we care about, and the markets do nothing but aggregate information about what will happen. What I cannot get straight is whether that separation is conceptually stable. I would like an explanation, pitched at someone who knows the rudiments of mechanism design but has never read the futarchy literature, of exactly what the conditional markets are supposed to be pricing and why that is harder than it sounds.

Could you walk me through the conceptual issue in stages? First, what a conditional market of this kind actually asks traders to price — the counterfactual difference between two policy worlds — and why the payoff structure has to be defined carefully for the market to have a well-defined equilibrium price at all. Second, the standard worries that a defender would have to address: that the metric is not an external thermometer but is itself affected by the policy and by the trading, so that a trader may profit by making the measured outcome move rather than by predicting it; that the same policy can change what the metric means; and that if the market is efficient there is no obvious reason for anyone to trade, while if it is not efficient there is no obvious reason to trust the price. Third, the deeper point I keep circling: markets aggregate beliefs, not preferences, so all the normative work is done by the choice of metric — which means futarchy has not so much solved the problem of political disagreement as relocated it to a prior question about who sets the metric and how it can be revised.

I would especially like the explanation to separate three things that our discussion ran together: the technical question of whether a conditional market can have a well-defined price for a counterfactual, the incentive question of whether traders would have reason to trade truthfully rather than to manipulate the metric, and the normative question of whether any single metric can legitimately stand in for the plurality of things citizens value. If the answer to any of these is that futarchy's defenders have a standard reply, please state the reply and then say how much it actually buys them, rather than just reporting that there is a debate. A concrete toy example — two policies, one metric, a handful of traders — would help me see where the conceptual joints are.
