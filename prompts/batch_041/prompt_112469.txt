In many jurisdictions, animal-welfare law forbids inflicting suffering on animals that is unnecessary or unjustified, permits killing them for food provided they are first rendered insensible to pain, and in some places exempts religious slaughter from the stunning requirement, while a handful of jurisdictions prohibit exactly that exemption. I am a PhD student in applied ethics, and I want to reconstruct the theory of value and of harm that this pattern presupposes. Does permitting painless killing commit the law to the view that death is not in itself a harm to an animal, or only that death is a comparatively minor harm that ordinary human interests can outweigh? Which rival accounts — deprivation views of the harm of death, animal-rights views, Kantian indirect-duty views, relational or care-based views — does the pattern rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Is it internally consistent: can the same welfarist premise that forbids suffering justify allowing unstunned slaughter for religious reasons, or does that exemption show that animal suffering is being weighed against human interests rather than treated as a wrong? Finally, where is the practice underdetermined — what is the thinnest set of commitments that would still generate these rules, and what would have to change if the law took a deprivation account of the harm of death seriously?
