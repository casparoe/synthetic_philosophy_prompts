For about fifteen years now I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums, and there is one recurring fight in that corner of the internet that has never once resolved. The vocabulary is Dennett's: cranes versus skyhooks, with greedy reductionism as the mirror-image sin. A crane is a sub-process that does real lifting work because it stands on something already built; a skyhook is an explanation that lifts itself; greedy reductionism is the move that tries to skip the intermediate levels and explain a high-level phenomenon straight out of the lowest one. Everyone in these threads agrees about the easy cases — nobody is out here defending vitalism, nobody thinks a life force earned its keep — and yet the same argument comes back every few months over consciousness, agency, meaning, and lately over whether so-called emergent abilities in large models name anything at all. The reductionist says: show me the crane, or admit you are positing a skyhook. The other side says: you have changed the subject; the thing I was asking about was never going to be a fact about the parts.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I want it argued from what the two sides actually say rather than surveyed. Is this a substantive disagreement about the world, a verbal one about the word explanation (or about the word emergence), two camps answering different questions under one heading — one ontological, one about what we can compute and predict — or a clash of starting intuitions about how much a satisfying explanation is allowed to leave brute, which neither side can argue for? My own hypothesis, and I would like it treated as a hypothesis rather than a premise, is that most of the heat comes from skyhook doing rhetorical work rather than classificatory work, so that in practice it means an explanation I do not accept yet, but that a real residue survives underneath: a disagreement about whether a brute fact at a higher level is ever acceptable when a lower-level account is available. I have been trying to sharpen that residue with a test I half invented, which I will call the compression test: a higher-level description is earning its keep, rather than begging the question, when it lets you answer questions about the system at a fraction of the cost of the lower-level description, given the same information. Is that the right shape for a criterion, or does it smuggle in an assumption the anti-reductionist would rightly reject?

Then tell me what progress would look like given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected. Continuous prose only, please: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just the way you would write it out in a long comment.
