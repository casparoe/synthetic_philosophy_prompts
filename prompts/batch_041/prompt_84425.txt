I am a senior at a public high school and I edit the satire page of our student literary magazine, which is putting together a philosophy-themed issue this spring; my contribution is meant to be a deadpan parody of an internal compliance document. I have been carrying around the paradox of knowability since our reading group went through it, and I would like to build the piece around it. As I understand the argument: start from the principle that every truth is knowable in principle — not that anyone knows it now, only that it could in principle be known by someone. Add two assumptions almost nobody wants to give up, first that knowledge is factive, so that if you know something then it is true, and second that knowledge distributes over conjunction, so that anyone who knows a conjunction knows each of its parts. Now take any truth that nobody knows. By the principle it could in principle be known, so there is a possible knower who knows both the truth and the fact that nobody knows it; by the distribution assumption that knower knows the fact that nobody knows it; by factivity, that fact is true; but the same knower also knows the truth, which is exactly what that fact denies. So the possibility in question is impossible, the truth that nobody knows cannot exist, and the principle that all truths are knowable appears to collapse into the claim that all truths are already known. One friend in the group insists the whole thing is an artifact of notation, which is part of why I want to see whether it can be made to run as bureaucracy.

What I want you to write is the paperwork of a world that takes this result seriously, rather than a discussion of it. Imagine a university, invented by me for the purpose, whose faculty has adopted the knowability principle as binding policy, and imagine the bulletin its epistemic-compliance office now circulates to staff, spelling out what the principle obliges everyone to do. Please write that bulletin in a flat, unexcitable bureaucratic register — no winking at the reader, no jokes that announce themselves, no rhetorical questions — and let the comedy come from the office following its own procedures honestly and arriving, clause by clause, at the conclusion that nothing is unknown and that there is therefore nothing left for it to do. The satire should land on the habit of saying that something is knowable in principle as if that settled anything, and on the way a merely possible knower is quietly treated as a post to be filled; but I want someone who knows this literature to find it fair rather than a cheap shot, so it should be plain that the office is doing exactly what the principle licenses rather than being stupid.

Please write it as exactly five numbered clauses, one or two sentences each, with no introduction, no framing and no conclusion: the document should begin with clause 1 and stop after clause 5. Then, kept clearly separate from the document and marked as your own comment rather than part of it, add a short note telling me which step of the argument the piece depends on most and where it takes liberties with the ideas; most importantly, tell me what would make the joke fail — if some standard way of restricting which truths the principle covers, or of changing the logic underneath it, would leave the office with real work to do, say so, because that is the thing most likely to deflate the whole piece.
