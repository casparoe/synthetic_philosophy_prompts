A newspaper I read has a rule for its reporters: a claim may be stated flatly only if it can be traced to at least two independent sources; with a single source it may be stated only as an allegation; and if the reporter's only ground is his own firsthand knowledge, the claim must be left out of the story altogether. The same paper's opinion columnists may assert whatever they please, with no sourcing at all. I keep wondering what theory of assertion this practice is built on. Does the reporting rule presuppose that one may assert only what is true, only what one knows, or only what one has adequate evidence for — and if it is really a knowledge norm, why does the paper forbid a reporter to state what he in fact knows? Does the latitude given to columnists show that the paper actually operates with a much weaker norm, or is opinion-writing a separate practice with its own rules? Or is the reporting rule not a norm of assertion at all, but a rule about what readers can be brought to trust, which would make it something else entirely? Please reconstruct the view the practice presupposes, separate what it strictly requires from what it merely fits with, tell me where it is underdetermined among the competing norms, and say what would have to change in the newsroom if the presupposed view were replaced.
