I am working on the metaphysics of material objects, specifically the problem of identity over time, and I want to use a real practice as a test case. The Ise Shrine in Japan, the Naiku and Geku, is rebuilt from scratch every twenty years on an adjacent, alternating site. The new buildings are constructed of new cypress wood on the empty site next to the existing ones. When they are complete, the deity is transferred from the old shrine to the new one in a night ceremony. The old structure is then dismantled, and in recent cycles much of its timber has been reused or distributed to other Shinto structures. There is no moment at which the old and new buildings are both occupied by the deity; during the transfer the deity is, as it were, in transit. The practice has continued for centuries, and the shrine is regarded as the same shrine throughout.

I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of the Ship of Theseus problem. Do not give me a survey of the literature; I know it. I want a careful mapping. First, state what the Ship of Theseus problem is actually asking, and distinguish the different criteria of identity that have been proposed for material objects: material continuity, spatiotemporal continuity, continuity of form or organization, and continuity of function. Then ask, for each criterion, whether the Ise Shrine survives the sengu. Does the shrine count as the same object under a material-continuity criterion? Under a spatiotemporal-continuity criterion? Under a form or functional criterion? Be explicit about what is doing the identity work in each case. Then consider the transfer of the deity: does that introduce a fourth criterion, something like continuity of a non-physical bearer, and if so, does that show that the Ship of Theseus problem was never really about physical objects in the first place? Or does it show that the problem is underdescribed, because it assumes that there is a single object whose identity is in question, whereas the Ise case suggests that the shrine is a composite of building, site, deity, ritual lineage, and community, each with its own identity conditions?

I also want you to consider the moment of transfer. During the night ceremony, the deity is moved from the old building to the new. Is there a moment when the shrine is neither the old nor the new? Or is the shrine the deity, so that the shrine moves? If the shrine is the deity, then the buildings are incidental, and the sengu is not a case of identity over time for a material object at all. If the shrine is the building, then the deity is incidental, and the shrine does not survive. Which of these is the more defensible reading, and what does it tell us about the Ship of Theseus? I suspect the episode shows that the puzzle is not a single puzzle but a family of puzzles, and that the answer depends on what kind of thing we take the shrine to be. But I want you to argue for a specific line.

Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the Ship of Theseus problem itself. Does it confirm one of the standard solutions? Does it reveal a case that the standard solutions cannot handle? Does it show that the problem is parochial, because it assumes a certain attitude toward material persistence that other traditions do not share? And does the fact that the Ise Shrine is rebuilt every twenty years, so that no one has ever seen the original, change anything? In the Ship of Theseus, the original ship is gradually replaced, and we are asked whether it is the same. In the Ise case, there is no original; there is only a lineage of replicas. Does that make the puzzle easier or harder? I want a disciplined answer that says where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
