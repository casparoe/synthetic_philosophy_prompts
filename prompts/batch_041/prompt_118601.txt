I am in a philosophy of science reading group that has been arguing about whether the special sciences classify real kinds or merely useful categories. Last week someone ended a long dispute about whether psychiatric categories such as major depression are natural kinds by saying that the whole point of science is to carve nature at its joints, so if psychiatry has not yet found the joints, that only shows the field is immature. I suspect that sentence is doing several different jobs at once and that the argument needed only one of them. I want a menu of the precise claims that slogan could be making, not a defense of any single one.

Please give me exactly five numbered readings, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. For each reading, state the thesis precisely, say whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a contentious metaphysical commitment, or simply false, and say whether that reading would actually support my colleague's dismissal of the psychiatric-kind dispute. The readings should range from the weakest or most innocuous to the strongest or most radical, and they should distinguish questions about whether there is a unique correct jointing of nature, whether science discovers or constructs the joints, whether joints must be essences or can be clusters, and whether the slogan is a descriptive claim about successful science or a methodological prescription for how to proceed.
