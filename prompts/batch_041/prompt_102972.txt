Assume the role of an expert in game theory and the philosophy of rational choice. I am an undergraduate in a course on decision theory, and I have tentatively adopted the following position: in a symmetric one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, superrationality gives the correct prescription—if each agent knows the other is symmetric and will reach the same conclusion, each should cooperate, even though defection dominates in the ordinary causal sense. I would like you to think carefully about what would count against this position. Begin by brainstorming the strongest objections, then present a ranked map of defeaters: which formal results, intuitions, observations, or practical consequences would be fatal to it, which would be merely awkward, and which a defender could concede without damage. Please also consider a variant that worries me: two agents are identical copies of the same deterministic program, each can inspect the other's source code, each cooperates only if it can prove the other's code cooperates, and each independently has a small chance of random corruption before choosing. Does superrationality still prescribe cooperation there, and what would the answer show about the view? Finally, say whether the position risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualifications, and tell me which of the defeaters I should personally find most persuasive. Do not give me a bibliography; I need the arguments themselves.
