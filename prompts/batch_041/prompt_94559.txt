So I've been reading alot about how wealthy families actually pass money down, and the tax system seems to believe two completely different things at the same time. Let me lay out what's bugging me.

In the US, when you inherit assets, your cost basis gets reset to whatever they were worth on the day the person died. So all the capital gains that built up over their lifetime just vanish — the heir can sell the next morning and pay nothing on the appreciation. But if the person had sold those same shares a week before dying, they'd have owed tax on the exact same gain. So dying erases a tax liability that living would have triggered.

Meanwhile there's an estate tax, but the exemption is now so high that only a tiny sliver of estates ever owe anything, and if you're rich enough to care you don't pay it anyway. You put the money in a dynasty trust in one of the states that abolished the old rule against perpetuities — the common-law rule that a trust can't tie up property past a certain point, roughly 21 years after the last person alive when the trust was created. South Dakota is the famous one. Now the money sits there across generations, outside the estate tax, outside the generation-skipping tax, and usually outside the heirs' divorces and creditors too. Or you park it in a family foundation, which only has to give away about 5% of its assets a year, so the rest compounds untaxed forever and your descendants keep the job of running the thing.

On one hand the system says: you can't take it with you, and we care about equality of opportunity, so we're not going to let a kid be born with a head start they did nothing to earn. On the other hand the system has been meticulously engineered, by lawyers, on purpose, so a family can keep a fortune intact for many generations.

What I want to know is what picture of the world this whole arrangement presupposes:

- What does it have to believe about dead people and their wishes? That the dead have a genuine claim on their property, that what they wanted matters after they're gone? Or is the whole apparatus of wills and trusts resting on something else — property rights that simply outlive the owner, or just a convention we could swap out without losing anything?

- What does it have to believe about the family as a unit? Is there an implicit assumption that parents are entitled to advantage their own children and that this trumps the interest in equal starting points? If so, how does that square with all the equality-of-opportunity talk — does the code just not take that talk seriously, or is there some actual theory about where the line sits?

- Is there a coherent story about desert in here? People say 'they earned it, they should be able to leave it to whoever they want.' But the money that passes untaxed isn't the earned income, it's the untaxed appreciation, and the person receiving it did nothing at all. So which claim is the practice really presupposing — that the earner's entitlement stretches past death, or that the heir's entitlement comes from being the one the owner picked?

- And the mismatch between the two halves: if the system really believed in equal opportunity, why allow perpetual trusts at all? If it really believed in dynastic freedom, why have an estate tax in the first place? Is the estate tax doing some other job than the one its defenders describe?

Then tell me which of these commitments the practice strictly needs and which it merely fits comfortably with — I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined, that you could keep the same trust law under a few different theories. And if you swapped out the idea that the dead's wishes carry weight, what would actually have to change? Would we stop enforcing wills? Tax everything at death? And would a lifetime accessions tax — tax each person cumulatively on everything they ever receive as a gift or inheritance, instead of taxing the estate — follow from a different set of commitments, and if so, which ones?

No need for citations or reading lists, I just want the reasoning.
