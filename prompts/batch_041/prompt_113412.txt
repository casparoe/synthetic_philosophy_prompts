The question:

Set out the demandingness objection to consequentialism, consider its relation to the other standard objections to the theory, assess the strongest reply available to the consequentialist, and say how far the objection succeeds.

The candidate's answer:

The demandingness objection is the charge that consequentialism asks more of us than morality can legitimately ask. The clearest statement of the difficulty is Singer's argument about famine relief. Singer holds that if it is in our power to prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, then we ought to do it, and that this principle is unaffected either by distance or by the fact that others are also in a position to help. Applied to global poverty, it implies that we ought to give until we reach the level of marginal utility — that is, until giving more would leave us as badly off as those we are helping. On that standard most of us are acting wrongly most of the time, and our ordinary concerns, such as a career, a home, or time with our families, turn out to be so much wrongful self-indulgence.

The objection is not that consequentialism gets its facts wrong. It is that a theory which condemns almost everything we do cannot be a correct account of morality, since we have reason to reject a moral code that leaves no room for a life of one's own. Understood in this way, the objection is really a version of the one Rawls presses against utilitarianism, that it does not take seriously the distinction between persons: the theory aggregates welfare across everyone and then treats the sum as a single quantity to be maximised, so that the agent's own life has standing only as a contribution to the total. Williams makes a related point when he argues that utilitarianism alienates the agent from his own projects and so destroys his integrity.

Consequentialists usually reply that the objection only touches the act-based version of the theory. Rule-consequentialism selects rules by asking which set of rules would maximise welfare if generally accepted, and any set that survived that test would contain permissions to favour one's own projects, because a morality of constant self-sacrifice would not be generally accepted and so would not maximise welfare. The demandingness objection therefore tells against act-utilitarianism, but not against consequentialism as such, and it does not show that consequentialism is false.
