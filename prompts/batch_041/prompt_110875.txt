Ok so this is going to be a bit of a mess but bear with me. My dad has early-stage dementia (nothing catastrophic yet, he still drives, he still beats me at chess, but the writing is on the wall) and I spent last weekend helping him redo his advance directive. Then, because apparently I'm a masochist, I went home and wrote my own, even though I'm 38. And ever since I keep going in circles on the ethics of the thing, so I typed up an argument and I'd like you to tear it apart.

Here's the gist of what I wrote:

Everyone treats an advance directive like a will — the earlier, competent you gets to dictate terms to the later, demented you. The usual justification is that the earlier you is the real you, the one with autonomy, and the later one is just... downstream. But that's begging the question. If my dad at 68 and my dad at 82 sincerely want different things, the 68-year-old isn't automatically the authentic one just because he can still do long division. We don't normally let past selves bind future selves. If I promise myself in January that I'll go to the gym, and in June I genuinely don't want to, nobody thinks June-me is being oppressed by January-me. He's just a guy who changed his mind.

Worse, if the later self really isn't the same person, then the directive is a dead man trying to run somebody else's life, and you can't be wronged by a broken promise when there's no longer a you to be wronged. So the standard story seems to collapse either way: either the later self is the same person (in which case, why do the earlier preferences win?) or it isn't (in which case, why does the document bind?).

So I tried to put the authority somewhere else, and this is the part I want you to attack. My claim: the directive's authority doesn't come from the earlier self at all. It comes from the people who have to read it. The only reason the document does anything is that a doctor or a spouse picks it up and feels bound by it. Which means what you're really doing when you sign one is conscripting other people to enforce it and relieving them of the burden of deciding. That's the actual moral work being done, and it's a promise you're making to them, not a command you're issuing to yourself.

Two things fall out of that. First, a decay clause: the directive loses force over time unless it's re-affirmed while you can still do it — maybe reading it aloud once a year in front of a witness, so it stays a live promise instead of a fossil. Second, a narrative veto: if the later, diminished you can actually give an account of the change — I know I said no dialysis, but here's why I've come around — the directive stops binding. Not because the later self automatically wins, but because being able to give an account of yourself is the thing we're actually trying to protect. My dad, further down the line, probably won't be able to do that. That's sort of the point.

The part I'm least sure of: the burden-relief move might be exactly backwards. Maybe conscripting your family into being your enforcers is the harm, not the service, since they're the ones who have to live with it after you're gone. I can't tell if that kills the whole thing or just means the decay clause needs to be stronger.

So: does this hang together, or have I just reinvented paternalism with extra steps? Where's it weakest, and which bit is doing the most unearned work? And what would change your mind — what would you have to find out, or what argument would you have to see, to think the decay-clause-plus-narrative-veto setup is actually better than a plain old directive, or worse? I'd rather know where your own reasoning is most likely to be off than get a pep talk, so please flag that too. Also sorry this is so long, my wife says I do this.
