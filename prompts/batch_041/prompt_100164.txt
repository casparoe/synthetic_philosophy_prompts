I am a doctoral student working on the ethics of collective harm, and before I commit to defending any particular account, I want to get clear about the standard of adequacy itself. So please give me the desiderata: what must a theory of individual moral responsibility for harms that no individual can bring about alone actually deliver in order to count as adequate? I want each requirement stated, the case or consideration that motivates it attached to it, and a judgment about which requirements are genuine and which are merely the prejudices of one camp. The familiar cases I care about are a single vote in a national election, a single portion of meat in a global market, and one person's annual carbon emissions, but I want the list built so that it also handles cases I construct myself.

Two of those constructed cases matter most to me. In the first, an individual's act is causally necessary for a harm, but only because an independent and entirely predictable process would have produced the same harm moments later, so the harm is overdetermined across time and no lasting difference turns on what the individual does. In the second, the individual's act is one of a vast number, none of which is necessary, and the harm is a non-threshold accumulation that is nonetheless discontinuous, appearing only once the accumulation crosses a level that no one can locate in advance. A theory that happens to get the familiar cases right may well fail these, so I want them built into the requirements rather than treated as afterthoughts. I am also interested in the asymmetry that arises when the very same act is harmless in isolation but wrong in concert with others, and in whether a theory must explain that asymmetry or may simply stipulate it.

Take the purpose to be action-guidance for an individual agent who reasonably expects the collective to keep failing; adequacy is relative to that purpose, so requirements that only make sense for a theory of collective blame should be marked as such. I would like you to flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and to say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive accounts. Then, briefly, indicate how the main families fare against the list: accounts that locate responsibility in causal contribution, in complicity with a collective, in one's fair share of a collective enterprise, and in the character that a contributing act expresses. I do not need an exhaustive scorecard; I want to see where the list itself is doing the work.

One constraint on the answer: write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a colleague setting out an argument in a letter. And please do not tell me what to read or cite; I want your own reconstruction of the requirements, not a survey of the literature.
