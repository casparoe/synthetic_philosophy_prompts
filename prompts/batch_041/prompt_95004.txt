In a decision-theory reading group last week, a colleague shut down an objection of mine with a slogan she repeats constantly: it can never hurt to know more. My objection was a courier who would rather not learn the names of the people in her network, because she knows that if she is caught and interrogated she will give them up; the information is free, she is under no obligation to receive it, and receiving it leaves her worse off. My colleague said the slogan is a theorem: take an agent who maximizes expected utility, who will receive the information at no cost, who will update on it correctly, and who will then do whatever is best in light of it; then her expected utility, calculated in advance, is never lower than it would have been without the information, whatever the information turns out to say. I could not tell whether that answered me or quietly changed the subject.

Lay out the distinct precise claims that "it can never hurt to know more" could be making — from the readings that are true but nearly empty, through the ones that are substantive and defensible, to the ones that are false — and say which of them her theorem establishes, which of them my courier case refutes, and which one she has to be relying on if the slogan is to do the work she was making it do. What I most need sorted out is what "hurt" is being measured by, in whose utility, and what sense of "more" is in play.
