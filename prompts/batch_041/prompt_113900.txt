I built an argument for a reading group and now I can't get it out of my head, because I can't tell whether it's a good objection to something or a good argument for something awful. Here it is.

(1) Being rational just is being a good Bayesian: you have a prior over the possible states of the world, and you update it on your evidence. So if two people are both rational in that sense, they can be modeled as starting from the same prior and updating on different evidence.
(2) There's a theorem here that I've checked and I beleive: if two people share a prior, and it's common knowledge between them what credence each assigns to some proposition, then their credences in that proposition are identical. Not close. Identical.
(3) When two people argue in public -- a seminar, a journal, a podcast -- each states a credence and each hears the other's, and if nobody's asleep at the switch then each knows the other heard, and knows the other knows, and so on up. So their credences become common knowledge.
(4) So two people who publicly and persistently disagree about a factual question can't both be rational. One of them must be starting from a different prior, or botching the update.
(5) But that's nuts. A colleague and I have read all the same papers on the same drug and we sit at 0.7 and 0.35 and have sat there for years, and neither of us thinks the other is a fool or a sloppy reasoner. Multiply that by every field there is.

I'd rather keep (2), a theorem is a theorem, and I'm not budging on (5). So which step do I give up? And tell me the price: if I give up (1), what am I left with once rational people are allowed to start from different priors, and if I give up (3), what exactly am I claiming about how much two people arguing in public really know about each other?
