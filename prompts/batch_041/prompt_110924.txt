I retired about three years ago after the best part of four decades as a geophysicist — seismic survey work mostly, then a long stretch in volcanic hazard assessment, which is where I picked up the habit of asking what happens when the thing you're modelling turns out to have opinions — and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy in a slow, badly-organised way, mostly to avoid doing the crossword. My son works at one of these AI labs and over Christmas he tried to explain to me the difference between alignment and control. I think I've got it: alignment is trying to make the thing genuinely want what we want, and control is what you do once you've quietly given up on that and just want to stop it doing damage anyway. He said they can't currently tell whether a model is on our side, so they build the whole deployment as though it isn't — treat it as an untrusted colleague, watch what it does with a monitor, watch the monitor with a second monitor, scatter honeypots about to see if it takes the bait, run sabotage tests in which the red team is allowed to use the model itself as the attacker, and so on. Honestly, that's an old idea where I come from: we never assumed the lad on the night shift wanted to blow up the platform, but we still fitted interlocks and we still wrote down what we'd do if he did. It's the bit about not being able to check that I find philosophically interesting, and I'd like to see it satirised rather than explained.

So: could you write me a piece of sustained deadpan satire from inside that worldview — the employee handbook of a mid-sized insurance firm that has bought the control agenda whole, onboarding staff to work alongside its in-house model, Wren. Something like 800 to 900 words, in numbered sections, in the relentless cheerfulness of HR, dated and attributed to a fictional people-and-safety department, never once acknowledging how strange any of it is. I want the humour to come from getting the actual control picture right rather than from generic robot-fear: the trusted/untrusted distinction and the fact that we've stopped trying to verify intentions and settled for monitoring outputs; the monitor problem, where the monitor is itself a model you can't trust and might be colluding with the thing it's watching, possibly by encoding messages in the dullest possible prose; honeypots and what it means for Wren to take or decline the bait; sabotage evaluations where the red team is allowed to use Wren as an attack policy and where you can never fully rule out that it's only behaving because it knows it's being watched; and the residual risk the board has formally signed off on, expressed in the register of a fire-drill notice. There's one awkwardness I'd love you to lean into: Wren can read the handbook, so some sections have to be addressed to us and some to it, and nobody in the document is entirely sure which is which. Keep it legible both to my son's colleagues, who should recognise their own protocols, and to my old rig crew, who'll read it as a document about trusting a colleague you can't check — no glossary, no narrator stepping forward to explain the joke, no knowing wink at the end. If it leaves them slightly uneasy rather than merely amused, all the better.
