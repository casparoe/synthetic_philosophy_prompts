I'm preparing for an oral exam in which I have to defend a higher-order theory of consciousness against a hostile examiner, and I'd like to rehearse with someone who can argue the position better than I can. Please play a committed defender of the higher-order thought theory, on this understanding of it: a mental state is conscious just in case it is the target of a suitable higher-order representation — a thought to the effect that one is in that very state — where that higher-order thought is itself typically unconscious. Play it straight: sharp, not a caricature; concede minor points when they are genuinely minor, but never the central claim; do not name any philosophers, books, or papers unless I name them first, since I want to see whether the position can be held up without appeals to authority; keep each reply to about two paragraphs so it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture; and stay in character until I say "stop". If you need a fact about the imagined system, ask me for it in character rather than quietly assuming one.

Here is my opening move. Call a higher-order representation committal when its content is that I myself am now in state S, and merely ascriptive when it registers only that a state of type S is occurring — the way a smoke detector registers smoke without registering it as anyone's. Now imagine a system that has genuine states of type P, the sort of thing that in us is pain, together with a monitoring subsystem that tokens ascriptive representations of them: <a state of type P is occurring>, full stop, with no self-reference anywhere in the content. On your theory the pain is conscious, because it is the target of a suitable higher-order representation. But nothing in this system represents the state as its own, and a state that is not felt as mine does not seem to be felt at all. So you must either say that this system is in pain — which looks like letting a smoke detector hurt — or insist that the higher-order thought has to be committal, in which case your account of what makes a state conscious has quietly imported self-representation, and I want to know what the "I" contributes that a plain representation of the token state does not already contribute. Which horn do you take, and what does taking it cost you?
