I am a novelist, and for several years now I have sat on the editorial board of an annotated reissue series — a small line that brings out-of-print novels back into circulation with notes on those passages whose meaning readers have genuinely fought over. Volume by volume, the board decides which reading of a contested passage the edition will set as the text's sense and which it will banish to a footnote as a rival. We have ruled on enough of these cruxes by now that I can feel a rule forming behind our decisions, though I would be hard pressed to state it.

Our record, as well as I can reconstruct it, is this. For Ashby's The Long Orchard, the closing reunion is written straight, and Ashby said as much in an interview at the time of publication; we printed the sincere reading. For Okonkwo's posthumous The Harmattan Papers, the author left no notes, no letters, nothing, and we settled the disputed crux on internal evidence alone. For Lindqvist's The Glassworks, the crux turns on an image that recurs at three points in the novel, and Lindqvist's letters confirm the recurrence was deliberate; we printed the reading that image supports. And for Ferreira's Nightbound, Ferreira told an interviewer twenty years after publication that a scene we had always read as tenderness had in fact been meant as cruelty, and we declined to change the printed reading, on the ground that a late remark cannot rewrite a published book.

A new crux has now landed on my desk. The author's estate has turned up her working notebooks from the months in which she drafted the novel in question, and those notebooks show beyond any doubt that she intended a particular scene to be read as a cold, deliberate betrayal. The scene as written is not ambiguous in the ordinary way: nothing in its language, its structure, or its context signals betrayal, and any careful reader would take it for an act of loyalty. The notebooks are private, unpublished, and were unknown to anyone until last month.

Tell me what the board will decide, or, if our record genuinely underdetermines the case, what we ought to decide in order to remain consistent with ourselves. Set out the candidate rules our past rulings are jointly compatible with, say what each of them predicts about this crux, and give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it. Identify which of our earlier cruxes is the closest analog. And name the further crux — a case we have not yet faced — that would best separate whichever rules are still standing after this one, in particular the rule that lets a private, contemporaneous intention override the text and the rule that confines intention to what was publicly available to the book's first readers or else recoverable from the text itself.
