I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student putting together a proposal for a small summer research grant — roughly ten weeks of part-time work, with a working paper as the deliverable — and I'd like you to draft the proposal for me. Please take the role of a philosopher who works at the intersection of normative ethics and the formal theory of bargaining and exchange: someone equally comfortable with the question of what makes a value commitment binding and with the apparatus of trade and Pareto improvement. Think carefully before writing, and start by brainstorming the space of possible positions so that the proposal lands on a genuinely open question rather than a well-worn one. Here is my half-formed idea. When two parties with different preferences exchange goods, both can end up better off by their own lights, and this mutual improvement requires no shared ranking of the goods — only that each party prefers its own resulting bundle. It is tempting to say the same of moral compromise between people with divergent value systems: two parties with conflicting commitments can reach an arrangement that each regards as an improvement over no agreement, so that compromise is not a sad concession but a mutual gain, a kind of moral gains from trade. But the analogy seems to strain at exactly the interesting points. Exchange presupposes that the parties' rankings are fixed and that what is traded is fungible and alienable; moral commitments often look non-negotiable, incommensurable, or partly constitutive of who the agent is, and a compromise can require a party to accept something she regards as simply wrong — which by her own lights is not an improvement at all. What I want the proposal to nail down is what the "gains from trade" thesis about moral compromise actually amounts to, the conditions under which it holds and the conditions under which it collapses, and what turns on the answer (for instance, whether it bears on how we should think about toleration, political legitimacy, or the ethics of negotiation).

The proposal should do the following. First, state the central question so that it has a determinate answer, and explain why the two obvious replies — that compromise is always a mutual gain, and that morality admits no trade at all — fail to settle it. Second, motivate one or two distinctions that give the project its angle rather than the standard framing: for example, a distinction between compromises over what to do and compromises over what to value, or between alienable and non-alienable "goods," and say why that distinction is the promising lever. Third, lay out a concrete method for the summer, with a stage-by-stage plan and the kinds of cases and toy models I would work through — including at least one semi-formal model of a bargaining situation in which some "goods" are non-fungible or partly constitutive of the parties' identities — and say why this approach rather than just more conceptual taxonomy. Fourth, say what a successful result would look like and, honestly, what would count as the project failing or proving too ambitious for ten part-time weeks. Keep the scope realistic, close with a short paragraph on why the question is worth the summer even if the answer turns out to be largely negative, and please do not include a literature review or a bibliography — refer to positions and arguments as needed and I will add the references myself. Write in American English.
