Ok so I'm a grad student and I have to lead a session on Descartes' cogito next week for a second-year history of phil class, and before I open my mouth infront of them I want someone to check my own line on it, because I have a nagging feeling it doesn't hang together and I can't see where. Please act as a philosopher who works on early modern metaphysics and philosophy of mind, and work through this carefully and step by step rather than giving me a verdict at the top — I'd rather see the reasoning than the bottom line.

Here's where I've landed:

(i) The cogito isn't a syllogism. If you run it as whatever thinks exists, I think, therefore I exist, you're presupposing the very thing you set out to prove, so the certainty has to come from the doubting itself, from the act, not from a general premise.

(ii) What that act actually gets you is the existence of thinking, not of a thinker. All we're entitled to is something impersonal — thinking is going on here — and the I is doing no real work in the argument. I'm sure somebody said this first but I can't remember who.

(iii) Still, I think Descartes is right that the I picks out a simple, immaterial substance. Something has to be the one thing that's doing the doubting, and no composite or bodily thing could be the subject of a single indivisible act of doubt.

(iv) But I'm also pretty convinced by the bundle/reductionist picture — there's no ego over and above the stream of mental states, just a constructed sense of self. Parfit's stuff on personal identity seems obviously right to me.

(v) And I'd say the I in I think can't be misidentified: even if I'm wrong about myself in every other respect, I can't be wrong that it's me who is thinking.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by how severe they are, and for each one tell me the cheapest way to fix it and what that fix would cost me.
