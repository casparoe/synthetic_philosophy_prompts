I want one essay question on typogenetics—a toy formal system in which strings over a four-letter alphabet encode enzymes that operate on strings—and the DNA–Gödel isomorphism, which is the claim that the system illustrates how a string can encode operations on strings in a way analogous to Gödel numbering. The question cannot be answered by reciting the standard story about strings, enzymes, and self-copying. To force students past recall, build the question around the following modification, which I have invented for the exam. Call it autogenic typogenetics. In this variant, enzymes are not a separate molecular species; an enzyme is simply a strand that has folded into an active shape, so every enzyme is written in the same four-letter alphabet as the strands it acts on. The only primitive operation is complementary copying; all other operations—cutting, inserting, deleting, moving—must be defined as compositions of copying and folding. The question should ask students to assess two things: first, whether autogenic typogenetics is a genuine self-replicating system or merely a self-maintaining one; second, whether the modification strengthens or weakens the claimed isomorphism between typogenetics and Gödel numbering. Students should have to distinguish syntactic from semantic self-replication, explain what the isomorphism is supposed to preserve, and identify what an interpreter or external mechanism contributes that the system itself does not.
