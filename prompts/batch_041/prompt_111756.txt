I have sat for seven years on the determinations panel of a small institute that documents mass violence, and last night its chair — an old and close friend — and I argued about the case we must decide on Thursday long after either of us had stopped listening. I want your help in seeing what she is likely to do, and what the rest of us would be agreeing to if we follow her.

She writes our findings herself and has never set down the rule she applies. Our determinations, in the order we made them:

A state and its allied militias killed some four hundred thousand members of a religious minority over three years, while official broadcasts called for the community's disappearance. Genocide.

A counterinsurgency campaign killed some eighty thousand civilians in one province over five years. The dead came from every community in the province, and we found no pattern of targeting any group as such. Not genocide; crimes against humanity.

A government deported an ethnic minority from its borderlands, marched them in winter, seized their property and dispersed the survivors among the majority population; some thirty thousand died of exposure and hunger. Its planning files spoke of clearing the region of that population permanently. Genocide.

A revolutionary regime killed some two hundred thousand people it classified as class enemies — landowners, former officials, members of a banned political movement — in a campaign whose declared aim was to remake the society. Not genocide; crimes against humanity.

A state spent a decade dismantling a religious minority's institutions — its schools, monasteries, press, courts and charitable societies — banned its language from public life, imprisoned its clergy, and killed some five thousand of its members in two sweeps. Her finding runs ten pages on the institutions and one on the killings. Genocide.

Thursday's case is a small mountain people, some sixty thousand, with its own language and its own church, in a province a neighbouring state has governed for forty years. There have been no massacres and no famine. But over those four decades the state has banned the language from the schools and from public life, closed the community's schools, presses, monasteries and associations, forbidden its festivals, taken its farmland and forests for state timber operations, and moved its families, village by village, into towns where only the majority language is spoken. Its documents speak of modernization and integration, and no official ever wrote down an intention to destroy the community; but one provincial adviser did warn, in writing, that within two generations the community would no longer exist, and the policy continued. The closures fall on this community's institutions and on no others, while the majority's schools and cultural bodies have been expanded throughout the same period. The language is now spoken only by the old, and the community's common life is gone. Perhaps four hundred people died over the four decades from the dislocations — hunger on the roads, cold, untreated illness — though no one was killed for being who they were. The neighbouring state is a considerable trading partner of ours, its ambassador has already telephoned our board, and the community has no diaspora and no one to speak for it. I do not believe any of that weighs with her, but I notice it.

She has always rejected my account of her: I say she is Lemkin's rather than the lawyers', that she counts the destruction of a people's language, faith and common life as genocide even where no one is massacred; she says she applies the law and nothing else. Tell me what she will find on Thursday. Be specific about the rule she is running: which of our earlier determinations it accounts for, which other rules account for those same determinations just as well, what each of them predicts for this case, which prediction you would bet on and on what grounds, and what further case — not this one — we would have to see before we could know which rule she has been running all along. If her record genuinely does not settle Thursday's case, say so plainly, and tell me what we would be committing ourselves to either way.
