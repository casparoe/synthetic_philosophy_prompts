I am in a graduate seminar on the ethics of memory — whether there is ever a duty to remember, and what could justify a right to forget — and I have spent two months arguing about it with a friend who thinks the whole notion of a duty to remember is sentimental claptrap dressed up as ethics. That has pushed me to stop choosing sides in the abstract and to try to work out where I actually stand by going through cases. I would rather end up with a principle that survives the cases than one that flatters my instincts, so I am asking you to be willing to tell me that I have been inconsistent.

My verdicts are these. (1) A city's founder paid for its library and its hospital, and also led a militia that burned a settlement of a few hundred people: I think the statue comes down, and that the good he did does not oblige the city to keep it up, but I also think the endowment should be kept and the library may keep his name — this is where I first feel unsteady. (2) A family learns that a grandmother they all loved passed information to an occupying army about neighbours, some of whom were deported, and one cousin wants to burn her diary: burning it is wrong, it should go to an archive or at least be kept, but the family is not obliged to publish it or to tell anyone outside the family. (3) A survivor of a massacre has spent thirty years organizing a public reading of testimony at the site each year; her grandchildren, who grew up in the town, want the readings to stop, saying they were conscripted into a grief that is not theirs — I think they are entitled to stop and that no one can compel them, even granting that the readings do real work. (4) A national archive seals police files naming informers for seventy-five years, on the ground that the informers' grandchildren would be ruined: the sealing is wrong, the files should be open, and I do not accept the descendants' interest as a reason. (5) A town that took part in a genocide, and that has over three generations genuinely changed, wants to demolish the prison where people were held and put a primary school on the site — I think it should not, even though the school would be a real good. (6) A man nationally shamed at nineteen for a crime he has served time for wants the news coverage delisted so that employers stop finding it: he should get that.

What I keep circling is whether these add up to anything, and my tentative thought is that I am treating two different things very differently: keeping the record, and keeping the observance. The record — the diary in the archive, the files in the box, the name on the plaque — I seem to think must be kept more or less always; the observance — the annual reading, the statue in the square, the school trip to the site — I seem to think can lapse, and that people who were not themselves wronged are not bound to keep it up. But that will not get case (4), where what I want opened is a record and the objectors are people nobody wronged in the ordinary sense, or case (5), where I want something more than a record kept: I want the town to go on having to look at it. I also notice that the family in (2) may keep a record private while the archive in (4) may not, and I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction between private and public custodians or just sentimentality about families. So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that captures my verdicts, stated exactly rather than as a restatement of the cases? Give me the clauses, each tied to the case that forces it; tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best version has to overturn, since I would rather lose a verdict than keep something that only looks like a principle; and apply the result to two cases I have not considered — a museum that wants to display, without identifying anyone, atrocity photographs taken by the perpetrators, and a village that has decided, after open argument, not to mark the site of its own dead because it wants its children to have somewhere that is not a grave. After the prose, give me a JSON object with these fields: principle (a one-sentence statement of the principle you would defend, or 'none' if my verdicts do not support one); clauses (an array of objects, each with clause, motivating_case, and what_it_forbids); verdicts_on_new_cases (an array of objects, each with case, verdict, and why); overturned_verdicts (an array of objects, each with the case as I stated it, my_verdict, principled_verdict, and reason); and residual_tensions (an array of strings, naming the places where my verdicts still pull against each other after your repair).
