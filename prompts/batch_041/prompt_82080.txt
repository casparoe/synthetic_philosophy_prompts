I am a high school student applying to a semester-long independent research program in the humanities, and I need a short research proposal, roughly two pages, for a project I would carry out under a faculty mentor. The proposal is the deliverable here, not the finished project.

My topic is Turing's account of how a machine might come to think, and in particular the role he assigns to education. The idea I want to build on is his suggestion, in the paper where he introduces the imitation game, that we should not try to program an adult mind directly but should instead build a child machine and then educate it. What interests me is that this comparison to a child does a great deal of work in his argument while remaining almost entirely undeveloped. He says the child machine would be simpler than an adult one, that it should be educated much as a child is, and that rewards and punishments would have a part; but he never says what education is supposed to be in the machine's case, nor what it would mean for the machine to learn something rather than merely to be adjusted until its outputs look right.

To make the question tractable I want to introduce a distinction. Let us call one sense of education training and the other cultivation. Training takes the target behaviour as fixed in advance: the learner is shaped until it matches a specification the teacher already possesses. Cultivation treats the learner's own developing capacities as partly authoritative: the teacher cannot fully specify in advance what the learner will become, and good teaching consists partly in responding to what the learner turns out to be able to do. My hypothesis is that Turing's child machine can only be trained, never cultivated, and that this is not an oversight but a consequence of his behavioural criterion for thought. If the only evidence for thinking is performance in the imitation game, then the teacher has no grip on the machine's inner development except through its outputs, and the distinction between training and cultivation collapses. I would like the proposal to take a stand on whether that consequence is real and, if it is, whether it is a defect in Turing's position or an acceptable feature of it.

Please write the proposal so that it does the following: state the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer rather than being a general musing about machines and education; explain why the obvious readings of Turing leave the question open; describe the method, which I imagine as a close reading of the passages where he develops the child-machine comparison, followed by testing the training and cultivation distinction against the cases he actually mentions; say what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of whether this is doable in a semester by a student at my level, including what I would have to leave out. Please refer to positions and arguments rather than citations, and do not include a bibliography or reading list, since I will locate and add the sources myself.
