I'm writing a final paper for my metaphysics of science seminar on dispositions, and I've arrived at a conclusion I'd like to check before I commit to it. As I understand the literature, the counterexamples involving finks and masks show that a disposition ascription cannot be analysed as a simple counterfactual: a fragile glass may fail to break when struck because it has been wrapped in foam, a live wire may fail to deliver a shock because a device cuts the current as you approach it, and a sturdy object rigged to shatter on impact may break when struck despite not being fragile at all. I've also devised a case of my own that I think reinforces the point: a metal bar that is not fragile, but turns brittle the instant anything approaches to strike it, because the approach itself triggers a phase change, so it would break if struck. But it seems to me the lesson must be stronger than the failure of one analysis. If a disposition can be present without its manifestation, and absent with it, then "fragile" and "soluble" don't pick out any genuine feature of the object at all; they are shorthand for what happens under conditions we could never specify non-circularly. My thesis, then, is that the fink and mask cases amount to an argument against dispositional realism, and that a serious metaphysics should treat dispositional predicates as useful fictions. Am I right about this? End your answer with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence whether the inference I have drawn is sound.
