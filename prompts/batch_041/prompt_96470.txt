I spent most of last weekend in a long and inconclusive argument with a friend who now works on risk modelling at a bank, and I would like a careful treatment of the underlying issue rather than an adjudication of who won. The disagreement was about the rating of collateralised debt obligations in the years before 2008 — the practice of assigning a top rating to a security on the strength of a model whose inputs were thousands of separate loan-level assumptions, each of which the model treated as very probably correct, with the rating then attached to the combination of all of them. My friend's position was that nobody involved need have believed anything false: each individual assumption could be well supported by the evidence available, the model could be internally coherent, and the rating could be the honest output of a defensible procedure, so the failure was one of incentives and of the housing market rather than of anyone's epistemic standards. I said that this is exactly the structure that the lottery paradox and the preface paradox exist to expose — that one cannot coherently treat each of a very large number of individually probable propositions as established and then treat the whole set as established as well — but I could not say what the correct response to the paradox is, or whether this episode illustrates the paradoxes or instead embarrasses the way philosophers have posed them.

What I want from you is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. Take the pre-2008 ratings practice as the episode and the two paradoxes as the lens. Say what rule of acceptance the analysts' practice actually embodies, and whether that rule can be stated coherently.
