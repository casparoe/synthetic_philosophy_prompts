For several years I have taught a graduate seminar on the axiology of suffering, and every run ends in the same place: the session on the commensurability of pleasure and pain, which I schedule for the penultimate week, generates the most heat and the least movement. The dispute is not over cases. Everyone in the room agrees that a toothache is bad, that decades of mild contentment are good, and that certain experiences are so terrible that no ordinary good seems to answer them. What divides us is the shape of the value space. One camp holds that pleasures and pains lie on a single dimension — opposite in sign, continuous in magnitude — so that in principle any amount of one can be balanced against any amount of the other, however unevenly the scale happens to be weighted in practice. The other camp defends what I have been calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: below a certain intensity and duration of suffering, pains and pleasures do trade off on a common scale, and a small bad may even be outweighed by a large good; but once suffering crosses the threshold it becomes lexically prior to any amount of pleasure whatsoever, so that no finite heap of goods answers it, though greater suffering still outweighs lesser. The two sides appear to share every premise about which states are bad and every premise about the badness of suffering; they part only over whether the threshold is a real feature of the value relation or an artifact of how we describe our intuitions — and, relatedly, over whether an ordering that is lexicographic above the threshold can be represented by any real-valued welfare function at all.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I would rather it engage the arguments than survey the literature. Is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of value, a verbal dispute about how generously we are willing to apply the word 'commensurable,' or two camps answering different questions under one heading — one about how to compare two options facing a single agent, the other about how to rank total states of the world? If it is substantive, is the crux a formal or empirical question (does the threshold have a determinate location; can any lexicographic ordering be represented cardinally; does the arithmetic of 'no finite amount' even make sense) or a pair of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for and that each side hears the other as merely restating? My own hypothesis is that the impasse is mostly the latter, with a residual verbal dispute about 'commensurable' doing the rest of the work, but I offer that as a hypothesis and want it tested against what each side actually says.

Close by telling me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, a change in the question — or an argument that no progress should be expected here, and why. Be specific about which of the familiar moves on offer would genuinely move the two camps and which would merely be heard by each side as a restatement of its own starting point.
