I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-odd years as a soil scientist at an agricultural research station, mostly working on drought tolerance in cereals, and I've spent the time since reading philosophy and economic history, which I probably should have done decades ago. A while back I started volunteering a couple of days a week at a small humanitarian outfit that runs an early-warning desk on food crises. We put out short alerts when a region looks like it's sliding into a hunger emergency, and those alerts get read by donors, journalists, and occasionally parliamentarians, so the wording travels further than it has any right to. Here's the problem. Everyone on the desk is a generalist, including me, and the alerts keep sliding between two framings: a crisis gets described as a natural disaster (drought, flood, blight, locusts) or as something man-made, sometimes as a political crime. The framing changes who gets blamed, whether anyone picks the story up, and which funding lines open, and right now it's decided by whoever happens to be writing that morning, mostly on gut. I want to hand them a decision procedure they can actually run -- two hours per alert at most, no specialists on hand, the desk lead signs off.

So please build me one, in the spirit of Sen's entitlement approach -- the thought that people starve not because there's no food in the region but because their command over food collapses, through their endowments (land, labour, livestock, savings) and what those can be exchanged for (wages, prices, transfers, access to markets) -- but written so that someone who has never read a page of Sen can use it. A fixed sequence of questions, each with a clear description of what counts as a sufficient answer, and at each step a note on whether we're asking an empirical question or have already wandered into a value judgement. The distinction I care most about, and that I think people on the desk keep collapsing, is between what caused the shortfall of food and what caused the famine: a blight can be the first and a government's refusal to move grain the second, and I want the procedure to force that separation out into the open rather than letting it hide inside a single verdict. It needs to handle at least: a crisis where the food is physically present but priced out of reach; one where a government is actively obstructing relief; and one that's mostly weather but where the response made things far worse -- I suspect most real cases land in that last bucket and I don't want a fake tie-breaker that just picks a side for tidiness. And tell me precisely where the procedure has to stop and hand the call to a person; I'm fairly sure that whether to use the word crime, and whether to impute intent to named officials, can't come out of a checklist, but I want to know exactly which questions those are and what a human has to supply.

Then run the thing on three cases and show me the audit trail: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward in China. For each one, walk through the questions, give the verdict, and -- this is the part I actually need -- flag where the verdict rests on a causal claim that people who work on this still fight about. The death tolls are obviously contested, I don't need that pointed out; I mean things like how much of the Bengal mortality was entitlement failure as against a real shortfall in rice, or how much of the Chinese catastrophe was weather as against policy, or whether the Irish case is better read as ideology, or as the design of the poor law, or as simple administrative delay. Where the procedure lands somewhere the three of us on the desk would find uncomfortable or contrary to our instincts, say so instead of smoothing it over. And throughout, keep what's settled among people who actually study famine clearly apart from what's genuinely contested -- I'd much rather have a procedure with a visible fault line in it than a confident one that makes a live disagreement sound like the consensus.
