I'm in a computational creativity seminar and we spent a week on the Letter Spirit idea -- the one where a program has to design a whole alphabet in a consistent style, and the real puzzle is supposed to be what makes every 'a' an 'a' when the shapes vary so much from typeface to typeface. After class my friend, who's a graphic design MFA and much more confident than me, gave me his summary of what the project was actually claiming. He said: the program was basically trying to find the abstract skeleton of each letter, and style is just the surface stuff you lay on top -- serifs, stroke contrast, terminal shapes, that kind of thing. So an 'a' is an 'a' because it has the correct skeleton, and a typeface is just a family of surface variations on the same set of skeletons. He also said that when the program generated a letter that was hard to recognize, that showed the letter wasn't really an 'a' at all, because category membership is all-or-nothing and determined by the skeleton. And he concluded from this that letterforms have a style-independent essence, which is why no typeface, however weird, can make an 'a' stop being an 'a'.

Something about this feels off to me. I know enough about type design to know that a Didone 'a' and a blackletter 'a' and a geometric sans 'a' don't seem to share one clean skeleton, and I've heard people say that style can go "all the way down" and change what counts as the letter. But I don't know the actual Letter Spirit arguments, and he's the one with the design degree. Can you audit his summary for me? Which parts would someone who actually worked on the project accept, which are common oversimplifications, and which are just wrong? In particular, does his conclusion about a style-independent essence follow from anything true in the summary, or is it the kind of thing the project was designed to question? I'd rather have a claim-by-claim breakdown than a general "it's complicated."
