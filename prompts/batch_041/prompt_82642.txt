I host a small interview podcast about practical ethics, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending the position usually associated with John Taurek: that when you can save either one person or five, and cannot save all of them, the fact that there are five does not by itself give you a reason to save the five, so that saving the one is not wrong. She is unusual among defenders of that line in two ways I want the interview to bring out. First, she insists the argument is not about death in particular — it applies to any situation in which two sets of claims cannot both be satisfied, including cases involving much smaller harms. Second, she thinks the position has concrete consequences for how rescue decisions should be made under uncertainty and for the triage protocols that emergency responders and hospitals actually use, which is what got me interested in it in the first place; I am a software engineer by training, with no formal philosophy background, and I came to this through the literature on how engineering teams allocate scarce capacity under time pressure. Please take the role of a philosopher who works on the ethics of rescue and on distributive questions, and who knows this debate and its dead ends well. Before writing anything, spend a few lines mapping out where the pressure on a position like hers actually comes from and what the best available replies are, then draft nine to eleven interview questions, each with a one- or two-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: if the likely answer is a concession, the follow-up should ask what she is giving up; if it is a distinction, the follow-up should test whether the distinction does any work or is ad hoc. I do not need reading suggestions or citations — please keep particular papers, authors and dates out of the questions entirely, since I would rather the argument be visible in the wording than have me name something I cannot check on air.

I want at least one question on each of the following, because these are the places where I suspect a general audience will stop believing her, and I want her to have to work:

- the case where the choice is between certainly saving one person and having only, say, a one-in-three chance of saving five — whether rejecting the counting of numbers also commits her to rejecting expected-survivor reasoning, and what that would mean for anyone actually writing a rescue protocol;
- whether the argument is confined to cases where she lets someone die, or also covers cases where she would have to redirect a threat onto the one in order to save the five, and if it is confined, what is supposed to ground the asymmetry;
- the thought that the five can simply pool their claims and present themselves as one large claim, so that the choice is really between the one's claim and the five's joint claim — and what she thinks is wrong with that move;
- the lottery: whether deciding by equal chances is merely permitted or actually required, and what makes a lottery fair if the claims are equal and cannot all be met — in particular, whether equal chances quietly reintroduce a kind of counting that she has rejected;
- whether the view is one she would still hold from the position of the single person on the rock rather than from the rescuer's position, and whether she takes it to guide the agent, the bystander, or everyone;
- the unequal-harms case, where the alternative to saving one person from death is saving five people from a permanent disability — since her view seems to need the claims on each side to be comparable before it can declare them equal, and here they plainly are not;
- the iterated case: a standing policy, or a hundred separate incidents, each of which ends with one saved rather than five, and how she separates a single decision from a rule.

Keep every question in plain language — if a question needs a technical term, gloss it inside the question itself, because I cannot rescue a bad question live — and make sure the questions do not simply invite her to restate the position: at least one should make her state the strongest argument on the other side in her own words before she says why it fails, and at least two should turn on cases she has probably not been asked about before rather than the standard drowning-rock setup. End the document with a short note giving your overall confidence in the questions and in the answers you have predicted she will give, and identifying the single prediction you are least sure of, with your reason for hesitating there.
