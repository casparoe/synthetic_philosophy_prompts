I serve on the death-determination review committee of a large academic hospital network. We decide the cases the bedside teams escalate, and because we operate under an internal policy rather than a statutory definition of death, our own past rulings carry most of the weight. A difficult case is on next week's agenda and I want to work out my position before I draft the memo.

Our record, reduced to essentials:

1. An anencephalic newborn — no cerebral hemispheres, intact brainstem, breathing spontaneously — was ruled alive and entitled to ordinary neonatal care.
2. An adult after massive intracranial hemorrhage and herniation: absent brainstem reflexes, no respiratory drive, ventilator-dependent, heart still beating. Ruled dead. The patient displayed elaborate spinal reflexes, which the committee held irrelevant.
3. An adult whose circulation and respiration ceased after withdrawal of life support and did not resume within our observation interval. Ruled dead.
4. An adult with a continuous-flow ventricular assist device: no palpable pulse, no recordable cuff pressure, but awake and conversant. Ruled alive.
5. An adult in a vegetative state — intact brainstem, spontaneous breathing, sleep-wake cycles, no awareness — was ruled alive, with a note that the ruling would not change even if awareness never returned.

The pending case. An adult who, after a catastrophic insult, satisfies every element of our neurological protocol: unresponsive coma, absent brainstem reflexes, no respiratory effort on apnea testing. Yet the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary — structures that sit above the brainstem and belong to the brain but not to the brainstem — continue to function. The patient never develops diabetes insipidus, holds normal water and electrolyte balance without hormone replacement, and shows a preserved diurnal secretory pattern. The heart beats unaided. Assume for the sake of the case that the endocrinological findings are confirmed and genuine. The family has asked whether the patient is dead.

What I want from you:

- Reconstruct the rules that could be generating our decisions. Give me at least two that fit all five rulings, and say which candidate rules our record rules out.
- Say what each surviving rule predicts for the pending case.
- Give me your best guess at how the committee will in fact rule, and why. If the record genuinely underdetermines it, say so and tell me which way the balance of reasons points.
- Then tell me what we ought to decide if we mean to be consistent with ourselves: which precedent is the closest analog, and what principle we would be adopting if we declare the patient dead and what principle we would be adopting if we declare the patient alive.
- Name the further case — real or stipulated — that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, and explain why it does.
- Be explicit about your confidence and about where your reasoning is likeliest to break down. Tell me what would change your mind: which argument or finding, if it held, would push you toward the opposite verdict.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I will supply those myself. I want the reasoning.
