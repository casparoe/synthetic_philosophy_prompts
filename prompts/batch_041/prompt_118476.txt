I have been arguing about anthropic reasoning with a physicist friend, and I have ended up with a handful of verdicts that I cannot get to fit together. The two candidates I have been given are, as I understand them, that I should reason as if I were a random draw from the observers who actually exist, past and future, within some reference class of mine, and that I should reason as if I were a random draw from all the observers who could possibly exist, weighted by how likely each is to exist. I am told these are called the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions, though I care far less about the labels than about whether anything actually earns my verdicts. What I want is the principle, not the literature.

Here are the cases as I find them. First, rooms: I am told that a coin decided whether my hotel has ten rooms or ten billion, and that I was then assigned to a room uniformly at random; my door says 7, and I take that to be strong evidence for the ten-room hotel. Second, birth rank: I am told that I am roughly the sixty-billionth human to have been born, and the familiar argument says this should pull me toward the view that the total number of humans will not be vastly larger; I feel almost none of that pull, and my diagnosis is that I do not know which pool I am supposed to be a random member of — humans, hominins, mammals, observers — and that each choice yields a different and equally unearned conclusion, so the argument is at best one lottery ticket among several. Third, two cosmologies: two theories, equally likely at the outset, agree about everything I could ever observe, including the proportion of observers who share my evidence, but one says the cosmos contains a thousand times as many observers as the other; a philosopher announces, without leaving his chair, that the second is a thousand times more probable, and I think that is nonsense, since my existence tells me nothing about how many galaxies there are. Fourth, the copying machine: a coin decides whether a machine makes one copy of me or a billion copies, each with my memories and each going its own way afterwards; I am one of the results and am asked for my credence that a billion were made, and I find that I want to favour the billion-copy outcome substantially, on the ground that there are vastly more of me about in that case. Fifth, the long future: suppose the setup fixes my reference class for me, telling me that humanity either ends within a few centuries or spreads for a billion years with an enormous population, the two being equally likely at the outset; my excuse from the second case is unavailable here, and yet I still do not want the fact that I find myself early in human history to move me toward the short scenario.

My own guess at a principle is that I am entitled to treat myself as a random draw from a pool of observers only when the setup hands me the pool — a room list, a serial number — and not when I must choose it myself, and that the mere number of observers a hypothesis posits should never raise its probability, with the exception of the copying machine, where the extra observers are copies of me. I can see that this is two principles wearing one coat, and that the exception may be doing all the work. Please tell me what principle, or small set of principles, comes closest to earning all five verdicts; state each candidate precisely enough that I can see which verdict each clause was built to save; say what each implies about the long-future case and about a case I have not described that would separate the candidates; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts has to go. If the only way to keep everything is a disjunction, say whether the disjunction is principled or merely a patch.
