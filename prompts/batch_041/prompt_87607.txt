I'm putting together a short talk for a philosophy of perception reading group, and I want to do something a bit different from the usual Matrix-this, brain-in-a-vat-that. I've been rereading Berkeley for the first time since undergrad — the master argument, the whole business that you can't form the idea of a thing existing unconceived, because the moment you conceive it you've conceived it, and the slogan that to be is to be perceived — and it struck me that Toy Story is a much better Berkeley text than it has any right to be. My flatmate thinks I've gone a bit wierd, but hear me out: the whole premise is that the toys have full inner lives that we the audience are shown, while Andy only ever sees inert plastic, and they freeze the second a human walks in. So the film keeps insisting on a world of unperceived objects that nevertheless have their own perceptual lives, which is either the most Berkeleyan thing imaginable (everything that exists is a mind or an idea in a mind, no dead matter anywhere) or the flat contradiction of him (there are minds the argument never reaches, existing happily with no perceiver at all, which makes God look redundant).

What I'd like from you is a proper reading, not a cute one. Take the master argument seriously and say where the film does and doesn't line up. Is Woody the Berkeleyan, and is Buzz in the first film the naive realist who has to be converted — he thinks he's a real space ranger on a mission in a mind-independent galaxy, and the TV advert is basically his to-be-is-to-be-perceived moment? What keeps the toys in existence when Andy's at school, and does the film quietly need something like God, or does it get away with making the toys their own perceivers instead? I'm also curious about the attic-and-storage business in the third one and Jessie's backstory in the second, where being loved and then not being loved seems to function as a kind of existence and then a kind of death — very close to Berkeley, but with the theology swapped out for sentimentality. Someone in the group is bound to say the master argument only works if you equivocate on 'conceive' (imagining a thing versus understanding a proposition about it), so I'd like you to say whether the film pulls the same trick, since it keeps showing us toys we're told nobody is watching.

And please be honest about where the reading breaks down: the toys are meant to be physical objects in a physical room, and there's a whole gag economy about bodies, batteries, rips and repairs that a strict idealist shouldn't be able to make sense of at all. Don't just list parallels — tell me what the film dramatises that the argument on its own doesn't, and whether this is a fair reading of Berkeley or just me seeing idealism everywhere because I've been reading too much of it. Write it in American English, in prose.
