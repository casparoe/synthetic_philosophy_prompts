I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting the last touches on a feature about the liar paradox -- the old sentence that says of itself that its false. I want to close the piece with a poem instead of the usual wrap-up paragraph, and I've been staring at a blank page for two days, so I'm hoping you can bail me out.

Here's the stuff I keep circling in the reporting, in case it helps you get the shape of it. It isn't only the liar. There's its cousin, the sentence that says of itself just that it's true, and that one never contradicts itself, it just hangs there -- nothing in the language forces it to be true and nothing forces it to be false, so you can take it either way and not get in trouble. Then there's the idea of groundedness: a sentence is grounded when you can trace its truth all the way back down to boring facts about the world, and ungrounded when the tracing never bottoms out. The liar never bottoms out. Some people work it out as an endless revision: call it true and the next pass says false, call it false and the next pass says true, and it never settles down.

So: could you write me a pantoum? I'm thinking four or five stanzas, in the form where the second and fourth line of each stanza come back as the first and third of the next one, and the last stanza feeds back into the first, so the whole thing is a loop -- a poem that keeps saying itself, which is what I want the reader to feel the paradox is doing. Two things I really want out of it. First, the lines that come back should shift what they're doing when they come back -- same words, different job -- so the repetition reads like the flipping back and forth rather than just a chant. Second, I want exactly one line in there that's the ungrounded one: in the place it first shows up it should read as plainly true, and when it returns it should read as plainly false, and the poem shouldnt ever settle which one it is. If a logician reads it they shouldnt wince, and if my mom reads it she should get it anyway.
