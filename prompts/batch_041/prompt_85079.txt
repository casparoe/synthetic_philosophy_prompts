I hung up my lab coat about three years ago — 32 years as an experimental physicist, mostly optical metrology — and since retiring I've been mainlining philosophy and, more recently, the AI safety literature, which my wife finds funny given that I couldn't get a computer to print a pdf until 2019. Anyway, I keep running into a distinction that I'm pretty sure I understand and then, a day later, realize I don't: the one between "alignment" and "control." As I've got it, alignment is about getting a model to actually want the right things, or at least to be reliably disposed to do them, while control is about making sure that even if it doesn't want the right things it can't do much damage — you keep it boxed in, you let it write code but have a weaker model you trust more review the code, you throw out anything the reviewer flags, you cap what it's allowed to touch. Both sound sensible to me. But I can't tell whether these are two genuinely different kinds of enterprise or the same enterprise described at two different levels, and I'd like you to straighten out the conceptual issue rather than sell me on either camp.

Here's where the confusion actually lives. I've been trying to split control into two flavors. (a) Control that bounds what the model can do at all: permissions it doesn't have, capabilities it simply lacks, a sandbox with no outbound anything. (b) Control that bounds what it actually does by watching it and vetoing: monitors, reviewers, resampling until a trusted checker signs off, editing outputs after the fact. The first flavor seems to need no assumption whatsoever about the model's motives — it's just plumbing. The second seems to need a very particular assumption, namely that the model can't outsmart or quietly subvert the watcher, and that's exactly the assumption that looks shakiest in the case everyone is actually worried about. And then there's the part that really ties me in knots: the watcher is supposed to be "trusted," and as far as I can tell that just means we're assuming its aligned. So doesn't the whole control story bottom out in an alignment assumption one level down? And if it does, is control a seperate strategy at all, or is it alignment all the way down with a few extra procedural steps?

So what I want is an explanation of the concept, not a pitch: what is the real dividing line, if there is one, between alignment and control as research programs? Which flavor of control, if either, can be justified without assuming anything about what the model wants? And is there a coherent version of the second flavor that doesn't implicitly assume the misaligned model is bad at precisely the thing that makes it dangerous — that is, that it isn't already modeling the protocol it's being run through? I'm happy to be told my (a)/(b) split is confused, or that I've smuggled in a false dichotomy; I'd just rather hear why. No need for a reading list, I won't get to it.
