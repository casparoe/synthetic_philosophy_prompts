I'm in a second-year course on history and ethics (cross-listed with philosophy) and we've just finished the block on judging the past — Jefferson, Churchill, Columbus, statues, the whole thing. In my tute on Tuesday someone said 'well he was a man of his time, you can't judge him by our standards', the tutor basically nodded and moved on, and the discussion died on the spot. What bugs me is that the slogan was being used to settle something quite specific — whether the department should stop putting the guy's name on the building — and everyone acted like it settled that as well.

So I want a breakdown of what that one sentence could actually be claiming. I'm sure one reading is nearly a tautology (he held the beliefs his neighbours held — fine, so what), there's probably a much stronger and more interesting one, and I'd bet money one of them is just false. Lay them out as a menu instead of picking your favourite: for each, say exactly what it commits you to, whether it's plausible, what would count as evidence for or against it in a particular case, and — this is the bit I care about — whether it actually supports the conclusion the guy in my tute wanted (keep the name, stop the pile-on). The jump from 'he isn't really to blame' to 'so the plaque stays' looks like it needs an extra premise and I want to know what it is.

Two things to fold in. Our lecturer said something in passing about the defence maybe being about what he could have known versus about what the standards actually were back then, and I didn't follow it — does that cut across your menu or is it one of the items? And the reductio: if being a product of your time excuses you, doesn't it excuse everyone, including people doing dodgy things right now? The tutor said 'yes, but our standards are the right ones', which felt like it gave the game away, but I can't say why. Related worry: the defence seems to assume everybody back then agreed, and I'm not convinced that's usually true — if it isn't, does the defence collapse or just get weaker?

Don't tell me it's complicated, or that we should seperate understanding from endorsing; I've had that. Give me the list with verdicts, and tell me which reading the tutor was actually relying on. My essay's on Churchill, so run the menu against him as the worked case if that helps — but I don't want a rap sheet, I want to know which reading is doing the work.
