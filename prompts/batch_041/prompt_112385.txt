Write a poem of thirty-one lines on Zeno's arrow, in the voice of Zeno himself addressing Parmenides, in unrhymed iambic pentameter. Two formal constraints should carry the argument rather than decorate it, and I would like both obeyed exactly.

The first governs the lines. Every line must be a still frame: no line may contain a verb of motion, nor any noun, adjective, or adverb naming movement, change, passage, or haste — nothing flying, running, hastening, crossing, arriving, becoming, or their kin. Each line may say only what holds of the arrow at a time: its position, the bowstring's tension, the angle of the shaft, the distance still ahead, the face of the target. Nothing else. Yet the poem as a whole must move. A reader given only the sequence of lines must be able to reconstruct a single continuous flight from the drawn string to the target, and must be able to tell that the flight is continuous — that the arrow does not jump from one still to the next. The prohibition is lifted for the last line alone: that line, and only that line, may name motion, and it should be the poem's turn.

The second governs the shape. Let the stanzas halve as the poem proceeds — sixteen lines, then eight, then four, then two, then one — so that the poem's own body approaches its end the way the runner approaches the wall, and the last line stands where the limit would be.

As for what the poem must know, without ever stating it as a thesis: Zeno's arguments were not puzzles about infinity but weapons in Parmenides' war, meant to show that what is, is one and unchanging and that motion and plurality are the deceptions of the senses, so that the arrow's flight is not a fact to be saved but an appearance to be dissolved. The modern treatment of the paradox — that motion is not a state the arrow is in at any instant but a relation among the positions it occupies at different instants, and that its velocity is a limit rather than a fact at a time — must press on the poem's form and never appear as a sentence in its mouth. When the poem ends, a reader who knows the paradoxes should be unable to say whether the modern answer meets Zeno's question or quietly substitutes another; a reader who does not know them should find a good poem, and should be able to see the flight.
