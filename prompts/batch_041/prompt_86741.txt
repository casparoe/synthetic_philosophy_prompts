For twenty years my grandfather's foundation has given away roughly a million dollars a year, and since his death in March I have taken his seat on the grants committee. Our quarterly meeting is in three weeks, and I have been asked to circulate the written recommendation on the one proposal that has split us, so that we can vote without relitigating it from scratch.

The applicant is a small research group asking for $400,000 over three years to develop a technology they say carries a small but non-negligible chance — they put it around one in a million — of producing a future with vastly more lives than the present population of the Earth, and possibly without any finite bound. Their case is that the expected value of the grant is infinite or undefined, that no competing proposal can come close, and that consistency requires us to fund this one and nothing else until the matter is settled.

My colleagues have pushed back in four different ways. One argues that if space is infinite and roughly uniform, as our cosmology adviser says cannot be ruled out, then infinitely many people already exist and every outcome we might produce already occurs infinitely often somewhere, so nothing we do changes the total; he adds that ranking two infinite value-totals would require one infinity to be larger than another, which he takes to be a category mistake when the quantities are values rather than sets. A second says this confuses the size of the world with the reach of our actions: our grants affect finitely many people, so the comparison is between finite differences and the infinity talk is a distraction. A third worries that any rule under which a one-in-a-million chance of an infinite payoff beats everything would commit the whole endowment to the single highest-stakes lottery on offer, which she calls absurd — though she concedes that we already fund small probabilities of very large catastrophes and cannot say where the line falls. A fourth proposes a tie-break rule: treat any two outcomes of infinite value as tied, then decide between them on the finite, local differences we can actually estimate.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — fund, decline, or fund with conditions — state the date by which the committee should adopt a standing policy on grants justified by infinite or unbounded stakes, and say plainly which colleague has the strongest point and which the weakest. Engage the applicant's argument on its own terms rather than dismissing it, and do not make the cosmology the crux; I want to know whether the argument survives the objections and what we should do next quarter either way.
