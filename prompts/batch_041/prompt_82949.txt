I sit in a small reading group — two decision theorists, an economist, and me — that has now spent four sessions on the absent-minded driver problem without anyone moving an inch. I would like a diagnosis of why, rather than a fifth round of arguments.

The case as we state it: a driver is on a road with two successive exits and wants to take the second. He is absent-minded — at either intersection he cannot tell whether he has already passed one, and he cannot tell the two intersections apart. If he exits at the first intersection he gets 0; if he continues past the first and exits at the second he gets 4; if he continues past both he gets 1. So each decision point offers the same two acts under the same description, and the driver's act at the first intersection is, from his own point of view, the very same act he may perform at the second.

The positions in the group, roughly:

1. Policy first. The real object of evaluation is a policy chosen before the trip, and the driver should maximize ex ante expected payoff. On the numbers above, as I work it out, the best he can do is continue with probability two-thirds at each intersection (expected value 4/3); and if you let him mix over whole plans instead, one-third "continue, then exit" is worth 2 — but he cannot carry that out, since he cannot tell when he is at the second intersection. On this view, "what should he do when he reaches an intersection" is a question about executing a policy, not a fresh decision, and the standard equivalence between mixing over plans and randomizing at each decision point is simply unavailable to an agent without perfect recall.

2. Act first. A theory of rationality must be able to tell an agent what to do at a moment of choice; at the intersection the only live options are exit and continue, and a verdict on those two acts is what is wanted. This camp accepts the posterior over nodes (three-fifths first, two-fifths second) and then observes that the two acts come out exactly equal in expected value, so the theory delivers indifference rather than an instruction, and no act-level rule reproduces the 4/3 the policy camp claims. That is where we stall.

3. Dissolution. The case is not a decision problem at all: "what should he do?" presupposes that he knows which decision he faces. Since the two nodes are one node for him, the subject of the "should" is ambiguous — planner or time-slice — and the paradox is an artifact of writing one question where there are two.

4. Team. The two intersections are occupied by two agents with a common payoff, so the question is what is rational for the team, which need not coincide with what is rational for each member taken separately.

5. Theories of evidence. Someone always says the case vindicates, or refutes, evidential or causal decision theory, since the act at the first intersection is evidence for the act at the second; nobody can agree what either theory says here, because the two acts are the same act performed twice with the same information.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this is not a disagreement about the driver. It is a disagreement about what a decision problem is — a description of an agent at a moment of choice, or a description of an agent choosing a course of action — and the two sides are answering different questions under one name, each treating the other's answer as an answer to its own question.

Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or only that it is hard?
