My mate from a reading group has been on a Pascal kick and has built this argument that I cant stop thinking about but also can't accept. He says it's basically Pascal's view of justice and force, just cleaned up. I'm hoping you can tell me where it breaks, or if it doesn't break, which piece I should actually give up. Here it is:

(1) There's no standard of justice we can actually get hold of that's independent of the laws and customs we grew up in. Every appeal to 'natural' justice, or to what's obviously right, turns out to just be some particular society's habits in a nicer outfit. (His Pascal gloss: custom is the whole of equity, and only because it's accepted.)

(2) So there's nothing to measure a law against. When we call a law unjust, we aren't comparing it to any standard.

(3) So 'unjust' can't mean 'fails to meet the standard.' The only thing it can mean is 'isn't the custom I happen to favor.'

(4) Laws and customs are kept in place by force. A custom just is whatever pattern the prevailing force protects.

(5) So a law is just -- in the only sense left -- exactly to the extent it's backed by enough force, and unjust exactly to the extent it isn't.

(6) So any exercise of power that succeeds makes itself just, and any rebellion that fails was unjust by definition.

(7) So there's no such thing as an unjust law, only stronger and weaker ones.

That last bit obviously can't be right -- we know some laws are unjust, that's the whole reason the word exists. But I dont want to give up (1); I find it kind of convincing. I'd rather keep (1) and lose something else. So: which step do I drop, and what does dropping it cost me?

Can you lay it out in four bits, roughly like this: 'Where it breaks' (one paragraph, naming the step and saying why that one and not the others), 'Why it looked fine' (a short paragraph on why that step seemed so plausible), 'What giving it up costs' (a paragraph or two, and include what I'd pay if instead I gave up (1) or (3)), and 'Bottom line' (two or three sentences).
