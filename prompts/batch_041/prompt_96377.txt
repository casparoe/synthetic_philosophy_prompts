I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, drafting a chapter on the rule-following considerations, and I need a case study to anchor one section of the argument. The section deals with the dispositionalist response to the sceptical paradox — the proposal that what a speaker means is fixed by what they are disposed to say — and with the objections standardly pressed against it. Two of those objections are usually run together, and I want them prised apart. The first is the finitude objection: dispositions are finite and the rule is not, and for hard cases we are anyway disposed to err, so the dispositionalist's facts run out. The second is the normativity objection: a disposition to respond is a fact about what one will say, not about what one ought to say; it can explain a response but cannot justify it, and so cannot constitute correctness. If the second objection is sound, dispositionalism cannot make room for the distinction between following a rule and merely conforming to it, and therefore cannot make the possibility of a systematic mistake intelligible.

Please construct a thought experiment that neutralises the first objection by stipulation while leaving the second fully in force. Specifications:

- The subject is an ordinary finite human being. No supertasks, no infinite minds, no science-fictional devices. An explicit idealisation of the subject's dispositions (for instance, "the dispositions he would have if…") is permitted, provided it is flagged as a stipulation rather than smuggled in.
- The dispositions must be stipulated to be total: for every case the rule covers, the subject has a determinate disposition. The finitude objection must find no purchase whatsoever.
- The rule must not be arithmetical. No plus/quus variants, no number-series continuations; choose a rule of some other kind entirely.
- The case must not turn on the subject's ignorance. He must be able to state the rule correctly and to recognise the sceptic's reinterpretation for what it is.
- The verdict the dispositionalist is driven to must fall out of what dispositionalism is actually committed to, not be stipulated by the case. A case that simply announces "and here the dispositionalist says something absurd" fails.

Then, in the same response:

1. The scenario itself, in one paragraph.
2. The verdict each of three positions is committed to, and why: the plain dispositionalist (dispositions as they stand), the idealised dispositionalist (dispositions under the stipulated ideal conditions), and the sceptic.
3. Which single feature of the case does the work, and what happens if that feature is removed — does the case collapse back into the finitude objection, into the ordinary sceptical hypothesis, or into neither?
4. The strongest line of attack open to a dispositionalist who wishes to resist the case, stated as they would state it.

The whole response should be no more than 500 words. Write in British English.
