My reading group has been working through Machiavelli since January and one of us — an old friend, retired from the diplomatic service, extremely sure of herself — has settled into a stable set of verdicts about who had virtù and who was merely lucky or merely ruined, and I've been writing them down because they're weirdly consistent. On her ledger: Cesare Borgia had virtù, and she refuses to charge his collapse to him at all — it came after his father died and Julius took the papacy, and she puts the whole thing down to a stretch of fortune so extreme and so spiteful that no man could have stood against it. Savonarola had none, being a prophet with no arms of his own, and went under the instant the mood in Florence turned. Soderini had none, and this is the one that gets her heated: ten years of patience and humanity in Florence ending in exile, purely because he never once changed his mode when the times shifted underneath him. Fabius and Scipio both had virtù, which she uses to argue there is no single winning style, since the delayer and the gambler both came out ahead. And Agathocles, whom she'll grant was effective to a degree that frightens her, she says had no virtù at all, because virtù is not the same thing as getting what you want.

Now it's my turn to bring the next case, and I've deliberately built one she can't recite from memory: a long-serving head of a big, boring, heavily capitalised bank, twelve years of refusing every fashionable expansion, no scandals, no leverage, no fun, and one line he was known for internally, that his only job was to be the last man standing. Eighteen months before the end he was offered a merger that would have cost him his independence and saved the institution, and he turned it down on the grounds that the whole point of the place was not to become like everybody else. Then a run that nobody saw coming — not him, not the regulator, who had published a clean stability report three weeks earlier — takes the bank under in about ten days, and he's out while the operators he spent a decade lecturing are still standing.

Will she say he had virtù? What I actually want is the machinery: the rules her earlier rulings are running, which of those rules the earlier cases genuinly leave underdetermined, what each one predicts for the banker, which rule she most likely means and why, and what further case — his or somebody else's — would force the choice instead of letting her have it both ways. Please just write it out in continuous prose, like you'd explain it to me over a beer; no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And don't give me a general summary of what Machiavelli says about fortune, because I've read it — I want the verdict and the argument.
