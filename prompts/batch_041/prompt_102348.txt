I am a novelist, and the book I am at work on turns on a single afternoon in the late 1940s, when a young anthropologist, hired as a translator for the committee drafting what will become the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, is quietly asked to help her own discipline compose its formal objection to the entire enterprise — the argument, put to the United Nations by the American Anthropological Association while the Declaration was still being written, that a single code of rights is merely the parochial morality of one civilization dressed up as a universal one, and that to press it upon others is a fresh species of colonial violence. My translator is caught between the relativists who trained her and the drafters she serves, and I want her dilemma to be intellectually honest rather than costume. So I am asking you, not for sources or a reading list — I have no library and no time — but for a genuine case analysis that runs in both directions: use the contemporary research on cross-cultural variation in moral judgment, the body of work usually filed under the WEIRD critique, to illuminate what was actually at stake in that room, and then use the episode to test that research and the meta-ethical conclusion people draw from it.

I want you to map the dispute the way you would map a proof. Who were the parties, really — the drafters and the anthropologists, but also the states that abstained and the constituencies none of them spoke for? What did each side treat as its evidence, what did each tacitly assume about whether moral disagreement is disagreement about facts or about values, and what would each have had to concede to reach the other? Then bring the theory to bear, and be disciplined about it. The relativist case rests on the fact of variation — that societies condemn and permit quite different things — but I need you to separate two questions my characters keep collapsing: whether people's moral intuitions actually vary across cultures, and whether the truth of a moral claim could depend on a culture. Tell me how far the empirical findings carry the first question and how little they can do for the second, and take seriously the analogy that keeps surfacing in this literature — that moral intuitions may be like perceptual illusions, or like grammatical intuitions, a shared underlying competence with culturally variable surface performance — together with the suggestion that certain distinctions, between harm and mere convention, say, might survive translation where the substantive verdicts do not. Say whether the analogy helps or is a way of having it both ways.

Finally, turn the lens around. Does the episode confirm the theory, embarrass it, or present a case it never anticipated? I am especially drawn to the possibility that the universality claim was being made, in that room, by an unusually narrow and unrepresentative sample of humanity, and to what that does to the relativists' objection — whether it strengthens it, or whether it exposes that the objection and the thing objected to were both being argued by the same kind of person. Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak; where my translator's position cannot be resolved by any of this, tell me that as well, because that unresolved place is the scene I am actually trying to write.
