I'm in a seminar on global justice and after class on thursday two people in my section, Marta and Dev, got into it about whether we owe more to our fellow citizens than to strangers abroad, and it went on for like forty minutes and I genuinely couldn't tell who was coming out ahead. I gotta write a response paper on it and I'd rather not just pick a side — I want to know who's right about what. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move?

Roughly how it went:

Marta opened by saying partiality to compatriots is morally arbitrary in the same way racial or religious partiality is — you don't choose your country, and the fact that someone shares your passport is no more a reason to favor them than sharing your skin color. Dev said the analogy flops, because nationality (unlike race) tracks participation in a real cooperative scheme — you pay into the same tax base, you're covered by the same welfare and defense institutions — so favoring compatriots is earned by participation, not ascribed at birth. More like being loyal to a co-author or a teammate than like favoring your own race.

Marta said fine, but you didn't opt into the scheme. You're born into it, leaving is costly, and the benefits were basically foisted on you; fair-play style obligations need some kind of acceptance or at least a real chance to decline, and most citizens never get one. Dev said tacit consent plus the fact that the scheme is indispensable — you can't have justice at all without some coercive institutions, so requiring an enthusiastic opt-in sets the bar where no legitimate state could ever clear it. He also said her voluntariness bar would nuke family obligations too, and she probably doesn't want that.

Marta said family is different because it involves actual relationships — people you know, love, interact with — whereas compatriots are strangers you'll never meet, so there's no relationship for the partiality to be about. Dev said proximity isn't the point; what matters is institutional reciprocity, and you can be bound to co-participants you'll never meet, like shareholders in a company or members of a mutual insurance pool. That's where he brought in his own little model, which he called the insurance-club view of the nation — a country is basically a mutual insurance cooperative you're enrolled in at birth, and partiality to compatriots is just the internal logic of the pool.

Marta pounced on that: she said a real insurance pool sorts people by risk, not by passport — you'd happily pool with a low-risk foreigner over a high-risk compatriot — so the insurance model predicts risk-based partiality, not national partiality, and therefore doesn't justify what Dev wants it to. Dev came back that it's not about risk, it's about shared liability and mutual accountability — the pool is defined by who bears whose losses, and in practice that's your fellow citizens. Marta said then the duty just tracks whoever happens to bear your losses, which could be foreign creditors, future generations, or the migrants who staff your care homes, so the view proves too much and doesn't single out compatriots after all. Dev said something about democratic self-rule needing members to weight each other's interests, or the whole point of collective self-determination evaporates, and Marta said self-determination could be organized functionally without nationality, so that's not an argument for the nation specifically.

Then they both kind of ran out of steam and it ended with Dev saying she was ignoring how the actual world works and Marta saying he was dressing up sentiment as principle. I have to write a response paper on this and I want an honest scorecard, not a verdict for whichever side I like. So: go through it move by move, say who's right about which specific claim and why, and tell me what both of them are missing — especially whether the insurance-club model can definately be repaired into something that actually justifies compatriot partiality, or whether Marta's objection kills it. And who's more right overall, if that's even the right question.
