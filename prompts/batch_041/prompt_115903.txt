I'm midway through a graduate seminar on memory, nationalism, and the writing of history, and I've been trying to make sense of a public lecture I attended last month. The speaker was a well-regarded popular historian with trade books on the American Civil War and on twentieth-century Europe, and her thesis was that the "lost cause" is not a specifically Southern or American phenomenon but a general form of historical myth-making, of which the postbellum American South is only the paradigm case; Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk, she argued, are further instances of the same underlying form. What unites them, on her account, is a shared grammar: a catastrophic defeat; a claim that the losers fought heroically against overwhelming odds or were betrayed from within; a relocation of the struggle's real meaning away from its ostensible cause and toward the nobility of the sacrifice; and a muted promise of eventual vindication. She then made four claims about the individual cases. The Confederate Lost Cause, she said, was above all a work of mourning and sectional reconciliation, and the states'-rights reading of the war that we now associate with it was a later hardening, since the immediate postwar writing was mostly concerned to honor the dead. Masada had been a byword for Jewish heroism since antiquity, with the mass suicide of some nine hundred defenders fixed in the tradition. Kosovo, though it destroyed the medieval Serbian state, survived for centuries in oral epic and became politically explosive only when it was revived in the nationalist politics of the late 1980s. And Dunkirk, where the little boats famously rescued the army, was the clearest case of a military failure being successfully sold to the public as a triumph, though she did note, fairly, that Churchill himself had warned against treating the deliverance as a victory. Her closing line was that all national memory is myth, and that the honest posture toward any national story is to treat it as propaganda until proven otherwise.

I found the lecture persuasive at the time and I am still inclined to think the parallels are real, but I do not know these four literatures well enough to tell where she was speaking loosely and where she was simply wrong, and the last line bothers me in a way I cannot quite articulate. So I would like an audit rather than a reading list. Go claim by claim and tell me which parts a specialist in each area would accept as sound, which are defensible but overstated, which are common simplifications that a specialist would wince at, and which are false; where her four-case comparison papers over a difference that matters, say what the difference is; and then give me the strongest case for the memories she is debunking, since I suspect she would say there is none. Finally, tell me whether her closing claim actually follows from whatever survives of the rest, or whether it quietly needs a further premise she never supplied — and if it does, whether that premise is itself defensible.
