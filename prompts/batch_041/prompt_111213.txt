I run a decision-theory reading group — a dozen people, a mix of graduate students and autodidacts from the online rationality scene, ninety minutes, one whiteboard, no projector. I want a runnable activity that makes them feel the force of Kavka's toxin puzzle rather than recognize it. My aim is that they attempt, under a live incentive, to hold an intention whose payoff does not depend on their acting on it, watch the attempt fail in a way they can introspect, and then have to reconstruct why it failed before I name anything.

What I want them to collide with is not the bare claim that one cannot intend at will, but the finer structure underneath. In the puzzle the reward attaches to a state of the person that is evidenced by, but not caused by, the action the intention concerns; the intention cannot be formed for the reward because the future self who has already been paid has no reason to act, so any intention manufactured on the spot is a prediction one already knows to be false. I want them to feel the seam between three attitudes that introspection blurs — intending to do X, predicting that one will do X, and wanting to do X — and to discover that the puzzle's grip rests on a hidden premise, namely certainty that one's future self will not want to do X. Loosen that premise and the impossibility softens; that should be something they find, not something I announce.

Here is the mechanic I want the activity built around, though you should improve it if you can. Pair the participants. One of each pair is offered a reward to hold, at a checkpoint, a genuine intention to perform some mildly unpleasant act later; the other is the reader, who decides whether to release the reward on the strength of their read of the first. The reader stands in for the billionaire's judgment — a good human judge of people, and everyone knows it. The intender is thus trying to bring about a mental state for a payoff detached from the act, while the reader is trying to detect a state that, being detached from action, has no reliable behavioral signature. Then let the pairs run, let the intenders fail or succeed, and let them try to buy their way out with commitment devices of their own invention.

Constraints: no real money — tokens, snacks, privileges, or points, with the exchange rate stated; ninety minutes total including the debrief; offline and self-contained; and no citations, reading lists, or named literature in what you hand me, since I will be using this away from any library. The group should meet the puzzle in what happens to them, not in what I say afterward; they should not hear Kavka's name, or the word toxin, until the debrief.

Give me the rules and the full payoff scheme, the materials and room setup, a minute-by-minute timeline, the roles and any scripted moves for the reader and the facilitator, and the debrief. The debrief should map particular moments from the run onto the parts of the puzzle — the detachment of reward from action, the intending/predicting/wanting seam, the evidential reading of one's own future conduct, the role of commitment — and should show where the future-preference-uncertainty point enters. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where pairs will find a loophole, where a reader will feel coerced or squeamish, where someone will simply lie — and how to handle each without collapsing the activity into a lecture. Finally, be honest about which feature of the puzzle the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
