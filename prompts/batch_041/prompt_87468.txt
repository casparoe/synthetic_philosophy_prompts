I'm running a two-hour session for a general-audience philosophy reading group — mostly non-specialists, plus one art historian and one member convinced that all aesthetic judgement is mere personal preference — on a distinction I want to introduce and defend. I claim that aesthetic testimony operates in two modes: a transmission mode, in which a speaker hands over a verdict ("this is beautiful; take it as beautiful") and asks you to believe it on their say-so, and a relocation mode, in which a speaker directs your attention to a feature ("notice how the light in the upper left refuses to resolve"), leaving the verdict yours to reach. My thesis is that only relocation is epistemically legitimate, and that the familiar quarrel over whether one may defer to others about taste is confused because it assumes all testimony is transmission.

Don't argue the thesis for me — plan how I should teach it. I need the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what: in particular, whether the group must feel the puzzle the distinction is meant to solve before they get the distinction, and what has to be in place before "epistemically legitimate" can mean anything to them. Tell me which single example should carry the session and what it must have and lack to do so — I had planned a wine recommendation, but say if something does the work better. Then map the misconceptions this audience will arrive with or form, in the order they will surface, and mark exactly where in the sequence I should head each off; I expect "so no testimony about art is ever any use" and "the art historian is simply a better authority," but tell me what I am missing. Close with one question I can put to the room that would show whether the distinction landed rather than was merely memorised, and state what I should explicitly decline to go into at this level.
