I'm redesigning the comparative unit of a graduate seminar on the politics of memory, and I want to get past the reflex verdict that Germany did its homework, Japan dodged, Turkey denies, and the American South is still fighting the war. The exercise I've set myself is this: take the four cases we cover — Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung (treating the Federal Republic's Holocaust-centred reckoning and the quite different East German one as a single case for now), Japan's postwar handling of the war in Asia, Turkey's handling of the Armenian genocide, and the American South's long reckoning with slavery, the Confederacy, and Jim Crow — and rank them by how durable their respective reckonings are likely to prove over the next twenty-five years, most durable first.

To stop the ranking from collapsing into vibes, I want each case scored on two axes before they are combined. The first axis is foundational versus supplemental: is the reckoning load-bearing for the legitimacy of the present order, such that abandoning it would delegitimate the state itself, or is it a supplement bolted onto a national story that would survive its removal more or less intact? The second is self-applying versus crisis-driven: are there standing mechanisms — curriculum requirements, courts, a ritual calendar, museum and memorial mandates — that will keep generating fresh acts of acknowledgement without a new political rupture, or does acknowledgement arrive only when something external forces it? For each of the four cases give me a 0–1 score on each axis, a one-sentence justification per score, and then the combined ranking. Tell me where the ranking flips if I weight the two axes differently, and name the case you found hardest to place and why.

Lead with the ranking itself in your first sentence, then give the scores and the reasoning underneath it, with no preamble and no restatement of what I've asked.
