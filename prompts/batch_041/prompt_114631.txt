I am a teaching assistant on a second-year undergraduate course in philosophy of law. The final examination included the question set out below, which was worth 25 marks; the students had read Devlin's Maccabaean Lecture and Hart's Law, Liberty and Morality (1963), along with the Wolfenden Report that prompted both. I have typed up one candidate's answer exactly as it was written. I would like you to mark it and to give me the itemised comments that justify the mark, since the marks and the written feedback have to go back together this week and this candidate sits close to a grade boundary.

The question

(a) Set out Devlin's case for the legal enforcement of morality, and Hart's principal objections to it. (10 marks)

(b) The Republic of Valdoria has enacted the Reverie Prohibition Act. A reverie is a small neurostimulation device that places its user in an immersive and intensely pleasurable dream-state for as long as the user wishes. Its use is private and consensual; users are competent and informed, they are physiologically stable, they can leave the reverie whenever they choose, and there is no evidence of harm to users or to anyone else and no evidence of dependency. The Act criminalises the manufacture, sale and use of reveries. The preamble offers two justifications. The first is that a life spent in induced reverie is a debased form of existence, unworthy of a rational agent, and that the law ought not to lend its authority to the promotion of such a life. The second is that if the use of reveries became widespread, the shared valuation of effort, achievement and mutual obligation on which Valdorian civic life depends would dissolve. Taking the two justifications separately, assess whether either of them can support criminal prohibition, using the Hart–Devlin debate as your framework. (10 marks)

Clarity of exposition and argument carries the remaining 5 marks.

The candidate's answer

(a) Devlin's argument begins from a conception of society as a community of shared ideas, moral as well as political. A shared morality is part of what holds a society together, and a sufficient erosion of it puts the society itself at risk of dissolution. Since the state has an interest in the survival of the society it governs, it has a prima facie right to legislate against conduct that offends the shared morality, even where that conduct harms nobody. The right is only prima facie, because Devlin also holds that the law must leave the greatest possible room for individual freedom that is consistent with the integrity of society, and that tolerance is among the factors that limit the use of the criminal law. On his account the standard is not the legislator's private morality but the morality that ordinary people actually share, and the law should not attempt to enforce every immorality.

Hart's objections are three. First, the disintegration thesis is an empirical claim for which Devlin offers no evidence; the examples he gives show that moralities change, not that societies collapse when they do, and moral change is not the same thing as moral decay. Second, Devlin moves from the fact that a morality is shared to the conclusion that it ought to be enforced. Hart's distinction between positive morality, the morality that is actually accepted in a society, and critical morality, the morality that can be defended by reasons, is intended to block that move: only the latter can justify coercion, and widespread disapproval is not in itself a reason. Third, Devlin's principle has no stopping point: taken literally it would license the enforcement of any shared morality, however benighted, since it offers no principle for distinguishing the moralities a society may enforce from those it may not.

(b) The first justification in the preamble is paternalistic. The state proposes to protect users from a form of life that is bad for them, and it proposes to do so against their wishes, since the users are stipulated to be competent, informed, content and free to leave. On Hart's harm principle the only legitimate ground for coercion is the prevention of harm to others, and this rationale does not even purport to identify any such harm. The first justification therefore cannot support the Act.

The second justification is a different matter, and Hart's framework is of limited use here. It does allege a harm, but a collective one, the dissolution of a shared culture. The harm principle is framed in terms of harm to other persons, and it is not clear that a culture or a society is the kind of thing that can be harmed in the relevant sense, or that its dissolution is a harm to anyone in particular. If the second justification is to be assessed at all, it must be assessed as an empirical prediction about what would happen to Valdorian society if the use of reveries spread, and as such it stands or falls with the evidence; Devlin's tolerance limit would be the natural standard, since the question would become how much moral divergence a society can absorb before it is at risk.

The Act is therefore not justified, though for different reasons in the two cases: the first rationale is defeated by the harm principle, and the second rests on a prediction that no one has tried to substantiate.
