After thirty years as a physicist I have spent the last several in philosophy of science, and the debate I keep circling back to—structural realism—looks to me as though it has been running in place. Before I ask you to diagnose it, let me set out what I take the two sides to be saying, because I want a diagnosis of the dispute and not another survey of it.

The epistemic structural realists hold that we should be realists about the structural content of our theories—the equations, the invariants, the relations—while remaining agnostic about the unobservable entities those theories posit. The stock illustration is the passage from a wave theory of light that required a mechanical ether to its successor, which kept the mathematics and dropped the medium: the equations survived the ontology, so the equations, and not the ontology, are what we were entitled to believe all along. The ontic structural realists reply that this is too modest by half: structure is not merely what we can know about the world, it is what the world is; objects are at most nodes in a structure, and the relata are in some sense dispensable in favour of the relations. That is the shape of it, and I do not think I am misrepresenting either side.

Each camp has a well-rehearsed battery of objections, and here is what puzzles me. The critics of ontic structural realism say that relations without relata is not a coherent posit—that a relation must relate something, and that if you strip the relata of any intrinsic nature you have not described a world but a piece of notation. The defenders reply that the objection simply assumes the very thing at issue, and they point to mathematics, where a structure just is a set of relations and the nodes are individuated only by their place in the pattern. Meanwhile the epistemic structural realists are met with a formal objection—I know it as the Newman problem—to the effect that if the structural content of a theory is captured by existentially quantifying over its theoretical terms, the resulting claim is satisfied by any domain of the right cardinality, since one can always define a relation over it that mimics the required pattern; on that reading, believing in the structure of a theory amounts to believing only that the world is large enough. The published replies to this objection seem to me to divide the camp rather than to settle anything, and the exchange between the two camps reads less like a contest than like two conversations laid side by side.

My hypothesis—offered as a hypothesis, and I would be glad to be told it is wrong—is that structure is being used for at least two different things: the logician's structure of a Ramsey sentence, which is a matter of the logical form of a quantified claim, and the physicist's structure of a symmetry or an invariant, which is a matter of what transformations leave a system unchanged. If that is right, the two camps are answering different questions under one name. A competing hypothesis is that the real disagreement is a hidden formal one about the individuation of relations, and a third is that one camp is doing epistemology and the other metaphysics, so that neither can in principle refute the other.

What I want from you is a diagnosis with reasons, drawing on what each side actually says. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, resting on an equivocation the parties could be brought to abandon? Is it substantive, and if so whether it turns on a distinction or a formal result that someone could supply? Are the sides answering different questions under one heading—one about what we can know, one about what there is—and if so, does that make the dispute ill-posed or merely hard? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, or nothing, with the reason why nothing should be expected. I would rather have a candid verdict that it cannot be moved than a hopeful inventory of the positions.
