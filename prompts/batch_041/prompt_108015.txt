I am a retired natural scientist. For most of my career I designed controlled experiments; since retiring I have been reading philosophy, especially Dewey. I have agreed to run a session at a small residential workshop for early-career researchers from philosophy, the social sciences, and the natural sciences. I want the session to be a laboratory exercise in Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry, not a lecture about it.

Please design a runnable 90-minute activity for 16 participants. The room is ordinary: movable chairs, a whiteboard, index cards, and a small supply of tokens. No real money is needed. The participants will not have read Dewey, and I do not want his name mentioned until the debrief. I want them to feel, in the doing, three connected claims: that inquiry begins in an indeterminate situation rather than a well-formed problem; that a public is not a pre-existing collection of individuals with fixed preferences but something formed around shared consequences; and that democratic decision-making is itself a mode of experimental inquiry in which ends are revised by the consequences of trying to realize them. They should also feel the temptation to treat the first formulation of the problem as the problem, and the cost of doing so.

The activity should work roughly as follows, though you should improve the mechanics where they are weak. Each participant receives a private consequence card describing something they will experience if a proposed institutional change is adopted. No one may show a card; participants may speak only in testimony, proposals, and predictions of consequences. They must first discover with whom they share consequences, then frame a problem, then design a small trial or intervention, then decide whether to adopt it. Halfway through, I will introduce a perturbation that changes who is affected and how. The group must then revise not only its proposal but its statement of the problem. The aim is that they experience the difference between aggregating fixed preferences and conducting a shared inquiry. You may invent the specific institutional change; it need not correspond to a real policy controversy, and it should be simple enough to grasp in the first five minutes.

I would like the design to include the following: a premise and learning aims; materials and room setup; roles and rules, including how the private consequence cards work and what may and may not be said; a minute-by-minute run sheet; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto Dewey's ideas without turning into a lecture; an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle those failures; and a frank statement of which feature of Dewey's experimentalism the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply in the debrief.

Please structure your reply under the following headings, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated:
1. Premise and learning aims (150 words)
2. Materials and setup (100 words)
3. Roles and rules (500 words)
4. Run sheet (350 words)
5. Debrief (500 words)
6. Predictable failures and fixes (250 words)
7. What the activity cannot reproduce (200 words)

No bibliography, reading list, or citations are needed. Do not quote Dewey; paraphrase the ideas in plain language. I care more about the mechanics than about the labels.
