I am a novelist who teaches an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of fiction, and I am drafting the take-home final. I want a single essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard menu of solutions to the paradox of fiction — denying that we feel anything, denying that emotion requires belief in the object, or relocating the emotion into a game of make-believe — and that instead forces students to reason about a proposal they have not seen before.

Give them the following invented proposal and case, then ask them to assess it. The proposal, which I call the subscription model: a reader who engages a fiction temporarily subscribes to its existential presuppositions, treating the character as existing "for the duration," while her standing beliefs remain unchanged; her emotions are directed at the character as believed-in under the subscription, so the troublesome premise is suspended during engagement rather than denied outright. The case: a reader finishes a novel in which a character dies; the subscription lapses; and yet she goes on grieving, and days later quietly donates to a charity the character would have supported, in the character's memory. Ask students to say whether the subscription model can accommodate this case without collapsing into either the illusion theory or the make-believe theory, and to name the specific commitment it must surrender in order to do so.

Then supply (a) a model answer of no more than 550 words that opens with a single sentence stating the crux, and (b) a marking scheme naming five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth out of 20, and, for each, one sentence on the characteristic error that costs the mark. Keep the entire response within 900 words.
