I teach a ninety-minute session on rational choice to about sixteen advanced undergraduates, and I want an in-class activity that lets them run into Good's theorem on the value of information rather than hear it asserted. As I present it, the theorem says that the expected utility of deciding after learning the answer to some question is never lower than that of deciding without it — the value of information is non-negative in expectation — because a Bayesian who has learned something can always ignore it and act as before. What I want the students to feel is both halves of that: the pull of the theorem, and the way it stops holding the moment its quiet assumptions fail. Please build in two less-familiar cases so they cannot pattern-match to a textbook treatment: one where the reason to refuse the information is that the agent has preferences over her own beliefs — she would rather keep a surprise, or avoid a diagnosis she cannot un-know — and one where the information is about which choice she herself is going to make, so that receiving it can change what she does. Commit to one design; make the specific calls yourself — the payoff numbers, the exact moment the signal is offered, whether it can be declined — and give your reasons rather than laying out alternatives for me to choose from.

Return the plan as exactly five numbered items, each no more than three sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the setting, group size, roles, and materials; (2) the rules and the ordered phases, including precisely when the optional signal appears and whether it may be refused; (3) the payoff scheme, using no real money, with the specific numbers that make both the theorem and its failure bite; (4) the debrief, mapping each staged moment to the ex ante guarantee, to the suspense case, and to the self-prediction case, and naming the assumptions whose failure does the work; and (5) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, together with the one feature of Good's theorem that the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words.
