You are to act as a philosopher of mathematics who also works on automated theorem proving and logical uncertainty. Work carefully and deliberately, and open with a brief scratch section in which you brainstorm the distinct questions that a claim of this kind actually depends on, before you commit to any numbers. Only then give the estimate.

Here is the background, stated in my own terms so that nothing in it requires you to consult a literature. Mathematicians routinely reason as though the objects they study had been drawn at random: that the decimal expansion of π contains no exploitable pattern, that the error term in a count of primes behaves like a random walk, that two quantities produced by unrelated constructions will not happen to satisfy an unexpected identity. Call this the presumption of independence — the default that correlation demands explanation, so that in the absence of a reason to expect structure, structure is assumed absent. It is not a theorem and is not derived from anything; yet heuristic arguments resting on it reliably predict what later turns out to be true, and they fail reliably in just those places where hidden structure exists, the small-number coincidences and low-dimensional exceptional cases that grow into whole subfields.

Now the proposition:

P: There exists a formal system which (i) represents the presumption of independence as a defeasible rule of inference rather than as a derived result, (ii) attaches to the heuristic arguments it licenses a numerical confidence that is calibrated, in the sense that statements it rates at 0.9 are true at roughly that rate, and (iii) does this without being supplied in advance with the structure of the mathematical universe it reasons about.

Give me the following, in this order.

(a) Your credence in P, as a number.

(b) The sub-questions P decomposes into, each with its own probability and a single line of justification. I expect the list to include at least: whether any canonical measure or prior over mathematical statements can be defined at all, given that there is no obvious probability space of which the natural numbers are a draw; whether the reliability of the heuristic is a fact about mathematical reality or merely about our ignorance and our bounded proof search; whether the system can recognize in advance that a reason to expect structure is present, without circularity, when the only tool it has for recognizing such reasons is the defeasible rule itself; and whether the calibration claim must be relativized to a reference class of statements someone would bother to state, which would make it a selection effect rather than a property of the heuristic.

(c) How those numbers combine into your answer to (a), stated explicitly, together with a note on which of the sub-questions are not independent of one another.

(d) The one sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and the argument or evidence that would move it most.

Finally, suppose a competent colleague puts her credence in P at 0.8 while you land lower. Say where the disagreement most plausibly lives, and which sub-question the two of you are really disagreeing about.
