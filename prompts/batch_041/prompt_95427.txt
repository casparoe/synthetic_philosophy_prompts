I'm the only engineer in a book club full of lit people, and next month's pick is Pygmalion (Shaw's play, not just the musical). I went down a rabbit hole on Locke's book about education and now I can't unsee it: praise and shame instead of beatings, lessons made to feel like play, character and good habits before Latin, the tutor's own example as the real instrument, and under all of it the assumption that you already know what station the kid is headed for. Read the play through that. Higgins is running an experiment on a person, and Eliza's question — what becomes of me now that I'm a lady who can't sell flowers — is the question Locke's scheme never has to ask. So is Shaw showing Locke's method working (the manners do take) or showing that the method is quietly parasitic on a class position it won't admit? Is Higgins a tutor in Locke's sense at all, or just a technician with better drills? And where does the play push back — I dont want to turn Shaw into an illustration. I've got ten minutes at the meeting and I want the lit people to actually think.
