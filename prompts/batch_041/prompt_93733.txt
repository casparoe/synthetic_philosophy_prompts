I recently spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about a case I had put to him, and we parted without either of us conceding anything. The case was this: you must choose between one person being tortured for fifty years and an enormous number of people each receiving a single speck of dust in the eye — a momentary annoyance that leaves no trace. My friend insisted that once the number in the second option is large enough, the second option is plainly the better choice; I found that conclusion intolerable, yet every argument I produced against it seemed to prove too much, since it appeared to forbid weighing any quantity of small harms against one large one. Toward the end I tried to change the case. Suppose the harm in the second option is so slight that the person suffering it would not notice it, would not remember it, and would not pay a penny to avoid it; and suppose the crowd is not some unimaginable cardinal but merely as large as the number of atoms in the observable universe. My friend said this changed nothing. I am not sure he is wrong, and I want to understand what follows.

Please set out the most important implications of the aggregationist verdict in this family of cases — that sufficiently many trivial harms outweigh one catastrophic harm — for how I should reason about real choices of the same shape: choices that impose imperceptible costs on very large numbers of people in order to spare a few people something terrible, or the reverse. Assume that I already accept that harms can in principle be summed and that finding a conclusion distasteful is not an argument against it; I want the consequences, not a summary of the dispute. You may use whatever distinctions you find useful, but I do not want a survey of what various writers have said.

Structure your answer as follows.

Bottom line: three or four sentences stating the single most important implication you see and why it has that status.

The implications that matter most: the substance of your answer, roughly six hundred to eight hundred words, presented as a numbered list of no more than five implications. State each in one sentence and then unpack it. Each should be a consequence that a thoughtful person would not have seen immediately, and each should be tied to the modified version of the case I described — the sub-threshold harm and the merely astronomical crowd — rather than to the familiar original. Where an implication rests on a premise, name the premise explicitly, and say what happens to the implication if that premise fails.

The strongest objection: roughly one hundred and fifty words giving the most serious reason to think these implications do not actually follow, stated as forcefully as you can.

What would change your answer: roughly one hundred words on the considerations that would most alter your verdict.
