I'm trying to get clear on Butler's sermon on self-deceit (the tenth one in the Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel) and there's a paragraph I keep bouncing off. He's just told the Nathan-and-David story — David pronounces a furious sentence on the rich man in the parable, and Nathan says "Thou art the man" — and then Butler writes this:

"There is plainly, in the generality of mankind, an absence of doubt or distrust, in a very great measure, as to their moral character and behaviour; and likewise a disposition to take for granted, that all is right and well with them in these respects. The former is owing to their not reflecting, not exercising their judgment upon themselves; the latter, to self-love."

Help me understand what he's actually claiming, because I can't get it to add up. Three things I'm stuck on:

(a) Does the self-deceived person have a belief here — that all is right and well with them — or is the point that no belief ever gets formed? "Absence of doubt or distrust" sounds like a blank; "a disposition to take for granted" sounds like a belief. Are these two different states he's bundling together, and if so, which one is supposed to be the self-deception?

(b) If it's the blank reading, in what sense is anyone deceived? Nobody lied to anybody, there's no false belief with a fishy etiolgy. I'd want to say that's just self-ignorance or an attention failure, not self-deceit at all, and that the title of the sermon has oversold it. Is there anything in the passage that blocks me from saying that?

(c) What work is self-love doing, exactly? A motive needs something to push on. If the question never comes up, what's left for the motive to operate on? And if it does come up, we're back to a person who has the evidence and mishandles it, which is a seperate story.

The distinction I keep reaching for, and I don't know if it's Butler's or just mine: deceiving yourself about the verdict vs. never putting the case on the docket at all. I can see how self-love rigs a docket — which questions get asked, which bits of evidence get picked up, when you change the subject. What I can't see is how a motive reaches the verdict directly without the person noticing that it happened. Is that a real distinction or am I just renaming his "not reflecting"?

And one smaller thing that bugs me. David gets the parable case right. He's outraged at the right man for the right reason and passes the correct sentence; the failure is only in the application. So is Butler's self-deceit a failure of knowledge or a failure of application? If it's the latter, the deceiver starts to look less like someone who knows and buries the facts than like a competent judge who never gets told the facts of his own case — and that doesn't sound like deception. Butler makes it harder, not easier, by saying David's crimes were done "with the utmost deliberation, thought, and contrivance."

Don't walk me through the literature on self-deception, I've read plenty of that. Just get me straight on this paragraph: what each clause is doing, and what would have to be true of a person for the whole sentence to be true of them at once.
