Act as a philosopher of probability and decision theory with a specialist's knowledge of self-locating belief and its applications. I sit on the review board of a small clinic, and we have been asked to approve a protocol that its designer cheerfully calls "Sleeping Beauty in a treatment room": each enrolled patient is assigned by fair coin to a one-infusion or a two-infusion course of the same drug, the two infusions being separated by an interval during which a short-acting amnesiac prevents the patient from knowing whether the infusion now being given is the first or the second; at every infusion the patient records a pain score and a credence that she is on the two-infusion course, and our statistician proposes to treat each recorded response as an independent observation. I would like to understand the most important implications of the Sleeping Beauty problem, and of the fact that competent philosophers still disagree about its answer, for what we should make of this protocol. In particular: what the patients' reported credences can and cannot be taken to mean; whether the statistician's counting rule is defensible, and what its failure modes would be; and what, if anything, the board should require of the designer. Please think this through carefully. Begin by setting out in your own words what the disagreement over Sleeping Beauty is really about and why the case is hard, then order the implications by importance, distinguishing those that follow from the structure of the case itself from those that depend on a contested view about self-locating credence, and flag any point where the clinic's setting differs relevantly from the original puzzle.
