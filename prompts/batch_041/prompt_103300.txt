I am a high school junior, and I run our school's philosophy club, which meets once a week in the back of the library, where we have movable tables. About fifteen students come, mostly juniors and seniors, and most of them have had one semester of ethics, so they know the harm principle in something like the form Mill gives it and they are quick to apply it. What I would like from you is a single sixty-minute session built around a game or simulation that makes them feel the force of the other side of the Hart–Devlin debate — the thought that a shared morality is itself something a community can lose, that its erosion damages everyone, and that a society may therefore have a stake in conduct that, taken one act at a time, harms no one in particular. I do not want them to be told this. I want the mechanics to produce the conclusion in their own mouths before the debrief names it, so that the pull of legal moralism is something they have felt from the inside rather than a position they have read about and dismissed. It is fine if they end up resisting it, as long as the resistance visibly costs them something.

Please give me rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme that does not need real money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the hour, and the debrief. My rough idea, which you should feel free to improve or replace: the group plays a small fictional community that maintains some shared practice — a memorial for its dead, a way of keeping promises, a custom of not humiliating its weakest member — and each participant holds a private card telling them how much they gain by quietly departing from that practice when nobody is watching. Alongside the individual points, keep one public meter of the community's shared life that visibly decays whenever anyone departs from the practice, and make the community's ability to do something it genuinely cares about, such as rebuilding the well or holding the harvest or caring for the sick, depend on that meter staying above a threshold. Let them discover over four or five rounds that a string of individually harmless private acts has cost the group something no one can recover on their own, and then put the question to them directly: should the community make the practice a law and punish people who break it in private? I expect them to vote yes. If they do, run a second and shorter phase in which they are told that a small group among them holds a different practice, that the majority finds that practice distasteful, and that there is now a proposal to extend the law to cover it — with one participant scripted in advance as the member of that minority and a second scripted as the community's reasonable, non-fanatical spokesperson, so that the majority has to work out where, if anywhere, to stop.

The debrief should map particular moments in the game onto particular pieces of the debate: the moment the meter fell onto the disintegration thesis; the vote onto the claim that a society may enforce its shared morality as such; the extension proposal onto the worry that this principle licenses enforcing any shared morality at all, including one the majority happens to hold and the minority does not; and the argument about the minority onto the question of whose morality counts and how it changes over time. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guesses are that they will treat it as a free-rider problem and argue about incentives instead of about morality, that the meter makes the causal link so obvious that someone says 'so ban it' and the tension deflates, and that whoever takes the minority role either feels got at or plays it as a villain — and how to handle each of those. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: I take it that it cannot support the empirical premise, since it hard-wires the very link between private immorality and social collapse that Hart denies and that Devlin asserted on contested sociological grounds, and that it therefore cannot tell us whether the premise is true. I would also like to know what the facilitator has to supply that the game cannot, and which side of the debate the game quietly assumes. Please write in American English, and end by telling me how confident you are that this will work in a room of fifteen teenagers, singling out the element of the design you are least sure about.
