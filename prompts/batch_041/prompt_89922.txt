I'm taking this course on ancient ethics and we just finished Cicero's On Old Age — the Cato Maior dialogue where old Cato, at 84, walks through the four standard gripes against old age (it drags you out of public life, wrecks your body, kills off your pleasures, and parks you right next to death) and swats each one away. His answer is basically that old age keeps what actually matters: judgment, counsel, and authority, and that strength was never the thing that ran the world anyway. He even says the mind stays sharp if you keep working it — he keeps pointing at people still writing, still learning, still running things in their old age — and that if an old person is sour and gloomy, that's their character, not their years.

For my essay I want to try something more interesting than just restating his replies. I want to read King Lear through the dialogue.

My rough hunch: Cato's whole defense leans on auctoritas — the deference old age supposedly commands — and that's a relational thing, it's something other people grant you. And Lear is the nightmare version of that. He tries to keep the trappings while giving away the actual power, and then nobody honors him, and he comes apart. So is the play a counterexample to Cato — showing that old age's authority secretly depends on retained power and on other people choosing to defer, which Cato just takes for granted — or is it actually a confirmation, since Lear is exactly the man whose earlier life never cultivated the wisdom Cato says you need, so the collapse is his own fault and not age's?

Things I'd want you to actually work through:
- The bit where Cato insists the weakening body and the nearness of death aren't real grounds for complaint — how does that sit against Lear's terror of losing his reason, and against the way he dies? He seems to fear madness more than death, which I'm not sure Cato has a category for.
- Whether the wisdom Lear does claw his way to at the end (the whole thing about the poor and the wretched, having taken too little care of them) counts as the fruit of a well-spent life that Cato promises old age brings, or whether it's a wisdom he only gets by losing absolutely everything — which would be a much darker story than Cicero's.
- Whether the framing of the dialogue itself matters here: Cato is surrounded by younger men who defer to him, and Cicero wrote the thing while he was himself politically sidelined and getting old, so the whole setup may be a consolation for a man whose authority had already been taken from him. Does that make it closer to Lear than Cicero wants to admit?

One thing though. Please seperate out for me what's actually settled about how Cicero's arguments are supposed to work, versus what's genuinely contested among people who know the dialogue, and don't hand me a contested reading dressed up as the consensus. If the comparison is a stretch in places, say so — I'd rather know where to back off than hand in something that oversells the parallel.
