I write about science and ideas for a living, and I also run a scrappy upper-level seminar on philosophy of biology for working science journalists — the whole point being that we stop writing "the function of X is Y" as if that were a neutral statement. I'm putting together the take-home final and I want one question that students can't answer by just parroting back that the selected-effects view ties function to history and the causal-role view ties it to current contribution. That's the recital I want to defeat.

Build the question around these two cases, and fold both of them in:

(a) A lab team splices a brand-new gene into a bacterium that lets it break down an antibiotic. Nothing in that lineage's evolutionary history was ever selected for antibiotic resistance — it's a one-off insert, and these bacteria had never met the drug before the experiment.

(b) A whale's leftover hip bone. It was definately selected for, way back, when it supported hind limbs. Now it supports nothing; it just sits there.

Write me the exam question, then a model answer of roughly 500 words, then a marking scheme that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. The question needs to force students to say what each account of function is actually comitted to on case (a) and case (b) — and I want them to notice the two accounts come apart in opposite directions on the two cases — and then take a stand on whether the accounts are really disagreeing about anything or are just answering different questions. A weak answer will summarize both views and shrug. I want the question to make that shrug impossible.

Don't ask for citations or reading lists anywhere in it — the students won't have web access during the take-home. And keep the whole reply under 900 words, with the exam question itself right at the top.
