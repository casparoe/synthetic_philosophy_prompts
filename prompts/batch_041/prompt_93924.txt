I've been asked to give the after-dinner speech at this year's meeting of our county abolition-history society, marking the anniversary of the founding of the local anti-slavery committee, and I need you to write it. The room is genuinely mixed: two academic historians of abolition who have read Bernard Williams's Truth and Truthfulness and Kwame Anthony Appiah's The Honor Code and will be listening for whether I soften the hard questions; a great-great-granddaughter of one of the founders, who speaks after me and who understandably wants her ancestor honored; a handful of schoolteachers who bring classes to our small museum; and a large table of members who come for the fellowship and the pie. The awkwardness is that I am on record, in a seminar and now in the newsletter, as holding a view that makes the usual tribute hard to give. I don't think the moral arguments against slavery were the engine of abolition. The arguments were available and largely unrefuted for a very long time and changed almost nothing; what changed was who could afford to be seen owning people, and what honor required of a gentleman, and I suspect that when dueling died out it died the same way, not because anyone refuted it but because the class that conferred honor on duelists lost its grip on what counted as a serious man. So I cannot stand up and say that our founders' pamphlets won the argument, because I don't believe it, and I also can't stand up and say they were merely lucky cogs in an economic machine, because that would be false to what they did and cruel to the people at that table.

What I want from you is a speech that takes this seriously rather than papering over it, and that finds formulations I could actually defend if one of the historians stands up. Give real credit without the causal claim: say what it was the founders did, and what it cost them, on a picture where argument was not the mechanism. Say plainly what I think the mechanism was, in the language of a dinner rather than a seminar, and let the honor story carry weight without turning into a lecture about Appiah. Handle the dueling comparison in a sentence or two, only if it earns its place, since it's the one case where the room will have an intuition I can lean on. And make it land as gratitude rather than as a debunking, because I do mean the gratitude.

One more thing I'd like you to fold in, because it's the part of my own view that I can't yet state cleanly and I want to hear how it sounds out loud. I've been calling it status substitution, and the rough idea is that a norm dies when the people who enforce it find that enforcing it now costs them standing in a different and more valuable hierarchy than the one the norm belonged to, so the same people who upheld it can abandon it without ever conceding that they were wrong, which is why these revolutions feel sudden and why the arguments from before are suddenly quoted as though they had always been decisive. I don't know whether that's right, and I don't want it presented as a finding or attributed to anyone, just as something I've been chewing on. After the speech, in a short paragraph of plain prose, tell me which claims I've deliberately kept out of the speech and why, so I know where the seams are if I'm challenged over the coffee. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, the way one would write a letter.
