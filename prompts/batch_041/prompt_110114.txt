I keep running into the proverb that history repeats itself, most recently when a colleague used it to argue that a country's slide toward authoritarianism was inevitable — the wheel always turns, he said, so we may as well wait for it to come back around. I have seen the same phrase pressed into service for much humbler claims (people keep making the same mistakes) and for much grander ones (time is a circle, and the whole cosmos recurs). Lay out the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, from the trivial to the metaphysically extravagant, using the familiar candidates where they fit — Polybius's cycle of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun's rise and decay of dynasties, Spengler's cultures with fixed lifespans, Toynbee's challenge and response. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical claim, or something that could be true only under a particular metaphysics of time, and whether it is defensible. Then say which reading is doing the work when the phrase is used to make some particular outcome look inevitable, and whether any of the readings actually licenses that use.
