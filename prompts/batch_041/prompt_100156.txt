Question 4 (15 points). Condorcet's jury theorem is often summarized like this: if each member of a group is more likely than not to pick the correct one of two options, and the members choose independently of one another, then (i) the probability that the group's majority picks correctly is higher than the probability that any single member does, and (ii) that probability tends to 1 as the group gets larger.

(a) [4] State the assumptions the theorem actually needs, and say what work each one does — i.e., what goes wrong if you drop it.

(b) [5] A committee of nine members votes on a two-option question. Each member independently has probability 0.6 of choosing correctly. Write the exact expression for the probability that the majority (at least five members) is correct, and evaluate it.

(c) [6] A colleague proposes replacing the single nine-member vote with a two-tier scheme: split the nine members into three panels of three; each panel takes its own majority verdict; then the three panel verdicts are decided by majority. Is this two-tier scheme guaranteed to be at least as accurate as the single nine-member majority? Explain.

And here's the student's answer:

(a) The theorem needs four assumptions. First, there have to be exactly two options and one of them must be the correct one — that's what makes "competence" a probability of being right rather than just a preference. Second, every member must be better than a coin flip, p > 0.5. Third, the members must choose independently. Fourth, everyone votes their honest judgment rather than strategically. The independence condition is there so that the votes are a representative sample of the group's judgment, so that one member's error can't drag the others along with it; if you drop it — suppose the members all take their cue from the same newspaper — the result doesn't just weaken, it flips, and the majority becomes less accurate than a single member. The p > 0.5 condition is what produces the improvement over any individual voter, and the two-option condition is what makes "the majority" well defined in the first place.

(b) Let X be the number of correct votes, X ~ Binomial(9, 0.6). The majority is correct when X ≥ 5, so the probability is

sum from k = 5 to 9 of C(9,k) (0.6)^k (0.4)^(9-k)

= 0.2508 + 0.2508 + 0.1612 + 0.0605 + 0.0101

≈ 0.733.

(c) The scheme should be fine. Each three-member panel is more accurate than any of its individual members — the panel is right with probability 3(0.6)^2(0.4) + (0.6)^3 = 0.648, versus 0.6 for a single member. The final decision is then a majority of the three panel verdicts, so it sits one more level up the same logic. Nesting the majorities doesn't throw any information away; it just lets the group correct itself in stages, so the theorem's guarantee carries over to the whole structure and the two-tier scheme is at least as accurate as the single nine-member vote.
