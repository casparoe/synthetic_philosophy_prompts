Ok so I'm writing a term paper for a course on long-run social explanation and I keep getting stuck on path dependence. The problem is that everyone in the seminar is using "path dependent" to mean three different things and I cant tell which of them is supposed to be doing the actual work. One person means "it happened that way and it stuck around"; another means "it stuck around even though it's worse than what could have been"; a third means "there was a moment where two options were genuinely live and a small nudge picked one". I'm not after something formalisable in a model, I just need something sharp enough to tell me which of those three uses is legitimate and which is a fancy way of saying "it happened". So before I read any more case studies I want the spec, not the cases: what would a theory of path dependence actually have to deliver to be any good?

So: give me the desiderata list. What does any adequate theory of lock-in have to be able to do -- which cases must it get right, which distinctions must it respect (genuine self-reinforcing lock-in vs. plain inertia or slow adjustment; contingency of the event vs. contingency of the outcome; "nobody has bothered to change it" vs. "it can't be changed"), which mechanisms it has to be able to name and separate (increasing returns, network effects, learning-by-doing, coordination and expectation effects, sunk costs -- and what each of those is supposed to predict, not just that it's on the list), and what it must say about the counterfactual "would the same outcome have arrived anyway". For each requirement give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. I especially want you to flag any two requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied -- my suspicion is that there's a straight tension between wanting the theory to make strong claims about locked-in outcomes being worse than the road not taken, and wanting those claims to be the sort of thing anyone could in principle check. If you agree, say why; if you think I've set the tension up wrong, say that instead. And which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out?

I've got a concrete case I'm trying to fit, by the way. My flatmate is from a place where two neighbouring provinces had the same legal system, and in the 1920s both adopted a rule about who eats the cost when a house turns out to have a hidden defect -- one by a commission that was told to pick whichever rule the local merchants wanted, the other by a judge who basically flipped a coin between two options he thought equally good. Tell me which requirements in your list that case is actually testing, and what the theory would have to say to settle our argument.
