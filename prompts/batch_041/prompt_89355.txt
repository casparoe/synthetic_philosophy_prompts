For a graduate seminar on the figure of the undeserving poor, construct a decision tree that sorts a reader from a few starting commitments to a named position on when, if ever, the state may attach behavioural conditions to means-tested benefits. Build the tree as a logical space rather than a list of schools: every branch node should be a question the reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each should be anchored in a concrete case — a claimant who refuses a job that pays less than the last one; a sanction triggered by a misfiled form rather than any refusal; a district with far fewer vacancies than claimants. Five distinctions should do real work somewhere on the tree: whether a condition bears on entitlement itself or only on the timing of payment, so that a right can survive while disbursement is withheld; whether conditionality rests on reciprocity, understood as something owed for what is received, or on a perfectionist claim about the value of work to the claimant; whether reciprocity is enforceable only when the scheme's terms are adequate, so that it lapses in a slack labour market, or binds regardless; whether the state can know who is shirking, and whether that epistemic problem is fatal to individually targeted sanctions; and whether conditionality must actually change behaviour to be justified, or may instead be justified by expressing a reciprocity norm or by holding together the political coalition that funds the scheme. Aim for six branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves. Name each leaf where a name exists and mark the rest unnamed; give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment; flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied, saying in each case whether the missing view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended.
