I am drafting a paper for a graduate seminar on Indian epistemology, and I want your help thinking through one problem in it. Please write a formal essay in response.

The Nyāya school recognizes four pramāṇas — pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison or analogy), and śabda (testimony) — and it treats them as mutually irreducible: each is admitted because it delivers a kind of valid cognition that the others, by their nature, cannot supply. Other schools disputed both the length of the list and the irreducibility of its members (Buddhist epistemologists, for instance, compress testimony and comparison into perception and inference; some Mīmāṃsā authors add further members such as postulation and non-apprehension). For the purposes of this essay, take the Nyāya four and the Nyāya demand for irreducibility as your starting point, and treat that demand as a genuine criterion rather than a slogan: a candidate counts as a distinct pramāṇa only if it cannot be analyzed without residue into one of the existing four.

Now consider a case the classical authors could not have anticipated. I will call it algorithmic aggregation (my own label, not a term of art). It is the case in which a person comes to believe that p on the basis of the output of a trained statistical system: a model that, asked whether one drug interacts with another, returns a probability; or that, asked to rank applicants, returns an ordering; or that, asked a factual question, returns a sentence. Three features distinguish it from the familiar pramāṇas. First, the user cannot reconstruct the reasoning that produced the output — there is no inferential chain she could rehearse, and often no human being who could. Second, there is no speaker in the ordinary sense: whatever produced the output had no communicative intention, and the output is the residue of an aggregation over countless unidentifiable sources rather than the assertion of anyone. Third, the output is defeasible in a peculiar way — it can be revised not because the user learns a new fact, but because the underlying system is retrained, with no trace of what changed.

The controversy I want you to adjudicate is this: should Nyāya admit algorithmic aggregation as a fifth pramāṇa in its own right, or is it reducible to one or more of the existing four — most plausibly to anumāna, since the user may be said to infer from the system's output as an effect to its cause, or to śabda, if the system is treated as a kind of testimony, or to some combination of the two?

Write an essay that does the following, in whatever order you find most persuasive:

1. State, as precisely as you can, the criterion Nyāya actually uses to distinguish one pramāṇa from another, and say what would have to be true of a candidate for it to satisfy that criterion.
2. Characterize algorithmic aggregation carefully enough that it can be tested against the criterion — do not let the case collapse into "using a tool" or "trusting an expert."
3. Present the strongest case for reduction, taking seriously each of the two main reductionist strategies (to inference and to testimony) and explaining where each succeeds and where it leaves a residue.
4. Present the strongest case for distinctness, and then raise the most serious objection to it.
5. Reach a verdict and defend it. A qualified verdict is acceptable (for example, "distinct in one respect but not in the respect that matters for pramāṇa-status"), but do not end on a non-committal survey; commit to a position and say what it costs you.
