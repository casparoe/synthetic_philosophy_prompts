Last term I sat in on a lecture course on comparative history, and there is one lecture I have not been able to stop chewing on. I have no way to check the lecturer's sources, so I would like the substance audited rather than the citations. Here is his argument as I recorded it.

The old question, he said, is Needham's: why did modern science, and then industrial capitalism, arise in Europe and not in China, when for most of the preceding millennium China had been the more advanced civilization? He credited what he called the California School with reframing this. Pomeranz, he said, established that the divergence was long-standing — that Europe's lead runs back to the medieval period and reflects deep cultural difference, above all a Confucian examination system indifferent to the mechanical arts. On that account the Needham question is answered by culture: China had the ingredients but not the temperament.

He then argued that this makes the contrastive form of the question the crux. Why Britain and not China is a why-question with a contrast class, and the answer you give depends entirely on what you put in the contrast slot. Ask why Britain rather than the Netherlands and you get an answer about coal and wages; ask why Europe rather than Asia and you get an answer about colonies and the New World; ask why the eighteenth century rather than the fourteenth and you get an answer about plague and factor prices. He named this phenomenon explanandum drift, and drew the conclusion that the question is ill-posed and that historians should simply stop asking it.

To make the point precise he proposed a test, which he called contrast-rigidity. A why-question is contrast-rigid when its answer survives the substitution of a neighbouring contrast term, and contrast-elastic when the answer changes. His verdict was that every question in this debate is contrast-elastic, and that elasticity is precisely what separates a genuinely explanatory question from a merely rhetorical one.

He closed with a methodological footnote: since there is only one case of industrialization, any explanation of it must be underdetermined, so the honest response is to stop hunting for causes and to describe the transition instead.

I have two vague misgivings and no way to sharpen them. The second paragraph feels like a caricature of a position that somebody holds, though I suspect it is not the position he has attached it to. And the last two paragraphs seem to me to be doing something slick, though I cannot say what. I do not need a reading list; I want the argument assessed on its merits.

Please go through the lecture claim by claim. Which of these would a historian or philosopher of history working on the Great Divergence accept, which would they treat as a standard simplification that is harmless in a lecture, and which are simply wrong? Then tell me whether contrast-rigidity is a real criterion or a relabelling of something obvious, and whether it would not condemn every causal why-question equally — and if so, what that shows. Finally, say whether the abandonment of the question follows from anything true in the lecture, and, if the question can be posed properly, what a proper statement of it would look like.
