Ok so I keep getting dragged into the numbers arguement — you know the one, you can save either one person or five, and one camp says obviously you save the five, and the other camp says no, you can't just add strangers up like that, each person has their own claim and no single claim outweighs another, so you'd be within your rights to choose at random or flip a coin. I don't want to relitigate that fight here. I want the spec first: what would a theory of when numbers count in rescue cases actually have to get right?

So give me the requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't just collapse, the considerations it has to stay consistent with — and for each one, the case or thought that puts it on the list. And say which of these are genuine requirements and which are just house rules of one of the camps.

Things I think have to be on the list already, but sort them out and add what I'm missing:

- the plain one-or-five case, and the fact that most people's answer to it is stubborn and doesn't budge under pressure
- my own case, which is the one I actually can't get straight: you can either save one person for certain, or run a lottery that gives each of five people a one-in-five chance of being saved. Same expected number of survivors either way. Does the theory have to call this a tie? If yes, it looks like it's treating chances themselves as the good, and I want to know whether that's a cost or just the right answer. If no, it owes me a story about why one sure person and five probabilistic people aren't equal when the arithmetic says they are.
- a case where what's at stake is unequal rather than the head count — save one person from dying versus save a hundred people from a broken leg, or the reverse — because a theory has to say whether numbers count people or count welfare, and it can't quietly do both
- the version where you're one of the people involved, since I don't know whether the answer is supposed to change when it's you-or-them, and if it does I want to know whether that's a feature or a bug
- whether the same answer has to hold in a one-off rescue and in a standing rule, because I want to be able to defend this in front of a triage committee, not just win a seminar

Then the two things I actually want out of this:

1. is any subset of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable? If the list can't be satisfied all at once then the inconsistency is the result, and I'd rather know that now than after working through a pile of theories
2. which single requirement does the most work at ruling candidate views out

You can score the main positions against the list at the end — the pure adder, the coin-flipper, the one that says you must save the five but only as a tie-break between equal claims, whatever else you think is live — but keep that part short, the criteria are what I'm here for.
