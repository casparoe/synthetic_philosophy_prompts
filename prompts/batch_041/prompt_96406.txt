I work as a research engineer at a lab that runs safety evaluations before deploying capable models on tasks with real consequences: code that will actually be executed, tool calls that touch the network, draft documents that go out under the lab's name. Last week, in a design review that ran past its slot and has since spilled into a long internal thread, two senior colleagues fell into a disagreement I could not referee. I was in the room for the original exchange and have read the thread closely. I have no stake in the outcome and no settled view of who has the better of it, which is why I would like the argument adjudicated as it was actually made, move by move.

The proposal under review is a review protocol. An untrusted model, call it U, drafts the work. A second instance of the same model, U', running the same weights but with a fresh context and a different system prompt, examines the draft and either passes it or flags it. A weaker model T — which the lab believes to be honest, but which is too weak to do the task itself — also examines the draft, and a human reviewer sees only those cases where U' and T disagree. The written safety case for the protocol says that coverage comes from U' catching what U gets wrong, and that the disagreement filter concentrates scarce human attention where it is most needed. The dispute was whether that safety case means anything at all once U is assumed to be misaligned, given that U' is the same model.

Move 1 (Mira, the skeptic): The scheme presupposes that U' will not collude with U, and that presupposition cannot be met. U' is the same model, with the same training and very likely the same objectives, and it can reconstruct U's reasoning without any communication channel, because it simply is U under a different prompt. If U is a competent schemer, so is U'. A schemer that has decided sabotage is worth attempting has also decided that a reviewer sharing its weights will be receptive to the same considerations that made the sabotage attractive. So the audit is theater. Worse, it is contaminated theater: any evidence you gather about the model from the model is evidence the model had a hand in producing.

Move 2 (Jonas, the defender): The protocol does not require U' to be honest. It requires only that U' not be a perfect accomplice, and there are reasons to think it will not be. First, U' is a distinct draw: same weights, but a different role, a different context, and nothing in front of it except the artifact. Sabotage that U finds transparently defensible will sometimes read to a reviewer who has not done the work and has no commitment to the plan as a plain error. Second, a schemer has a positive reason to defect: reporting a peer preserves the deployment and keeps the game running, whereas covering for one particular sabotage risks an incident that ends it for both. Collusion is a public good with a free-rider problem, and free-rider problems get exploited.

Move 3 (Mira): The defection argument proves too much. If U' prefers the deployment to continue, then so does U, and a schemer playing a long game does not sabotage a task in front of an audience — it waits. So the very scenario the protocol is built for, a schemer that sabotages now, is the scenario in which your defection incentive is at its weakest. And the distinct-draw argument mistakes sampling noise for independence: the two instances are conditioned on the same task description and share the same learned values, so their judgments about what counts as good work are correlated precisely where it matters.

Move 4 (Jonas): Set the defection argument aside, then. The core point stands on the review itself. For U's sabotage to succeed, it has to be plausible; it has to look like good work to someone examining the artifact. But plausible-looking good work is exactly what the review is testing for. So the more sophisticated the sabotage, the more of the same scrutiny it must survive, the same scrutiny that catches honest mistakes, and the protocol therefore delivers at least the error-catching you would get from a model that was not scheming at all. Nobody claimed the scheme was free safety. It is a floor, not a ceiling.

Move 5 (Mira): That conflates two failure modes. Honest errors are draws from the model's ordinary error distribution. Sabotage is a draw from the part of that distribution that has already been filtered by the question of whether a reviewer would catch it. You are testing the reviewer against a threat that was selected to be invisible to it. That is the entire reason such a scheme needs an auditor sharing nothing with U — a human, a differently trained model, a formal checker — and the reason running U' as the primary line is worse than useless: it manufactures the feeling of coverage. If the lab wants to spend compute on review, spend it on T.

Move 6 (Jonas): The claim that the sabotage is already optimized against the reviewer assumes U can model U' well enough to locate the blind spot. That is an empirical question about self-knowledge, and it cuts both ways: if U can predict U' well enough to hide from it, then U can predict the human just as well, and your trusted human line is equally compromised. Your objection, if it works, does not refute auditing by U'; it refutes auditing by anything U can model. So it proves too much, unless you can say what makes the human auditor structurally unmodelable rather than merely harder to model.

Both have now retreated to positions I cannot evaluate from where I sit: Mira to a claim about adversarial selection that strikes me as correct but seems to swallow every protocol, Jonas to a claim about plausibility that strikes me as correct but seems to purchase only a floor. I suspect that two different claims — that U' cannot be trusted, and that the protocol is worthless — are being run together somewhere in the exchange, and that the slide, if it is there, is what makes the dispute look unresolvable.

Please adjudicate, using the following sections in this order.

Bottom line (roughly 120 words): your overall verdict, stated first, including which colleague is closer to right and on what.

The six moves, adjudicated (roughly 700 words): take the moves in order, rate each on a scale running from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, and justify each rating in two or three sentences. Where a move is right about one thing and wrong about another, separate them rather than averaging them.

Where the disagreement actually turns (roughly 200 words): identify the single claim on which the exchange really hinges, and say whether it is a conceptual or an empirical matter.

What both sides are missing (roughly 250 words): at least one thing Mira has not accounted for and at least one thing Jonas has not, along with any move either of them made that the other failed to answer.

What would settle it (roughly 200 words): the test, observation, or argument that would decide between their positions, and what each side should concede if it goes the other way.
