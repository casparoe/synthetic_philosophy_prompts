I've been having the same argument for about a month now with a friend who keeps saying the paradox of tolerance settles all of it, and I've started keeping a note on my phone of cases and what I actually think about them, because I keep catching myself contradicting myself. Roughly: (1) neo-Nazis want to march through a town where a lot of Holocaust survivors live — let them march, with a police escort, and I'd defend any lawyer who took their case, though it turns my stomach. (2) A party that campaigns on abolishing elections once it wins — keep it off the ballot, I don't need to think about that one. (3) A church of a few hundred people that teaches apostates deserve to die, but never does anything about it and has no political weight — leave them alone; punishing a belief feels worse than the belief. (4) A group that says the country can only be fixed by expelling an ethnic group, and won't rule out doing it by force — not protected, and I don't care that it's only words. (5) A lecturer who publishes work arguing one group of people is genetically less intelligent than another — he keeps his job, though I'd want students to be able to avoid the class. (6) A group of ex-Muslims who call Islam a violent political ideology and want it criticised the way we criticise fascism — allowed, obviously, even though millions find it deeply offensive. (7) A movement, currently small, whose whole platform is that criticising its religion should be illegal — I want this banned, and I can't tell you why without sounding like I've just picked my favourite team, since it isn't violent, isn't large, and isn't asking for anything except one topic being off-limits. (8) A magazine that prints a cartoon of Muhammad knowing there will be riots and deaths — print it; the deaths are on the rioters. (9) A community that wants to live by its own rules and opt out of the wider society without imposing anything on anyone — leave them completely alone.

My friend's line is that tolerance is a contract and you can't invoke it after you've torn it up, but that doesn't seem to get (3) or (5), and it seems to get (1) wrong. My own best guess, after staring at the list, is that what I'm actually tracking isn't whether the group is intolerant of people, it's whether the group's success would take a topic off the table — (2) and (7) kill the argument itself, while (1), (3), (5), (6) and (9) only want to win it, or want nothing to do with it. But then I've talked myself into saying that silencing a critic is worse than killing apostates, which is a monstrous thing to believe, and I don't know whether to give up (3) or (7).

So: is there a principle that gets all nine, or do I have to drop a verdict, and if so which one? Please state whatever principle you land on precisely enough that I can actually apply it, tell me what it says about two cases I haven't listed — a popular, entirely tolerant movement that wants to ban a hostile foreign state's TV channel from broadcasting here, and a satirical party that runs on abolishing the senate as a joke and then genuinely wins seats — and be honest about which of my nine calls the principle can't accommodate. I'd rather have a simple principle that makes me abandon (3) or (7) then a five-clause thing that saves everything.
