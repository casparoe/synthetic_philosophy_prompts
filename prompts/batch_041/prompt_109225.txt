Heres something I keep going back and forth on. Every lifeboat ethics case I've seen gives the boat a fixed capacity — n seats, n+1 people in the water — so the whole problem collapses into triage: decide who's salvageable and who gets left. A friend of mine thinks that's a rigged picture. A real boat stays afloat only because the people in it keep bailing and shifting their weight, so capacity isnt a number fixed in advance; it depends on how many are aboard and how well they cooperate. On her view the interesting question was never who to exclude but how to get the coordination right, and every lifeboat case is really a commons problem in disguise.

Please explain the conceptual issue here. What work is the fixed-capacity assumption actually doing — which standard conclusions depend on it and which survive without it? And is the raft reframing a genuine conceptual shift or just a metaphor swap, given that even a raft has some point where one more body sinks it? If capacity really is endogenous, does the ethics turn additive (more hands, more floating) or does it just move the threshold question one step back?
