I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind and religion, and I want one 90-minute session that makes about 18 students feel the force of Ayer's position on life after death — specifically the claim that the belief in survival after the death of the body, in the form it is usually held, is not a genuine hypothesis, because we lack a criterion of personal identity that could be applied across the gap. I want them to run into the problem themselves: that the difficulty is not a shortage of evidence but the absence of any specification of what would count as evidence either way. Ayer had a near-death experience late in life, after which he reported a red light and a presence he took to be responsible for the universe, and he still refused to treat the episode as establishing survival; I want that refusal to feel like the honest response rather than a stubborn one.

Design a runnable activity for the session. The rules, roles, or incentives should arrange things so that the students construct criteria of personal identity, apply them to cases of apparent survival, and then discover for themselves that their criteria either collapse into bodily continuity, or become so permissive that they cannot distinguish survival from ordinary psychological change, or have no independent test. They should reach the impasse before anyone names Ayer or his position.

Please include:

- The setting and a timed sequence for the session.
- The rules, roles, and materials, including any case cards, handouts, or scoring sheets written out so I can use them directly.
- At least four cases: one near-death experience of the kind Ayer had; one claim of reincarnation or mediumship; one case of gradual or partial continuity (such as brain replacement, fission, or a slow change of character and memory); and one case in which the only evidence is testimony from a single person.
- A debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the structure of Ayer's argument, including the distinction between saying that survival is false and saying that it is not a genuine hypothesis.
- An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it.
- A frank note on which feature of Ayer's view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

The students should not read anything in advance, and the answer should not include quotations from Ayer or from secondary literature. Please write your answer in British English.
