I'm in a small reading group on the philosophy of physics — five of us, mostly physics graduate students with one philosopher — and we have now spent three consecutive meetings circling the phrase the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics. It comes from the title of Wigner's essay, published in 1960, and I have read it once; I am not asking you to summarise it. What I want is an inventory of what the phrase is doing when people deploy it now, because in our group it has plainly come loose from the essay. In the essay the point seems to be, roughly, that the usefulness of mathematics in the natural sciences is "something bordering on the mysterious and that there is no rational explanation for it" — but the phrase as we use it has drifted, and I can no longer tell which drift is legitimate. Last week, for instance, one member invoked it to argue that we should expect an eventual theory of quantum gravity to be expressible in mathematics that either already exists or will be invented for exactly that purpose — that the phrase licenses optimism about unification. Another member means by it only that the same mathematics turns up in places nobody expected: geometry developed as pure mathematics turning out to be the geometry of spacetime, or group theory turning out to be the language of particle physics. A third treats it as near-tautological, on the grounds that we count a domain as physics only once it has been mathematised, so the slogan tells us nothing about the world. And someone else, half joking, said it shows the universe is made of mathematics. My suspicion is that at least two of these are the same claim wearing different hats, that one is not a claim at all, and that the version with the pure-mathematics examples supports less than its users think.

Here is the draft list I made on the train, which I don't trust: (1) mathematics is useful in physics; (2) the usefulness of mathematics in physics has no explanation; (3) the universe is mathematical, so of course mathematics describes it; (4) physics uses mathematics because we only call something physics when we can mathematise it. I think (2) and (4) cannot both be the point, and I suspect (1) is what most people actually mean without noticing. Please treat this list as a starting point rather than a plan: tell me which items are really the same reading, which are ill-formed or vacuous, and what I have left out — in particular any reading between the trivial and the cosmic that I have missed, and any reading on which the phrase would be straightforwardly false rather than merely unproven.

Lay your answer out under exactly these headings, in this order. First, "The uses in my group" (about 100 words): restate the four uses I described and say which distinct readings they seem to presuppose. Second, "The menu" (800–1000 words): a numbered inventory of the distinct, precisely stated claims the phrase could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical, each given as a sentence with a truth value rather than a gesture, and for each one a verdict — trivially true, substantive and defensible, probably false, or not a claim at all — plus one line on what evidence or argument would bear on it and which of my group's uses it can actually support. Third, "Verdicts at a glance": a table with one row per reading, columns for the reading in a few words, its status, and what it would take to be true. Fourth, "Which reading my friend needs" (about 150 words): the friend who thinks the phrase licenses optimism about unification — say which reading would actually give him that conclusion and whether it is one of the defensible ones. Fifth, "Which reading is worth arguing about" (about 150 words): your own view of the one reading that is both substantive and live. I don't want a literature survey or reading recommendations; I want the analysis, so please don't spend words on citations or on who said what.
