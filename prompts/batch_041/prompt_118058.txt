Ok so I'm in this philosophy of mind seminar and we just spent two weeks on original vs. derived intentionality — Dennett, Searle, all the stuff about whether a thermostat "believes" anything and whether a machine can have genuine aboutness or only the borrowed kind. For the final paper I have to write an essay that actually takes a position, and I really don't want to rehash the Chinese Room for the millionth time, so I want to build it around a case of my own. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mind slash cognitive scientist who knows both the analytic lit and how actual ML systems work, and write me that essay. Before you commit to a thesis, spend a bit of time brainstorming (in your head is fine) about where the real crux is and which framings are traps, then argue it out properly. Assume I've read the standard stuff, so skip the intro-level exposition and get in the weeds.

Here's the case. Imagine a fully automated factory — call it the Foundry — that humans built and seeded a few centuries ago, but that has since been running entirely on its own: it designs and builds its own machines, redesigns itself, and nobody alive has any idea how most of its design lineage came to be what it is. It's not intelligent in any interesting sense, it's just an enormous automated design-and-build process, kind of like a compiler that keeps rewriting itself. Now take one of the devices it spits out — say a sorter with an internal state that reliably co-varies with whether a bin is about to overflow, and that behaves accordingly. Does that device have intentionality, and if so of what kind? Here's the twist I actually care about: suppose a human engineer, working seperately and never having seen the Foundry's device, happens to build a physically identical device with the same internal state and the same behavior. Are the two devices different, intentionality-wise, in any principled way? The human-made one looks like a textbook case of derived intentionality — whatever aboutness it has is inherited from the engineer's intentions. But the Foundry one's lineage traces back through a process nobody understands and maybe to no intentions at all, other than the original humans who built the Foundry (and behind them evolution, and behind that...?). So either the two identical devices differ in a way that can't be read off their physical makeup, or the original/derived distinction is doing less work than it's supposed to.

Write the essay so it weighs the strongest arguments on both sides. On one side, the Searle-ish line that there's a real, intrinsic kind of intentionality that only biological brains have, and that everything else — Foundry devices, the Foundry itself, my laptop, even me reading off a script — has at best derived or as-if intentionality, which I suspect is a definately awkward place to land once you notice how the Foundry muddies the inheritance story. On the other side, the Dennett-ish line that the original/derived split isn't a distinction in nature at all, just a fact about how useful the intentional stance is for predicting the thing (his vending machine case is basically my Foundry minus the weird genealogy). Also seriously consider a third option, namely that "derived intentionality" is not one thing — derivation from a designer's intent, from a selection process, and from training might come apart, and the Foundry case might be what pries them apart. Don't end on a fence: reach a verdict about whether the two devices differ and whether original intentionality is even a coherent thing to want, and defend it against the best objection you can come up with.
