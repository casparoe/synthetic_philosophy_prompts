I am a novelist by trade and, for the past eleven years, have also taught a final-year undergraduate seminar on consciousness to students reading literature and philosophy jointly. I am now setting the paper for that seminar. I want the closing essay question to be one that cannot be answered by reciting the slogan that consciousness is not a single place but a shifting coalition of drafts, and I want it to force candidates to adjudicate between the model and the demands of storytelling rather than to paraphrase either. The difficulty is this: the multiple drafts model is a claim about how content is fixed in the brain, yet narrative — my own trade — appears to require precisely the single vantage point that the model denies.

To keep the question from being answered by recall, I have introduced two terms of my own into the rubric. Candidates will meet them for the first time on the paper and are required to use them. The first is the staging fallacy: inferring, from the fact that any telling occupies one vantage point, that the mind being told about must likewise occupy one. The second is the converse drafting fallacy: inferring, from the absence of any vantage point in the brain, that there is no vantage point in experience. Candidates must decide which, if either, of these is a genuine fallacy, and whether Dennett's own position commits the second.

Candidates have ninety minutes for this question and should write no more than 1,500 words. No reading list is supplied and no citations are expected; the argument is the deliverable.

**Question**

Dennett's multiple drafts model denies that there is any single place or moment at which the contents of a mind are assembled for inspection, and the Cartesian theatre is the name he gives to the picture that this denial is meant to displace. Consider a narrative device I shall call the withheld vantage: a novel narrated throughout without any seat of consciousness, so that the reader receives only public occurrences — speech, gesture, the position of the furniture — and must assemble whatever inner life the characters possess from these fragments alone.

(i) Does the withheld vantage vindicate the multiple drafts model, or does it merely reproduce the Cartesian theatre one level up, in the reader?

(ii) Is the staging fallacy a real fallacy, or does the multiple drafts model depend on denying something that even a fictional mind must possess?

(iii) If there is no fact of the matter about the moment at which a content becomes conscious, is that claim metaphysical or merely epistemic, and does the answer change what the model can claim about a reader's reconstruction of a character?

**Model answer (approximately 650 words; the shape of a first-class response)**

The multiple drafts model should be reconstructed with care before it is applied. Its central claim is not that the brain is busy, but that content fixation has no finish line: discriminations are made in parallel, at many sites, over intervals, and what a creature comes to be in a position to report, remember and act upon is a matter of which of these distributed processes come to dominate the ongoing economy of its behaviour. The Cartesian theatre is therefore not merely the doctrine that a little person sits in the head. It is any picture that requires a place where the order of experience is finally imposed, and it survives in wholly materialist theories that retain a privileged moment of "entry into consciousness".

On (i), the tempting answer is that the withheld vantage dramatises the model, because it refuses to seat the character anywhere. That answer is too quick. The device is an authorial construction, and the reader's reconstruction of the character is an act performed by a single reader at a single sitting. But the reconstruction is not a Cartesian show, and the reason is instructive: it is distributed across the sentences and endlessly revisable, and there is no privileged moment at which the character's mind is pronounced finished. So the withheld vantage does illustrate something — but it illustrates the epistemic predicament, that a mind must be inferred from traces, rather than the metaphysical claim, that there is nothing behind the traces to be inferred. If the theatre reappears anywhere it reappears in the reader's cognitive architecture, which is a different thesis about a different subject.

On (ii), the staging fallacy is genuine as a narratological inference and question-begging as a philosophical one. It is invalid to move from the fact that tellers occupy a vantage point to the conclusion that minds do, since a fact about representation is not a fact about the represented. But the philosopher who holds that experiential unity is a datum rather than a construction will not accept that the inference has been shown to be fallacious; for such a philosopher, being for someone is not a representational fact at all. The converse drafting fallacy is where the real contest lies. Dennett moves from the absence of a finish line in the brain to the absence of a subject of experience, and that transition is the contentious step. To resist it is not nostalgia for the theatre; it is to hold that the datum the model set out to explain has been explained away instead.

On (iii), the denial that there is a fact of the matter is meant metaphysically: there is no determinate time at which a content becomes conscious, not merely no way for us to tell. Read epistemically, the model would be a truism about measurement and would threaten nothing. Read metaphysically, it makes our first-person reports systematically non-authoritative about the very events they purport to report, which is what generates the objection that the model cannot accommodate the datum it began from. A candidate who slides between the two readings will produce a competent but second-class answer, because the whole dispute turns on which reading the model needs.

The fictional case is finally a disanalogy, and a good answer should say so. A fictional mind is constituted by its text; for such a mind there is a fact of the matter, fixed by the author, and unity is guaranteed rather than discovered. Fiction is therefore the wrong tribunal for the model. But it is a useful model of the model: a novel can be written not to conceal the drafting but to represent it, so that the reader's own revisions become the subject. That, rather than the withheld vantage, is the device worth defending.

**Marking scheme (total 20 marks)**

Five must-hit points, four marks each.

1. **Reconstruction of the model (4).** Must state that the denial is of a finish line and that content fixation is distributed, and must not reduce the model to the claim that the brain processes things in parallel.
2. **The Cartesian theatre (4).** Must recognise that the target survives in materialist guise — that the homunculus need not be a little person, only a place where order is imposed.
3. **Metaphysical versus epistemic (4).** Must distinguish the two readings in (iii) and give a verdict on which Dennett requires. *Typical error:* treating the claim as a modest point about our inability to time experiences.
4. **The two fallacies (4).** Must diagnose the staging fallacy as invalid narratologically but question-begging philosophically, and must locate the converse drafting fallacy as Dennett's contentious step. *Typical error:* declaring the staging fallacy obviously a fallacy without noticing that the reply begs the question.
5. **The fictional case (4).** Must identify the disanalogy — author-constituted minds have a determinate fact of the matter — and must nonetheless extract the constructive move, that a text can represent drafting rather than conceal it. *Typical error:* treating the withheld vantage as straightforward evidence for the model.

**Separating an A from a B.** An A answer contains all five points, keeps the metaphysical and epistemic readings apart throughout, and turns the fictional case against the model while still using it. A B answer reconstructs the model accurately and sees the Cartesian theatre, but either collapses the two readings, or treats the novelistic case as evidence rather than analogy, or fails to see that the staging fallacy is question-begging when turned on the defender of experiential unity. No marks are awarded for naming further literature; candidates are marked on the argument alone.
