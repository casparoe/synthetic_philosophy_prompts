I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level seminar in decision theory and rational choice, and I need one essay question on Pascal's mugging — one that a student can't pass by reproducing the standard statement of the scenario and the stock list of replies. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each of them wrong. The exam is closed-book and no citations should be required; keep the model answer to what a strong candidate could produce in 90 minutes.

The question should turn on two variants I use in lecture. In the first, the mugger promises a payoff that is enormous but finite — nothing infinite, nothing that breaks arithmetic — at a probability that is minuscule but which you have no principled way to bound from below. In the second, the mugger threatens a loss of comparable size unless he is paid. Students reliably find the first absurd and the second frightening, and I want the question to force them to say whether that asymmetry has any basis in the theory or is an illusion. It should also make them take a side on whether the mugging is a reductio of expected-utility maximization itself or a reductio of the anti-fanatic intuitions we bring to it, and to say what a principled repair would have to give up — and why repairs that merely cap utility or rule out "astronomical" probabilities risk being ad hoc.
