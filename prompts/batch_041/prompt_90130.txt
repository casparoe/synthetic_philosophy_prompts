Ok so this one is a bit of an odd request but I've been chewing on it. I want you to predict what John Stuart Mill would have thought about Indian independence. Not the vague "Victorian liberal, product of his time, probably ambivalent" mush. An actual prediction, argued for.

Here's the context I'm working from, and please tell me up front if any of it is off, because some of it I'm going from memory: Mill spent basically his whole working life, 1823 to 1858, in the East India Company's London office, ending up as the head of the office that drafted the dispatches sent out to India. When Parliament took India away from the Company and handed it to the Crown in 1858, he was against it, I believe on the grounds that the people who actually knew Indian administration were being shoved aside and that Crown rule would turn into a patronage toy for MPs and a mess of parliamentary meddling. Then there's the passage in On Liberty that I keep coming back to: "Despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement, and the means justified by actually effecting that end." A little later in the same chapter he says liberty as a principle has no application to any state of things before mankind has become "capable of being improved by free and equal discussion," and that until then there's nothing for people but implicit obedience to an Akbar or a Charlemagne if they're lucky enough to get one.

So here's the scenario he never lived to see. It's 1940. India is in open ferment, there's a mass nationalist movement, and there's a real argument in London about whether to move seriously toward self-government or to dig in and hold on. Mill gets to weigh in. What does he say?

Some things I want you to respect:

Don't let him be anachronistic. He doesn't get to use words or concepts he wouldn't have had, and he doesn't get to conveniently anticipate every good 20th-century anti-colonial argument. If his framework pushes him somewhere modern readers find ugly, let it. I want the actual Mill, not the admirable one.

Notice that his own sentence has an empirical escape hatch built into it. The despotism is only legitimate "provided the end be their improvement, and the means justified by actually effecting that end." That clause makes his position falsifiable. So part of what I want is: what evidence would he take as showing that British rule in India had or hadn't actually effected the improvement it was supposed to? And how would he have handled the fact that he'd be judging that from London, from a career's worth of dispatches, decades after leaving the job?

Also, the phrase "capable of being improved by free and equal discussion" is doing a lot of work. Would he think India had reached that point by 1940? And here's the thing I'd most like you to dig into: is there a way for him to answer that question that isn't circular? If the test for readiness is whether a people can be improved by free and equal discussion, and the British are the ones deciding when the discussion counts as free and equal, he seems to have built himself a machine for never letting go. Does he have a non-circular exit condition available? If yes, say what it is. If no, say so and say what he'd probably do anyway.

One more wrinkle, since it's the part that makes me doubt the tidy answer: the Indian nationalist case was often put in the language of rights and self-determination, which is not really Mill's native language for this, and the liberal case for holding on was often put in exactly the terms Mill himself supplied, about majority tyranny, about the fragility of institutions among populations that hadn't been educated into them, about the danger that a premature handover produces something worse than what it replaced. A prediction that just has him siding with the nationalists because nationalism eventually won is worthless to me.
