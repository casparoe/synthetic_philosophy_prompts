I am trying to determine whether a certain family of objections in ethics has a name — the family, rather than its several members, which I think I can already tell apart. The family has this shape. A theory is on the table, and nobody in the discussion disputes its verdict about what is to be done: the act it recommends is the act everyone agrees is right, or at least the act the objector concedes to be right. What is objected to is instead what the theory requires the agent to be thinking in the course of doing it — that, in order to act as the theory directs, the agent must have before her mind, and be moved by, a consideration that a good agent would not have before her mind at that moment. The offending thought is not treated as evidence that the act is wrong; the thought itself is said to be the defect.

Two instances. First, a small one. A woman I know has adopted, as a policy for her close friendships, the rule of doing in each interaction what a prudent steward of the relationship would do: investing where the friendship has been neglected, avoiding overdrawing, reciprocating what she receives. What she does is indistinguishable from what an unreflective good friend does. Yet when, in the middle of a conversation, she remarks that she has decided to invest a little more in this friendship because it has been running at a deficit, something seems to me to have gone wrong, and it is not that she has done the wrong thing. Second, a larger one. A man holds that promises are to be kept because breaking them erodes an institution everyone needs. He keeps his promises, and keeps exactly the ones a conscientious person keeps. But he cannot keep one without the thought that the institution would suffer if he did not, and the complaint I hear is that a promise kept that way is not a promise but a policy.

I have heard such cases described in three ways — as failures of integrity, as a kind of alienation, and as the theory's asking for one thought too many — and, half-remembered, a fourth as ethical theories inducing a kind of schizophrenia. I cannot tell whether these name four distinct things, one thing under several aspects, or whether any of them names the family I have described rather than one of its members.

What I want is this. First: does the family, characterised as above, have an established name? If so, which term, and what exactly does it cover? If not, coin one and define it sharply enough that I can see what falls outside. Second: for each of those descriptions, the claim it standardly carries and how it differs from the others — a family tree, not an essay on any single member. Third: what separates this family from two neighbours I keep collapsing into it, namely the objection that a theory demands too much of an agent (where the cost of the act is the complaint) and the objection that a theory is self-effacing (where the complaint is that the agent cannot coherently consult the theory). Fourth: where the edges lie. Does the objection apply when the theory merely permits the offending thought rather than requiring it? When the agent has the thought but it does no work in the deliberation? When the theory never mentions the thought, but the act would be irrational by the theory's own lights without it? And does the shape survive outside morality — in a policy for friendship, or a rule for giving gifts?

I am not asking whether these objections succeed; I want the taxonomy and the boundaries, with my two instances placed inside it. Answer in no more than 650 words.
