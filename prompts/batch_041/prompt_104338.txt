A recurring move in contemporary metaethics treats moral properties as analogous to colors. On this comparison, an object's being red is neither a bare mind-independent physical property of the sort a naive realist posits nor a mere projection of the observer's attitudes; it is a disposition to elicit a certain kind of experience in suitably constituted observers, and yet color discourse remains truth-apt, corrigible, and governed by standards of correctness. The suggestion is that an action's being wrong might be real and knowable in the same way — real enough to sustain truth and knowledge, but not a mind-independent feature of the world of the kind that naturalistic ontologies struggle to accommodate. A colleague in my department leans on this comparison to defend what she calls a middle position, and she treats the color case as proof that combining realism with anti-realism is coherent rather than a merely verbal compromise. I would like an audit of how far the comparison actually holds.

Please respond with exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, and with no introduction or conclusion. The first should set out the correspondence between the color case and the moral case, element by element. The second should say which parts of the comparison are doing genuine work for her argument and which are decorative. The third should isolate the disanalogies that matter specifically for the use to which she puts the analogy, as opposed to those that do not. The fourth should identify what the color case quietly imports — in particular about the observer, about the standard of correctness, and about the causal relation between the property and the experience — that the moral case may not be entitled to. The fifth should say whether her middle position survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along, and, if it cannot bear the weight, what a better comparison would be.
