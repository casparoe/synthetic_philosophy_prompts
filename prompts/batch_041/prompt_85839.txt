I'm a retired physiologist — forty years in a lab, mostly cardiovascular and renal stuff — and since I packed it in I've been doing alot of reading in philosophy of science, badly and happily. I've gotten stuck on the philosophy of psychiatric classification. I think I've got the basic move behind Ian Hacking's looping effects: people aren't electrons, so when you sort them the sorting changes them, the membership of the category comes back different, you revise and it loops again — no fixed target the way there is for a particle or a species. What I want isn't an exegesis of Hacking though. I want the spec sheet. Write me the desiderata: what would any adequate theory of psychiatric classification have to deliver before I should take it seriously?

Put the case behind each requirement, and include at least these: where the line falls between disorder and ordinary variation (the grief/depression boundary, which the manual has now moved more than once; shyness vs. social anxiety; the attenuated-psychosis category they parked at the back of the manual for further study; and the cautionary tale from my own old field, where hypertension is a threshold on a continuum that a committee moved, which is a very different animal from TB); the fact that disorder seems to mix a factual claim that something is broken with a value judgment that the break is bad for you, with the declassification of homosexuality and the nineteenth-century invention of a diagnosis for slaves who ran away sitting behind it; the looping problem, with cases like the dissociative-identity-disorder epidemic, anorexia, autism and the neurodiversity pushback, and the culture-bound syndromes plus the worry that we export our categories and then find them everywhere; reliability vs. validity, i.e. the fact that the 1980 revolution bought agreement between clinicians by going operational and may have paid for it in validity, the huge comorbidity and heterogeneity, and the fact that no biomarkers have turned up despite decades of looking; the awkward fact that these categories have to serve the clinic, the insurance form and the courtroom at once; and the risk of diagnostic inflation, i.e. that widening a category turns ordinary life into illness. Then tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudice of one camp (is a classification required to be a natural kind, or is that a taste? is clinical usefulness a requirement on a theory of classification, or a different job entirely?), flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — I especially want to know whether the demand that the categories be culture-free and universal can survive the looping effect, and whether the administrative demand can survive the validity demand — and say which single requirement knocks out the most contenders. I don't need the contenders scored in any detail, I'll do that in the reading group.

One last thing: seperate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's a live disagreement, and don't hand me a contested view dressed up as the consensus — I'd rather see where you're unsure than get a confident line.
