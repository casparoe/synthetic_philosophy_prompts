I'm a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through a book set inside a hospital's crisis-triage commitee — the people who, when there aren't enough ventilators or ICU beds to go around, have to decide who gets one. I've been reading up on how these committees actually operate, and the thing that keeps tripping me up is that they all seem to do the same two-step. First they score every patient on a single number, usually something like expected life-years gained, adjusted for quality of life, and then they just rank: highest first, down the list, until the resources run out. One scale, everything on it, and "better" simply means higher on the scale. But then in the actual meetings, especially at the margins where two patients are close, they stop doing that and switch into a completely different language — who has the strongest claim, whether someone already had a turn, whether the same kind of person always seems to lose. They'll override a higher score for reasons that don't fit on the scale at all, and nobody in the room acts like they've contradicted themselves. I've noticed a milder version of the same thing in the way regulators rank safety rules by cost per life saved, so I don't think it's just hospitals.

Here's what I'd like from you. Tell me what this practice — the single score, the ranking, the cutoff — has to presuppose about value and about what "better than" even means. Does it need "better than" to be transitive, so that if A beats B and B beats C then A beats C? Does it need the goods at stake to be commensurable, all convertible onto one scale with no genuine trade-off between things that can't be weighed against each other? Does it need the scale to be complete, so any two patients are comparable, and does it need it to be cardinal rather than a mere ordering, so that a ten-point gain means the same thing at the top of the scale as at the bottom? Then the reverse direction: which rival pictures of value does the practice quietly rule out — the one where some goods can't be traded off at all, or where the ranking is only local and contextual, or where the honest answer is that there is no fact of the matter and we simply have to choose? Please be strict about the difference between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely goes well with. My hunch is that the two-step needs less than it looks like it needs, and I'd like you to either confirm that or show me where I'm wrong.

And the part I actually care about for the novel. Take seriously the idea that "better than" can cycle — the kind of case where each item in a long series is better than the next, but the last is better than the first. What concretely breaks in this committee's procedure if that's how value really works? Would it show up as a decision that flips depending on the order the patients happen to come through the door, or on who's sitting on the committee that night? Would the score have to go entirely, or could you keep it and just add rules around it? And if they gave up the single scale, what would they have to start doing instead — because they still have to decide tonight, with whoever is in front of them, and I need to know what my characters would plausibly be arguing about.
