I am a second-year PhD student preparing an application for a nine-month writing fellowship, and I need a two-page research proposal on an angle in Carnap's political thought that I believe is underexplored.

My half-formed idea is this. Carnap is usually read as having two separable lives: the technician of logic, semantics, and inductive probability on one side, and the socialist and pacifist of the Vienna Circle's public front on the other. The technical work gets treated as value-neutral machinery; the politics gets treated as biography, or as scenery from the Red Vienna milieu. I want to argue that the two are structurally continuous, and that the continuity runs through a quantity I have not seen named, which I would call framework risk: the degree to which adopting a conceptual framework, or a formal system for reasoning under uncertainty, commits you in advance of the evidence to a particular distribution of expectation — how quickly the system generalizes from the observed to the unobserved, how hard it discounts surprising data, and therefore how much a community relying on it exposes itself to being wrong in a coordinated rather than a scattered way.

Why I think this is a real question and not a pun: Carnap treats the choice of a linguistic framework as a practical decision rather than a truth-evaluable one, and treats the construction of precise concepts as engineering rather than discovery. If framework choice is practical, it can be done well or badly relative to a purpose, and some of the purposes are collective ones — coordinating inquiry, allocating credence across a population of researchers, deciding what to do before the evidence is in. That is the shape of a planning problem. My hypothesis is that Carnap's socialism is not an ornament on his epistemology but is legible in the design constraints he accepts: he wants frameworks that are tolerant, revisable, and public, and he wants the machinery of inductive reasoning to be candid about how much of its output is convention rather than discovery. Framework risk is the name I want to give to the quantity those constraints are meant to manage.

What I need from you is a two-page proposal for a nine-month fellowship. It should state the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer — not explore the relationship between Carnap's logic and his politics, but something a reader could accept or reject. It should explain why the standard readings leave the question open, without becoming a literature review: refer to positions and arguments rather than to citations, since I will supply the references myself and I do not want any invented. It should set out a method — what I would actually do month by month, what materials I would work from (the published philosophical texts, the programmatic and manifesto-style writing from the Vienna Circle period, the later intellectual autobiography, and whatever else you judge relevant), and what form a result would take. It should say explicitly what would count as the project failing; I would rather have a proposal that names how this thesis could turn out to be empty than one that pretends it cannot. It should include a short section on what would change my mind — what textual evidence, or what counterexample drawn from the technical material, would show that framework risk is a projection of mine onto Carnap rather than something operating in his work — along with a candid statement of where the argument is most likely to break, especially the danger that I am merely redescribing features common to any probabilistic system as a political commitment.

Scope it honestly for nine months. If the honest answer is that this is a three-year project wearing a nine-month coat, say so, and tell me which slice the nine months should be.

Begin with the proposal itself, with no preface and no commentary on what you are doing.
