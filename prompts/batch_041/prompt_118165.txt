I run a monthly discussion group at my local library, and next month we're taking on who should be allowed to vote — lowering the voting age, votes for people who aren't citizens, and whether voting should be compulsory. I have no academic background and I don't want the evening to turn into people swapping slogans about taxation and representation, so what I'm after is a map of the positions rather than a summary of the usual arguments for and against each reform. I've been trying to work out the shape of the disagreement myself, and I'd like you to build the map out of my attempt — correcting it wherever I've put the joints in the wrong place.

I think at least three questions are tangled together, and most people answer all three at once without noticing. The first is what grounds the vote. One answer is that the law binds you: you can be fined, prosecuted, conscripted or evicted under it, and whoever is bound should have a say. Another is that it's enough to be affected by what the law does — a foreign firm regulated by our environmental rules, a tourist, a child whose school is being closed — whether or not it binds you directly. These two come apart in both directions, and I want a map that keeps them apart rather than running them together. The second question is what the vote is for: protecting your interests, marking you as a full member of the community with the standing to speak in its name, or simply extracting information about what people want — information that might come out better if only the well-informed voted. The third is whether casting a vote is a right, a duty owed to your fellow citizens, or a burden, because that is what decides whether compulsory voting is a way of enforcing fairness, a violation of something important, or forced labour.

There's a fourth thing I only noticed afterwards, and I think it breaks the map if it isn't handled. If the answer to the first question is that everyone affected ought to have a say, then who decides who is affected? The people who already hold the vote seem to be judging their own case. I'd like to know whether that is a genuine circularity or something that can be defused, and I'd like the map to show which positions are stuck with it.

Then there are cases I keep getting stuck on, and I'd like the map to place each of them: a sixteen-year-old who works and pays tax; someone who has lived here for thirty years, pays tax, and has never applied for citizenship; a citizen who moved abroad at twenty-five and hasn't lived here since; an adult under a guardianship order because of dementia; a prisoner serving a long sentence; a homeowner living abroad who pays local taxes here; children, who are bound by everything and can vote on nothing, along with the question of whether a parent might hold a vote in trust for a child until the child comes of age; and people who will be affected by decisions taken now but who don't exist yet.

What I want back is a taxonomy built from the questions rather than from a list of labels: first the questions that split the space, then the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual positions that live in those combinations. Where a combination is empty, tell me why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Give each position the one thing its holder has the hardest time explaining, and where two positions can't be told apart by any question you could put to someone, say so rather than inventing a distinction. Use a name where a position has one and mark it unnamed where it doesn't, and don't bother with who said what — I won't be citing anyone. I'd be disappointed if the map had no room for the view that everyone bound by the law should vote, for compulsory voting understood as a duty, and for restricting the vote to those who can pass some sort of test, but only if those really do fall out of the questions; don't bolt them on.

A dozen or so positions at most, please, and write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you'd explain it to me in a long letter. I know a tree usually wants a diagram; sentences will do.
