I'm a second-year cog sci student and I've been trying to write a short essay for a seminar called "Minds and Machines" on the phrase "eliciting latent knowledge" that's been floating around the alignment world for a few years now. My advisor read a draft and said the whole topic is mush and I should either sharpen it or drop it, so I want comments on whether what I've got actually cuts anything at all. The two things I'm most worried about are (a) whether my central distinction is doing real work or is just a fancy relabeling of stuff people already say, and (b) whether I've got the empirical side right, because I'm not an interpretability person and I'm scared I've written something that anyone who actually trains these things will find obviously false. I also argued about this for an hour with a friend who does mech interp and she thinks I'm overthinking the whole thing and that the answer is just "train better probes," which may be a sign I'm missing something. Here's the draft:

*I want to argue that "latent knowledge" is usually framed in a way that quietly assumes the model has one channel running from the world to its words, and that the interesting gap isn't between what a model knows and what it says — it's between two different uses that the model's internal state gets put to. Call a proposition p known-for-use-U by a system S if there's some intervention on S's internal state that changes S's performance on U, and the effect of that intervention on U tracks the truth of p. On this definition, "knowledge" isn't a property of a system on its own; it's a three-place relation between a system, a proposition, and a downstream use. The reason "latent knowledge" feels spooky is that we leave the third slot implicit — we mean our use, the use we wanted, not any use the model has.*

*The standard example is the pharmacist who insists she doesn't remember which drugs interact, but catches the interaction every time she checks a prescription. Most people say she knows it and just can't report it. But what licenses the verdict is that we can point to a use — checking prescriptions — where her behavior tracks the truth. The model case is supposed to be the same, and usually isn't, because when a network gets the right answer in a context where the truth is rewarded, we can't tell whether it's tracking the truth or tracking surface features that merely correlated with the truth during training. So the honest version of the question is: which uses, reachable by realistic prompting, does the network's state serve truth-tracking-ly? That's an empirical question about the network, and it has nothing to do with introspection, deception, or whether there's anything it's like to be the network. I think most of the spookiness drains out there, but I'm not sure what's left is still interesting.*

*The bit I'm most attached to: the pharmacist analogy suggests latent knowledge is a fact about the knower, but on my account it's a fact about the knower and the audience. Two deployments of the same weights — one that asks the model to answer exam questions, one that asks it to flag bad reasoning in a document — can differ in which propositions the model knows-for-that-use without anything inside the model changing. If that's right, "eliciting latent knowledge" is a slightly misleading name for what's really a question about deployment contracts, i.e. about whose downstream task gets to define the target, and the technical work of finding a truth-tracking probe is downstream of a normative choice we haven't made explicit. I don't know if that's a real contribution or just me re-describing the standard point that evaluations are task-relative.*

If you think the central distinction is vacuous, spend one of the five explaining why rather than tacking it on at the end.
