So I've gone down a rabbit hole on Skolem's paradox and I want your help sorting the escape routes. Setting, so we're aligned: take a first-order theory like ZF. If it has a model at all, Löwenheim–Skolem hands you a countable one. But inside that model you can run the proof of Cantor's theorem and conclude that there are uncountable sets — the model has no bijection between those sets and its naturals, and there's no such bijection in the model to be found. So "uncountable" is true in the model and false of the model. Great, love that.

Here's my list of responses. Rank them, best to worst, by how much of the paradox each one actually dissovles, with a score out of 1 for each and a sentence or two on why it lands where it does. If two of them are the same answer wearing different hats, collapse them and say which is the real one. Please don't hand me "each captures something true" — I want an order, and I'm fine with a clear last place.

(a) Relativism: cardinality is only ever relative to a model or a background theory. There's no absolute fact about whether a set is countable, so nothing is contradicted; "uncountable in M" and "countable, full stop" just aren't the same predicate.

(b) The "your numbers aren't our numbers" line: the countable model's naturals aren't the naturals, and its power set isn't the real power set, so it was never talking about our objects at all. The appearance of conflict comes from reading the model's words as though they meant ours.

(c) The "so much the worse for first-order logic" line: the paradox shows first-order logic can't pin down an intended interpretation, so the real lesson is that we need second-order logic with full semantics (or something in that neighborhood) before we can even say what we mean.

(d) Deflationism: it's just Löwenheim–Skolem with philosophical stage lighting. It tells you that first-order satisfiability is model-relative and nothing else; it only looks paradoxical to someone who thought a first-order theory could tie down its own subject matter.

(e) The power-set line (I think I made this one up — tell me if it's just (b) again): what's really doing the work is that "every subset" is never fully captured by a first-order description. So the paradox is a fact about our grip on the power set of an infinite set, not about logic, and it would survive a switch to second-order logic.

(f) The unrestricted-quantifier line (also mine): the paradox only bites if you think your own quantifiers range over absolutely everything. Drop that and "countable in mine, uncountable in yours" is just two domains, no tension.

(g) Scope-confusion / error theory: the whole thing rests on equivocating between "M is countable" (said from outside) and "M says it's uncountable" (said from inside), so there's no paradox, just a mix-up about who's doing the talking.

Two extra things. First, for (b): does it survive the case of a countable model whose naturals really are the naturals — say a countable transitive model, if there is one? There the "your numbers aren't ours" move seems to have nothing to grip, since the model's ω just is ω. If it doesn't survive that, say so, and tell me whether it can be patched by relocating the complaint to the power set, or whether that's really a different resolution in disguise. Second, at the end: if you had to bet on one, which one, and does your winner make trouble for the idea that set-theoretic truth is determinate?
