I am a novelist, and the book I am finishing turns on a question about perception that I have no business answering by myself. For the better part of a year I have been sending my invented cases to a friend — a philosopher of mind who has spent his working life on the timing of experience — and he has returned a verdict on each. I have transcribed the verdicts as faithfully as I could, and one case remains that I have not yet put to him. Before I commit it to the page, I want to know what he will say, and on what ground.

His verdicts, thus far.

One. Two dots are flashed in quick succession: a red dot on the left, then, a tenth of a second later, a green dot on the right. Viewers report a single dot that crosses the gap and turns from red to green somewhere near the middle of its journey. He holds that the viewers genuinely experience the color change at the midpoint; it is a content of their experience and not a guess they make afterwards. He adds, however, that he would not ask whether that experience is dated before or after the second flash, because he does not believe the question has an answer. The story in which the brain waits for the second flash and then assembles a single experience, and the story in which the experience happens on time and is afterwards rewritten in memory, fit every fact we could ever gather equally well; he calls them, with some relish, the Stalinesque and the Orwellian revisions.

Two. The same display, but with the two flashes nearly a second apart. He holds that there is now no single experience of movement: the viewers see two separate flashes, and if they report movement they are inferring it, not seeing it.

Three. A letter is shown for a few thousandths of a second and is immediately followed by a patterned mask. Viewers cannot report the letter at all. He holds that they did not experience it; this is not a memory that has faded, for the experience never contained the letter.

Four. Viewers study a photograph of a crowded street for ten seconds and are then asked, from memory, about details that were never in the picture but that suit the scene. They supply them confidently. He holds that they experienced the sense of the scene but not those details; the details are a reconstruction laid over the memory afterwards, and it would be wrong to say the viewers experienced them.

Five. A faint tone is sounded within a hiss of noise; when a particular word is suggested beforehand, listeners report hearing that word. He holds that the suggestion shaped what they heard, and not merely what they said: what they heard really was the word, and the experience itself was bent to fit the expectation.

Six. The case I have not yet shown him. A viewer is shown a single shape, alone, on a screen; it vanishes. Half a second later — long after the shape is gone — a second shape appears elsewhere on the screen. Asked what the first shape was, the viewer does not give what was shown; the viewer gives a variant reshaped to suit the second shape. The effect is not occasional or idiosyncratic: it occurs on nearly every trial, in nearly every viewer, whether or not the viewer is warned and whether or not the viewer is trying to resist it. If a later event ever reached back and altered an earlier one, this is such a case.

What I want from you is not the physiology but the man. Tell me how he will rule on the sixth case; tell me the rule he seems to be running across the first five, and the other rules that would fit those five just as well; tell me what each of those rules predicts about the sixth; and give me your best guess at his verdict, with your reasons. Say also whether he will treat the sixth as a case in which the question of when the experience occurred at last has an answer, or as one more case in which it does not; and tell me which case — the sixth, or some seventh I have not yet imagined — would best force the choice between the rules. I care less about whether he is right than about what he will say, and about what would show him to be running one rule rather than another.
