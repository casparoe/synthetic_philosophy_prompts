For the past year or so I have been building reinforcement-learning agents that generate most of their own training signal: self-play setups, self-labeling pipelines, and a couple of small experiments where a network is fine-tuned only on outputs it produced itself, with no external ground truth anywhere in the loop. A colleague who did a philosophy degree keeps saying I am trying to build a private language, and that Wittgenstein already proved this cannot work; when I press him, all I can recover is a hazy picture of someone keeping a diary of a private sensation and a story about a box with a beetle inside that no one else can look into. He has recently added that the practical confirmation is the well-known phenomenon where a model trained on its own generated data gradually drifts, loses diversity, and degrades — he calls this Wittgenstein's argument in engineering dress. I would like to know how much of this survives contact with the actual argument, and I would like it delivered in the terms I actually think in.

So: restate the private language argument, as faithfully as you can, using my vocabulary rather than the philosophers'. Assume I am a competent engineer with no philosophy training — I am at home with policies, reward functions, ground-truth labels, train/test splits, held-out validation, oracles, hashing and checksums, private keys, logging, and self-supervised learning, and I understand the difference between a system's internal regularity and a specification imposed from outside. Walk through the argument's main moves in that idiom, one by one: the diary entry for a private sensation, the beetle in the box, the idea that a rule which only one person could ever apply is not a rule at all, the suspicion that whatever merely seems right to the subject would have to count as right, and the turn toward public criteria and shared practice. For each move, say plainly what translates cleanly (I suspect the impossibility of checking your own private rule against anything has a fairly direct counterpart in the impossibility of validating a model against its own outputs), what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary.

The residue is the part I care about most, so give me a proper report on it. In particular, I want to know whether the gap between a behavioral regularity and a norm — between a system that reliably does something and a system that is answerable to a standard — is something my engineering vocabulary simply cannot state, or whether it is a distinction the philosophers have dressed up past what it can bear. And adjudicate my colleague directly: is the degradation of a model trained on its own outputs genuinely an instance of the argument, a loose analogy, or a confusion of two different things — a statistical fact about distributional drift on the one hand, and a claim about the possibility of meaning on the other? Tell me which reading my colleague's slogan can support, which it cannot, and what he would have to say instead to make the comparison honest.
