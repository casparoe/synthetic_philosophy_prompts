I recently read Douglas Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop, and I have been chewing on the toy world he builds there — the careenium — because a colleague keeps invoking it in an argument I am not sure I can evaluate, and I would like a careful audit of how much weight it can actually bear. As I understand the setup: picture a vast billiard table covered with an enormous number of tiny magnetic balls that careen around, colliding with one another and with the walls; the bounciness of the walls varies with conditions in the world outside, so information about that world leaks inward. When you zoom out, the balls are not behaving randomly — they repeatedly gather into stable, recognizable higher-level clumps that form, persist, and dissolve, and those clumps seem to track regularities in the outside world. Hofstadter calls the table the careenium, the little balls the simms, and the higher-level clumps the simmballs, and the whole point is that there appear to be two true stories about what is happening: at the level of the simms, every event is just one ball knocking into another; at the level of the simmballs, the clumps are interacting with one another in a way that mirrors the world. He then presses the question of which level is really doing the pushing.

My colleague uses this picture to argue that mental and symbolic-level explanations are just as genuinely causal as the underlying physical ones — that when I say a program crashed because a function returned null, or that a person acted because they believed something, I am not offering a second-best shorthand that the real physics underneath will eventually replace, but naming a real cause at a real level of description. I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and the same question nags at me whenever someone insists that a bug is really just transistors flipping, so I would like to know whether the careenium actually supports that conclusion or whether it quietly assumes it.

Please provide the following, in order. First, a table of correspondences: what in the careenium maps onto what in the intended target (brains, symbols, causes, levels of description), stated explicitly. Second, the places where the mapping breaks — where the careenium has a feature the target lacks, or lacks one the target has — and, separately, which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion my colleague wants and which are harmless. Third, the features the analogy quietly imports from the source picture, especially any assumptions about how the clumps get their content, whether the clumps do any work the balls could not, and whether the two levels are genuinely distinct or just two ways of bookkeeping the same events. Fourth, an assessment of whether the conclusion — that higher-level, symbolic descriptions are real causes in their own right — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. Fifth, if the careenium is a poor vehicle for that conclusion, describe a better analogy for the same point and say what it does that the careenium cannot. Please reason as if you cannot look anything up, and treat my summary of the setup as your source for the careenium itself.
