I'm a backend engineer, and I help run a small internal reading group at my company; last month we went through a coworker's write-up on the two-envelope problem, and I was asked to lead the discussion, which went poorly because I couldn't tell which parts of his summary were load-bearing. His summary runs roughly like this. You're shown two envelopes, one contains twice as much money as the other, you pick one, and before opening it you're offered the chance to swap. The standard argument for swapping goes: let A be the amount in your envelope; it's equally likely that A is the smaller amount or the larger one, so the other envelope holds either 2A or A/2 with equal probability, giving an expected value of one-half times 2A plus one-half times A/2, which equals 5A/4, and that is more than A — so you should always swap, and since the same reasoning then applies to the envelope you just acquired, you should keep swapping indefinitely. He then says the paradox is fully resolved once you notice that the symbol A is being used for two different quantities — the amount in the chosen envelope in the case where it is the smaller one, and the amount in the case where it is the larger one — and that once you keep the conditioning straight, the 5A/4 calculation collapses and the correct answer is that swapping makes no difference. He adds that this also settles what to do if you do open the envelope first: whatever amount you see, the other envelope is just as likely to hold half of it as double it, so opening changes nothing and you should remain indifferent. And he closes by saying the whole thing is a curiosity about sloppy notation rather than a genuine problem for decision theory, and that the only lesson is that you shouldn't apply expected values to situations where the money got there by some unspecified process — the process doesn't matter, the math does.

Some of that strikes me as right and some of it seems off, but I don't have the vocabulary to say which. Could you audit the summary claim by claim, in American English, the way someone who actually works on this would: which statements a careful person would accept as stated, which are defensible but contested simplifications being presented as settled, and which are simply wrong? I'm especially interested in three things. First, whether the ambiguity-of-A diagnosis is the resolution or rather one proposed resolution among several, and whether it even engages the version of the puzzle where you get to look inside your envelope before deciding. Second, whether there really is a version of the setup — something like: the contents of the first envelope are fixed, and then a coin flip decides whether the second envelope holds double or half of that — in which the naive-looking expected-value argument is actually correct and you should switch, and if so, what that does to his claim that the filling process is irrelevant. Third, whether his closing claim that the puzzle poses no challenge to expected-value reasoning or to decision theory survives the corrections.
