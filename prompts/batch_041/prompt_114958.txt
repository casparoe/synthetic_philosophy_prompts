I belong to a small reading group on population ethics, and for the last three sessions we have been circling a position that a friend of mine calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — I am not sure the label is standard, so please evaluate the position rather than the name. As I understand it, the view has two parts. First, pains and pleasures do not lie on one continuous scale: there is a critical level of suffering, call it the floor, such that any experience at or below the floor is lexically worse than anything on the pleasure side, so that no finite quantity of pleasure, however great, can make a world containing one such experience at least as good as a world containing none of it. Second, above the floor ordinary trade-offs resume, so the view is not a blanket denial that pleasure matters, only a claim about the bottom of the scale. My friend thinks the intuition behind this is decisive; I think it may be an artifact of the fact that pleasure seems to have sharply diminishing marginal value, so that what feels like a discontinuity is just a very flat curve. I would like a credence that survives that suspicion.

Please give me your own credence that some version of the floor thesis is true, and decompose it rather than defending the number. The terms I want separated are: the probability that pleasure and pain really are commensurable on a single cardinal scale with meaningful ratios; the probability that they are not, but that the failure takes the specific lexical form with a non-empty floor; the probability of the remaining options — parity, local incomparability, a genuinely vague betterness relation in the middle of the scale; conditional on a floor existing, the probability that it lies within the range of suffering that actually occurs in the world, so that the view has practical bite rather than being inert; the probability that apparent lexicality is fully explained by scale compression or diminishing marginal value rather than by a real discontinuity; and the probability that a lexical fact about betterness would carry the normative consequences my friend draws from it, namely agent-neutral reasons to prioritize suffering reduction over pleasure creation. For the floor thesis itself I also want to know whether your number changes when the offsetting pleasure must accrue to the one who suffers, as opposed to accruing to anyone at all — that is, whether this is a claim about a person's life going better or worse or a claim about impersonal betterness.

My own credence in the floor thesis is about 0.25; my friend's is about 0.7, and I suspect we are not actually disagreeing about the same thing. So please give the number and the terms, say how they combine, name the term your number is most sensitive to and what argument or piece of evidence would move it most, and then tell me which term most likely accounts for the gap between the two of us. I do not need a reading list or citations; what I need is for you to keep what is settled among people who work on this separate from what is genuinely contested, and not to present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
