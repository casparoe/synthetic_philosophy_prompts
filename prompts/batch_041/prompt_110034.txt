Write a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about the situationist critique of virtue ethics. Both should be first-rate: neither a strawman, neither allowed to win by caricature, and each arguing as though they mean it rather than hedging into neutrality.

The first, call her A, is a neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicist who accepts the empirical literature — the bystander-intervention studies, the hurry manipulations, the ambient-noise and mood effects, the obedience experiments — but denies that it refutes her position. She concedes that it destroys a crude globalism, on which a virtuous person is expected to behave well across every situation. Her constructive proposal is to reconceive a virtue on a model that has been influential in personality psychology, where the person-versus-situation dispute was partly defused by treating a trait not as cross-situationally consistent behavior but as a stable if-then pattern — a reliable, predictable way of responding to a psychologically salient kind of situation. So understood, a virtue can be real and robust without being situation-indifferent. She adds two defensive points: that the classic experiments place subjects in conditions that are not the conditions of virtuous action at all (anonymity, time pressure, no scope for deliberation or for the exercise of practical wisdom), and that a trait can be genuine yet easily blocked or masked by countervailing pressure, so a failure to manifest is not evidence of absence.

The second, call him B, is a situationist who takes all of this seriously and thinks it fails. He should argue, in his own voice and in whatever order makes for the best exchange, that the move to situation-indexed dispositions surrenders what made virtue ethics attractive — the integration of the virtues, the unity of the virtuous life, the thought that practical wisdom ranges across situations rather than being indexed to them; that the claim that the experiments do not test virtue under the right conditions threatens to make the view unfalsifiable, since some such escape is always available; that even if local traits are real, they may be too fine-grained and too unconnected to do the explanatory and normative work virtue ethicists want; and that A's revised position may by now be empirically indistinguishable from a sophisticated situationism, differing only in vocabulary.

Have them engage directly. B should ask A what would falsify her revised view and then test whether her answer genuinely exposes it; A should answer, and should also press B on whether his own position can account for the normative authority that virtue talk carries. Each should grant what seems right in the other's case and press where it does not, rather than talking past each other. Let the dialogue end without a forced reconciliation, but have each character state, near the end and in character, the single consideration that most tells in their favor and the one point they are prepared to concede.

Then add a short closing note in my voice rather than the characters'. Tell me which of the two you think has the better of the argument and why — a verdict, not a survey of the options — and flag any place where the dialogue leans on a contested reading of the empirical literature or takes a liberty with it.
