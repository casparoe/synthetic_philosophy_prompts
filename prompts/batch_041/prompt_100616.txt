For the better part of an evening I argued with a close friend about divine hiddenness, and I came away with a position I had not previously considered and cannot easily dismiss. Her view is that God's self-disclosure is not uniformly withheld but calibrated case by case: for each person, given their upbringing, temperament, culture, and intellectual capacities, the evidence is set so that the question of God's existence remains genuinely open for them -- never settled by the force of evidence, never foreclosed by its total absence. She calls this the calibration thesis and takes it to dissolve the problem of hiddenness rather than sharpen it. I am not persuaded, but I cannot see my way to a clean refutation, so I want to understand what accepting the thesis would cost.

Setting aside whether it is true, please set out the most important implications of the calibration thesis. I am especially interested in what it would mean for whether unbelief can be culpable, for the nature and value of faith, for what it is reasonable to call a loving God, for the epistemic standing of other religions and for the ethics of religious upbringing and evangelism, and for whether hiddenness survives as a problem at all. Where an implication follows only on further contested assumptions, mark it as conditional; where two of the implications pull against each other, say so plainly; and tell me which single implication does the most work for my friend's position and which does the most damage to it. A ranked list with reasons is more useful to me than an exhaustive one.
