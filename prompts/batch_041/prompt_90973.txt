I'm in a reading group on collective action and we keep hitting the same wall. My friend Ben has a line he's very confident about: he says the tragedy of the commons, once you strip the pastoral decoration off it, is just a prisoner's dilemma with more than two players. His picture is that each herder is a player with a binary choice -- add a cow or don't -- that the payoffs are symmetric and the game is one-shot, that adding is dominant for every player, and that the pasture gets wrecked as the sum of individually rational moves. He uses it to argue that commons problems have no internal solution: you can't get cooperation out of a dilemma without changing the payoffs from outside (a tax, a quota, auctioned permits) or handing enforcement to somebody above the players. And he's got a second move he's fond of -- he says if the game is repeated, the players are few and they can talk to each other, the whole thing dissolves, so the tragedy really only exists for large anonymous groups, which he takes to show that the community self-governance crowd is romanticising villages. Someone else in the group keeps insisting the real distinction is rivalry versus excludability and that Ben is sliding between a fishery (where my cow really does eat your grass) and the atmosphere (where it doesn't), and that this is doing more work than he admits.

I half buy it but something feels off and I can't tell whether the analogy is wrong or just incomplete. Can you do the audit? Give me the mapping first -- who is the prisoner, what's the sentence, what plays the role of the payoff matrix and the number of rounds -- then tell me where it breaks. In particular, does the "add a cow / don't add a cow" binary survive contact with the cases he actually cites (antibiotic prescribing, an aquifer, the atmosphere, take whichever you think is cleanest), or is defection not even dominant in a lot of them, and if it isn't dominant does the PD label still earn its keep? Which features does the PD picture quietly import that the old pasture story didn't have -- symmetry, one-shot-ness, a fixed player set, nobody able to change the rules -- and which of those imports are load-bearing for his conclusion? Take the second move seriously too: if repetition and cheap talk genuinely defuse it, is that a point about the analogy or a point against it, given that he's using the PD framing to argue the opposite?

And the part I care most about: does the conclusion survive if the analogy is only partly right? Suppose the real structure is closer to an assurance game or chicken, or the resource has a threshold past which it flips rather than degrading smoothly, or the players are wildly asymmetric so that the one with the most to lose has an incentive to just fix it himself. Does "external enforcement or bust" still follow, or was the PD framing carrying that conclusion the whole time? If you think it was, what's a better analogy for commons problems -- one that doesn't bake the answer in before the analysis starts. Keep it concrete: I'd rather have a correspondence table and a few worked cases than a survey of the literature.
