I retired a couple of years back from environmental geochemistry — thirty-odd years, most of it on contaminated land, which is probably why this case came to me so easily — and, somewhat to my own suprise, I've ended up doing an MA in philosophy. This term I'm TA-ing a final-year seminar on ethics and future generations, and the convener has asked each of us to draft one essay question for the final exam together with a model answer and a marking scheme, so she can see how we'd grade it. I've been landed with the nonidentity problem, and I'd like to try something better than the standard explain-the-problem-and-say-whether-it-sinks-person-affecting-views question, which most of the class can do from their notes without ever thinking.

Here's what I want the question built around. Picture a tailings pond above a farming valley — nothing exotic. Two things in the seepage matter to us. First, its a mild developmental neurotoxicant: children exposed in the womb come out with a handful of IQ points gone and a tremor that comes and goes. Small, real, not catastrophic — lives clearly worth living, but clearly worse than they would have been. Second, it's rough on sperm motility, and in the couples living downwind it pushes conception back by a few months; since which sperm meets which egg is a lottery, a few months is more than enough to make it a different child. So there are two cohorts. Cohort A: the children already conceived when the seepage started. Same individuals either way, mildly impaired — everyone agrees the company harmed them. Cohort B: the children conceived after the seepage started, to the same parents who would otherwise have had a child a few months earlier. Different gametes, different person. They're impaired in exactly the same way, by exactly the same mechanism, as cohort A — but they would never have existed at all had the company behaved. And the two cohorts are otherwise indistinguishable; nobody looking at a child could tell you which group they're in.

What I keep chewing on, and why I want this to be the question, is this. The company could have dumped the same stuff six months earlier, before any of the cohort-B couples had conceived, and those very same children would then have been cohort A and would have had a complaint. Same chemical, same impairment, same children, same lives — only the calendar moves. And there's a third arm: they could have built the pond somewhere else entirely, in which case none of the affected children would ever have existed, and by a strictly person-affecting standard it looks like no wrong was done at all, which strikes me as plainly false. So please write me: (1) the exam question itself, answerable in about 90 minutes by a good final-year student, and built so that they have to go through the A/B contrast and the timing point rather than recite; (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words that I could read out as the standard; and (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, the marks for each, and one line per point on how candidates typically miss it. I don't want any of it to rest on citing literature — my students can't bring books into the exam and half of them read everything on a phone — so the question should be answerable by someone who has the ideas but not the bibliography. And please be honest in the marking scheme about where a smart student could legitimately go a different way from the model answer; I don't want to punish someone who takes the impersonal line if they can defend it.
