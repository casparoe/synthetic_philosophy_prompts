I teach an upper-level seminar on intuitionism and constructive mathematics, and I'm assembling the take-home final. My students have absorbed the standard material well enough to recite the talking points — that intuitionistic logic drops excluded middle, that a proof of a disjunction must yield a proof of one of the disjuncts, that the BHK reading assigns proof conditions rather than truth conditions to the connectives — and I want a single essay question that this recitation cannot answer. A colleague who co-teaches the course insists the students can't be expected to say anything serious here without a reading assigned, and I'd like the question to prove her wrong. Please write that question, a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme.

The crux I want the question to turn on is whether the constructivist's rejection of classical reasoning should be read as the claim that classical mathematics is *unjustified* or as the stronger claim that the classical connectives are *meaningless*, and whether that reading can be sustained once students confront two things I want the question to force into the open. The first is that the BHK clauses are only an informal gloss on what a construction is: they do not by themselves select a unique logic, they can be regimented in ways that validate classical reasoning, and classical logic in fact embeds into intuitionistic logic through the standard negative translation, with a matching computational reading of classical proofs in terms of control. The second is that constructivists routinely reason classically in the metatheory — for instance, when they establish that a given sequent is not derivable, or prove a completeness result. I want students to say precisely what the constructivist is entitled to claim about excluded middle, and where the line between object theory and metatheory has to be drawn if the position is to remain coherent.

The marking scheme should consist of four or five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, each accompanied by a one-line note on the characteristic way students miss it — for example, sliding from "not constructively valid" to "false," or supposing that the negative translation vindicates the constructivist when it may only relocate the dispute. Keep the question answerable in roughly ninety minutes, and write it so that it leans on no particular author's name or citation, since I can't guarantee what they've read.
