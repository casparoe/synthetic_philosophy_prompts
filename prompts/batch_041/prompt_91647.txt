I'm a novelist, but I teach an upper-level seminar on historical fiction and national memory, and I'm setting the take-home final. I want a single essay question on lost causes and national myths, the usual suspects, the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk, that a student can't pass just by reciting what each one means. The thing I keep pushing in class is a distinction I made up and probly over-use: every lost-cause myth has a wound and an errand, the injury that was done and the task it hands down to whoever inherits it, and the myth only does its work while both are still live. I want the question to make them test that pairing rather than illustrate it, including on the case I think breaks it, Dunkirk, where a retreat got turned into an origin story, so the errand is either already finished or there was never a defeat to mourn. And I want them to have to take a side on whether these myths are even the kind of thing that's true or false, or whether that's the wrong question to put to them. If you can work it in, I'd love the question to leave room for them to build a made-up country, a nation that won the war and kept the defeat myth anyway, when that's cleaner than arguing about a real one. 90 minutes, one essay, around 600 words, no citations. Write me the question, a model answer about that length, and a marking scheme that names the moves an A answer has to make that a B answer won't, plus where the B's usually go wrong.
