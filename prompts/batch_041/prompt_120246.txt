For the past three months my employer has enrolled all of us in something it calls a wellbeing architecture programme, administered by an outside contractor, and I have spent that time watching small things about my working day get quietly rearranged: the fruit bowl now sits where the biscuits used to be, the pension form arrives pre-ticked, the stairs have been carpeted and the lift has somehow become slower, and the emails about my annual health check have been rewritten three times by someone whose job title nobody can explain to me. I registered this term for a seminar on paternalism and the ethics of choice architecture, partly because of all this, and I have been trying to decide whether the contractor is doing something I should object to or merely something I find irritating. Last week I got hold of their master terms of service, and it is the strangest document I have read all year: it is full of confident assertions about what I really want, about what my future self will thank them for, and about when my consent may be assumed, and because none of it is written as an argument, none of it can be answered.

I would like to bring it to the seminar, so write me a parody terms of service agreement — headline plus roughly 800 words of numbered clauses — for an invented contractor called Better Selves, LLC, which runs this kind of programme for employers, insurers and municipalities. Keep it deadpan: legal register, no winking, no narrator, nothing that reads as a joke to the company itself. The comedy has to come from taking the contractor's own doctrine seriously and writing out its consequences in the fine print. Here is the doctrine to build on, and you should remain internally consistent with it: the company holds that every client has two preference records, a Declared You (what you say you want when asked) and an Observed You (what your choices reveal when nobody is asking), and it treats the Declared You as authoritative only where the two agree, on the ground that a stated preference contradicting your own behaviour is precisely the condition the programme exists to correct. The clauses should follow from that, and from the ordinary machinery of the field: the distinction between a nudge and a shove; the distinction between a nudge and a boost, where a boost aims to make you more competent rather than to exploit the bias you already have; the friction that gets called sludge when it works against you and a safeguard when it works for you; the insistence that the whole apparatus be transparent and publicly defensible; and the question of who is liable when a nudge misfires and someone ends up worse off than if they had simply been left alone. The company should never admit to anything; it should be entirely sincere, and it should insist somewhere that it is not in the business of telling anyone what to want.

A reader who has thought about this literature should be able to laugh at every clause and should still come away with the real objections lodged in mind — that the standard of your own good is being applied by somebody else; that consenting to a default is not consenting to what the default does; that a nudge which bypasses your deliberation is manipulative in a way a transparency requirement does not cure; that a programme built to close the gap between what you say and what you do must eventually say whose reading of the gap prevails; and that an architect who is confident about your true preferences is making an epistemic claim that nobody has checked. Do not let any clause state these objections outright; let them surface only as consequences. Include at least one clause in which the opt-out is designed with precisely the same tools the contract describes, and one schedule or appendix that prices something that ought not to have a price. No named living people, no real companies, no citations.
