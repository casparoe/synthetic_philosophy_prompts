Everyone remembers Turing's child machine and the bit about teaching a machine with rewards and punishments, and then everyone reads that as behaviourism with extra steps. I want you to argue that this is the wrong reading. Turing's real point, I think, is about the starting state: he keeps insisting that the learner begin genuinely unorganized -- in the 1948 report he wires up networks of artificial neurons at random and then "educates" them by interfering with the connections, and in the 1950 paper the whole point of starting with a child rather than an adult is that you need something whose structure isn't already fixed -- and that the teacher's job is to shape an arbitrary substrate rather than to pour content into an empty but structured vessel. Rewards and punishments are then just the cheap interface for that shaping, not the theory of learning.

Argue for that reading, and then press it hard: if the unorganized starting state is doing the real work, what does that predict about when education can get off the ground at all, and does the reading survive the obvious objection that random wiring is just a sneaky way of installing a prior? Also say what it implies for the way we now talk about pretraining and fine-tuning, since I suspect the analogy cuts against us.
