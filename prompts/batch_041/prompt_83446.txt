Two of my colleagues have been feuding for about three years over how far you can get from objects to beliefs, and I've ended up chairing the session where they're supposed to produce something joint. Can you draft the document they could both actually sign?

Call them the thin camp and the thick camp. Thin: from material remains you can licence claims about behaviour, economy, ecology and maybe some very coarse social facts — settlement scale, a few proxies for hierarchy — but claims about beliefs, meanings, ritual content, cosmology, ancestors, aren't claims at all, they're glosses; the honest statement is only that people did something repeatedly and rule-governedly. Same for institutions: you can say a settlement had a big building, not that it had a council. Thick: with enough patterning, plus bridging principles motivated independently of the case in hand — taphonomy, experimental work, cross-cultural distributions, constraints on ritual form — you can support real, graded claims about what people believed and how they were organised, and refusing to make them isn't caution, its just refusing to explain.

What I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both of them can sign, including what the record can and can't do, and where each thinks the other's rhetoric overreaches; (2) the surviving disagreements, each phrased so the side it belongs to would accept the wording as fair — a table is fine; (3) the cruxes, the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody. I suspect one is whether a bridging principle can be warranted independently of the very cases it's used to interpret, and another is whether the underdetermination they keep invoking is specific to belief-claims or bites just as hard on the thin camp's own behavioural claims — if the latter, I want to know what that costs them; (4) for each crux, the case or test both of them would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance; and (5) if part of the fight is verbal, say which part and why.

Hang it on this. At a settlement I've been digging — late Bronze Age, occupied more or less continuously for four centuries — nearly every adult burial contains a small pot, and in about nine out of ten of them the pot has a hole punched through the base before it goes into the ground. The fabric is the same as the everyday cooking pots, but the forms are miniatures, and they turn up only in graves: never in houses, never in middens. There's also a pit under one house threshold with eleven of them in it, all holed. My gut says the claim to make is that the dead were given a vessel that had been killed, i.e. a belief claim, and that it's a good one. My colleague says I'm just projecting a modern idea of what a hole in a pot means.

No reading lists, no citations — we all know the literature, I just want the document. British English spelling please.
