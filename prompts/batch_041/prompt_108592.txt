My reading group has been grinding through ethics-of-war stuff for the last month and my friend Dana dropped an argument on us that I still can't shake. Her grandad was conscripted into an army that basically everyone now agrees was the aggressor, and I think thats part of why she cares, but the argument itself is pretty abstract. I've been turning it over alot. She wrote it on the whiteboard like this:

(1) You're permitted to kill an enemy combatant only if he's responsible for an unjust threat. No responsibility, no license.
(2) Someone is responsible for an act only if they had a fair shot at it -- they could have known what they were doing, and they could have done otherwise.
(3) The ordinary conscript on the aggressor's side has no fair shot. He can't verify his government's story (the state controls the news, dissent means prison, he's been marinated in it since he was six), and he can't refuse (desertion is a firing squad, and his refusing wouldn't move the invasion an inch). Same for the major who just relays orders he didn't write, and arguably same for the voters and taxpayers back home who were told the other side was about to attack them.
(4) So none of them is responsible for the threat they're part of.
(5) So none of them is a legitimate target, and dropping the conscript is morally the same as dropping his little sister.
(6) But the same three steps run on the defenders too, since they're also conscripts who can't check anything and can't walk away.
(7) So nobody in a war is a legitimate target, and a country may not use lethal force even to stop a genocide. Which is just wrong.

Dana says the argument is valid, so the only move left is to give up (1) -- liability to be killed doesn't require responsibility, it only requires that you're the one actually doing the threatening. But that seems to commit her to saying you may shoot a nine-year-old who's been handed a rifle and shoved out the door, and she doesn't much like that either.

So tell me: which step do I actually reject, why did it look so obviously fine, and what does giving it up cost me? I mostly want to keep the conclusion that stopping a genocide with force is allowed, and I'd rather not throw out the whole idea that people aren't to blame for things they genuinely couldn't have known or avoided -- those feel like the two ends I'm not willing to cut off. Not looking for a reading list or citations, I just want to know where the seam is and what's on the other side of it.
