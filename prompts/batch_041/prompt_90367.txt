I teach the philosophy of biology unit in a mid-level undergrad course, and the session on biological function is the one where everyone nods along and then, two weeks latter, writes that the function of a trait is just whatever it happens to do. I've got 18 students and 80 minutes. Take the role of an experienced instructor in philosophy of biology and think this through carefully before you write anything: brainstorm the case material first, then the structure.

Design me a runnable classroom activity — roles, materials, timeline, debrief — that makes them feel the pull of both the etiological (selected-effects) account and the causal-role account by forcing them to use each one, instead of me explaining the difference up front. Here's the shape I'm after. Early on, pairs sort case cards into has-a-function / doesn't / can't tell, each card carrying a short history paragraph and a short what-it-does-now paragraph. Most cases should land cleanly so the room builds confidence; two or three should split them. Then split the room in two, hand each team one doctrine (don't name the theorists yet), and have them re-sort the swing cases. Build the cards so each team has to swallow something: the etiological team ends up granting a function to a shriveled remnant that does nothing now (and ideally discovers that their own doctrine splits over whether the relevant history has to be ongoing), while the causal-role team ends up handing out functions to byproducts and to contributions nobody could care about. Then two absurdity probes — does the eye have the function of being pokeable? does the heart's murmur have a function? — and let them squirm before you debrief.

Include the materials and setup, the case types you'd build (a vestigial remnant; a recent useful byproduct of selection on something else; a trait doing two jobs at once), a minute-by-minute timeline, alot of detail on what predictably goes wrong the first time and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.
