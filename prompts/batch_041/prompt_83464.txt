Next term I am presenting a paper at a small joint workshop, and I need to be ready for the discussion. The room is split roughly in half. One half is a behavioural economics group that works on intertemporal choice and treats present bias as a modelling primitive; they are allergic to folk-psychological vocabulary and will want to know what my claims predict that a one- or two-parameter discounting model does not. The other half is an ancient-philosophy reading group that has spent the year on Aristotle's treatment of akrasia and enkrateia; they care about the structure of the practical syllogism, about the difference between the akratic and the merely tempted, and about whether the akratic agent really holds the relevant judgment at the time of action or only "has" it in some weaker sense. I am a philosopher, not an economist, and I expect to be the only person in the room defending the thesis I am about to describe.

The paper defends a split that I expect to be unwelcome on both sides. I distinguish thin akrasia — acting against the all-things-considered judgment one holds at the moment of action — from thick akrasia, in which the agent acts against a standing plan that she continues to endorse before, during, and after the act, and endorses even as she departs from it. My claim is that the discounting apparatus captures thin akrasia rather well and is simply silent about thick akrasia, because the preference reversal it models is already complete before the action begins, so nothing in the model corresponds to acting against a plan one still endorses; and, conversely, that the philosophical tradition has been describing thick akrasia the whole time, so the two literatures have been talking past each other because neither side has noticed that it is describing a different phenomenon from the other. I am willing to concede that the boundary between the two kinds is vague, but not that it is arbitrary, and I would rather be told now where that concession costs me than discover it from the floor.

Please prepare me a Q&A sheet. I want the objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are, each phrased the way a member of that half of the room would phrase it — the economists' version of an objection and the classicists' version are usually not the same objection, and where they differ I want both. For each, give me a short spoken reply (two minutes at most), a note on which part of the reply is soft, and a judgement about what I should concede outright rather than defend. I am especially interested in the objections that go to whether my two kinds are genuinely distinct rather than a relabelling of a familiar distinction the tradition already draws, and to whether the split does any explanatory work that a unified account could not do. I do not need a reading list or citations; I need the questions and what to say back, including the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
