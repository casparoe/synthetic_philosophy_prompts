You are marking the short-answer section of the final examination in a second-year undergraduate course on rational choice and collective action. Each answer is worth twelve points, four for each of the three parts, and is assessed both for accuracy and for responsiveness to what the question actually asks. Two conventions from the course bear on the question below, and students were expected to observe them.

The first concerns terminology. The course reserves the expression "the paradox of voting" for the turnout puzzle — the question of why an instrumentally rational agent votes at all when her single vote is very unlikely to be decisive. The separate problem of intransitive majority preference (the cycling problem), together with the impossibility results on aggregating individual preferences into a collective ranking, is treated as a distinct topic, and the two are not to be run together.

The second concerns how a proposed resolution is to be assessed. The course applies a model-invariance requirement: a proposal counts as a resolution of the paradox only if it retains both the model's rationality assumptions and its payoff structure, and shows that voting maximizes expected utility as originally specified. A proposal that instead introduces new terms into the utility function, or that substitutes a different standard of rationality, is not a resolution of the paradox but a change of subject, and it is to be identified as such rather than presented as a solution.

The examination question read as follows.

(a) State the paradox of voting precisely, and explain why it is a puzzle rather than a contradiction. (b) Explain the expressive, or duty-and-solidarity, response to the paradox, and say precisely what it does and does not establish. (c) Does the paradox of voting give us any reason to doubt the rationality of democratic decision-making? Justify your answer.

The student's answer is reproduced in full below.

(a) The paradox of voting is the tension between what the standard expected-utility model predicts about turnout and what we in fact observe. The model treats the decision to vote as a comparison between the cost of voting — the time, the effort, and any wages forgone — and the expected benefit, which is the value the voter assigns to her preferred outcome multiplied by the probability that her vote is decisive. In a large electorate that probability is tiny. In the simple version of the model in which each voter independently backs one side with probability one half, the probability that a single vote breaks a tie is of order one over the square root of the electorate size, so in a national election it is minuscule; the expected benefit therefore falls below the cost, and the model predicts that the voter abstains. What makes this a puzzle rather than a contradiction is that the prediction follows from a substantive model rather than from logic alone: the assumptions might be false, the inference might be challenged, or we might conclude that most voters are not instrumentally rational. Nothing in the model forbids the data; the model merely fails to predict it.

(b) The expressive response denies that the only payoff a voter receives is the instrumental one. On this view, voting also carries a private satisfaction — the affirmation of a preference, the sense of standing with others, the discharge of a felt duty — and this satisfaction accrues to the voter whether or not her vote is decisive. Writing the calculus of voting as R = PB − C + D, where D is this duty-and-solidarity payoff, the account observes that D can be large enough to make the total positive, so that voting is rational after all. It is worth stressing that this does not require us to abandon the model: expected-utility maximization is left exactly where it was, and we have simply been more careful about what counts as a payoff, counting the whole of what the voter receives rather than truncating the description at the payoffs that operate through the outcome. Once the payoffs are described accurately, the paradox dissolves.

(c) Yes, and the reason lies in what the paradox exposes about the structure of majority rule. If a single vote is instrumentally pointless, then the normative force of democratic participation cannot rest on its consequences, and what is left is at best a symbolic gesture. This is the same lesson we draw from the other impossibility results in social choice: just as no aggregation rule can satisfy a reasonable set of fairness conditions, no individual act of voting can be justified by its expected effect, and in both cases the conclusion is that the procedure rests on a fiction. Democratic decision-making may still be defended on expressive or civic grounds, but it cannot be defended as a rational procedure for tracking the public good.
