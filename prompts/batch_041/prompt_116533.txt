I teach early modern philosophy now, but before I drifted into it I did a master's in operations research, so I still reach for the optimizer vocabulary when I'm thinking at my desk -- objective functions, feasible sets, search landscapes, constraints, global vs. local optima. I'm putting together a lecture on Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds, and I keep wanting to restate the whole thing in that vocabulary and then noticing that something gets lost every time I do. So what I want from you is the translation plus the residue report. Take the position apart piece by piece -- God as the chooser, the totality of possible worlds as the space he's choosing from, the constraint that things have to be able to co-exist in a single world (his compossibility), the principle of sufficient reason as the demand that the choice be non-arbitrary, the distinction between absolute necessity and the merely hypothetical necessity that binds God's choice once he's committed to acting for the best, the idea that the world's perfection belongs to the whole rather than being summed from parts, and the awkward bit where a world containing sin and suffering might still be the best one (the felix culpa move) -- and render each of these as faithfully as you can in the language of optimization and decision-making under a given objective.

Then tell me what didn't survive the trip. I want to know which of these translate cleanly, which only go through if I distort them, and which have no counterpart at all -- and for each piece of residue, whether that's a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that Leibniz was papering over something. My suspicion, which I'd like you to either back up or knock down, is that two things are doing suspicious work in the translation: the assumption that the feasible set is given in advance (in Leibniz it looks like the possibles are just God's ideas, so the space may be endogenous to the choice), and the assumption that there's a single scalar "perfection" that can be maximized (the more I read him, the less sure I am that his comparisons are additive or even transitive). And don't cheat by smuggling the optimizer's assumptions in and calling that the translation -- I'd rather you flag the places where you had to fudge. Finally, I have a hunch that once you write "best of all possible worlds" as an argmax over a loss function, it stops sounding like a claim about goodness at all and starts sounding like a tautology about whatever the objective happens to be; tell me whether that's a real feature of the view or an artifact of my framing.
