I sit on my district's instructional-materials adoption committee, and until this spring I would have described the job as clerical: check that the textbooks cover the standards, fit the budget, and will survive a few years of student use. My daughter starts eighth grade in the fall, which is how I ended up paying attention at all — and then the committee received two competing eighth-grade U.S. history submissions, and the public comment sessions turned into exactly the kind of fight I had previously only read about. I have to circulate my written recommendation before Thursday's vote, and I would like your help drafting it.

Here is the situation as neutrally as I can state it. Submission A is a conventional survey. It presents the Revolution and the founding as the period's central achievement, treats slavery as a profound contradiction the founders failed to resolve, and gives the Civil War and Reconstruction their own chapters. Submission B organizes the same period around the claim that slavery and its afterlife are constitutive of American development rather than a contradiction within it; it opens in 1619 rather than 1776, and it reads the founding documents as in part a defense of a slaveholding order. Both cover the required standards. On every particular I can check — dates, documents, casualty figures, sequence of events — both are accurate. The disagreement between them is not about the facts. It is about what the facts are facts about: whether the Revolution or the slave economy is the organizing center of the story, and therefore which events are load-bearing and which are mere context.

Our committee has three factions, and I find all three unsatisfying. One wants A because it is the traditional narrative and B is advocacy. One wants B because A sanitizes the past, and insists the traditional narrative is itself an advocacy position. The third, which I believe is the largest and which worries me most, wants to adopt both and require teachers to present both sides so that students can make up their own minds. I must recommend a concrete decision — A, B, neither, or one of them with conditions — and I want the recommendation to rest on something sturdier than a taste for the middle.

Two things I want the memo to do beyond the procedural. First, take seriously the idea that a narrative can be accurate in every verifiable particular and still mislead, and say what misleading could mean here without smuggling in a political preference. Second, be honest about the present-both-sides option: whether framing the choice as a debate between two narratives is a genuine epistemological or pedagogical improvement, or whether it quietly concedes that there is no fact of the matter about which narrative is better — and if there is a fact of the matter, what kind of fact it is.

One further complication, because it bears on the vote. Our district has a large Korean-American population, and the same adoption cycle covers a world-history volume treating the Japanese colonial period in Korea and the wartime record in East Asia. Objections from Korean-American parents to that volume have produced a second controversy, and the committee keeps trying to treat the two fights as unrelated. I suspect they are one fight — a fight about who gets to author the story of a period — and I would like the memo to say whether the comparison illuminates the U.S. case or merely inflames it. Do not let the comparison do the argument's work for it.

Draft the opinion I will circulate. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — which submission, and with what conditions if any — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of what I have told you.
