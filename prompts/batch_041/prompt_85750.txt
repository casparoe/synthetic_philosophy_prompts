Before I read any more about Moore's paradox, I want the specification any account of it would have to meet. Assume only this much about the phenomenon: a speaker who says "It's raining, but I don't believe it" is doing something defective, and the defect is not that she has contradicted herself — the sentence can be true, and its second half is about her own mind. I want the desiderata: the cases the account must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the outcomes it must not predict, and the neighbouring phenomena it must not misclassify, each attached to the case or consideration that motivates it. Say which requirements are genuine and which are merely house rules of one camp, and whether some subset of them cannot be satisfied together; if so, the list itself is the result and I want that stated plainly. The purpose is diagnostic rather than semantic: I want to be able to say of any candidate sentence whether it is Moore-paradoxical and why, without first settling the semantics of belief ascriptions.

The cases the list has to cover, at a minimum:

(a) "It's raining, but I don't believe it."
(b) "It's raining, but I believe it isn't."
(c) "It's raining, but she doesn't believe it."
(d) "It was raining, but I didn't believe it."
(e) "It's raining, but we don't believe it," said by one of us.
(f) The sentence as it appears in the mouths of two speakers, the second finishing the first's sentence; the sentence quoted by a third party; the sentence embedded under "suppose" or in the antecedent of a conditional.
(g) The speaker who takes herself to be reporting her own denial: she utters the whole sentence, meaning the second half as a description of a state she is in and the first half as something she has it on good authority but cannot bring herself to believe. I want a verdict on whether this is the same phenomenon or an exception a theory has to earn.
(h) The belief form rather than the utterance form: whether the conjunction can be coherently believed by the person it concerns, and if not, what the impossibility of believing it adds to the impossibility of asserting it.
(i) "It's raining, but I don't know that it's raining," which I am not sure belongs in the class at all; I want the criterion that decides, not just a verdict.

Three grades of badness need keeping apart, since I suspect candidates run them together: cannot be true; can be true but cannot be rationally believed by the person it is about; can be rationally believed but cannot be asserted by her. Place each case, and say whether any case forces the grades apart.

A colleague offers the following diagnosis, and I want the list to serve as its test. What is wrong with the sentence is that its first half can only be asserted by someone in a certain state — believing what she asserts — while the second half denies that she is in it: a single act undercutting its own precondition. She takes this to be a general pattern, not a fact about the first person, and offers as an instance "I promise to be there, but I have no intention of being there." A rival diagnosis holds that the absurdity is an artefact of what "I believe" does in the first person, so that the second half is not even a candidate for truth or falsity in the speaker's mouth. Tell me which of your requirements separate these two, which one they both satisfy, and how each fares, briefly.

Open with your verdict on whether the full list is jointly satisfiable and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates; then give the list and the rest, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
