I host a small podcast about consciousness and AI stuff — mostly it's me getting researchers to explain their work in plain terms, since my background is software and Ive never taken a philosophy class beyond one ethics elective. Next month's guest is a philosopher defending what she calls the compression view of phenomenal consciousness. The gist, as best I can tell from her papers and two talks I watched: introspection isn't a readout of what's happening inside, it's a lossy predictive process that builds a compressed model of the brain's own states, and the compression throws off structure — artifacts — that get read back as an intrinsic, private, ineffable feel. So the felt quality of experience is more like a rendering artifact than a window onto some special inner property, and she thinks that dissolves the hard problem instead of solving it.

I want 9 to 11 questions that actually put pressure on this, not softballs. Please cover at least:

1. Self-defeat. If introspection is sloppy enough to generate a total illusion about our own minds, why should I trust it when it tells her the compression view is true? And what stops this from being illusions about illusions all the way down?
2. The meta-problem. Why would evolution, or learning, build a self-model that's systematically wrong about something so central, when being roughly right about your own states seems useful? Whats the selection pressure for the artifact?
3. Whether this is just eliminativism with extra steps. If nothing has phenomenal properties, what is the illusion a misrepresentation of, and does she need there to be a fact of the matter that the brain gets wrong?
4. Falsifiability. Is there any report or result that would count against the view, or does every vivid report just become more data for it? Push on aphantasia and blindsight here — do those come out as thinner illusions, or as cases where the compression fails, and does the view predict which?

Also include a couple questions exploiting a distinction I think she slides over: is the illusion a false belief, a perceptual-level effect, or just an artifact of how language forces us to describe experience? Those are very different theories and I want to pin her down on which one she's defending, and what breaks in the other two.

And one on the red-vs-trumpet thing: if there are no phenomenal properties, what grounds the difference between seeing red and hearing a trumpet? When she says it's a representational difference, I want a follow-up asking whether that just smuggles the feel back in under a new name.

For each question, give me a one line note on the answer she's most likely to give, and a follow-up that won't let her just restate the compression line. No what-got-you-into-philosophy stuff, and please no reading list or citations — I won't have time, and I can't look anything up while we're recording.
