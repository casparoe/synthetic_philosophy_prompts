I'm working up a short paper on consent, and before I inflict it on anyone I want you to go after the argument. Here's the gist of it, roughly as it stands:

The standard list of conditions on valid consent — competence, information, voluntariness — is really a list of conditions on two different things we've been calling by one name, and most of the puzzles in this area come from running them together.

Call the first thing *authorization*: you lift a boundary and make it permissible for someone to do a thing to you. Call the second *risk-assumption*: you take on the possibility that the thing goes badly. Authorization is about the act's nature and scope; risk-assumption is about its consequences. The conditions attach differently. Competence to authorize is competence to grasp what act is being performed; competence to assume a risk is competence to grasp stakes and probabilities, which is a different and often lower bar. Deception about what act is being done kills authorization and leaves risk-assumption untouched — told it's a biopsy when it's an experimental implant, you didn't authorize it, and the fact that you'd have accepted the odds is irrelevant. Deception about consequences leaves authorization intact and kills risk-assumption: you authorized the operation, the surgeon committed no trespass, but he has no claim on your having accepted the odds. Coercion cuts the same way. A threat attached to the very act ("sign or I'll shoot") means the signature isn't yours, so no authorization. A threat elsewhere in your life ("take the job or lose the house") leaves the contract authorized but poisons your acceptance of its risks.

Two things fall out that I like. First, you can say both "she consented" and "she was wronged" without contradiction: she authorized the act, and the actor still had no valid claim on her acceptance of the risk, so he's on the hook for the harm but not for battery. Second, cases that look like one hard problem split cleanly and seperately. Someone lies about having had a vasectomy — on my view that's a lie about consequences, so the sex is authorized and the risk-acceptance isn't; it's a wrong but not a rape. Someone secretly takes the condom off mid-act — that changes what act is being done, so authorization fails and it is. Likewise a study that lies about its hypothesis (the participant still authorizes the pill-taking, and it's the risk-acceptance that's defective) versus one that lies about what's in the pill.

Comments, please. Four things specifically: (1) is the authorization/risk-assumption split a real distinction or just the act/omission or scope/risk line with new labels; (2) are my verdicts on those cases right, especially the vasectomy one, since if the split can't handle it the whole thing is dead; (3) what's the strongest objection to the claim that someone can validly authorize an act while invalidly assuming its risk; (4) anywhere I've overstated how settled the standard view actually is. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate what I've asked.
