I've been going down a rabbit hole on this "Moloch" thing people talk about online -- the idea that you can end up with a horrible collective outcome even though nobody involved is a villain, because everyone is just doing the locally sensible thing. Arms races, a fishery getting fished to nothing, every store in a mall staying open later than the last one, that kind of pattern. I'm not a student, I just read a lot of blogs and argue with my friends about it, and the argument always dies the same death: somebody says "well that's just a prisoners dilemma," and then everyone nods, because we all know how you're supposed to solve a PD -- you cooperate, or you set up tit-for-tat, or you get some third party to enforce the deal, problem solved. But that feels like it's smuggling in a ton of stuff the label can't actually pay for, and I want to know if I'm right about that.

So what I'm after is a real audit of the analogy, not just a vibe. Give me the correspondence table first -- in the PD story, who exactly are the players, what counts as defecting, what are the payoffs, is it one-shot or repeated, and where do those things actually land when we're talking about a multipolar trap or a race to the bottom. Then the places it breaks, and which of those breaks actually matter for the work people are using the analogy to do. Here's what keeps nagging at me. In a PD there's a specific cell -- mutual cooperation -- that both players would genuinely rather be in, so the whole problem is commitment. But in a lot of these races it feels like that cell is just empty: there's no version of the outcome everybody would prefer that's actually on the table, and "just cooperate" translates to "unilaterally disarm and get eaten alive." Second, there aren't two players, there are hundreds or thousands, and nobody's picking between two buttons -- they're dialing a continuous level of effort up and down, and the prize keeps changing value as the race goes on, so the payoffs aren't even fixed. Third, and this is the one that bugs me most, half the time the "defection" isn't experienced as a choice at all -- it's just doing your job, meeting the quota, staying competitive. Nobody in an arms race wakes up feeling like a traitor.

So: how far does the PD / tragedy-of-the-commons picture really hold, what does it quietly import (my suspicion is that it imports the assumption that the trap is solvable by the players themselves if they'd only get their act together), and does the conclusion people draw from it -- that we just need cooperation, or a treaty, or a referee -- survive once the disanologies are on the table? If it doesn't survive, what's a better analogy for the thing, or is the whole "it's like X" move the actual problem here? And I'd especially like this turned on the move I hear the most, which is "well if the game were iterated, tit-for-tat would fix it" -- does that work for these traps or is it wishful thinking?
