A friend in my reading group has been marinating in rationalist-blog epistemology and handed me the following. He says it's a reductio of pragmatic encroachment; I suspect it's a reductio of the intuition that motivates it, and I can't tell which. Here it is:

(1) Knowledge is the epistemic standard a belief must meet if it is to be proper to rely on that belief in practical deliberation.
(2) Whether it is proper to rely on a belief depends on what is at stake if the belief is wrong: the more at stake, the stronger the evidence must be.
(3) Stakes are set by circumstances, not by the believer, and can be raised without limit.
(4) So for any true belief there is some circumstance in which relying on it is improper.
(5) By (1), in that circumstance the belief falls short of knowledge.
(6) So for any true belief there is a circumstance in which the believer does not know — even though the believer's evidence and reliability are identical across those circumstances.
(7) If knowledge can be stripped from any true belief by a third party raising the stakes — a bettor, an auditor, an audience — then "S knows that p" never reports a fact about S; it reports only how much is riding on p for whoever happens to care.
(8) A term that never reports a fact about its apparent subject is empty. So knowledge is empty, and pragmatic encroachment, which implies this, is false.

I find (2) about as obvious as anything in epistemology, and I can't bring myself to accept (8). Tell me which step to give up, and be specific about the kind of move it is: is it an equivocation, a scope or quantifier slide, a step that's valid only under a reading the premises don't supply, or a premise that's genuinely optional? Then explain why that step looked innocent — what makes it tempting — and price the alternatives: what does a pragmatic encroacher have to give up to block the argument, what does a contextualist about "knows" have to give up, and what does someone who simply accepts the conclusion owe us by way of an account of what we were tracking all along when we thought knowledge was a state of the believer? If the argument is valid and one premise must go, say which one is cheapest to abandon and what it costs to abandon it.
