Ok so I need help with something thats turned into a bit of a nightmare. I'm the outgoing chair of our state's chemicals and public health agency — nineteen years there, the last five as chair — and next month is the agency's thirtieth anniversary dinner, and the director has asked me to give the after-dinner speech. Ten minutes, tops. The room is going to be a minefield. Up front: the original staff, a few of the toxicologists who wrote our first screening rules, two of the activists who spent the eighties lobbying to get us created, and the retired judge who chaired our board for a decade. Our founding statute writes the precautionary principle into law, and everyone in that front row has a different idea of what that means, so they will notice the second I describe the principle we're built on as anything other than what it is. In the back: the industry folks we regulate (several of whom now quietly fund our education programs, which is it's own awkwardness), a couple of state legislators, and my husband's family, who are lovely and think I work in 'environmental something.' There's alot of history in that room and I want it to land for both halves.

Here's the thing that makes it hard. The board just voted through a change to how we do business, and I have to announce it in the speech. The way it works now: when we restrict a chemical because the hazard is plausible but unproven, the restriction carries a mandatory review date, and the duty to bring it back is on us, not on the company — if we can't come back to the board within the window with something better than 'still no evidence of harm,' it lapses automatically and the company can ask for the record to be cleared. The founders are going to hear that as us handing the burden of proof back to industry, which, honestly, is sort of what it is. But our science director keeps saying the new rule only works if we can say what 'the evidence came in clean' would even look like, and I'm not sure we can say that, and neither is she. The activists in the room will have a version of that objection ready before dessert, and the industry people will be nodding along for entirely different reasons.

So: I don't want to pretend the new rule is just the old principle with better paperwork if it isn't, and I don't want to spend ten minutes relitigating the vote either. I'd rather the speech be honest and a little awkward than smooth and false, and I'd rather it be genuinely moving about the people who built the place than clever. Can you write it? And tell me which one sentence you think is carrying all the weight, so I know where to slow down.
