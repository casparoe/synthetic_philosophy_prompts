I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and I keep having to turn a number into prose: a 1-in-100-year flood, a 30 percent chance of a pandemic in the next decade, a risk model's 0.7 score for a defendant reoffending, a physicist's claim that a given nucleus will decay within the hour. My trouble is not arithmetic but interpretation. The same numeral can be a long-run frequency in some reference class, a degree of belief held by a particular forecaster, or an objective single-case propensity, and my column has to commit to one of them. Please give me a decision procedure I can actually run in the ten minutes before I file: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a plain print, where it must print the number with its interpretation attached, and where it ends by refusing the number altogether. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Build in two things. First, the reference class problem: whenever the number is a frequency, the procedure must force me to name the class, and it must say what I do when two defensible classes give different frequencies. Second, the singular cases, where no frequency exists at all: there the procedure must keep a forecaster's credence from being passed off as an objective chance, and it must say what a credence claim still licenses me to write.

Then run the procedure on the flood, the risk score, and the pandemic forecast, including at least one where it tells me not to print the number the way the source wants. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do — the cases it resolves only because I decide, and how I should flag that in the column instead of letting the number settle it. American English, please.
