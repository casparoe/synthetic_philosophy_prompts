John Stuart Mill spent his working life in the East India Company's London office, rising to head of the Examiners' Office at India House and leaving in 1858 when the government of India passed to the Crown. In On Liberty he wrote that we may leave out of consideration those backward states of society in which the race itself may be considered as in its nonage, and that "Despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement, and the means justified by actually effecting that end." In the chapter "Of the Government of Dependencies by a Free State" in Considerations on Representative Government (1861) he worked out how a free state should govern subject peoples. He died in 1873.

Suppose Mill were revived, given no new reading, and asked to comment on four developments he did not live to see:

1. The rise of organized Indian nationalism, culminating in independence and partition in 1947 — self-government demanded and won by the very people he had treated as wards.
2. The interwar mandate system and the post-1945 UN trusteeship system, in which rule over peoples judged not yet fit for self-government was made answerable to an international body rather than to a single imperial state; and the later UN declaration treating colonial rule as a denial of fundamental human rights.
3. A modern controversy over whether British rule in India was on balance beneficial — railways, famine administration, legal codification and English education set against extraction, deindustrialization and mass famine — in which the pro-empire side argues that the balance of consequences decides the question and the anti-empire side argues that consent, not consequences, decides it.
4. The charge, pressed by present-day critics, that his imperial exception is not a lapse but the logical consequence of his own criterion of improvement: that any doctrine which measures a people's fitness for self-government licenses rule over those judged unfit, and that this is why liberalism and empire were companions rather than opposites.

Reconstruct Mill's probable responses. Take the four items in turn, giving for each his most likely verdict and the strongest argument he could make for it out of his own principles — the greatest-happiness standard, the liberty principle, and the criterion of capacity as the trigger for liberty's application — and say which of the three carries the weight and where they pull apart. Then (a) name the single development that constitutes the strongest evidence against his earlier position and say whether he would concede, and on what terms; (b) identify at least one issue on which two consistent Mills could reach opposite verdicts, and explain what his framework leaves genuinely open; and (c) flag any point at which the reconstruction requires him to have acquired a concept — a right of national self-determination, or the moral equality of cultures — that he plainly did not have, and say whether his own premises would have led him to it or merely forced it on him. Close with a brief paragraph in his own voice summarizing where he ends up.

Argue from the positions themselves. Do not supply citations or a reading list, and do not put any further words in his mouth beyond the two passages quoted above.
