I'm putting together materials for a philosophy club at my high school, and we keep getting stuck on the same problem: whether the numbers should count when you can save either one person or several. One of us always gives the "each person's claim is equal, so a coin toss is the only fair way to choose" speech, and someone else gives the "five deaths are plainly worse than one" speech, and then we just repeat ourselves. I want to see the disagreement actually worked out rather than restated. So please write a dialogue between two people who both understand the issue deeply but have landed on opposite sides: one holds that saving the one rather than the five (or deciding by a random procedure) is defensible, because each person's life has an equal and non-addable claim on us, and the other holds that the numbers of lives at stake genuinely make one outcome worse and so settle what we ought to do. They should be equals in competence — no strawman, no cheap rhetorical wins — and they should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another, conceding where a point lands and pressing where it doesn't.

To keep it from becoming the same tired exchange, build it around a concrete situation the two of them are arguing over: a hospital has a limited supply of a drug, and the choice is between giving it to one patient whose survival is certain, or to five patients each of whom has only a partial chance of surviving without it, so that the expected number of lives saved comes out roughly equal either way. I'd like the dialogue to work through what each position says about this case specifically — whether the "numbers" argument still has force when the lives at stake are only probable rather than certain, whether a coin toss or some other procedure is still the right way to decide, and what exactly each side thinks is doing the moral work (the separateness of persons, the badness of the outcome, fairness in the procedure, or something else). Let them range a bit into the related cases if it helps, but keep this drug case as the spine of the conversation. Neither character should cite books, articles, or dates; I don't want a reading list, just the arguments put in their own words.

One more thing: throughout, please keep track of which claims are settled or uncontroversial among people who study this topic and which are genuinely contested, and make sure neither character presents a contested view as though it were the agreed-upon consensus. If there's a point on which the two sides are really just talking past each other, I'd rather see that diagnosed than papered over.
