I am the newly elected chair of my department's governance committee, and for the past several weeks I have been trying to design a voting procedure for allocating our limited pool of graduate teaching assignments. The committee has five members, and there are five possible allocation plans. I initially proposed a ranked-ballot method with sequential elimination, but a colleague observed that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem appears to show that any non-dictatorial procedure for choosing among three or more alternatives—one that is strategy-proof, onto, and defined over unrestricted preferences—will be vulnerable to strategic misrepresentation. I understand that the theorem's assumptions can be relaxed in various ways: by restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences, by admitting cardinal utilities and transfers, or by accepting randomized outcomes. Yet each relaxation seems to purchase strategy-proofness at the cost of something I value, such as anonymity, neutrality, or the ability to express intensity of preference. I am not a mathematician, but I need to present a recommendation at next week's meeting.

Write an essay on the following controversy: Is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem a decisive objection to designing a genuinely democratic procedure for a small committee like ours, or is it a technical result whose force depends on assumptions that a well-designed procedure can reasonably reject? Weigh the main arguments on both sides. Then defend a verdict: either that we should abandon the search for full strategy-proofness and focus instead on making manipulation difficult or self-defeating, or that we should adopt one of the escape routes, specifying which and why, or that the theorem should lead us to accept a non-democratic decision rule in certain contexts. Do not end on a noncommittal note. To make your discussion concrete, consider the following procedure I have been developing, which I call approval-with-fallback: each member may approve any number of the five plans; the plan with the most approvals wins, unless no plan receives approvals from at least three members, in which case the chair's previously announced default plan is adopted. Does this procedure evade the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result? If so, at what cost? If not, what is its strategic vulnerability, and does that vulnerability matter in practice?
