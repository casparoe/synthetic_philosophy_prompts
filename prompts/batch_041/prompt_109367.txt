I'm a novelist — currently wrestling with a book about a gambler — and I've been teaching myself a bit of decision theory to get the math right, mostly from a textbook a philosopher friend lent me. But I keep having gut reactions to these games that don't line up with the expected values, and I want to know if there's a single principle behind my verdicts or if I'm just being inconsistent. Here's what I think, going case by case. The St. Petersburg game — you flip a fair coin until heads, and if heads first appears on toss n you win 2^n dollars — has an infinite expected value, but I wouldn't pay more than about twenty bucks to play. The Pasadena game, where the payoff on toss n is (-1)^n times 2^n divided by n, has an expected value of negative ln 2 if you sum the terms in the natural order, so the math says it's slightly bad for me, yet I'd probably still pay a dollar or two to play, because the chance of a huge win feels worth it. But here's where it gets wierd: if you rearrange the terms of that same Pasadena game — same coin, same payoffs, just grouping the wins and losses differently — you can make the sum come out to any number you like, positive or negative. If someone told me the rearranged version has an expected value of a million dollars, I wouldn't believe it changes anything about the actual game; it's the same physical process. And then there's a game where you first flip a fair coin to decide whether you're playing for wins or losses, and then you play a St. Petersburg for that sign: the expected value is undefined, since the positive and negative parts both diverge. I wouldn't pay to play that, but I also wouldn't refuse it if it were free.

So my verdicts are: (1) St. Petersburg: infinite EV, pay about $20. (2) Pasadena: EV = -ln2, pay about $1. (3) Rearranged Pasadena: EV = +$1,000,000, but same verdict as (2). (4) Signed St. Petersburg: undefined EV, pay $0 but accept for free. Is there a principle that captures all four? I suspect it has something to do with whether the expectation is well-defined and order-independent, but I don't know what to do when it isn't. Also, what does your principle say about a game I haven't listed: the reverse Pasadena, where the payoff is (-1)^{n+1} 2^n/n, so the natural sum is +ln2? Would I pay more for that than for the original Pasadena? My gut says yes, but only a little, which seems wierd if the only difference is the sign of the expected value. I'm definately not a philosopher, so go easy on the jargon, but I do want the real answer, not a dodge.
