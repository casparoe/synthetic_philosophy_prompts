Ok, so heres a puzzle about church taxes that's been bugging me. Take a country that's officially secular — no established church, the constitution says the state is neutral on religion, there's no religious test for holding office, public schools teach no catechism. Fine. But the same state's tax office, using the ordinary payroll-withholding machinery, collects a "church tax" for the big churches: it's a set percentage of your income tax, your employer pulls it straight out of your paycheck because your religious membership sits in a government database that employers get excerpts from, and the money goes to the church. And membership is sticky — your counted as a member basically from baptism, by default, and the only way to stop paying is to go file a formal declaration with a civil authority (not the church) to leave. Some churches then treat that civil declaration as an act of apostasy and won't marry or bury you.

So here's what I want. What theory of religious belonging, consent, and the church–state relationship does this whole arrangement presuppose? Does it presuppose that being a member of a religion is more like citizenship or a default status you're born into, rather than a voluntary association you join? Which rival views does it quietly rule out — the "church as a private club" or contractual picture, the strict voluntarist picture where nothing is yours until you actively opt in? And is it actually consistent with the same state's insistence that it's religiously neutral, or is "neutral" doing something odd here?

Then, to test it: imagine the same country extends the identical machinery to a secular humanist association and to a mosque — same withholding, same opt-out-by-civil-declaration. Does the implicit theory survive that, or does it fall apart? If it falls apart, what does that tell us about what the original arrangement was really assuming all along?
