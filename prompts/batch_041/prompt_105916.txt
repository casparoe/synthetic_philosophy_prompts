For a satirical passage in the novel I'm writing, I need an in-universe document: the coverage determination letter, with an attached adjuster's report, issued by a fictional insurer in the deadpan register of real claims correspondence, about 700 words. The loss is a clay sculpture crushed by a forklift at the foundry, and the studio's policy schedules the sculpture and its clay stock as separate insured items with separate limits, which forces the company to answer on the record how many objects were destroyed and which of them the policy actually covered. The humour should come from the metaphysics being right and the bureaucracy being airtight. The adjuster has to work through colocation — two things in one place at one time — then through the fact that the lump and the statue seem to differ modally, the lump surviving being squashed into a ball while the statue does not, even though at every moment they exist they share all their parts and all their qualitative properties; then through the grounding problem, what could make that difference non-arbitrary; then through the possibility that identity is sortal-relative, so that one and the same thing may be a single object under one sortal and two objects under another. Let the company settle the matter by fiat, in a clause making coverage follow the sortal under which the item was scheduled, and let that fiat quietly presuppose one contested view rather than announcing it. Give the adjuster one clause of unease over whether a lump is a rigid embodiment exhausted by its parts while a statue is a variable embodiment that survives the replacement of those parts, and whether the answer moves the payout. Keep the prose flat and unironic throughout; a reader should be able to tell what is being satirized without the document winking. Then, separately and clearly marked as not part of the document, a short note for my own use: where the piece takes liberties with the actual positions, which of the moves it treats as settled are in fact contested, and what would lead you to think the satire had been unfair to its target.
