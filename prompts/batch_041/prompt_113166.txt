I've been trying to get my head around Avicenna's flying man (some people call it the suspended man) arguement and I keep hitting a wall. I get the setup — you imagine yourself brought into existence all at once, hanging in midair, eyes covered, limbs splayed so you're touching nothing, no sound, no smell, no sensory input whatsoever — and the claim is you'd still affirm that you exist. Fine, it sounds Cartesian. But here's what I actually want explained, and it's the part the usual summaries skip.

Is the arguement supposed to establish that the soul is *immaterial* — a thing that isn't a body at all — or only that the soul's existence doesn't *depend* on the body, i.e. that the self isn't identical with this body or any body? Those sound close but they're not the same claim, and I've seen people insist Avicenna only needs the weaker one and others say the whole point is the stronger one. So what actually hangs on which reading, and does the scenario as described get you to the strong claim or does it stall at the weak one?

The other thing that keeps coming up is that it maybe isn't a thought experiment in the modern sense at all — that Avicenna isn't asking you to imagine a case and then run an inference off it, but is pointing at a kind of immediate, primitive self-awareness that was never derived from the body in the first place, so the scenario is more of a nudge than a premise. If that's right, does the arguement even have the structure of an arguement, and if it doesn't, does that make it stronger or weaker? Lay out whats at stake between these readings for me, and say plainly which piece of the scenario is doing the real work versus which bits are just stage dressing.
