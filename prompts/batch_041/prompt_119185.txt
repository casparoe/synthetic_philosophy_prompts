In retirement I have turned to philosophy, after a career spent studying how animals choose between rewards available at different delays, and I want to see whether Aristotle's account of weakness of will can answer to facts my old field took for granted. Ethologists routinely describe animals as reversing their preferences as a reward draws near, yet much of that apparent present bias can be derived from maximizing the overall rate of return across a session rather than from any primitive tilt toward the present; on that reading the reversal is a property of the decision rule, and not obviously a failure of will at all.

Suppose the modern apparatus were set before him: the distinction between time-consistent and time-inconsistent preferences, a discount function split into a present-bias factor and a constant rate (the hyperbolic form usually fitted to such data), and precommitment devices of the sort commonly called Ulysses contracts. Which parts of the phenomenon would his account of akrasia absorb, and which would it reject as a misdescription? Would he count the man who changes his mind as the deadline approaches as akratic, given how tightly he ties choice to a settled conception of the end? Does his condition on the akratic's knowledge fit an agent who grasps the general premise and still takes the smaller, sooner reward? And can his notion of choice make room for precommitment, or must binding oneself in advance appear to him as self-manipulation rather than self-command?

Argue from his own commitments rather than recasting him as a behavioral economist; where the texts leave the matter open, say so and indicate what would decide it. No references are needed.
