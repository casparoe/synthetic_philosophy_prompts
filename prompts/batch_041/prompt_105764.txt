I am writing the memorial address I will give this June for my doctoral advisor, the historian Mara Ellison, who died in the spring at eighty-three. The service will hold two audiences that want opposite things from the same twenty minutes. Roughly half the room are historians and philosophers of history who knew her work and will be listening for whether I get it right; the other half are her family — her two sons, their partners, four grandchildren between the ages of nine and nineteen — who never read a word of it and who have come, quite reasonably, to be comforted.

The difficulty is that the eulogy is itself an instrument of the thing she spent her career attacking. Her whole body of work was an argument against hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation — against the habit of reading the past backward from how it happened to turn out, treating the actual outcome as the one door that was ever open, and quietly crediting the people involved with foresight they could not have had. She had her own vocabulary for the two errors, and I would like the address to use it. She called the first the funnel: the collapse of an open, branching situation into the single line that was actually walked. She called the second the borrowed lantern: the light we lend past actors so they can see what was coming. And she kept one standing rule, which she applied to other people's lives and, in the last years, pointedly to her own — never say what an event had to lead to.

So the address I need is one that honors a woman by refusing to do to her the very thing she spent forty years forbidding. It cannot be a cradle-to-grave arc. It cannot say that her childhood made her, that the loss of her first husband turned her toward the archive, or that everything she wrote was leading to the book she never finished. It has to hold her life the way she insisted lives should be held: as a scatter of moments that were each open at the time, with no meaning retrofitted from the vantage of the grave. And it still has to comfort a room that includes a nine-year-old, and it still has to land for the specialists as a genuine piece of her own thought rather than a pious gesture at it. The tension between those demands is the task, not a problem to be dissolved. I am not looking for a clever escape from it; I am looking for an address that lives inside it.
