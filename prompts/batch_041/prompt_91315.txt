You are a philosopher of public policy and social ethics. I have no academic training, but I have been thinking about how our welfare system actually works, and I want a careful reconstruction of what it assumes rather than a summary of familiar debates. Consider a concrete arrangement, which I describe because it is not a famous law: a benefits agency pays a basic unemployment allowance, but also runs a tiered conditionality regime. Claimants judged job-ready must keep a weekly log of applications, attend appointments, and accept any offer above a floor wage within a ninety-minute commute, or face a sanction cutting the allowance for thirteen weeks. Claimants with a documented disability, or caring for a child under three, are put in a lower conditionality group with no work-search requirement, though their allowance is lower. A separate fraud unit pursues people who work while claiming, and the agency publishes monthly sanction and fraud statistics under a slogan about getting tough on people who will not help themselves. What view of desert, responsibility, and the line between the deserving and undeserving poor does this arrangement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then tell me which parts are strictly presupposed by the rules and which merely fit them; which rival theories of entitlement or distributive justice the arrangement quietly rules out; whether the disability and care exemptions are consistent with the work-search requirement or reveal a second, competing theory of who counts; and what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with one that treats unemployment as structurally caused rather than as a failure of effort. Start by brainstorming the possible readings before committing to one, and be explicit about where the practice is underdetermined, so that several theories could license it. I am not asking for reading lists or citations; I want your reasoning.
