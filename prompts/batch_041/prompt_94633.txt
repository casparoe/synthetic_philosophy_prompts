I keep going back and fourth on Montaigne's essay about the cannibals — the Brazil one — and I need help with a specific bit. Near the end he talks about three men from that country who'd been brought over to France, and he asked them what they found most astonishing about how we live. If I'm remembering right they gave three answers: that so many big bearded armed men around the king would obey a child instead of picking one of themselves to lead; that some people here were stuffed with every kind of good while others were begging at their doors; and that they couldn't understand why those beggars put up with it instead of grabbing the others by the throat or burning their houses down. And he says he couldn't get anything more out of them, partly because they didn't understand the language and he was working through someone who'd lived over there.

The thing I'm stuck on is what this episode is supposed to be doing, because it seems to sit awkwardly with the essay's most famous claim — the one where he says we call barbarism whatever isn't our own custom, and that we have no other test of truth and reason than the example of our own country's opinions. That sounds like relativism: no judging across cultures, full stop. But then the whole essay is full of judgment. He says the cannibals eating their enemies for revenge is less barbarous than what Europeans do to living bodies, he praises their poetry and their way of war, and the three-men passage is clearly holding up their incomprehension as a mirror for how weird French society looks from outside. So he seems to be a relativist in the thesis and a critic everywhere else.

What I want help with: is this a real inconsistency, or am I misreading the relativist bit? Maybe the claim about custom is narrower than it looks — something about the word barbarism being a term of prejudice rather than a claim that cross-cultural moral criticism is impossible — in which case there's no contradiction, he's just saying you can't condemn something merely for being foreign. Or maybe he really does hold both and the tension is the point. And I want to know how much work the revenge detail does: does cannibalism-for-revenge rather than cannibalism-for-food matter to the arguement, or is it just color? And does the fact that his whole report is second-hand, through a servant and a broken conversation, undercut the confident judgments he makes, or is that caveat itself part of the argument?

Give me your bottom line in the first sentence and then the reasoning, no preamble, don't restate what I asked. And tell me how confident you are in your reading and which point you're least sure about.
