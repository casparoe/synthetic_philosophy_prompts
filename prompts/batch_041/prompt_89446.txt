I'm TAing a grad seminar on social choice and I want a problem-set question that forces students to build something rather than recite Gibbard–Satterthwaite, so I'm asking you to do it first and show me it actually works. Build me a voting scenario with exactly five voters and exactly three alternatives, where the alternatives sit on a single left-right line (pick something mundane — three sites for a fire station, three levels of a parking levy, whatever, but give them concrete names and tell me where each sits on the line). Everyone's true preferences are strict and single-peaked on that line, and the body does not use the median-of-peaks rule; it uses plurality (first past the post) with a fixed tie-break that you state explicitly. I need the case to do three things at once: the median rule would pick one alternative, plurality with your tie-break picks a different one, and at least one voter has a strict incentive to misreport — that is, there's a ranking they can submit, different from their true one, that produces an outcome strictly better for them by their own true preferrences. Here's the part I actually care about: after they lie, all five submitted rankings must still be single-peaked with respect to the same left-right ordering. No bizarre ballots, no burying a rival for no reason — the lie should be the mild, plausible compromise a normal voter would make, so the reported profile still satisfies the very domain restriction the escape hatch is supposed to give you. No lotteries, no money, no logrolling across separate votes, no abstention, one-shot simultaneous secret ballot.

After the scenario, show the profile as a table (true preferences, then the misreported ones), the plurality outcome under your tie-break, the median-rule outcome, and the before/after comparison from the manipulating voter's own point of view, being explicit about which pair of outcomes they're effectively choosing between. Then walk me through why everybody's single-peakedness fails to protect plurality here, and name the single feature of the case that's doing the work. Two follow-ups I want answered: (a) if you drop to two alternatives, does the incentive vanish, and why — and does the case still show anything interesting about the theorem; (b) if you go to an even number of voters and change nothing else, what breaks, and does the manipulation get easier or harder. Then tell me how a defender of plurality would attack the case — they'll say the voter needs to know the others' ballots, or that strategy-proofness is too strong a demand — and whether that objection actually lands. Flag where you're least confident (my money's on the tie-break doing more work than it should, but tell me if I'm wrong) and don't paper over any step where you're guessing about what the theorem does and doesn't cover. End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in under fifteen words, the feature of the case that does the work.
