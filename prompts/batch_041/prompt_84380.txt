Problem set question

Distinguish the is–ought gap from the naturalistic fallacy. Then assess the following argument:

Human beings are naturally disposed to care for their offspring; therefore, parents ought to care for their children.

Does the argument commit a fallacy, and if so, which one? Justify your answer.

Student's answer

The is–ought gap is the principle, associated with Hume, that no valid argument can move from purely descriptive premises to a normative conclusion. The naturalistic fallacy, a term Hume also coined, is what happens when someone violates this principle by trying to define moral terms such as 'good' in natural terms — for instance, by equating the good with what is pleasant or with what we are evolutionarily disposed to want.

The argument above does move from a descriptive premise about our natural dispositions to a normative conclusion about how parents ought to act, so it does commit a fallacy. Because it tries to derive an ought from an is, and because it treats a natural disposition as a source of moral authority, the right label is the naturalistic fallacy. Moore's open-question argument shows that any such definition of 'good' in natural terms must fail: whatever natural property is proposed, we can always meaningfully ask whether things with that property are really good, which shows that the proposed definition has not captured what we mean. That is why the argument is invalid.

A defender of the argument might supply a suppressed premise — for example, that parents ought to do what they are naturally disposed to do — and then the inference would go through. But this only pushes the fallacy back a step, since the suppressed premise itself still needs to be derived from something descriptive, which the is–ought gap forbids.
