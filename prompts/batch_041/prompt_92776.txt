I chair the Committee on Faith and Learning at a religiously affiliated research university. The provost has asked us for a written opinion on a matter now before the board of trustees, and I need a draft I can circulate before we meet.

The case: a professor of physics in our college of arts and sciences has published a trade book arguing that the universe need not have had an absolute beginning — she favors a family of cyclic and emergent models on which the observed expansion is a phase rather than an origin — and that the opening chapter of Genesis is accommodated language about God's sovereign relation to the whole of things, not a claim about a first moment of time. The physics is genuinely unsettled: no model in her family is established, and several are widely thought untestable in principle. Our confessional statement affirms that God is the creator of all things and that the created order is neither eternal nor self-explanatory. A group of alumni and two trustees have petitioned the board to require her to append a disclaimer to her syllabi and her public lectures, stating that her cosmological views are speculative and do not represent the position of the university. The provost wants our opinion on whether the board should adopt that requirement, reject it, or adopt some narrower measure.

I do not want another general essay on science and religion. I want a memo that reasons carefully through the Galilean framework and then applies it honestly, including where it cuts against the professor. The memo must keep distinct, and then reconcile or pointedly refuse to reconcile, the following strands of Galileo's position on scripture: that scripture and nature cannot contradict one another because both proceed from God; that scripture is accommodated to the understanding of ordinary people and so speaks in the idiom of appearances rather than in the vocabulary of natural philosophy; that a scriptural passage should be reinterpreted in light of a physical result only when that result has been demonstrated, since merely probable or elegant hypotheses do not license the move; that binding a scriptural reading to a cosmology that later collapses does grave harm to the faith of the unlearned and supplies ammunition to unbelievers; that scripture's purpose is to teach what is needed for salvation rather than to deliver a cosmology; that theologians as such are not competent to settle questions in natural philosophy, nor natural philosophers to settle questions of faith; and that Galileo himself conceded that a genuine demonstration of the earth's immobility would oblige him to give up the Copernican reading. Mark where these strands pull apart — above all, whether generous accommodation renders the demonstration condition idle, and whether the demonstration condition, taken strictly, leaves our professor without a defense.

Assume the committee knows the outline of the Galileo affair; do not retell it, and do not stage the case as a morality play in which the professor is Galileo and the trustees are the Inquisition. Paraphrase Galileo's arguments in your own words. Do not quote from the letter or from any other text, and do not cite editions, translators, or page numbers; a memo that leans on them will be useless to us.

Structure the memo in exactly these sections, in this order:

Recommendation — one paragraph, roughly 120 words, naming the measure you favor and the timeframe for adopting it.
The Question Before the Committee — roughly 150 words, stating precisely what is and is not at issue.
The Galilean Framework, Stated Precisely — roughly 400 words, reconstructing the strands above as a coherent position before turning to the difficulties.
Where the Framework Comes Under Strain — roughly 400 words.
Application to This Case — roughly 350 words, reaching a conclusion about the disclaimer and about what the professor may and may not present as established.
The Strongest Case Against My Recommendation — roughly 250 words, stated as its proponents would state it, then answered.
Implementation — roughly 150 words, with concrete steps and dates.

Aim for roughly 1,800 words total. Write in a register suitable for circulation to trustees: precise, unhurried, and free of hedging.
