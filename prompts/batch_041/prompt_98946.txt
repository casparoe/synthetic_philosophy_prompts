I'm a novelist, mostly literary sci-fi, and I'm about 40k words into a draft that I'm definately starting to think is a philosophy paper wearing a lab coat. It's set at a near-future institute that answers hard questions by factoring them: a question goes to an analyst, who splits it into sub-questions, each of which goes to another analyst -- in the ideal version, every analyst is a copy of the same person, and the whole thing is supposed to be equivalent to one careful thinker with unlimited time and no coordination costs. The researchers call it HCH, or factored cognition, or just "the tree," and the official line is that the tree's answer is exactly what that ideal deliberator would conclude, so the output is trustworthy by construction. My protagonist is a mid-level auditor who checks decomposition logs. She starts noticing that the tree is finite, that branches get pruned when they get expensive, that the aggregation step sometimes averages away a dissenting leaf, and that the deepest sub-questions are quietly answered by a cheap model that was never part of the idealization. I want the story to turn on the realization that the idealization is doing all the moral work: nobody is claiming the real system is good, only that it approximates something good, and no one has checked whether the approximation preserves whatever made the ideal good in the first place.

Could you write me a short story, maybe 1,500-2,500 words, that dramatizes this? I'd love it to stay inside the institute -- bad coffee, alot of whiteboards covered in question trees, a break room where the simulated analysts leave passive-aggressive notes for each other -- and I want the conceptual stuff to remain in the weeds rather than getting explained away in dialogue. Maybe include one concrete factored question that goes wrong in an interesting way: say, "should we warn the public about the leak" gets split into "what's the probability" and "what's the cost of panic," and because the two branches never talk, the final answer is technically responsive and practically useless. The protagonist could discover she's one of the copies, or could just be the human who has to sign off on the report; either way, I'd rather it end on the quieter horror that the ideal was never meant to be built, only invoked to keep the building open. Also, if you can, make the story feel like it's about deliberation as a place of work, not just a metaphor for AI.
