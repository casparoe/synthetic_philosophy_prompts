I'm TAing a 300-level history of ethics course and the prof is away at a conference this week, so the midterm short-answers landed on me. The unit was Augustine on lying, and the question (she approved it before she left) was this:

"Augustine insists that no lie is ever permissible, and yet Scripture praises the Hebrew midwives for deceiving Pharaoh and praises Rahab for hiding the spies. How does Augustine handle these cases? Then explain what his account of lying implies for each of the following: (a) a joke in which you say something false; (b) a statement you believe to be true but that is in fact false; (c) staying silent when you know the truth and your silence would mislead someone. Finally, say something about the ranked catalogue of kinds of lies he gives, and where the so-called helpful lie falls on it."

Please don't be soft: if a claim is subtly off, say so and say why, even if the overall shape of the answer is fine. Here's the answer:

Augustine thinks lying is always wrong because a lie is a kind of doubleness — you have one thing in your mind and you utter another, with the aim of deceiving. So the sin isn't merely that what you say is false; it's the intention to mislead. That's why he says a joke isn't a lie, since the tone and the setting make clear you don't mean anyone to be taken in. And it's why someone who says something false but believes it true isn't lying, while someone who says something true but thinks it's false, and says it in order to deceive, is lying.

The hard cases are the midwives and Rahab. Augustine doesn't say their lies were fine. He says Scripture is praising their mercy or their faith, not the lie itself — God rewarded the midwives for fearing him and sparing the children, and Rahab is commended for her faith and her hospitality to the spies. Their lies still need forgiveness. So there's no contradiction: Scripture can praise a person for a good motive even when the act they did was wrong. He's very strict about this and won't allow a lie even to save someone from being murdered, or to keep heretics from being found out.

On (a), (b), (c): (a) a joke is not a lie, because there's no intention to deceive — jokes have never been counted as lies. (b) If you believe it's true but it's false, you're not lying, you're just mistaken; he treats that as rashness or error rather than lying. (c) Here I think Augustine is stricter than people expect: since the wrong of lying is that your speech doesn't match your mind, staying silent when you know the truth and your silence would mislead is also a kind of lying, because you're still producing a mismatch between what you know and what you let the other person believe. So he'd forbid that too.

On the ranked catalogue: he does rank the kinds of lies, and the helpful lie — the one where you lie to save someone's life or keep them from harm — is the worst of the eight, because it's the one that most directly sets your own judgment above God's law and treats a serious evil as a good. The least bad are the trivial social ones, like lies told to please people in conversation. This is why he's so opposed to the Catholics who wanted to pretend to be Priscillianists in order to infiltrate them: that would be a helpful lie, and helpful lies are the gravest. I think this is consistent with what he says in the later work against lying, where he doubles down on the same position.
