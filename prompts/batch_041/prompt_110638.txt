I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working on early modern metaphysics — my dissertation is on Leibniz's account of body — and I have been dragooned into leading next month's session of our philosophy-of-physics reading group on the hole argument. My colleagues there, mostly relativists with one philosopher of physics among them, have asked me, half in jest, to say what Leibniz would have made of the whole business, and I have discovered that I do not have a good answer. I know the shape of the modern argument well enough: take a solution of the field equations of a generally covariant theory, choose a region of spacetime lying wholly to the future of some initial surface, apply a diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside that region but not inside it, and you obtain a second solution that agrees with the first on the initial data while differing within the region; the substantivalist who treats the points of the manifold as genuine individuals seems committed to calling these two genuinely distinct situations, whereupon the theory looks indeterministic; the relationist, and more recently the anti-haecceitist who denies that there is any fact about which point plays which role, replies that the two solutions are one and the same physical situation — a conclusion the contemporary literature tends to file under the heading of Leibniz equivalence. What I need from you is not a survey but a prediction: what would Leibniz's position have been, argued in his own vocabulary, had the argument been put to him?

I do not want the obvious answer that he was a relationist and so would have welcomed it. I want to know which of his commitments would be doing the real work — the principle of sufficient reason, the identity of indiscernibles, the thesis that space is the order of things that coexist, his treatment of bodies as well-founded phenomena — and whether each of those commitments can be carried over into the modern setting at all, or whether the whole debate rests on a picture of a manifold populated by bare individuals that he would simply refuse to engage. Two tangles in particular I would like you to sort out. First, the modern argument appears to run through determinism: the complaint is that the laws leave the future unfixed. Leibniz's own version of the argument, the one about the material world being shifted bodily through space, seems to run instead through the thought that God could have had no reason to place things one way rather than the other. Are these one argument or two, and if Leibniz were shown the modern version would he recognise it as his own? Second, I would like the reconstruction tested against a case I have invented for the session, since I suspect it is where the Leibnizian line either earns its keep or comes apart: a toy world containing nothing but three exactly similar globes, together with a relabelling that permutes which globe is which while leaving every relation among them unaltered. Would Leibniz call the relabelled world a different possibility, the same possibility, or a confusion, and would he give the same answer there as in the hole case? Where the two answers diverge, tell me what in his system is responsible for the divergence.

Finally, be candid about the limits of the exercise. Tell me which part of the reconstruction is most likely to be anachronistic, what the strongest objection is to the attribution you are making, and what a sceptical historian in the audience will say to deflate it — I would rather have that in advance than be ambushed by it. And if the honest result is that a faithful Leibniz would turn out to be closer to the modern anti-haecceitist than to the relationist, or that his own argument cannot be detached from the theology that motivates it, say so plainly; that would be the interesting outcome, and I would far rather have it than a tidy one.
