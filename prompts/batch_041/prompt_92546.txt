You are helping to design a graduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, and I need a calibration exercise for the session on adaptationism. I have invented a fictional clade so that the exercise cannot be answered from memory; you should treat every biological detail below as stipulated and reason from it rather than from anything you recall about real organisms. The clade is a group of arboreal mammals, the penduline gliders, and for each trait I list I also give a proposed adaptationist explanation together with one or more complications.

Please rate each proposed explanation on a scale from 0 to 1, where 1 means a careful adaptationist should be highly confident that the trait is a genuine adaptation shaped by the proposed selection pressure, and 0 means the explanation should be rejected outright. Then rank the seven explanations from most to least defensible, and for each one do three things: identify the single most serious Gould-and-Lewontin-style objection it faces (or, if it faces none, say why it survives), name the general category the trait most likely falls into — genuine adaptation, exaptation, developmental byproduct, or product of phylogenetic inertia — and state what one piece of evidence would most efficiently move the score up or down. Finally, indicate which single item the spandrels critique would treat as its clearest poster child, and which item is the strongest evidence against the critique being applied indiscriminately.

1. Caudal rod. The tail ends in a stiff, elongated rod. Proposed: an adaptation for bracing the body against vertical trunks while climbing. Complication: every close relative possesses the same rod, including species that move only along horizontal branches, and the rod is already present in the embryo well before any climbing behavior appears.

2. Gular patch. A carotenoid-based orange throat patch appears in both sexes. Proposed: a mate-choice signal advertising the bearer's foraging quality. Complication: patch saturation is fully determined by the carotenoid content of that season's fruit, so a heavily parasitized individual in a good fruit year looks brighter than a healthy one in a poor year.

3. Cranial crest. Males bear a bony crest; females bear a small nub in the same position. Proposed: sexual selection through male head-butting contests. Complication: crest size tracks body size almost perfectly, and a sister species in which males head-butt just as violently has no crest at all.

4. Enlarged liver. The liver is unusually large. Proposed: adaptation for detoxifying a secondary compound in the species' staple leaf. Complication: a sister species that eats an entirely different leaf has an equally large liver, and liver size in both tracks a shared developmental growth field.

5. Cranial strut. A bony strut bridges two plates of the skull. Proposed: reinforcement permitting a more powerful bite. Complication: the strut sits exactly where two ossification centers meet in every member of the order, including species with notably weak bites.

6. Slow metabolism. The species has a low basal metabolic rate and enters torpor readily. Proposed: adaptation to seasonal famine. Complication: the trait is shared across a large clade whose members eat a wide range of diets and occupy habitats with very different seasonalities.

7. Wrist lock. A specialized wrist bone allows the hand to lock while the animal hangs. Proposed: adaptation for suspensory, upside-down feeding. Complication: the bone is present only in the two species that feed upside down; it ossifies after birth in step with the emergence of the behavior; and immobilizing the joint in juveniles prevents them from feeding in the suspended posture at all.

Give the seven scores as a list, then the ranking, then the per-item reasoning, and keep the reasoning for each item to a few sentences. I am less interested in a verdict on adaptationism as a research program than in seeing whether the distinctions between byproduct, exaptation, inertia, and genuine adaptation are actually being applied to each case rather than merely named.
