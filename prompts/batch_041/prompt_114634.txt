I retired from the bench three years ago — thirty-one years of analytical chemistry, mostly pigments and coatings, which I mention because it matters in a second — and now that I've got the time I've been reading alot of philosophy. Slower going than I expected, but I'm hooked.

Here's what I want. There's this idea that what your words mean isn't just a fact about what's going on in your skull; it depends on the stuff and the people around you. Everyone reaches for the water/H2O case to explain it, and I've read that one so many times it's gone dead on me — I nod along and feel nothing. So I'd like you to write me a short story that does the same job a different way.

The case I'd like it built on: two small towns on opposite sides of a strait, joined now by a bridge, speaking the same language. Both towns have a word for the blue they grind out of local stone to dye cloth — you can make the word up, I dont care what it is, as long as it isn't borrowed from a real language. The stones are not the same mineral. They're indistinguishable to the eye, they behave identically in the pot, they fade at the same rate, and nobody on either shore has any reason to suspect a thing: each town's word took hold by people pointing at their own local stone, and in each town its a guild of dyers, not a dictionary, that settles what does and doesn't count. So the word picks out one thing on one shore and a different thing on the other, and no speaker could ever tell the difference just by being careful.

What I actually care about is the next part. Tell it from inside one head — a woman who grew up on one shore and moved across the bridge as an adult, and who works with the stuff every day, a dyer or a painter or a restorer. Two things I want from that. First, she must never be able to discover the difference by introspection, and neither should we: no unease, no itch, no sense that something is off. She uses the word the way her neighbors do, confidently, in ordinary sentences, and she isn't wrong to. Second, I want a scene where her memory of some particular thing from childhood — say a length of cloth she watched her mother dye — is, by the logic of the story, a memory with a content she could not have had as a child, even though nothing inside her has changed at all. If she finds out anything, it can only be from the outside: a lab, an old ledger, a customs form. Never by thinking harder.

Please don't let anyone explain the philosophy. No lectures, no knowing stranger, no closing moment where she understands that meaning was never in her head. It should just be a story, and the strangeness should come out of the facts of the case. And no doubles, no copies of people, no second planet — one world, one woman, one bridge. Water, H2O, and twins are off the table.

A thousand words or so is plenty. Then two or three sentences on which bit of the story is doing the real work, and what would happen to the case if that bit were removed.

If the mineralogy or the dye chemistry is load-bearing, don't fake it — either get it roughly right or keep it in the background. I spent my whole career around people who could smell when someone was bluffing about a pigment.
