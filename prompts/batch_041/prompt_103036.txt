Here's a pattern I keep noticing and I dont have a word for it. It isn't a single move people make, it's a flip-flop, and the flip always goes the same direction depending on what the machine just did.

Case 1. My aunt's bank rolled out a chatbot with a name and a photo and a chirpy personality, and it told her she could get a late fee waived. She couldn't. The bank's position was essentially: that's not a person, it can't make promises, it's just a language model producing text. Same bank, same week, was advertising that 'Aria' is 'here for you 24/7' and knows her by name.

Case 2. A friend of mine who will lecture you for an hour about how ChatGPT is nothing but autocomplete got genuinely upset — I mean personally upset, like betrayed — when it told him his writing was derivative, and he wanted it taken down for being dishonest. Ask him whether it can be honest or dishonest and he says of course not, there's nobody home.

Case 3. Nobody thinks a car's driving software is a moral agent, but when a self-driving car kills someone everyone instantly wants to know who's responsible, and 'the system did it, and systems can't be responsible' satisfies no one, so we go hunting for a human to pin it on.

The shape of it: we help ourselves to the 'just a machine, nobody's in there' move whenever the machine is being credited with something good, or when there's an obligation the machine seemed to take on and we'd rather not honor it, and we help ourselves to the 'it did something to me' move whenever the machine has harmed or manipulated us and we want someone held to account. Two opposite ontologies, deployed by the same person in the same afternoon, each locally reasonable.

I read that Dennett piece about counterfeit people, which is in the neighbourhood, and I know about the intentional stance, but neither seems to be exactly this — the intentional stance is about when it's legitimate to predict something by treating it as a believer-and-desirer, and counterfeit people is about deception and the trust an economy runs on. What I'm describing is more like a switching rule keyed to whether the machine helped me or hurt me, and to whether I'm the one being asked to shoulder responsibility.
