I'm a third-year undergraduate currently taking a course on the philosophy of probability, and we've just spent a few weeks on the three standard families of interpretation — the frequentist, the subjective/Bayesian, and the propensity or "chance" views — and the more I read, the more I notice that the statistics I actually use in my other courses doesn't line up cleanly with any of them. Concretely, I'm thinking of the whole apparatus of null-hypothesis significance testing as it's really practiced in my psychology methods course and in the medical literature: a study specifies a null and an alternative, computes a p-value, and calls the result "significant" when p < 0.05, reporting alongside it a 95% confidence interval for the effect. What interpretation of probability does this practice presuppose, if it presupposes one at all? The official frequentist story seems to be that a p-value is nothing but the long-run rate at which this procedure would throw up false positives, and that it makes no sense to attach a probability to a hypothesis in the first place; yet in every lab I've been in, and in most of the textbooks, people read p = 0.03 as though it meant "there's only a 3% chance this is a fluke," and read a 95% interval as though the particular interval in front of them had a 95% chance of containing the true value. Those readings sound subjectivist, or maybe like a propensity claim about the underlying chance setup, and I genuinely can't tell whether the practice is just being sloppy or whether it is quietly committed to a view it won't own up to.

Second, I'd like you to press on whether the practice is internally consistent. Take the result I've seen discussed in which a frequentist test rejects a point null at the 0.05 level while a Bayesian analysis with a vague prior ends up strongly favoring that very null — the two frameworks seem to be answering different questions, and I want to know which question the ordinary scientific practice is actually answering when it declares a finding significant. So please reconstruct the view (or views) of probability that significance testing and interval reporting really commit you to; distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with; say which of the rival interpretations it rules out and which it can be reconciled with; and tell me what would actually have to change in day-to-day practice — what we would report, how we would phrase it, how we would design and pre-register studies — if we replaced the implicit view with a consistently frequentist one, and then separately with a consistently subjective one. I'd much rather have the carving-up of the commitments than a history of the debate, and please don't just tell me that "it depends on your philosophy" — I want to know which commitments the practice cannot avoid.
