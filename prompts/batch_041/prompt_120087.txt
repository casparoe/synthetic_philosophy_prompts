I teach an upper-level epistemology course, and I want to spend one 75-minute session on something my students find easy to state and almost impossible to feel: the thought that if you are in a mental state, you are in a position to know that you are in it, and that if you know something, you know that you know it. On the page, both claims sound either obviously true or obviously false, and students settle into one of those verdicts within five minutes and stop thinking. I want an activity that makes the other verdict — that these claims fail at the margins, and fail in a way you cannot repair by attending harder or by climbing one rung up the ladder of higher-order knowledge — into something they run into with their own ears before I say a word about it.

Group: 14 students, one 75-minute session, a normal seminar room. Assume they can handle the vocabulary of evidence, reliability, and margins for error, but that they have no settled view. Everything must run with equipment I can carry in: a laptop with a tone generator or a desk lamp on a dimmer, paper, and tokens. Nothing should require students to take my word for what they are experiencing, and I should never have to tell them where the transition is.

The mechanism I want at the center of it is this. Participants make a series of judgments about a stimulus that ramps in steps too small for anyone to discriminate, and they are permitted to stake tokens on those judgments under a rule that a stake pays only if the same judgment would also have come out correct at every level the participant cannot distinguish from the actual level. Stakes close to the transition pay double; stakes far from it pay little. There is a second column for a second-order claim — not just whether the tone was sounding, but whether they know that they know whether it was sounding — scored the same way, so they discover for themselves whether the extra rung buys any purchase. And there is a bonus for being right at every level, which is where the trap closes, because that bonus can only be collected by detecting precisely the thing the safety rule forbids them from claiming to have detected. Do not flag any of this to the participants in advance; they should walk into it.

Give me the activity in the following sections, in this order:

**Setup and materials** (about 150 words): the room, the stimulus, the equipment, the sheets, the tokens, and anything I need to prepare or measure beforehand.

**Phase 1: Calibration** (about 200 words): how I establish the group's discrimination threshold and get the students to feel it as a threshold rather than hear me assert it, including how to handle the student who insists they can tell levels apart that most of the room cannot.

**Phase 2: The Ledger** (about 400 words): the full rules as the participants receive them, the exact payoff table, the exact wording of the safety rule as it would appear printed on the score sheet, the timeline of the rounds, and what I do and say while it runs.

**Scoring and the reveal** (about 250 words): how the ledger is settled, how the second-order column is scored against the first, and the order in which I disclose the ground truth, the group threshold, and the payoff bands.

**Debrief** (about 500 words): the discussion, mapped moment by moment onto the argument — which moment in the game corresponds to which step, where the students will try to escape (attending harder, staking only in the safe interior, refusing to stake at all), and what the second-order column is meant to show about whether knowledge of one's own knowledge is any easier than knowledge of the world. Describe the argument in your own words and do not hang any of it on a particular author, text, or passage; I will supply the reading.

**What will go wrong the first time** (about 300 words): the predictable failure modes — gaming the payoff table, disputes about whose threshold governs, students who claim the safety rule is simply unfair, students who try to reverse-engineer the transition instead of reporting what they hear — and what to do about each.

**What the activity cannot reproduce** (about 250 words): be honest about which feature of the idea has to be supplied by me in the debrief because the game's rules cannot stage it, and say where the game's picture of knowledge is cruder than the real thing.
