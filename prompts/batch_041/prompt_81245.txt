I am trying to get clear on the relation between Frege's remarks about truth and the family of views we now call deflationary or redundancy theories, and there are two passages I cannot reconcile. Both are given below as rendered in English translation; the first is earlier, the second later.

First passage: One can, indeed, say: "The thought that 5 is a prime number is true." But closer examination shows that nothing more has been said than in the simple sentence "5 is a prime number." The truth claim arises in each case from the form of the declarative sentence, and when the latter lacks its usual force, e.g., in the mouth of an actor upon the stage, even the sentence "The thought that 5 is a prime number is true" contains only a thought, and indeed the same thought as the simple "5 is a prime number."

Second passage: It is worthy of notice that the sentence "I smell the scent of violets" has the same content as the sentence "it is true that I smell the scent of violets". So it seems, then, that nothing is added to the thought by my ascribing to it the property of truth.

Help me understand what is going on here, in the following respects.

First, what work is "the form of the declarative sentence" doing in the first passage? Reconstruct as carefully as you can the distinction Frege is drawing among (i) the thought expressed, (ii) the act or force of judging or asserting, and (iii) the truth claim. Is the truth claim a component of the thought, a component of the force, or neither? Where exactly does the word "true" sit in that structure?

Second, take the actor case seriously as a test rather than as decoration. On a flat redundancy reading, a sentence and its truth-predicated counterpart say the same thing, so whatever befalls the one should befall the other. Frege appears to say that in the actor's mouth both collapse to a mere thought, which is consistent, but he also says the truth claim is precisely what vanishes along with force. If the truth claim comes from force rather than from the word "true," what is left for "is true" to contribute, and why would anyone think it contributes nothing?

Third, distinguish three positions and say which, if any, the two passages commit Frege to: (a) truth is a property, but a trivial or non-explanatory one; (b) "is true" is a syncategorematic device whose whole function is disquotation; (c) truth-claiming belongs to force rather than content, so there is no truth property at all. Then say whether the actor case forces a fourth position, on which the truth predicate is idle at the level of content while the truth claim remains ineliminable from the assertive act.

Fourth, there is a familiar deflationist move, associated with Ramsey, to the effect that in explicit cases the truth predicate can simply be dropped, with the residual worry that natural language lacks a device — a pro-sentence — that would let us drop it in the quantified cases as well, as when we say that everything someone says is true. Imagine a deflationist who takes that suggestion seriously and holds that the truth predicate is a prosentence-forming device that inherits whatever force the surrounding context supplies. How would that deflationist handle the actor case, and does Frege's example give them a problem or a resource?

Fifth, flag where you are reconstructing rather than reporting Frege's own doctrine, and where the two passages may sit in tension with each other. I would rather have an honest map of the interpretive options, including the option that Frege simply had no settled view about force, than a tidy synthesis that papers over the difficulty.
