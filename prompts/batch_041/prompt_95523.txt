I was out for a drink with a friend who's a couple of years into a PhD in philosophy, and he gave me what he called the two-minute version of the Frege–Geach problem. It's been bothering me since, because it made expressivism sound like a dead end, and I don't have the background to know whether I've been sold a line. His version goes like this. Frege spotted that you can take one and the same sentence and assert it, ask it as a question, or merely entertain it, and the content doesn't change — only the 'force' changes, and by force he meant the strength with which you put the sentence forward. Geach then turned this against the expressivist, who says that 'lying is wrong' isn't describing anything, it's just giving voice to a feeling of disapproval. Take a standard bit of reasoning: if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; so getting your little brother to lie is wrong. The phrase 'lying is wrong' turns up in the first premise and again in the second, and the inference only works because it means the same thing in both places. But the first premise isn't asserted, so on the expressivist's story there's no feeling being expressed there at all — it's just an inert bit of unasserted language. So the expressivist is stuck saying the phrase does two seperate jobs in the two premises, which makes the argument a fallacy of equivocation; and since it obviously isn't an equivocation, expressivism is refuted. He added that the problem really only bites for conditionals, because negation and disjunction are easy — a negated moral sentence just expresses the opposite attitude — and that the reason nobody defends straight expressivism any more is that the only people who try to patch this up (the quasi-realists, Blackburn and Gibbard and that crowd) do it by positing a 'higher-order' attitude, an attitude to an attitude, which is really just smuggling beliefs back in under a different name.
