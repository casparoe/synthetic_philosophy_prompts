I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, and next month I am recording an episode with a decision theorist who has spent years defending a heterodox reading of the backward induction paradox, one she calls the deliberation-time view. I want a set of interview questions that engage the substance of the position rather than inviting her to restate it.

Her view, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. The backward induction theorem is not in dispute: in a finite game of perfect information with common knowledge of rationality, the only play consistent with that common knowledge is the one the induction picks out, which in the centipede means taking at the first node. What she denies is that the theorem can be run as a decision procedure. The induction tells you what the players would choose at nodes that the induction itself declares unreachable; to act on it at the first node you must be certain of what you would do at nodes you have already ruled out, and the certainty that would license taking the money is certainty about a subjunctive conditional that the theorem's own premise never supplies. She calls this the deliberation-time gap, and her conclusion is that the centipede proves neither that taking is correct nor that the theorem is wrong: the theorem is a consistency constraint on descriptions of play, and the paradox arises from mistaking a description-level result for a prescription.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Make sure the set includes at least one question each on:

- the load-bearing issue of whether the player who stays at the last node is irrational or merely unconventional on her view, since she seems to want both the induction to be sound and staying to be blameless, and I cannot see how she gets both;
- whether the argument proves too much: if it blocks the use of the induction as advice in the centipede, does it not also block it in finitely repeated prisoner's dilemmas and in chain-store games, and if so what she takes herself to be losing;
- how her position differs from the familiar objection that backward induction is unstable under the slightest doubt, given that she claims her point survives even with certainty;
- the analogy with the surprise-examination or hangman paradox, which has the same backward-rolling structure and which most people treat as exposing an error in the reasoning rather than a limit on what can be known;
- whether the deliberation-time gap is just team reasoning in another vocabulary, with the pair rather than the individual as the unit of agency;
- what she takes the experimental finding that subjects cooperate well past the first node to show: evidence against the theorem, evidence that the subjects are irrational, or something about which her view is simply silent;
- the connection to the decision-theory debates I read about, and whether the same description-versus-prescription move would dissolve the Newcomb and evidential-versus-causal disputes as well, or whether it bites only here;
- and, to close, what a defender of the orthodox reading would have to show her before she would give the view up.

Please write the questions so that they can be asked aloud without notes, keep them concrete rather than gestural, and write the whole answer in British English.
