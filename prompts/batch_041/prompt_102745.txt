Im a novelist, working on a book where one of the characters is a bit of an Enlightenment buff, and I want to make sure I dont put a load of nonsense in his mouth. A while back I read a popular history of philosophy — I wont name it, I lent it to someone and never got it back — and it had this summary of Leibniz that I've been chewing on ever since:

'Leibniz is famous for the doctrine known as optimism: since God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and perfectly good, when he decided to create he looked over all the possible worlds, saw which one was best, and made that one. So this is the best of all possible worlds. That means everything that happens here — wars, plagues, the fact that your back hurts — is for the best and ultimately works toward some greater good; every evil is outweighed by some good, and if we could see the whole picture we'd realize there isn't any real evil at all, only evil that looks that way from our limited angle. It's a tidy package, but Voltaire wrote Candide to make fun of it (Pangloss is basically Leibniz with the serial numbers filed off), the Lisbon earthquake is usually cited as the moment it died, and honestly nobody in philosophy takes the best-of-all-possible-worlds thing seriously anymore — it's a museum piece, kept around mostly so people can point at it and shudder.'

Something about this feels off to me, but I dont know the Leibniz well enough to say where. Is it a fair summary?
