I'm assembling a problem set for a graduate seminar on decision theory under imperfect recall, and I need one genuinely new worked example rather than a warmed-over version of the famous one. Everyone in the room already knows the absent-minded driver — the fellow who passes two identical exits and cannot tell whether the one in front of him is the first or the second, so that whatever rule he adopts he must adopt it at both. I do not want that case again, and I do not want a cosmetic reskin of it; the previous cohort simply recalled it from the literature instead of reasoning, which defeated the exercise. What I am after is a case in the same structural family whose point is one the standard example is usually taken to obscure: the familiar case is normally used to compare what competing theories say about which pure rule to adopt, whereas I want a case where the real action lies in the choice between a pure rule and a genuinely mixed one. So please build me an imperfect-recall scenario, set in ordinary life, with at least three subjectively indistinguishable decision points at which a single rule must govern every point; I am insisting on at least three so that the example cannot quietly collapse into a relabeled two-exit driver. Make it something an adult could actually find himself in, with no science fiction, no demons, no perfect predictors, and no exotic technology, and with payoffs that can be stated in plain terms.

The property I need the case to have is this: the best mixed rule — one that randomizes with the same probability at each point — must be strictly better than every pure rule. I would also like the result to be robust rather than knife-edge, meaning that modest changes to the payoffs should not reverse which kind of rule wins, and that the optimal randomization probability should sit strictly inside the unit interval rather than being forced to its endpoints.

After the scenario, I need the case to be demonstrated rather than merely asserted. Lay out the payoff structure, give the value of each pure rule and of the optimal mixed rule, and show that the mixed rule strictly dominates. Then identify the single feature of the structure that is doing the work, explain why the familiar two-exit driving case does not display it as cleanly, and say what becomes of the example if you remove that feature — for instance by letting the agent tell the decision points apart, or by letting him commit in advance to a plan. Next, at a single decision point, tell me what causal decision theory and what evidential decision theory would each recommend in your case, whether the two agree with one another there, and whether the mixed character of the optimum alters either verdict; if you think the usual claims about how those theories behave under imperfect recall do not carry over to your variant, say so and explain why. Finally, give me the strongest objection a skeptic would raise — most likely that an absent-minded agent cannot truly randomize, or that a decision ought to be a deterministic function of one's information — together with your reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest.
