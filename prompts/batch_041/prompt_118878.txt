Okay, I need a short story — call it 2,000 to 3,000 words — and I want it to actually be about something specific, not just "identity is weird, man."

The thing I keep chewing on: Hofstadter's line of thought that the self isn't the body, it's a pattern, and that a real chunk of you gets rebuilt inside other people's heads — the way your best friend can finish your sentence, the way your mother runs a version of you that anticipates what you'll say before you say it. So dying is less a switch going off and more a sandcastle going out with the tide, in stages, over years. Fine, that part is familiar and I dont need it explained to me.

What I want the story to take seriously is the counting problem, which I think everyone skips. If 30% of me is resident in each of six people, what's the number of me's? One self spread thin? Six partial selves? Zero, because a pattern smeared across six heads isn't a subject anymore, just a resemblance? And the wrinkle that the copies aren't passive: my sister's version of me is funnier and kinder than I am, my ex's version is pettier and more frightened. Those versions keep talking, keep acting, keep telling each other what I'd have wanted. So which one is the referent? I don't want this solved. I want it to feel like a real problem people are living inside of, badly.

Here's the invented device, so don't go hunting for it: the loop-mapper. Two chairs, a set of prompts — finish this joke, what would she order, what's she about to say, would she forgive this — and out comes a number. How much of a given person is resident in you. Insurance companies use it to settle payouts. Courts use it to argue about whether a will was made by a self that still existed. There's a grey market of people saturating themselves with a dead friend to score higher, and a smaller movement of people who refuse to be mapped on principal. Everybody treats the number as the thing it's a proxy for. That's the part I find genuinely creepy.

What I want out of it: put it somewhere mundane and institutional — a claims office, a probate hearing, a hospice, a school — not a lab. There should be a scene where somebody has to decide something irreversible on the basis of a mapper number, and a moment where a character is more themselves inside somebody else than in their own skin, and the story should treat that as eerie and also as a relief, both. And no character gets to explain Hofstadter to another character. If the idea doesn't come out of what people are doing to each other, it hasn't worked.
