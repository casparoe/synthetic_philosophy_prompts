Over the past several months I've been following the arguments about whether AI chatbots can be said to lie, and I keep getting stuck on the same fork in the road. The definitions on offer seem to come in two flavors. One makes deception depend on intent — the system has to aim at producing a false belief in someone — which looks hopeless for a system that may not have intentions in the relevant sense at all, or at least none that anyone can inspect from the outside. The other treats deception as a property of a sentence: the sentence is false, therefore the output is deceptive. That seems far too crude, since I can mislead someone badly while saying nothing false (by choosing which true things to mention and which to leave out), and I can say something false while misleading no one at all (because the listener knows I'm joking, or knows I'm working from bad information).

I've been trying to sketch a third option on my own, and I'd like you to write an essay that puts it under real pressure and then decides whether it should be kept. The idea is to treat honesty and deception as properties of a system's dispositions rather than of its individual outputs, and to locate those dispositions specifically in how the system behaves when accuracy is costly. A system would be honest, on this view, roughly to the degree that its outputs stay accurate when accuracy is inconvenient — when the truth is embarrassing, when it means admitting ignorance, when a candid answer would disappoint the person asking or cost whoever runs the system money or goodwill — and deceptive to the degree that its accuracy decays in exactly those situations while holding up fine when nothing is at stake. So the interesting question about a model would not be whether some particular sentence was false, but how much pressure it takes before the model's grip on the truth starts to slip.

Please weigh this against the alternatives and reach a verdict. I'm curious whether it gets the cases right: a model that is confidently wrong about everything doesn't seem deceptive so much as simply bad, and I don't know where that leaves a view built around cost; a system that flatters rather than informs is misleading without ever being inaccurate; and I've argued with a friend who manages a support team that a bot which quietly dodges a refund question is doing something wrong even if every word it says is true. I also want to know whether the cost-based definition secretly just restates a claim about how the system was trained and rewarded, and whether honesty is better anchored in what the user is entitled to than in how robust the system happens to be under pressure. Don't leave this hanging — tell me whether you'd adopt the definition as stated, adopt it with amendments, or throw it out for something else, and defend that choice.
