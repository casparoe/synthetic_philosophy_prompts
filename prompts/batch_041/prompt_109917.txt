I write a science-and-ideas newsletter, which means I keep opining about privacy without having any of it worked out, so before I file my next piece I want a stress test. Please take the role of a philosopher or legal scholar who works on privacy and data ethics — someone who knows the actual literature — and think carefully. Start by brainstorming how the following claims might interact before you commit to an audit.

Where I've landed: (1) Privacy is about control over information about you, not about whether the information is actually out of reach. (2) Consent is the master key — if I understood a use and agreed to it, it isn't a privacy violation, however creepy it is. (3) But the inference economy gets me. I post my own grocery receipts on my blog; a broker infers I'm pregnant and sells that to an insurer; I've been wronged, and my clicking through a 40-page policy doesn't undo it. (4) Reading my public tweets isn't a privacy violation, but folding those same tweets into a profile that predicts my politics is — same information, same access, and yet it seems like a different act. (5) The value of privacy is instrumental: it protects autonomy, intimacy, deliberation, safety. I don't believe there's a seperate wrong in just being known. (6) And yet I think you can wrong someone by publishing their psychiatric records after they've died, even if no relative ever reads it and nothing changes downstream. The wrong looks like it's to her. (7) I also think privacy is a social good and not only an individual one — a city where everything is logged is worse even if no one in it is measurably harmed — while still defending privacy as an individual right.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one tell me the cheapest repair and what that repair costs me. Then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most at the least cost — I don't want to give up more than I have to.
