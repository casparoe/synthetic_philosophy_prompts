A friend who knows I have been reading in the philosophy of biology lent me a chapter from a popular book on evolution and mind. The chapter's account of Daniel Dennett's notion of a free-floating rationale runs roughly as follows.

"Dennett's idea of a free-floating rationale is his answer to the question of how there can be reasons in nature when no one is doing any reasoning. A spider builds a web of just the right geometry to catch flies; a digger wasp carries out a fixed sequence of steps that provisions its burrow. There is plainly a rationale behind these performances — the web works, the sequence works — and yet the spider and the wasp never think anything through. The rationale floats free of any mind. What Dennett adds is that natural selection is itself a kind of reasoner: over evolutionary time it works out the design problems an organism faces and installs the solutions in the organism's body and instincts, so that the organism acts for reasons that selection has, as it were, thought of on its behalf. This is why Dennett holds that we may legitimately say such creatures believe and desire things: the intentional stance is not a useful fiction but a description of a real, if unconscious, rationality. The only thing distinguishing the spider's web from a human engineer's design is that the engineer holds the rationale in mind while the spider merely embodies it. And the notion is confined to biology, since only natural selection can produce a rationale without a reasoner. Critics object that a reason nobody has is not a reason at all, and that Dennett is covertly smuggling genuine mental content into creatures he insists are mindless; that objection has not been answered. Still, the framework does useful work elsewhere: it licenses us to say that a language model trained by gradient descent acts for reasons that no one, including the model, has."

I think I grasp the general shape of this, but several sentences feel off and I cannot tell which. I am inclined to side with the critics and to suspect the whole notion is either trivial or mysterious, though I do not trust that instinct. Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of Dennett would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what Dennett would deny outright. In particular: does the summary collapse a distinction Dennett is at pains to preserve; is his position as realist as the chapter makes it sound; is the "confined to biology" claim correct; and does the critics' objection actually refute the notion or merely restate a disagreement? Finally, does the extension to machine learning follow from Dennett's own view, or is it an overreach?
