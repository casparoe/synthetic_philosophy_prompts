My friend and I went at it for like two hours last night about whether you need evidence that someone is trustworthy before you believe what they tell you, and we kept going in circles because we were arguing about cases instead of about what we were even asking a theory to do. Before I go back at him, write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of testimonial justification has to deliver. Not for radical skepticism, just ordinary life plus the parts that get formal, like courts and science.

Here's where we got stuck, so you can see what the list has to cover. He's a hard reductionist and his line is the vending machine test: if a machine with that hit rate handed you a snack you'd believe it, so a person with the same hit rate is no different, testimony is just a reading off an instrument and you price it like any other instrument. I said fine, then how does a three year old do that, and how do you do it about things you'll never be able to check -- the past, what happened in a room you weren't in, what a migraine is like from the inside. He said you generalize from the cases you can check. I said most of what we know is stuff nobody in the chain ever checked. Then we got onto the group chat thing: five people in our chat all told me the same thing about a medication last month, and it turned out all five got it from one post, and I'd already noticed myself feeling more sure of it with each person who said it. Neither of us could say what a theory is supposed to do with that.

Two more cases that broke us both. My friend's uncle is a reliable exaggerator -- he multiplies everything by ten, every time, and everyone knows it, so we all believe the tenth. Does a theory have to count him as a source of knowledge? He's not trustworthy and he's extremely reliable, and I can't tell whether trust or reliability is doing the work there. And then testimony about its own source: my brother telling me not to believe anything my family says. Whatever the theory says about trusting a teller has to survive a teller whose entire message is about whether tellers are trustworthy.

So, the list. The cases it has to get right -- the stranger who tells you the time, the con artist, the child, the domain you can't in principle check, the echo, the inverted uncle, the self-undermining brother -- the distinctions it can't collapse (believing a person vs. believing a proposition, what you're permitted to believe vs. what it would be irrational not to, the psychological story of how we form the belief vs. the justification story about what makes it ok, sincerity vs. competence, and whatever it does with two tellers who look equally good and disagree), and any results from the psychology it has to be consistent with, like how early kids start tracking who's been right before. Say which of these are genuine requirements and which are just one camp's prejudice, and flag any pair you think can't be had together, because I suspect that's the actual reason we couldn't settle it. And tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the most theories -- that's the one I want to open with next time.

Skip the big survey, but a paragraph each on where plain reductionism and plain anti-reductionism come off worst against your list would help me figure out if either of us was even defending something coherent.
