On the first Saturday in June my town will unveil a bronze statue of the physician who ran its clinic for thirty-eight years, and the committee has asked me to give the dedication address. I spent forty years as a metallurgist, most of it on the corrosion of copper alloys — a career on the very layer that forms on bronze, which sculptors call the finish and conservators call deterioration — and I have lately been reading philosophy, which has left me with a question about this object that I cannot put down and cannot answer.

The facts will matter, so here they are. The statue is hollow, cast in sections, joined, then chased. Its bronze was melted down from a memorial that stood in the same square until 1974, which had itself been recast from older metal. The sculptor modeled in clay, a plaster was taken from the clay, and the working mold was taken from the plaster; that mold survived, and a second cast from it stands in her studio. Next spring the town will move the statue forty metres to a new plaza, and the conservator will strip and renew the patina — a film a fraction of a millimetre thick, chemically nothing like the alloy beneath it, and, to my eye, the part of the object people actually love.

So the address must say something about what, exactly, we are unveiling, and I would like the old puzzle of the statue and the lump of clay to do real structural work in it rather than serve as ornament. As I understand the puzzle, statue and lump occupy the same place at the same time, are made of the same stuff, and yet come apart: the lump can be melted down and recast as a door handle, the statue cannot; the statue can survive the replacement of a corroded arm, the lump cannot. My own trade muddies even the standard pair, since what stands in the square is not one lump but a welded assembly of castings, an alloy batch with at least two earlier lives, and a patina that is neither.

What I am asking for is the address itself — six to eight minutes, written to be spoken, not a lecture. The audience will be the sculptor, now in her eighties, two of the foundry hands who cast it, the physician's grandchildren, the town council, and a neighbour who taught philosophy for thirty years and will know whether I have understood my own puzzle. I want the philosophy to constrain what I am and am not allowed to say: I should not be able to claim that the bronze will outlast us, or that the statue is really only metal, or that a life is safe in this particular alloy, without the argument visibly costing me something. I would like the room to leave with the puzzle rather than with a solution, but not with a shrug.

Tell me plainly where the speech takes a side on a question that is genuinely contested, so that I know what I am signing. And if I have the puzzle wrong — if the metallurgical facts I have given you dissolve the problem rather than sharpen it, or if a dedication address is simply the wrong vehicle for this and I should be writing an essay instead — say so, rather than producing something that merely sounds like a good dedication address.
