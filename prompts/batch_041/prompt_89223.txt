I am a retired geologist — forty years of field mapping, core logging, and arguments over stratigraphy — and since I put down the hammer I have been reading philosophy of science in an attempt to settle a question that nagged at me throughout my working life: what exactly separates a scientific claim from a pseudoscientific one? I would like you to take on the role of an expert in the philosophy of science, someone who knows the demarcation literature thoroughly but is willing to reason from first principles rather than recite it, and to help me articulate the principle that actually lies behind my own judgments. Please think this through carefully, and begin with a candid brainstorming pass over the candidate criteria you think might do the work before you commit to any of them. I am not asking for a survey of the debate; I am asking what rule my verdicts add up to, if they add up to anything.

Here are the verdicts I find myself giving, stated as flatly as I can. Astrology and homeopathy are pseudoscience. The claim that the Earth is only a few thousand years old, and that the fossil record was laid down by a single flood, is pseudoscience as well, even though it is stated in a form that can be tested and has been tested and has failed. By contrast, phlogiston chemistry, the caloric theory of heat, and the luminiferous ether were wrong and were refuted, and I would not dream of calling any of them pseudoscience; they were science that lost. Psychoanalysis I am inclined to call pseudoscience, though I have no laboratory experience of it and am going on hearsay. String theory, the cosmological multiverse, and the many-worlds reading of quantum mechanics I want to call science, even though I cannot see what observation would refute them. Mathematics and logic are not empirical sciences, but they are not pseudoscience either. Paleontology is science; most history, I suspect, is not, though I cannot say what the difference consists in. And a claim I once heard from a colleague — that the phase of the moon affects the outcome of surgery — I would call crankery rather than failed science, although it was perfectly testable, was tested, and failed.

Two pairs defeat me. Refuted theories that I call science and refuted claims that I call pseudoscience look identical from the standpoint of testability; and string theory and young-earth geology look identical from that standpoint too, yet I sort them differently. I suspect that any criterion strict enough to exclude creationism will also exclude the ether, and that any criterion generous enough to admit string theory will also admit astrology. There is a further embarrassment. In the 1920s J Harlen Bretz argued that the Channeled Scablands of eastern Washington were carved by enormous glacial floods, and he was ridiculed for some forty years before the evidence won out. Was his claim pseudoscience in 1927 and science by 1960, or was it science all along and the community simply wrong? I cannot tell.

What I want from you is the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself, together with the case that forces each clause and an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot save and must overturn. Then tell me what it implies for two cases I have not mentioned: the hypothesis that we inhabit a computer simulation, and the claim that a particular herbal preparation works through a mechanism that no instrument has ever detected. Please write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
