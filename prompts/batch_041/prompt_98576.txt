I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner for my former boss, who spent twenty-six years chairing a municipal nuisance tribunal — the body that hears the complaints no court will touch: an extractor fan running two decibels over its limit, a security light spilling into a bedroom, a depot that wakes the same street at four in the morning three times a week. In the small world of people who adjudicate such things she became quietly famous for a rule she recited at the start of every hearing, which she called the threshold rule: a grievance that is beneath notice when it happens does not become a grievance by happening to a great many people, or to one person a great many times. She was immovable about it, and it made her both admired and infuriating. The dinner is at a hotel in Leeds on a Thursday; roughly half the room will be regulators, acousticians and the economists who have spent their careers arguing about whether she was right, and one of them — a man she has feuded with for two decades — is giving a second toast after mine. The other half will be her family: her brother, her two grandchildren, and a dozen people who have never heard a word of any of this and will be drinking steadily.

I want six or seven minutes that land for both halves: affectionate, funny in places, and honest that her rule is contested without turning into a seminar. Please build it around a case she never had to decide, which I have been turning over since her retirement and which I think is the sharpest thing that can be said against her. Take a nuisance so small that no one would ever complain of it on its own — one minute of dull headache, say, or a single squeak of a gate. Now suppose one person receives one of these every day for eighty years, at unpredictable hours, so that they can never point to the moment they were harmed and can never say that any particular day was ruined. Her rule says: nothing there. Now suppose instead that the very same quantity of irritation — the same thirty thousand minutes of headache — is scattered one minute apiece across thirty thousand strangers, each of whom has forgotten it by lunchtime. Her rule says: nothing there either, and, what I find hardest to swallow, it says the two cases are exactly equally nothing, because in neither case does a single instance rise above the threshold. I should like the toast to make the room feel why that verdict is uncomfortable without my having to argue it, and then either to let her off the hook or to leave her on it, as you judge — she will be listening, she has never been sentimental about being let off hooks, and she will know if the case has been softened. Please write it in British English, and please don't reach for names from the literature to do the work; the room knows them, and it would only make the evening feel like a lecture.
