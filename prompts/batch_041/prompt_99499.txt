Write me a joke about the strategy-stealing argument. I want the humour to come out of the argument itself rather than out of the mere fact that it is mathematics, so the punchline has to turn on what the argument actually does — not on a generic gag about mathematicians or game theory. The argument's shape is the material: it derives a contradiction from the supposition that the second player has a winning strategy, by having the first player make an arbitrary opening move and then simply adopt that strategy, which works only because the game is symmetric and because an extra move can never be a disadvantage. The richest seams, as I see it, are the two places where that shape shows through. The first is the gap between what the argument delivers and what a player needs: it proves a win exists while revealing nothing about how to bring it off, so that in games such as Hex or Chomp we know the first player wins and cannot say how. The second is the argument's dependence on monotonicity — the moment a game stops rewarding the extra move, as it does wherever zugzwang is possible, the theft collapses and the trickster is left holding a move they would rather not have made. Build the joke on one of those two seams, and keep it to a setup and a punchline, no more than a few sentences. Someone who knows why the first player wins at Hex should find it genuinely funny, and someone who does not should still be able to work out why it is funny once it is explained; a laugh at the expense of the strategy-stealing argument is fine, a laugh at the expense of people who do mathematics is not. You may invent a game for the occasion or use one that exists, but do not let the joke rest on a result about any particular board size, and do not let it depend on the reader recognising a named theorem. Close with two or three sentences identifying which premise of the argument your punchline exploits, and saying what would become of the joke if that premise were restored.
