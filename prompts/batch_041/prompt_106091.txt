Hey — I do risk work for a mid-size city, and I keep running into arguments of the form "this is the new Munich" or "that's Vietnam all over again" or the so-called Thucydides trap, where someone takes a war between Athens and Sparta 2,400 years ago and uses it to forecast US-China relations. I want to write out what a theory of historical analogy would have to deliver before I'd trust any particular analogy. Not "is the Munich analogy good" — the spec. So: role yourself as a philosopher of history / methodologist of the social sciences, someone who's thought hard about how historical reasoning actually works. Think out loud, brainstorm first, then give me the list.

What I want: the requirements list. For each one, the case or consideration that motivates it, one line each. Then mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp — the "history repeats" camp vs the "one damn thing after another" camp. I'd rather have six or eight sharp requirements than twenty vague ones.

Cases I want the theory to handle, as tests:

- Munich 1938 invoked to justify getting in early; then Vietnam invoked as the lesson that you shouldn't get in at all; then 2003 sold as the case where you can't let the last war stop you from preventing a new Munich. Same analogy family, opposite recommendations depending on the decade.
- 2008 read through 1929 (avoid deflation, stimulate hard) and 2008 read through the 1970s (watch for inflation, don't overdo it). Both readings had serious people behind them. The theory has to either say which reading is apt, or admit aptness doesn't settle the lesson.
- Thucydides: an analogy drawn from a war we know mainly through one guy's account, used to predict a rivalry between nuclear powers. What would it even mean for that to be apt?
- A made-up case, use it: a hospital board that built its pandemic plan around a SARS-style respiratory outbreak, nailed that part, and got wrecked by something that spread before symptoms. Their analogy was reasonable and their plan was still wrong. Is "apt analogy, wrong lesson" something the theory has to distinguish from "inapt analogy"?
- Also: analogies get used because they're memorable, not because they're apt. Nobody cites the crisis that was quietly averted by people doing nothing interesting.

Some tensions I already suspect — tell me if they're real:

(a) I want aptness to be judgeable before the fact, but what makes an analogy apt seems to be a shared causal mechanism, and you usually only learn the mechanism afterwards;
(b) I want it to be objective, but an analogy's whole function is rhetorical — it works on an audience, which makes it sound like aptness is partly about who's listening;
(c) history is unique in every case, so any theory needs a story about what makes two situations instances of the same kind, and that story is doing all the work.

Finally: which single requirement knocks out the most candidates? I'd bet on non-vacuity, or reflexivity — the fact that people learn from history and thereby change the thing they were analogizing to. And tell me honestly if the whole "lessons of history" frame is a mistake, and the right theory would say there are no lessons, only constraints.
