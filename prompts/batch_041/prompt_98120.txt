I've been keeping a list of my gut calls about vague words and I want to know if theres one rule behind them.

Boiling water: at some point it's just boiling, and I feel there's a real moment there, we just can't pin it to a degree. Heap of sand: take grains off one at a time and I dont think any single grain does it, but I also don't think the last grain leaves a heap — I don't think there's any fact about where heap stops. Bald: same as heap, no fact. Tall: also no fact, but unlike bald it flips depending on who else is in the room, so it doesn't feel like the same kind of vagueness. Adult: 18th birthday, sharp as a knife, and that's because we decided it, not because we found it. Dead: people cross over gradually but I still think there's a real moment when somebody's dead, and medicine just hasn't nailed it down. Melting snowman: real moment, and waiting around will show you it. Lukewarm soup: you can wait and it'll get there too, but "lukewarm" still feels like something we made up, not something the soup has.

So my rough guess is: a word is honestly vague when nature's doing the sorting and we're just bad at seeing the line (boiling, dead, melting), and cheaply vague when only our interests draw the line (heap, bald, tall, adult). But soup breaks that, because waiting settles it and it still feels cheap.

Is there a principle that gets all of these? If it forces me to give one up, which one, and what would it say about a case I haven't listed — when does a person stop being young?
