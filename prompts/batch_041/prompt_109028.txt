Argue for a position. Do not survey the literature, do not assemble a taxonomy of views, and do not hedge into a both-sides conclusion. The position to argue for is this: every account of indirect normativity — every account that fixes what an agent ought to do by reference to what some idealized deliberator, idealized community, or idealized procedure would endorse — fails, and fails for a structural reason rather than a repairable defect. The core of the argument is a dilemma. Either the verdict of the idealization is authoritative because of how the idealization was constructed, in which case the normative content was supplied by the construction and the idealization itself is doing no independent work; or the idealization is specified thinly enough to presuppose no normative content, in which case it does not determine a unique verdict and the account is silent about what to do. Every intermediate position is unstable.

To keep the argument sharp rather than gestural, work with the following stipulative vocabulary and treat it as fixed for the purposes of this prompt.

Construction parameters: whatever you must stipulate in order to run an idealization at all — which facts the idealized deliberator is stipulated to know or to ignore, whether deliberation is constrained by coherence requirements on the agent's existing attitudes or is free to revise them wholesale, how the attitudes of multiple idealized agents are aggregated, whether the idealization is run once or iterated to a fixed point, and how it treats agents whose existence or preferences depend on the outcome being chosen.

Leakage: the situation in which a verdict presented as an output of idealization is in fact entailed by one of the construction parameters. The claim to defend is that leakage is not a bug that better idealization theory will patch, but is unavoidable.

Transfer: the further step from what an idealized agent would endorse to what an actual, non-ideal agent has reason to do. A convergence result about idealized agents does not by itself yield transfer. Argue that the standard moves at this step — appeals to the agent's own rationality, to the authority of the procedure itself, or to the value of approximating the ideal — each presuppose what they are meant to establish.

The argument should have the following shape. First, state the target precisely enough that a defender would recognize it as their own view: choose the strongest form of indirect normativity you can, not a strawman, and say what makes it attractive in the first place. Second, run the dilemma against that form, using at least two concrete constructions — for instance a hypothetical-choice construction, an ideal-observer construction, or a convergence-of-inquiry construction — and show in each case exactly which construction parameter does the normative work. Third, take up the best escape routes and show why they fail: in particular the response that the idealization is self-specifying, so that the correct construction is itself whatever the idealization would select; the response that leakage is tolerable because the parameters are themselves normatively innocent; and the response that the account is not meant to generate first-order verdicts at all but only to organize ones we already have. Fourth, state as precisely as you can what would have to be true for the argument to fail — what a successful indirect-normativity account would have to look like — and be honest about whether you think that is achievable.

Two constraints. Do not carry the argument by appealing to any particular author's formulation; if you need a target, construct it. And do not let the conclusion collapse into quietism: if indirect normativity fails, say what is left standing, and assess whether anything in the vicinity — a coherence requirement on the agent's own attitudes, a contractualist procedure, a claim about what is reasonable from a shared standpoint — survives the same dilemma or escapes it.
