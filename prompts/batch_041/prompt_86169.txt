I want a case analysis rather than a survey, so let me fix the terms and the facts myself and ask you to reason from what I supply.

Take the standard specification of democratic backsliding: a sustained, incremental deterioration in the quality of a democracy, carried out by elected incumbents using formally legal instruments rather than by soldiers or self-appointed juntas. Three commitments travel with that specification, and I want them separated.

First, threshold versus gradient. On one reading, backsliding is a change in kind: a polity crosses from electoral democracy into competitive authoritarianism, and the substantive question is where the line lies. On the other, it is a change in degree across several dimensions — elections, courts, media, civil society, accountability — and the line is a convention we impose.

Second, agency. Naming backsliding is a speech act with consequences. Domestic courts, external indices and NGOs, supranational bodies, and opposition parties each have some standing to say it, and they disagree with one another. Is the diagnosis a measurement or an act of positioning?

Third, the baseline. A decline is a decline from something. Whether a country counts as backsliding depends on the reference year and on the weights you assign to the dimensions, and nothing in the concept fixes either.

The episode is Poland between 2015 and 2024. Treat the following as given. A party with a parliamentary majority and a sympathetic president remade the constitutional tribunal, the body that appoints judges, the supreme court, and the public broadcaster, all through ordinary legislation. EU institutions opened treaty proceedings, the Court of Justice struck down parts of the judicial architecture, and recovery funds were withheld and later released. The constitutional tribunal held in 2021 that parts of the EU treaties were incompatible with the Polish constitution. The opposition won the 2023 election and took office that December. It then tried to undo the captured bodies by statute; the president vetoed or referred the bills to the tribunal; and the tribunal — whose legitimacy the new government denies — ruled against the government's measures, so that each side could point to a different institution as the final authority. Even the legal effect of a presidential pardon turned out to have two final answers, depending on which court one accepted.

What I want:

1. Run the episode through each of the three commitments. Which reading of the threshold question does Poland support, and does the case give any reason to think the concept needs a threshold at all? Who in the Polish case counts as the decider, and what does the choice of decider do to the diagnosis? What is the defensible baseline, and how much of the verdict moves when it shifts?

2. Then run it the other way. Does the concept have a place for reversal — for a decline that is arrested and partly undone by electoral means? Is what happened after 2023 an instance of the concept, its negation, or a case the concept cannot classify? Pay particular attention to the bootstrapping problem: the instruments of restoration are the very bodies that were captured, and the new government's own legality is what those bodies are being asked to certify.

3. Finally, the worry that the concept is doing coalitional work rather than descriptive work. Say plainly whether the Polish case supports that worry or refutes it, and whether the concept can survive the observation that its application is contested by the actors it purports to describe.

No citations and no reading list; argue it. Close by telling me what would change your verdict — which argument or consideration would move you to a different answer — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
