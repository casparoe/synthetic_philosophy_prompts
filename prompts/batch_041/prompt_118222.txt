A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether it means anything to say that a large language model believes something, and the dispute ended not in disagreement about any particular fact but about what would count as settling the question. Her position was that once a system's outputs can be reliably predicted by treating it as a creature with beliefs and desires, there is nothing further to ask: the vocabulary of belief earns its keep by working, and on these systems it works remarkably well. I was unconvinced but could not say precisely what I thought was missing, and I should like to work it out on paper.

Write an essay on this controversy, pitched at a reader who already knows the terrain and does not need the basic positions introduced from scratch. It should do three things. First, set out the strongest case for taking the intentional stance towards such systems — not a caricature of it, but the version its ablest defenders would recognise as their own, including their reasons for thinking that the demand for some further fact, over and above the predictive success, is confused. Second, press two considerations that I believe the standard debate handles too quickly. The first is a condition on belief-attribution that I have not seen stated in quite this form, and which I should like you to assess: call it the revision condition. A state deserves to be called a belief only if it is disposed to be revised in the light of what the system itself treats as evidence, and is not merely a function of what the interlocutor appears to want to hear; a "belief" that flips under social pressure while every evidential consideration is held fixed is a performance rather than a belief, and the attribution of it was never more than a convenient fiction. Consider how far the observed behaviour of these systems satisfies or violates that condition, and whether any violation tells against the attribution or merely against the systems. The second is the thought that the pattern the stance latches onto may belong to the training corpus rather than to the model — that the model is a mirror in which patterns of human belief are reflected, so that to speak of its beliefs is to mislocate a pattern that was never its own. Then confront the obvious reply, that the same is true of human beings, whose convictions are likewise inherited; if the objection proves too much, say precisely where it stops proving anything, and whether that line can be drawn without arbitrariness. Third, reach a verdict: say what we are entitled to say about the inner life of such a system, what we are not, and which vocabulary you would adopt — belief, simulation, pretence, or something else — defending that choice against the strongest objection you can raise to it. I would far rather have a definite conclusion, even a deflationary one, than a survey that ends by declaring the question ill-posed; if you do think it ill-posed, you must show why the two considerations above do not merely beg it.

Write in British English. Argue from the ideas themselves: I do not want an inventory of who has said what, and there is no need to name works or supply references — where a position is worth engaging, state it and answer it.
