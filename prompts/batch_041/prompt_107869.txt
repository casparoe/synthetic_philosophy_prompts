I'm a TA for a second-year course that's half philosophy of biology and half philosophy of social science, and I'm sitting on a stack of short written answers on the gender-equality paradox. The question I set was: "In several cross-national studies, the gap between men and women in the share of STEM degrees (and, in some work, in STEM jobs or in expressed interest in science) is as large, or larger, in countries that score high on indices of gender equality than in countries that score low. Does this finding tell us anything about (a) how much of the gap is due to nature versus culture, and (b) what norms or policies around gender and work should look like? Defend a view." The reading packet gave them the standard scaffolding: that these indices are built out of outcomes like pay, employment and parliamentary seats rather than out of people's attitudes; that the finding is about relative shares rather than the raw number of women in science; and the "resource" idea that where a STEM career isn't the only route to a decent income, people sort more by interest. So they had what they needed to say something careful. Here's one answer I keep going back and fourth on, and I'd rather get a second pair of eyes than trust my gut:

"The finding only looks paradoxical if you assume that gendered job choices are produced by culture. If the most egalitarian countries have the biggest gaps, then culture can't be doing much of the work, so the gap must be driven mainly by biological differences between men and women — most likely in interests and temperament, which is why it shows up in what people choose to study rather than in what they're allowed to study. It fits that the gap is about relative shares: in poor countries women go into engineering because it pays, but in rich countries they're free to follow their preferences, and on average those preferences turn out to be different. The upshot for policy is that pushing for 50/50 representation in STEM is misguided, since it means overriding women's own choices, and a just society should let people sort themselves out. The one thing the reading left me unsure about is whether the index measures the right thing, since it's built out of outcomes like wages and seats in parliament rather than attitudes, and a country could score high on those while still holding pretty traditional ideas about who should do what. So the paradox may say more about what rich countries measure than about human nature."

Can you give me a letter grade for this, plus the specific comments I should write on the page — what's right, what's wrong, and what's quietly doing the work in the argument without having been earned? I don't want a general impression; I want it point by point and tied to particular sentences, including anything I might let slide because the answer sounds confident. The empirical literature here is genuinely contested, so please also flag which of the student's claims are settled, which are live disputes among people who work on this, and which are the student's own inferences rather than anything the reading supports — and don't dress up a contested reading as the consensus. Lead with the grade and the single biggest reason for it in your first sentence, then the rest; no preamble, and don't restate my question.
