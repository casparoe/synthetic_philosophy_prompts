I'm a novelist — third book, the one that's supposed to be the good one — and I need you to do a small job for me. Here's the setup, so you know what the joke has to do. My protaganist is a guy in his late fifties who spent his whole career in currency authentication (he worked for a firm that did anti-counterfeiting work — the EURion constellation, those little rings you can see on a banknote if you squint, that sort of thing) and now, laid off, he's taken a gig at a startup that sells "companions": chatbots trained on dead people. Grief sims. He's the guy who's supposed to certify whether a companion is a faithful reproduction of the client's mother or whether it's drifted into being a generic chatbot with the mother's name stapled on. He hates the job and he's funny about it in a bitter way. Think of yourself as a stand-up writer who happens to have read a lot of philosophy of mind.

So: write me a joke for him to tell. It has to land for readers who know Dennett's essay about counterfeit people — the one where he argues that AI-generated fakes who pass as people are like counterfeit money, that the real damage isn't to the person who gets fooled but to the trust the whole currency runs on, that they might be the most dangerous artifacts in human history, and that we should outlaw making them and passing them along. My guy has read it and is half convinced and half furious, because he spent thirty years at a job that assumed you can always tell a bill from a copy, and Dennett's line seems to be that with people the difference comes down to provenance and trust rather than any inner spark — which is exactly what he can't stand. He's also noticed something I'd like the joke to make use of if you can: Dennett is worried about counterfeits, things that pass as a person when there was never a person behind them, but my startup is making forgeries — sims of one particular dead woman, which in his book is a different offense, or maybe a worse one. And the other thing he keeps muttering is that the intentional stance, the habit of treating anything that talks back as a person, is easy to slip into and almost impossible to resist, even for experts — and he is an expert, he authenticates things for a living, and he still can't do it.

Before you write anything, brainstorm out loud for me — give me five or six candidate angles (the assay office, the watermark, the grief sim that's better company than the original, the counterfeit that's honest about being one, whatever you've got) and say which one has the best twist and why. Then write the joke: three or four sentences of setup and a punchline, in the voice of a guy who's had two drinks and is talking to a coworker at the office holiday party. Nothing that needs a footnote, and nothing that's just "AI bad, am I right" — the turn has to come from the actual content of Dennett's view. Then, since my editor thinks jokes about philsophy are a war crime and will ask, one short paragraph on why the punchline works, and one on whether there's a version of the joke that would still be funny to someone who thinks Dennett is wrong about all of it.
