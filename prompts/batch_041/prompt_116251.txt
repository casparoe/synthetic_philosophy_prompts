I retired two years ago after the better part of three decades in atmospheric chemistry — reaction kinetics first, then a long stretch building chemistry-transport models — and since then I've been sitting in on a philosphy of science reading group at the local university, which has turned out to be a much more humbling experience than I expected. They've asked me to write down where I stand on models and their assumptions so the rest of them can have a go at me, and I'd rather not walk in there with a set of views that don't actually hang together. So here's the list, roughly as I'd write it out for them. (1) Models are instruments. An assumption doesn't have to be true, it has to earn its keep: if the thing predicts what I care about, the assumption has done its job and nobody should lose sleep over it being false — I gather this is more or less what Friedman was after with that F-twist business, though the group insists I'm being too quick about it. (2) All the same, I think there's a real difference between a harmless idealisation and a distortion, and that you can usually tell which one you're looking at. Treating a gas as ideal or a planet as a point mass never bothered me for a second; but assuming a reactor or an airshed is perfectly well mixed when the entire point of the exercise is that it isn't — that isn't simplifying, that's changing the answer. (3) I'm a realist about the unobservables, and my reason is the old one: the only decent explanation of why these theories predict as well as they do is that they're at least roughly true, invisible molecules and short-lived radicals included. (4) I think the real test of a model is whether it has the causal wiring right, not whether it fits. I've watched too many people fit a curve beautifully while being completely wrong about what was doing what to what. (5) And where two rival models fit everything we can measure, I still think there's a fact of the matter about which one is closer to the truth, even though both are, strictly speaking, false.

Anyways — what I want isn't a lecture, it's an audit. I have a feeling some of this is pulling against itself but I honestly can't see where the seam is, and I'm not attached to any one of these over the others, so tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how bad each one is, worst first, and for each say what would actually have to give and what it would cost me to give it. Then tell me which of the pairs that look contradictory on a first pass really aren't, and why, because I suspect a couple of them only look bad to me because I'm still learning the vocabulary. And if there's one small revision that would clean up more than one of the real problems at once, say which one it is and what I'd be buying with it.
