I've spent the better part of a decade as a reader of the rationalist blogs and their spinoff forums — the decision-theory threads, the long ontology arguments that run to three in the morning — and I've lately drifted from that milieu into academic metaphysics, which is how I've arrived at the following predicament. Next month I'm presenting a paper to a small metaphysics reading group, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense. I've watched too many otherwise good sessions die in the question period, and I'd rather walk in knowing exactly which of my moves are load-bearing and which are decorative.

The thesis I'm defending is a degreed answer to the special composition question. I don't think composition is all-or-nothing, and I don't think it's a primitive fact that some things do and others don't compose a further thing. Roughly: some things compose a further thing to the extent that their joint causal profile is invariant under counterfactual perturbation of any one of them. I call this perturbation-invariance, and I claim it comes in degrees, so that questions of the form how many things are there here? have a determinate answer only once you fix a threshold, and the threshold is set by explanatory interest rather than discovered in the world. I'm pitching this as a repaired, degreed descendant of the familiar roster of candidate answers to the composition question — contact, fastening, cohesion, and the organicist answer that things compose a whole just when they are caught up in a single life — on the theory that the cohesion answer was never as dead as it was made out to be, only under-specified, and that degrees are what it always needed. My sketch of the argument runs: take any candidate composite, subject it to hypothetical interventions on its proper parts, and read off how much of the group's downstream causal behavior is preserved; a carbon atom scores high, a pile of sand scores low, and things like a refrigerator and a jellyfish score wherever my measure happens to put them, which I am aware is the sentence I most need to survive.

The audience is about ten people in a department reading group, and I need the objections in their vocabulary, not the ones I'd get from my online friends. Specifically: two committed van Inwagen-style organicists who hold that the only genuine composites are living organisms and that composition is strictly all-or-nothing, and who will say I have merely re-described the cohesion answer they already rejected and then hidden the failure behind a continuous parameter; one mereological nihilist who thinks the only defensible answer is never, and who will press that my degrees are a sentimental way of saying never while pretending otherwise; one universalist who thinks composition always occurs and that my thresholds are gerrymandered to land where common sense wants them; a philosopher of biology who cares about whether organisms really are the paradigmatic composites and who will ask why on my measure a well-built refrigerator outranks a jellyfish; a physicist who will want to know what the perturbed quantities actually are, given that the joint causal profile is doing an enormous amount of unanalyzed work and that perturbation talk in quantum contexts may not behave the way my metaphor assumes; and two quiet deflationists who suspect the whole question is verbal and will ask, simply, what turns on the answer and what I would give up if I got it wrong. Give me the objections in the order this particular room will actually raise them, each phrased the way the person who raises it would phrase it, each with a reply I can deliver in two minutes and a blunt note on where that reply is softest; tell me what to concede up front so I don't spend credibility defending the indefensible; tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session; and include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. End with a single final line in the form Verdict: <one sentence on whether the position, as stated, survives this room>.
