ok so I've never studied philosophy properly but I've been reading around on my own for a couple of years and I've ended up with a pile of views about what makes a life go well that I'm worried dont actually hang together. I'd like you to help me find the cracks.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. I think a life going well is just about how it feels from the inside. If your content and satisfied with your life, then it's going well — there's no external scorecard for a life, no checklist of things you have to have ticked off. You're the one who's the authority on whether your own life is good.

2. That said, I would never plug into the experience machine (the thing where you float in a tank and get a completely realistic simulated life). And I dont just mean I wouldn't do it myself — I think something is genuinely lost there. The person in the tank is worse off than they'd be living a real, messier life with more pain in it, and I think it's because a life with no real risk of loss isnt really a life.

3. But I also bristle when people tell other people whats good for them. If someone honestly wants the tank life and gets exactly the life they want, I dont see what gives me the right to call their life bad. Its their life.

4. Still, I dont think getting what you want is the whole story. A heroin addict who wants heroin and gets it isnt doing well. So wanting something cant be the final word on whether its good for you.

5. I also think you can do the right thing and be worse off for it — like a whistleblower who torches their career and their friendships to expose something. I think that's the right thing to do and I think it wrecks their life. Morality and well-being seem to pull apart.

6. And I think well-being is the only thing that ultimately matters — that right and wrong are, at rock bottom, entirely about how much good life there is in the world.

My worry is that some of these are quietly fighting each other and I cant see which ones. Some might only look like they conflict. Can you go through the set and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the real tensions by how bad they are? For each real one, give me the cheapest fix — the smallest thing I'd have to give up or restate — and what that fix costs me. Also tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong here, or what would make you come down differently on one of these pairings.

Please write it in British English. Finish with a last line that's just \answer{...} holding the single revision you'd make first, if I could only make one.
