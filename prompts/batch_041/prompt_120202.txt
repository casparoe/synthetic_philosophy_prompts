I'm a novelist — third book, first two were quiet domestic things, this one has a strand that keeps turning into a philosophy seminar and I can't get it to sit down. Two of my characters, Nadia and Theo, are stuck overnight on a ferry (long story, there's a dog, the bar's closed and its raining). Nadia runs a small foundation that funds work on the very long term; she's read all the expected-value stuff and she unembarrassedly believes that because the future could hold an astronomically large number of lives, she should be willing to stake almost anything on tiny chances of making that future go well, and her whole life is arranged around it. Theo is a cosmologist — not a scoffer, he takes her seriously — and he's come around to thinking the simulation argument is a real dampener on the whole project. His line is roughly: if ancestor simulations are cheap, sims vastly outnumber the one real world, so odds are we're inside one; and sims get switched off, or run short, or run on somebody else's budget, so the billion-year durable future Nadia is protecting may be a stage set, and you can't be a fanatic about a future that could be pulled out from under you. Nadia thinks he's got it exactly upside down. I genuinely don't know which of them I believe, which is probably why the scene keeps dying.

Write me the scene as a dialogue. Real argument, not a showcase — they should answer each other's actual last point, interrupt, and each give ground somewhere without turning into the other. I'd like Nadia to get her best shots in, especially: that if we probably *are* simulated then somebody out there evidently made it through the bottleneck and built all this, which sounds more like evidence the far future is achievable than a reason to doubt it; and that an off-switch is just one more improbable disaster among many, and fanaticism is precisely the view that says you keep going anyway, so added uncertainty is fuel rather than water. Theo needs answers to those — maybe something along the lines of "expected value is an average over the worlds you might be in, and you don't get to add a branch without subtracting something," or "the sim hypothesis doesn't shrink the value of the future, it shrinks your leverage over it, and leverage is the thing your fanaticism quietly assumes" — but only if he'd really say it. No strawmen, and please don't let him off with "well, we can't know." Keep it around 1,200 words, no citations, no lecture-dump of jargon; let them talk like two tired smart people on a boat who love each other a bit, and don't resolve it.
