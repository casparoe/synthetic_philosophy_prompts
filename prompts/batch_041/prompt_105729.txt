Question:

Explain the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. (i) Say what the problem is and why it's supposed to be a problem for a view on which moral sentences express attitudes rather than beliefs. (ii) Distinguish the debt of giving a compositional account of unasserted moral clauses from what's often called the negation problem — the demand to explain why a moral sentence and its negation come out inconsistent, rather than merely being two different attitudes. (iii) Assess whether a quasi-realist appeal to minimal truth plus a deflationary account of validity discharges both debts. Illustrate with a moral modus ponens of your own construction.

Answer:

The Frege–Geach problem starts from the point that moral sentences turn up inside larger sentences without being asserted. If I say 'if breaking a promise is wrong, then getting your friend to break one is wrong', I'm not asserting that breaking promises is wrong; the moral sentence is doing work as part of a conditional. The expressivist holds that in saying 'breaking promises is wrong' I express an attitude of disapproval rather than state a fact. So what is 'wrong' doing in the antecedent? On the expressivist story there's no disapproval being expressed there, since I'm only supposing. But the word has to mean the same in both places, otherwise the argument — breaking promises is wrong; if breaking promises is wrong then getting your friend to break one is wrong; so getting your friend to break one is wrong — would equivocate, and it plainly doesn't. So the expressivist owes a uniform account of what 'wrong' contributes in asserted and unasserted positions.

Hare's phrastic/neustic distinction was designed for exactly this: it separates the content of the judgement from the force with which it's put forward, so the antecedent can carry the content with the force stripped off. And since the antecedent isn't evaluative anyway — it's a supposition, not a judgement — the expressivist's account of asserted moral judgements doesn't have to be stretched to cover it. The negation problem is really the same problem with 'not' in place of 'if', so it doesn't call for any separate treatment.

The most promising line is Blackburn's quasi-realism. If we take minimalism about truth seriously — calling a sentence true is just a way of endorsing it, not ascribing a property — then moral sentences are truth-apt, and validity can be defined in the usual way as preservation of truth (or of acceptance). Moral modus ponens then comes out valid, and the Frege–Geach problem is dissolved rather than dodged: no special moral logic is needed, because once truth is cheap enough the moral case is just the ordinary case.
