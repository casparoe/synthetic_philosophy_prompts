ok, so I keep falling down the Boltzmann brain rabbit hole and I want to try something. Suppose you could teleport Hume (the Scottish empiricist, 1711-1776) into a seminar and explain the whole setup to him: that in a universe that's mostly empty thermal equilibrium, a random fluctuation could assemble a lone disembodied mind with whatever memories and perceptions you like, and that on some cosmological measures these "Boltzmann brains" should vastly outnumber ordinary evolved observers like us. Two things I want to know.

First: is this a result Hume would welcome or resist? On the one hand his whole story is that the self is just a bundle of perceptions with no underlying owner, and he's famously sceptical about induction and causation, so a mind that just pops into being with fake memories seems tailor-made for him. On the other hand maybe he'd say the idea is incoherent, or that a fluctuation-mind can't properly be said to reason at all. Which way does it cut, and why?

Second, the bit that actually keeps me up: if Boltzmann brains outnumber us, then our own evidence about cosmology is probably being generated by a fluctuation, which seems to saw off the branch its sitting on. Is there a Humean move here, or is this just his kind of scepticism pushed to the limit? And would he care whether the brain lasts a fraction of a second versus years, or whether it's a brain that itself hallucinates an entire universe full of other Boltzmann brains? I guess I'm asking whether the duration or the nesting changes anything for him, or whether he'd just shrug and say it's all bundles either way.
