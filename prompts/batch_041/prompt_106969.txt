I'm a high school student, and I worked out the following argument for a philosophy club meeting. I now suspect it is either genuinely interesting or quietly broken, and I can't tell which. Here it is.

(1) Last week I cried at the end of a novel. In that moment I was genuinely sad; I was not merely pretending or humoring the story.

(2) A genuine emotion is always directed at something. Sadness is sadness about some particular thing; it is not a free-floating mood.

(3) My sadness was about the character and what happened to her.

(4) If I am genuinely sad about something, then I must believe that thing is real or has really happened.

(5) I do not believe the character is real or that her fate really happened. I know she was invented.

(6) By (3) and (4), if (3) is true then I must believe the character is real; by (5) I do not. So (3) is false.

(7) Therefore my sadness was not really about the character. The character was only the occasion; its true object was something about the human situation, or about my own life.

(8) Notice, though, that the same reasoning applies wherever I am moved by something I know is not present or not actual: the past as I picture it, the future as I dread it, distant strangers I will never meet, people who might have existed but never did.

(9) In none of those cases do I believe the object is real or present in the way (4) requires.

(10) So either (4) is false, or almost none of my emotions are about what I take them to be about.

(11) One more case makes this worse. I have felt real grief over a character in a story I was writing myself. There the occasion mentioned in (7) is not available -- no author, no audience, nothing outside my own invention -- and yet the feeling was as strong and as stubborn as any I have had about a real person, and I could not switch it off by reminding myself that I had made her up.

I would most like to keep (1) and (3): the sadness was genuine, and it was about her. But (4) looks airtight to me -- how can you be sad about something you believe never happened? Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. If I give up (4), what stops me from saying that any emotion is about anything I choose? If I keep (4), what becomes of the emotions in (8) and (11) -- do I have to admit that I am never really afraid of my own future, or moved by real suffering I have only read about? I would like the diagnosis and the price, and I want to know whether the real action is at (4) or somewhere I haven't thought to look.
