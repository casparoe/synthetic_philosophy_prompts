A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether evolutionary psychology is a science or a machine for generating just-so stories. His position was that you can't test claims about the Pleistocene; mine was that the Pleistocene is not where the tests live; we ended up disagreeing about what "testable" even means, and we did not settle it. This morning I have a stack of short answers on exactly that question, from a second-year section, and I would like a careful grade and detailed comments on the one below.

The students were given this question:

"Adaptationist hypotheses in psychology are often dismissed as 'just-so stories.' Using the three criteria discussed in class, assess the following proposal: that human beings possess a specialized mechanism which detects being imitated by others, produces a distinctive discomfort, and disposes the person to differentiate themselves from the imitator, because imitation was a cue to a threat to one's distinctiveness within a small ancestral group. Is this hypothesis a just-so story, or is it testable? State what would count as evidence for and against it, and give the strongest version of the just-so-story objection."

The three criteria they were required to use were: (1) special design — does the hypothesis specify a mechanism whose features are not what a general-purpose alternative would produce; (2) functional fit — does the mechanism's output solve the problem it is supposed to have been selected for, under the conditions in which it would have been selected; (3) discrimination — does the hypothesis generate predictions that differ from those of at least one serious rival, whether a byproduct, an exaptation, or a domain-general learning account.

The answer:

"The charge that adaptationist hypotheses are just-so stories is that they explain any observation after the fact: if imitation produces discomfort, that shows the mechanism working; if it produces pleasure, that shows the mechanism being overridden or that imitation signals affiliation. A hypothesis that can accommodate every outcome has no predictive content. But the charge is properly aimed at individual hypotheses, not at a whole research programme; a programme can be legitimate even if many of its particular hypotheses are bad.

Testing the imitation hypothesis is hard because it concerns a mechanism that was selected in the Pleistocene, and we cannot observe the environment of evolutionary adaptedness. Strictly speaking, then, the hypothesis is unfalsifiable. What we can do is check whether the pattern is universal: if discomfort at being imitated turns up in every human society, that is what an adaptation predicts, and the hypothesis is supported.

The hypothesis can be made more testable by specifying the mechanism's design. A specialized mechanism should be triggered by imitation in particular and not by other kinds of copying, should be present in young children before they have had much opportunity to learn the response, and should have analogues in other species that face the same problem. If all of this held, the hypothesis would be confirmed.

The spandrel objection shows that most psychological traits are byproducts of adaptations rather than adaptations themselves, so the just-so-story charge does not merely target bad hypotheses; it applies to the whole programme, which is why evolutionary psychology has remained controversial."

Give the answer a grade out of 20, with the points broken down by component, followed by line-by-line comments. I want to know which claims are correct, which are wrong, and which are defensible but underargued, and how much each of the errors should cost at this level. Make the comments concrete enough that I can hand them back to the student.
