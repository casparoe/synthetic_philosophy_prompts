I teach a 90-minute session on the infinite for about 14 undergrads — its a mixed room, half math majors, half who've taken one philosophy of religion course and nothing else formal. So I can't assume set theory and I can't assume they'll be impressed by notation either. What I want is an in-class activity where they walk into Hilbert's hotel as a real problem before anyone has used the word paradox, and then have to get themselves out of it, so that the moves which feel like cheating on the page (renumbering everyone, insisting the hotel was never full in the relevant sense) turn out to be the only moves available, and they can see exactly what each one costs them.

I want the sequencing to be doing the teaching, so build it around three beats:

First, the front desk. They should be clerks who have to accommodate new arrivals in a hotel where every numbered room already has a guest, and the reassignment has to be given as a rule rather than as a list of infinitely many individual moves (otherwise the whole thing collapses into "you can't write that many cards" and the physics of the exercise eats the philosophy). Escalate: one new guest, then five, then a coachload that is itself countable. Each of them writes their own rule on a card and posts it, and then the group audits each other's rules. I want the moment where a rule they wrote themselves looks illegal and then turns out to be fine.

Second, an inspector. Give me a role (mine, or a student's) whose only job is to keep asking the question the setup can't answer — which room is empty? — and then, when they say none, to ask why we called it full when we arrived. I want them to find out for themselves that "full" is doing two jobs and that the pigeonhole principle is what has quietly stopped applying, rather than being told that.

Third, a bus that can't be checked in, and I want them to build the obstruction themselves rather than recognise it. If any of them already knows the diagonal argument I'd rather they be forced to reconstruct it out of their own failed ledger than recite it. So design the third beat so that the thing that beats them is produced from the assignment they just posted, and so that patching it (just add one more room, just add that guest too) visibly fails again in the same way. No lecture up front, and don't let the names Hilbert or Cantor into the room until the debrief.

Then give me: materials, rules, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the actual moving parts — the finite-only status of the pigeonhole principle, Dedekind's decision to define infinite by part-equals-whole instead of treating it as a discovery, the split between two senses of "same size" (cardinality versus something like natural density, since half of them will insist the evens are obviously half the numbers and I want that intuition given its due before it gets separated off), and the potential-infinite / finitist objection, which I want left as a live option rather than a punchline.

Three things I specifically need from you. One, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it in the room — I already expect someone to argue the whole thing fails because you can't write infinitely many cards, someone to try to put a guest in a last room, and someone to try to rescue the third beat by adding one more room and be genuinely puzzled when it doesn't work. Two, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: I suspect it can't make actualism versus potentialism feel like a real fork, because the setup already treats the collection as completed, and it will make it look as though any two infinite collections can be matched up when some claims of that shape need a choice principle the concrete cases don't — so tell me where in their own work the illicit generalisation happens and how I should hand off to the objection instead of pretending the game settled it. Three, don't make it a competition with winners; I want them stuck together, not racing.
