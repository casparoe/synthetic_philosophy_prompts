I'm a third-year undergraduate writing a term paper for a course on time, tense, and truth, and I've decided to work from my own judgments about statements concerning what has not yet happened rather than from any of the positions we've been assigned. I'd like you to tell me what principle — or small set of principles — fits those judgments, and which of them I ought to give up. My working thought, which I expect to need repair, is that a statement about the future is true just in case the world turns out the way it says, so that its truth is fixed at the time of utterance by what subsequently happens; I can't tell whether that leaves the future already settled or merely makes truth hostage to it.

Here is what I find myself thinking.

1. A forecaster says on Monday, "It will rain here on Tuesday," and it rains on Tuesday. I take that utterance to have been true on Monday — true when he made it, not merely vindicated later, and not something that became true only once the rain began.

2. Same forecaster, same sentence, no rain on Tuesday. I take the utterance to have been false on Monday, not neither-true-nor-false and then false.

3. What I resist is the step people take from 1 and 2. If the Monday utterance was true, and truth is not the sort of thing a claim acquires or sheds with the calendar, it looks as though it was already true in 1900 that it would rain here on that Tuesday. That I reject: I don't think there was any fact about that Tuesday's weather in 1900 for the claim to match. But I also don't want truth to switch on at the moment of utterance, as though the world acquired a past-tensed shadow of my claim only when someone spoke. I can't comfortably hold both.

4. A columnist predicts that the bill will pass; it fails; the paper runs a correction. I think the correction is owed, and that "there was no fact of the matter when he wrote it" would be a dodge rather than an excuse: he told his readers to expect something that did not happen.

5. Suppose instead a prediction that a particular unstable atom will decay within the hour, in a case where nothing whatever fixes in advance whether it does. I am much less willing to call that prediction true or false when it was made — it feels as though the world had not yet written the answer — but once the hour has passed I want to say it was, after all, right or wrong.

6. I don't think a true prediction about what I will do makes my action unfree or unavoidable. If someone says I will order the fish and I do, I still think I could have ordered the steak; being right about what I do is not the same as making me do it.

7. A bet on the future is settled by what happens. If I bet you that it will rain and it doesn't, I pay, and I pay because it didn't rain, not because of any verdict about whether my prediction was true when I made it. Told that there was no fact of the matter when I spoke, I would say: fine, I still owe you the money.

8. Before a coin lands I want to say both that it might land heads and that it might land tails, and I take that to report my ignorance rather than any openness in the world. Yet when I say that tomorrow's weather is unsettled I mean something about the weather and not only about me, and I couldn't say what that something is.

9. A pollster predicts the bill will fail; wavering members switch sides; it fails. I think his prediction was true when he made it and also helped bring about its own truth, and I don't find those two things in tension.

10. "There will be a battle tomorrow," said today, and "There was a battle yesterday," said the day after. I want to say these say the same thing about the same battle — believing one commits me to the other — but also that the first concerns what hasn't happened and the second what has, and those cannot be the same thing to believe.

Is there an account of the truth of statements about the future, together with an account of when it is right to assert one and when to withdraw it, that delivers these judgments or nearly all of them? If not, which should I abandon, and what does the surviving account imply for two cases I have not yet put to myself: a promise about what I will do, made in good faith and then broken, and a prediction later discovered to have been impossible from the start, the coin having been double-headed all along? Please also give me one further case of your own devising that separates the leading candidates. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I need the principle stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is bought with which judgment, and an honest note on the judgments it cannot save.
