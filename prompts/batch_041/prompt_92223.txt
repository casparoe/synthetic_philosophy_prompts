The dispute I have in mind is the absent-minded driver problem. It is a fixture of decision-theoretic discussion in the rationalist blogosphere, and every few months a thread re-litigates it; I have never once seen anyone come away having changed their mind. The setup, as I understand it: a driver is on a highway with two exits. The first exit gets him home by a roundabout route; the second gets him home directly; if he drives past both he is ruined. The catch is that he cannot tell the exits apart — his memory of the first is wiped before he reaches the second — so he faces the same decision problem twice with the same information, and whatever policy he adopts is applied at both. Exiting is safe, driving past both is a disaster, and continuing with some probability strictly between zero and one gives him a real chance at the good outcome. So the only way to get the good outcome with positive probability is to randomize.

Here are the camps as I have seen them stated, and I would like to know whether my summary is even fair to them:

1. The policy camp. The unit of rational choice is the policy, not the act. You evaluate a policy from the standpoint of an agent who will occupy the nodes that policy visits, and you pick the one with the greatest expected payoff, which here is a mixed policy. On this view, randomizing is not hedging against the world but against one's own future behavior, and there is nothing mysterious about it.

2. The act camp. Rational choice is the choice of an act at a node. Were the driver able to distinguish the exits, he would continue at the first and exit at the second; the fact that the optimal policy instructs him to do at each node something he would not endorse at either is taken to be a reductio, or at least a sign that the puzzle is a modeling artifact. The obvious repair — let him act on his credence about which node he is at — strikes me as having already smuggled in policy-level reasoning, but I may be missing a version that does not.

3. The ill-posed camp. The puzzle is an artifact of individuating the driver's two decision points as two nodes while demanding a single policy; it is really a question about how to count agents, or stages of agents, across time, and the amnesia is self-locating uncertainty in costume.

My hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that this is not a disagreement within decision theory at all, and that the parties are separated by two questions that look decision-theoretic but are not. The first is whether "randomizing" is an act the agent performs or a property of a mixed strategy in a game against nature; the second is whether the driver at the first exit and the driver at the second are one agent or two. If the second is doing the work, the impasse is the Sleeping Beauty impasse in different clothes, since the credence the policy camp assigns to "I am at the first exit" is precisely the self-locating credence that halfers and thirders fight over. If the first is doing the work, then the dispute is at bottom about what counts as an act, which decision theory does not settle by itself.
