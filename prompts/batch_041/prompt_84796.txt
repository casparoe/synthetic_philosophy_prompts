ok so I need a satirical piece for a dataset, and I want it genuinely in the weeds rather than a generic lol-bureaucrats thing.

The setting: a mid-sized city has a Department of Civic Experimentation (invent it, give it a plausible municipal-sounding name). It has just finished a three-year randomised controlled trial of Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry. The design is the joke and it needs to be exact. The control neighbourhood gets ordinary municipal governance, which the memo refers to throughout as the Great Society condition: people densely interacting, lots of communication infrastructure, but no public ever actually forming. The treatment neighbourhood gets the Great Community condition: residents furnished with the conditions for continuous shared inquiry into shared problems, with ends held as hypotheses and revised in light of consequences. The memo reports the treatment arm as the clear winner.

What I want: the internal evaluation memo itself, about 800 words, in flat program-evaluation-office voice the whole way through. No winking narrator, no wry asides, no knowing final line. The comedy has to come from the office having absorbed Dewey's vocabulary perfectly and his point not at all.

Things it should include, worked into normal memo sections:

- an executive summary reporting the pilot as an unqualified success and recommending scale-up
- a primary endpoint measured as growth in some Deweyan-sounding quantity (the office's own coinage for it, don't attribute it to anyone; something in the register of ordered richness, or make up your own)
- a statistics section where the office reports warranted-assertibility intervals rather than confidence intervals and declines to report p-values, on the grounds that inquiry terminates in warranted assertibility rather than in truth. The office presents this as a methodological upgrade. Don't let it be the punchline.
- a section on public formation: at several sites no public ever formed, and the office recommends counting these as successes under intention-to-treat, noting that non-formation may indicate a slow-onset public requiring further funding
- adverse events: treatment-arm residents developed an expectation of being consulted about subsequent decisions, logged as a compliance risk for the next intervention; control-arm disengagement logged as baseline civic entropy
- an ethics section where IRB review was waived because the intervention is, in the office's phrasing, just democracy, i.e. something everyone already endorses
- a section where the office tries to separate the means of the trial from its ends, finds it can't, and ends up unable to say what the trial was measuring — then files that under findings rather than limitations
- the recommendation: roll out citywide, retain the control neighbourhood as a comparison group indefinitely, do not inform them, on the grounds that ending the trial would itself require a decision by a public that hasn't been convened

Two things to be careful about. First, every move the office makes has to be a real feature of Dewey's position pushed one step, not an invented flaw: he really does want social inquiry to be experimental, he really does treat ends as hypotheses, he really does think the public is currently eclipsed and that communication is the condition of community — so the satire is the office taking him seriously. Second, the residents are never the butt of the joke and are never stupid; the office is. And don't have anyone state the objections (who gets to design the conditions, who consents to being in the comparison arm, whether the experiment has quietly decided that citizen judgment is the dependent variable, whether treating democracy as a means to a measured end is the one thing Dewey would refuse). Just let them fall out of the memo's own procedural language.

A reader who's never heard of Dewey should still enjoy it as a joke about program evaluation; a Dewey person should laugh and not wince. No citations, no quotes from anything, no real institutions or real officials, and no one explaining Dewey to the reader.
