Ok, bit of context first. I did bench chemistry for 34 years and retired a couple of years ago, and since then I've been sitting in on the philosophy departments open seminars like the old guy in the back row. I've written down the things I'd actually defend about knowledge and truth, and I want an audit before I say any of it out loud. Here's the list.

1. There are facts about the world that don't depend on us. Whether a particular band of sediment was laid down in a flood or a drought is a fact, and it was a fact before anybody looked at it.

2. No fact is in principle out of reach of inquiry. For any well-formed factual question there's some possible inquirer who could come to know the answer. And I mean a real possible inquirer: made of matter, obeying our physics, finite. Not a god, not an infinite mind, not some ideal community at the end of inquiry if that's just a fiction. Also, when I say known I mean somebody actually knows it, not that it's derivable in principle from things that are known.

3. But I'm not saying everything gets known. Some truths never have been and never will be known by anyone. Say there was a particular conversation in a village in 1600 — no witness, no record, nothing. There's a fact about exactly what was said. Nobody will ever know it. That's a gap in the knowing, not a gap in the world.

4. Knowledge of the world has to come from somewhere. If you know a fact about the world, there has to be some trace of it that a finite inquirer could actually follow back — an effect, a measurement, a record. Not just "everything is in the causal ancestry of everything else," which is cheap and true and useless.

5. Truth and our procedures for getting at it are seperate things. A hypothesis is true when it fits the world, not when it's the one everybody would settle on at the end of inquiry, and not when it's useful or coherent or whatever. I liked the spirit of the old positivists — nothing is a claim about the world if nobody could ever check it — but I never bought that checkability was what true meant.

6. Knowledge can be lost. A burned library is a real loss of knowledge, not just a loss of paper.

I'd have thought 2 and 3 were obviously compatible, one being about what could be known and the other about what happens to be known. But 4 keeps nagging at me: if knowing requires a recoverable trace, and some facts leave no trace, how is every fact knowable? So is 2 against 4 the real problem, or am I missing something worse? Go through the list and tell me which pairs are definately in tension, which only look like they are, and rank the real ones by how bad they are. For each real one, say what has to go and what that costs me, and whether there's one item that's doing most of the damage.
