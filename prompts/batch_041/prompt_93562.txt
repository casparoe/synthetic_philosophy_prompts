A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a sentence I have heard my whole life without ever examining: that no two things can be exactly alike. He was using it as a metaphysical hammer. His claim was that it is not merely improbable but impossible for two objects to agree in every respect, and that anyone who thinks he has described such a pair has in fact described one object twice. I offered what I took to be a simple counterexample — a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, the case I have seen called the two-spheres case — and he replied that the description refutes itself, since if the spheres differ in no respect there is nothing to make them two rather than one. We went in circles for two hours, and I came away suspecting that we were each defending a different principle while using the same eight words.

What I want from you is a menu rather than a verdict. Take that sentence and lay out the distinct, precise claims it could be making — beginning with the ones that are trivial, or true by construction, moving through the ones that are substantive and defensible, and ending with the ones that are simply false — and for each one tell me what it commits its holder to, how it would be argued for, and what would count as a counterexample. I would especially like the readings separated along these lines: whether the claim ranges over all properties or only over some restricted class of them (intrinsic, non-relational, qualitative, observable, or expressible in a given vocabulary); whether it concerns the objects themselves or only our ability to tell them apart and refer to them one at a time; whether the spheres' differing positions count as a difference in the spheres, as a difference in something else, or as no difference at all, and whether a case built on a symmetric arrangement can even be stated without smuggling in a distinction; and what becomes of each reading when the two things in question are not simultaneous duplicates but a perfect forgery and its original, or two items that are alike in every present respect but have different histories.

For each reading, tell me whether it is the one my friend needs in order to reach his conclusion, and whether some weaker reading would do just as well. Where two of the readings collapse into each other, say so rather than padding the list. And tell me which reading people usually mean when they say this in ordinary life, and whether that reading is true. Please write your answer in American English, and do not bother with citations or further reading — the distinctions themselves are what I am after.
