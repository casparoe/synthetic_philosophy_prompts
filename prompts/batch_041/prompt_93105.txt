Ok so I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on philosophy of psychiatry this fall (cross-listed with psych, so I get both the phil kids and the ones who want to be clinicians) and I have to write the final this week. Last two years I did the obvious thing and it's depressing: I ask about looping effects, they give me four paragraphs on how diagnoses are socially constructed and the categories change the people in them, everyone gets a B+. I want one essay question that actually sorts them.

Here's the thing I keep circling and I want the exam to make them argue about it. Hacking's story is that psychiatric categories are interactive kinds: the people classified can take up the label, and the label changes them, so the category is chasing a moving target. Fine, they all know that. But the ML people have a whole literature now on what happens when a model's predictions feed back into the distribution it's predicting: you deploy a risk score, people and institutions react to the score, and the thing the score was measuring stops meaning what it used to mean. My gut says these are not the same phenomenon, and that the difference matters for how much we should trust the categories. The specific wedge: Hacking's loop seems to need uptake, i.e. the classified party has to come to understand themselves through the label. The other kind of loop doesn't need anyone to understand anything about themselves, it just needs the incentives to move. So is the self-interpretation the load-bearing part, or is it a red herring and the whole thing is just Goodhart with extra steps? And whichever way they go, what does it do to the validity of, say, a suicide-risk model, or a diagnostic threshold that gates access to treatment?

So: one essay question, 60 minutes, closed book, 25 marks, no citations needed (they can name positions without footnotes). It has to be answerable by someone who did the reading and not by someone who memorised the reading, and it can't be answerable by writing social construction, discuss. Then a model answer, about 700 words, and a marking scheme: five must-hit points with marks attached summing to 25, and for each one a line on how a mediocre answer typically misses it. Also tell me what a top answer has to do that a merely competent one won't -- I want to seperate a 20 from a 24 without agonising over it.
