**Two Superrationalities, and a Case Where They Part Ways**

*§1.* In a one-shot symmetric prisoner's dilemma, ordinary game-theoretic reasoning says defect, since defection weakly dominates: it does at least as well against either action of the other player. Superrationality says something else. If both players know that they will reason to the same conclusion — that the situation is one problem solved twice rather than two problems solved separately — then the two apparent choices are really a single choice, and the relevant comparison is between the two diagonal outcomes rather than between cooperating and defecting for a fixed expectation about the other player. Mutual cooperation is better for each than mutual defection, so both cooperate. The correlation at work is not causal: my reasoning does not make the other player cooperate; the point is that the same reasoning, run twice, has the same output. Hofstadter's own formulation, as I have seen it quoted, is that superrational thinkers include in their calculations the fact that they are in a group of superrational thinkers.

*§2.* The reconstruction is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is invisible in the symmetric case. The slogan that one maximizes each player's payoff on the assumption that everyone uses the same strategy can be read in two ways. On the indexical reading, I am one of the players: I restrict myself to strategies such that my twin plays the same one, and then I maximize my own payoff. In the symmetric dilemma that gives me R for cooperating and P for defecting, with R > P, so I cooperate. On the class reading, the subject of the decision is the class of superrational players taken together: the thing to be maximized is the total or average payoff of the class, subject to everyone playing the same strategy. Cooperate again. Because the payoffs are symmetric, the two readings cannot be told apart.

*§3.* Here is a case where they separate. Two players run the same algorithm, but their payoff matrices differ. Write payoffs as (mine, hers). If both cooperate, (1, 10). If both defect, (5, 0). The off-diagonal outcomes are irrelevant, because the correlation premise rules them out. Indexically, I compare 1 with 5 and defect. Class-wise, I compare 11 with 5 and cooperate — and note that the class verdict means I cooperate against my own interest as scored, because I happen to be the low-payoff member of the pair. So the two readings return opposite answers, and the class reading instructs me to sacrifice on behalf of my twin.

*§4.* But the case seems to undermine itself, and I cannot tell whether that is a discovery or a confusion. The premise of superrationality is that we run the same algorithm. If the algorithm takes the local payoff matrix as an input, and our matrices differ, then the same algorithm need not produce the same action for both of us, and the correlation premise fails: we are no longer solving the same problem. So either the shared algorithm must be one that ignores the asymmetric part of its input — but then what it is really computing is a class-level optimum, which is the class reading rather than a constraint on a personal decision — or it does respond to the local matrix, and the twins have to be expected to reach the same output for some further reason that has to be built in rather than derived. It begins to look as though the indexical reading is not merely different from the class reading but unstable: it is well-defined only when the case is symmetric, and in the symmetric case it coincides with the class reading.

*§5.* If that is right, two consequences follow. (a) Superrationality is not a theory of individual practical rationality. It is a theory about what a class of identical agents should do, and the individual is asked to be impartial within her class. That is a miniature original position, with a class boundary that is suspiciously convenient: the class is defined as the agents who will do whatever I do, so the interests that get aggregated are exactly the interests of those who mirror me. Kantian universalizability has the same structure and has attracted the same complaint, namely that the description of the maxim fixes the class and the agent chooses the description. If that complaint is fatal there, is it fatal here? (b) The premise that she will reason as I do is a claim about the world — about what algorithm she is running — and not a claim about rationality. So superrationality is really a thesis about choice under correlation, and its natural home is the evidential side of the causal/evidential dispute, or the literature on type-causal reasoning, where what matters is the kind of agent you are because others can predict you from your type. Once that is said, cooperation seems to need a prior commitment story — you must be the sort of agent others can read off — and it is unclear whether the original argument did any work beyond motivating that commitment.

*§6.* What I am unsure about. Whether the asymmetric case in §3 is legitimate, or whether it simply falls outside superrationality's scope, in which case §4 is doing all the work and §3 should be cut. Whether the class-boundary objection has force or is only a restatement of the fact that superrationality is a conditional recommendation. Whether §5(b) is just the standard causal/evidential point with new labels. And whether the formal work on agents who submit programs to a mediator, in which cooperation in the one-shot dilemma becomes achievable, is a vindication of what I'm arguing or a different phenomenon altogether.
