Our reading group has now spent two years on a question we cannot settle, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of it. The question is how much evidential weight a report of one's own experience should carry.

Here is what the sides actually say. The first camp holds that introspective report, collected under the right conditions, is a serviceable instrument. Its members accept the choice-blindness results, in which subjects asked to justify a selection that the experimenter has secretly switched will defend the switched option fluently, but they read those results narrowly: what such studies show is that we are poor at identifying the causes of our choices and attitudes, not that we misdescribe what is presently before the mind. They add that think-aloud protocols, in which a subject narrates what they are attending to while performing a task, generate reports that predict performance and error patterns well enough to be used as data, and that the reconstruction objection therefore overreaches. The second camp reads the same results as evidence that introspective report is reconstruction all the way down, that its fluency and felt confidence carry no information about its accuracy, and that the first camp's preferred procedures beg the question, since the instruction to report on one's current experience already presupposes the faculty whose reliability is at issue; on this view, when trained observers sample particular moments of experience in a structured way and then agree with one another, the agreement is exactly what has been assumed rather than demonstrated. A third position, which I notice is spreading, treats the whole dispute as a distraction: the science of experience should proceed without reports at all, because a report is a behavioural event with its own cognitive and neural demands, a confound rather than a window, and progress lies in measures that do not route through what the subject says.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the first two camps are not contradicting each other. One is asking whether a report reliably indicates some occurrent state; the other is asking whether it reliably indicates the processes that produced a judgement; and once those are separated, what remains is a narrow empirical question about calibration, namely whether confidence in a report predicts its accuracy and whether any training or sampling procedure improves that prediction. I would like you to test this. Say whether the disagreement is verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading; identify any hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work; say where the third position changes the diagnosis, if it does; and say whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely that it is hard. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, an experiment that could actually be run (specify what would be measured and which outcome would favour which side), or an argument that none should be expected, and why. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, only for the diagnosis and the reasons for it.
