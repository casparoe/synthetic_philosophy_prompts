Write a sestina in the voice of a consequentialist who has just tried, one last time, to total up the consequences of a single small choice, and cannot get the sum to close. The difficulty is not the ordinary uncertainty of whether the choice will work, but the thought that its effects ramify without bound — through strangers, through second-order effects on institutions, into futures no one can survey — so that no amount of care yields a determinate verdict on whether the act was, on the whole, for the better. Let the six end-words carry that ramification: pick words that can each recur in a different grammatical role (a connective, a quantity, a time, a place, a person, a verb of continuing), so that the sestina's permutation of them enacts the branching chain rather than merely describing it. The envoi, where the form forces all six end-words into three lines, should be the moment the final tally is attempted and fails. Do not let the poem console itself by retreating to the near-term effects, and do not let it settle into mere despair. Someone who knows the debate about consequentialist cluelessness should find the problem rendered in the poem's form and not merely named in it; someone who has never heard of it should still find a good poem.
