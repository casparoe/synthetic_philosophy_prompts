So a friend of mine who's all-in on Lewis-style modal realism keeps trotting out this one move whenever someone tells him the view is crazy: being *actual*, he says, is supposed to work just like being *here* or being *now* — it's an indexical, nothing more. On his telling, asking "but why is our world the actual one?" is the same kind of question as "but why am I here, now?", i.e. a non-question, because it just picks out where you happen to be standing. He claims that single move dissolves the main objection, the one that says modal realism makes our world mysteriously special or owes us some explanation for why ours got to be the real one and the rest are just shadows.

I think it's cute but I don't trust it. Can you do a proper audit. First the correspondence table — what's supposed to map onto what: actual vs here/now, other worlds vs other places vs other times, the indexical operator itself, the "specialness" worry. Then tell me where the analogy actually breaks. I'm especially suspicious of three things: we can causally interact with other places and other times but not with other worlds; other times are supposed to belong to the same world while other worlds are disjoint by construction; and whether "actual" really behaves like an indexical in the contexts where the objection actually lives — belief, assertion, what we're able to refer to or name. Sort the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just noise. Then the payoff: does the conclusion survive — is the specialness objection genuinely a non-question, or does the analogy only make it *feel* like one? If the analogy can't carry it, tell me what a better analogy would be, or whether there's one at all.
