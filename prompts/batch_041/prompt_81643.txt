For some years I have been following the argument, among set theorists and philosophers of mathematics, about whether the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value, and I have come to suspect that it does not move because the participants are not, despite appearances, asserting and denying the same proposition. My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that once a background structure is fixed the dispute evaporates. Everyone agrees that the continuum hypothesis holds in some models of the standard axioms and fails in others, and that this is a theorem rather than an open question. What is left over is a disagreement about which structure, if any, the word "set" is meant to pick out, and that looks like a disagreement about reference and about the metaphysics of mathematical objects rather than about the continuum itself. If that is right, the two familiar camps are not so much contradicting each other as answering different questions under a single heading: one camp holds that there is a fact of the matter about the intended universe of sets and that we merely lack the axioms to see it, while the other holds that no universe is privileged and that the continuum hypothesis is like the parallel postulate, to be settled by stipulation rather than discovery.

I do not fully trust this diagnosis, because a residue looks genuinely mathematical. The same two camps also disagree about which extensions of the accepted axioms are legitimate to adopt, and about whether a new principle may be justified by its consequences — its unifying power, its fruitfulness — or only by something like self-evidence or reflection on the concept of set. That second disagreement does not obviously reduce to a difference in the meaning of a word.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on which side is right. Is this a verbal dispute, in the sense that "true" is being used in two ways, one of them bivalence over a fixed structure and the other provability from an accepted axiom system? Is it a disagreement about which results count as evidence — whether independence is a sign of indeterminacy or merely of our ignorance? Is it one substantive disagreement in philosophical dress, awaiting a new axiom or a formal result? Or is it several questions conflated, so that the apparent impasse is an artifact of a shared but ambiguous label?

Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims about the status of the continuum hypothesis, and be concrete about where, if anywhere, the parties genuinely contradict one another. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: what kind of theorem, distinction, or change of question could move the dispute, or whether the honest answer is that nothing should, because the question is ill posed. Finally, say what your diagnosis implies for a case I find telling — that the very people who treat the continuum hypothesis as unsettled often treat large-cardinal axioms as straightforwardly true. Does that pattern support a diagnosis of verbal disagreement, of a disagreement about the epistemology of axiom choice rather than about sets, or of something else entirely?
