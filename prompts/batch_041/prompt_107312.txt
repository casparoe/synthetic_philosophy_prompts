I'm a third-year cognitive science student currently taking a course on concepts, categorization, and analogy, and I've been asked to run one of the tutorial sessions. The claim I want my classmates to feel rather than merely hear is that concepts are fluid: a rule or pattern does not fix its own extension, and when people extend it differently they are usually following different but equally principled regularities rather than simply being sloppy. I have fourteen students for a sixty-minute session, no projector, and nothing but paper, pens, and whatever tokens I can carry in a bag.

To keep the domain small and self-contained, I've invented a notation rather than borrowing any published puzzle. A position is a row of beads read left to right, each bead one of three kinds, written as black, white, or half. The moves available in the game are: advance the last bead one step along the cycle black to white to half to black; duplicate the first bead at the front of the row; reverse the order of the two longest runs of identical beads; and insert one extra bead, of the color of the longer run, immediately after the longest run. I have also invented a recoding: the same row can be rewritten by renaming the colors, by reading it right to left, or by replacing each run with its color and its length. You may adjust or extend this menu, but the notation should stay this small.

Please design the activity so that the participants run into conceptual slippage themselves before I ever name it. Concretely: give me the rounds in order, the materials and how to lay them out on tables, the roles (I'm imagining a proposer and a judge, or competing teams, but choose what works), a scoring scheme with small real stakes that need no money, and the exact moment at which the design forces a divergence — that is, where two players can each derive a different continuation from the same source-and-target pair and each be able to defend it as the pattern. Include the recoding round, where some continuations survive the change of representation and others do not, and make clear what that round is for. I want the divergence to feel like a discovery rather than a trick, so the rules and the scoring should do the work; I will not lecture beforehand and I don't want the participants to hear the phrase fluid concept until the debrief.

Then give me the debrief, timed to the minute, mapping each moment of the activity onto the corresponding part of the idea: where a rule underdetermines its extension, where slippage tracks a genuine regularity rather than noise, and what the recoding round shows about which concepts are representation-invariant. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I'd guess that some players will hunt for the single intended answer and treat the whole thing as a puzzle with a hidden key, and that my judge will collapse into majority rule — and how I should handle each failure without giving the game away. Finally, be honest about which feature of the idea this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
