I teach a ninety-minute meeting of a third-year undergraduate course on philosophy of mind and cognitive science. There are eighteen students, the room has movable chairs and a whiteboard, and about half the group will have read the assigned chapter of Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences while the other half will arrive cold, so whatever I run has to work for people who have not done the reading. This is the session where I want them to feel, rather than be told, the book's central claim: that analogy is not an occasional ornament sitting on top of thought but the engine of thought itself; that categorizing something and seeing it as analogous to something else are one act rather than two; and that the boundary between "the same kind of thing" and "a different kind of thing" is drawn afresh in each context instead of being a fact that could in principle be read off the world. By the end I want them to have had two experiences they cannot easily shrug off. First, that they cannot get through sixty seconds of ordinary explaining without reaching for a prior case, a category label, or a comparison, and that they did this continuously and involuntarily without noticing. Second, that they themselves — arguing in good faith, following rules they agreed to — will have moved a concept's boundary and will be unable to say where the moving should have stopped. Note that I do not want the word "analogy" used as a technical term in front of them until the debrief; it should be the thing they were doing, not the thing they were told about.

Design me that activity. I have sketched a shape below, and you should replace it if you have something better — what I care about is that the rules, roles and incentives do the work rather than the facilitator's commentary, and that the group runs into the problem before it is named. My sketch: a first phase in which pairs have to get a partner to carry out a small unfamiliar task, or to recognise an unfamiliar object, using only concrete instructions — no comparisons, no category labels, no appeal to any prior case the partner might know. Then a second phase in which the whole group builds a chain from one description of a situation to a very different description of it, where each link changes exactly one thing and is accepted by a majority vote, and where any student may spend a scarce token to challenge a link as not really the same kind of thing and force a re-vote; a challenger who loses forfeits the token. I want the scoring arranged so that different students have different interests in where the boundary falls, so the group has to negotiate rather than discover. Give me the full rules, the materials (cards, tokens, any props), a timeline to the minute, and the exact minimum the facilitator must say at each transition. Then the debrief: map particular moments that will actually have occurred in the room onto particular parts of the claim — the moment someone reached for a comparison and couldn't stop themselves, the moment a link passed on the first vote and failed on the second with no new information in play, the moment a student's own earlier ruling came back to bite them — and tell me how to close without flattening the difference between "these concepts are negotiated" and "these concepts are arbitrary," which are not the same claim and which I do not want conflated. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: which students will game the token economy, where the group will bog down, what to do if the chain stalls, and how to handle the student who insists that there is obviously a fact of the matter about whether two things are the same kind of thing.

One further requirement. This is a philosophy course, and I do not want to sell my students a contested thesis as settled science. So in the debrief materials, keep separate what people who study this actually agree on from what is genuinely contested: mark which steps depend on the strong reading of Hofstadter and Sander — that analogy is the core of cognition, that categorisation just is analogical categorisation — as against weaker and much more widely shared claims, such as that analogy is a pervasive and early-developing contributor to thought, or merely that similarity judgements are sensitive to context and to the reasoner's goals. Do not let the strong version be presented as consensus, and where the activity's punchline needs the strong version in order to land, say so explicitly, so that I can frame it as a thesis my students are being invited to argue about rather than a result they are being taught. Be honest in the same way about the limits of the exercise: tell me which part of the idea no ninety-minute activity can make anyone feel, and which part I will simply have to state in the debrief.
