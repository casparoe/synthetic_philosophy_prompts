I run a small reading group in our philosophy department on infinity and paradox — 16 people, mostly second and third years plus two grad students who never say anything, 75 minutes, and for the last session of term I want to do supertasks and the Thomson lamp. The thing I keep failing to get across when I just explain it is that the problem isn't "is the lamp on or off?" with a clever answer hiding somewhere; its that the machine's description pins down everything before t=1 and pins down nothing at t=1, and that the parity argument gets its force from an extra assumption nobody wrote down. I want them to feel that rather than hear me say it. So: design me a runnable activity. No lecture up front, they shouldn't hear the word "underdetermined" until the debrief, and I'd rather the disagreement come out of the room than out of me. Also no reading list, nobody reads it anyway.

Heres the half-formed thing I've been sketching, take it, gut it, whatever. Three or four groups, each gets a "machine card" describing a lamp-and-switch rig and an infinite schedule of switch times (first switch at 1/2, then 3/4, then 7/8, and so on, each gap half the one before), and each group has to commit, in writing, to the colour of the lamp at t=1 plus a one-sentence reason. I want the cards to differ in ways that matter and that they'll only notice later: the plain toggler (off, on, off, on...); a machine that cycles red/blue/green instead of toggling, so the parity move has nothing to grab onto; a machine that goes red at the first switch and stays red through every later one; a machine whose colour at stage n is fixed by whether n is prime; and one card that simply stipulates the colour at t=1 as an extra clause of the description, which is cheating but legal. Then a second round where groups swap cards and try to falsify the other group's verdict using only whats on the card, and a third where each group may add exactly one extra stipulation to its own card and has to say what it bought them. I'd also like a betting layer if you can make it work without real money — I have a desk lamp, three coloured bulbs, index cards, and a whiteboard, and a bookie role that everyone ends up distrusting would be ideal.

Give me the whole thing: rules, roles, materials, a timeline that actually fits 75 minutes, and the debrief that maps each moment back to the paradox (the parity argument, the reply that says there's no fact of the matter at t=1, the continuity assumption doing the real work on the sticky-red card, and why "it has to be one or the other" is an intuition about lamps rather than about descriptions). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself at the end.
