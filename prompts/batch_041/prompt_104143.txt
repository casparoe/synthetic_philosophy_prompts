I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and every year I get talked into doing a one-off session in a science-writing master's program -- about 20 students, one 50-minute block, and I'm told I can do anything as long as it isn't a lecture. This year I want to do something on the result usually credited to I. J. Good: that information can't have negative expected value, because a free, credible piece of evidence you're allowed to ignore can never leave you worse off on average -- you can always just act as if you never saw it. What fascinates me is that everbody nods at this and nobody believes it. We dodge the test, the forecast, the number that will make us feel bad, and we're sure that knowing hurt us even when it demonstrably didn't change a single thing we did.

So design me a game. I want the students to play a few rounds of something with real (small) stakes -- points, pride, a chocolate bar -- where a free tip is offered before every decision, and I want them to walk out feeling in their bones both halves of the thing: that the tip can't hurt them if they're free to ignore it, and that the sting they feel is real and points straight at the assumptions the result needs (you can ignore it, it costs nothing, you act on it optimally, and value means expected value over the whole run, not how it happened to turn out this time). That last contrast is the one I care most about, since I think the ostrich intuition actually lives in the gap between expected and realized value.

Keep it runnable out of a stationery cupboard, no real money. I need the rules, the payoff scheme, a 50-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the result and onto its escape hatches -- the situations where info genuinely can hurt, like when you're not allowed to ignore it, when it costs something, or when knowing is itself part of the payoff (the patient who'd rather not know). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the room.

And if the setup I've described doesn't actually let them feel the result -- if the game as I've framed it just makes the whole thing trivially true and boring -- say so and give me a different one instead of humoring me. Flag anywhere I've got the result subtly wrong, too, because I'm a journalist and I probably do.
