I am preparing materials for a reading group on Mill's political thought, and I have become preoccupied with the part of his career that usually gets a paragraph at most: the decades he spent as an official of the East India Company, his rise to its senior post for India correspondence, his unsuccessful campaign against handing the government of India over to the Crown in the late 1850s, and the memorandum in which he is said to have protested against the annexation of Oudh — the argument being, roughly, that a native ruler's misgovernment does not license his dispossession. What strikes me is how seamless the colonial argument is with the rest of his work: government by a small, salaried, trained body insulated from patronage and answerable to Parliament; improvement as the end that justifies despotism, so that despotism is legitimate only for as long as it is educational; the claim that representative institutions are premature for a society not yet prepared for them, and that free institutions are next to impossible in a country composed of several nationalities; the insistence that governing India well requires a knowledge of India that a Westminster amateur will never acquire; and, at home, a lifelong hatred of arbitrary power. I want a satire that takes all of this seriously as an argument and follows it where it leads, rather than one that simply denounces Mill — and I want it unmistakable that the colonised are not the butt of the joke.

Please write, at about 800 words, a mock white paper from an invented management consultancy whose senior partner is a sincere admirer of Mill and has adapted his position for the contemporary development sector: something on the order of 'Benevolent Despotism: A Governance Framework for Clients Not Yet Fit for Self-Rule', addressed to a fictional client agency, with an executive summary, numbered sections, and a closing 'next steps'. The comedy has to come from the argument's own machinery rather than from jokes about empire in general. I would like a metrics section in which improvement cannot be measured but must nevertheless be reported quarterly, together with a proxy indicator; a sequencing section explaining that premature elections in a client territory would betray the very people the framework exists to serve; a risk register that includes the danger of the framework's own administrators acquiring too much sympathy for the governed; a case study of a ruler dispossessed for governing badly, presented as a lesson in sequencing rather than as a wrong; and a sidebar on individual liberty with a footnote confining it to those already fit for self-rule. Keep it deadpan, with no winking at the reader, and legible to someone who knows nothing of the nineteenth-century background. Write in British English.

Then add a short note, clearly outside the document, stating which historical and conceptual claims the satire leans on and how confident you are in each, naming the one you are least sure of, and saying where the piece takes liberties with Mill's actual positions or compresses the historical record.
