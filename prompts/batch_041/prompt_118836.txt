I am preparing a long-form interview for a departmental podcast on the foundations of rational choice, and I want the questions settled in advance. My guest is a game theorist whose recent book presses a dilemma about common knowledge of rationality. The familiar claim is that common knowledge of rationality — each player is rational, knows the others are rational, knows that they know it, and so on — explains or justifies play in Nash equilibrium. His claim is that the assumption yields at most rationalizability, the iterated elimination of strategies that are never a best response to any surviving conjecture, which in most games leaves many profiles standing. The further conditions that would be needed to isolate Nash equilibrium — mutual knowledge of the players' conjectures, together with a coherence requirement tying each player's beliefs about the others' actions to the others' beliefs about his — are, he argues, not conditions of rationality at all, and arguably presuppose the equilibrium structure they were meant to explain. Hence his summary verdict: the epistemic program is either idle or circular — idle where rationality alone already picks out a profile, circular where it does not. I have read the manuscript twice and still cannot decide whether this is a real dilemma or an artifact of how he sets up the conditions, which is what the interview is for.

Draft ten to twelve interview questions that press him on the substance of this position rather than inviting him to restate it, and give each a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I want at least one question each on:

(a) whether the idle-or-circular dichotomy admits a middle ground — some class of games in which the epistemic conditions both do independent work and are genuinely prior to the equilibrium concept;

(b) whether the argument depends on reading "common knowledge" as knowledge rather than as probability-one belief, and what becomes of the dilemma if players assign a small positive probability to one another's irrationality;

(c) the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma and the backward-induction puzzle, where the unique rationalizable profile is also the unique Nash profile, so that his "idle" case is precisely the case in which the standard reasoning is most disputed;

(d) whether the critique carries over to games of incomplete information, where opponents will say the type structure already supplies the mutual knowledge of conjectures his argument declares missing;

(e) what he takes the epistemic program to have accomplished, if not a justification of equilibrium; and

(f) the methodological upshot — whether a failure of foundational justification tells at all against using equilibrium concepts predictively.

Press where the literature presses, and do not let him defer by redescribing the objection as a research agenda; make him say what the agenda would have to deliver for the objection to be answered. No generic questions. Do not ask him to cite literature, name names, or recommend further reading; the questions must stand on their own. End with a single line of exactly the form "Hardest: <n>", where <n> is the number of the question you judge most likely to force him to concede ground.
