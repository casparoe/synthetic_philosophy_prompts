A state education authority must divide a fixed intervention budget among four outcomes: adult height, body-mass index, reading fluency, and conscientiousness. Its consultant has supplied heritability estimates for the authority's own population, drawn from twin studies — roughly 0.8 for height, 0.7 for body-mass index, 0.7 for reading fluency, and 0.4 for conscientiousness — together with a draft resting on three conclusions.

First, the draft ranks the four outcomes for funding by the quantity 1 − h², which it calls a trait's plasticity, on the reasoning that a trait that is only 40 per cent genetic is one on which environmental intervention is most likely to succeed.

Second, it places the authority's height estimate beside an estimate of about 0.4 from a poorer neighbouring country whose mean adult height is six centimetres lower, and concludes that roughly half of that six-centimetre gap is genetic and half environmental.

Third, it argues that since reading fluency has a heritability of about 0.7, the most a new tutoring programme could achieve is to close some 30 per cent of the observed gap in reading fluency, the remaining 70 per cent being fixed by genes.

Three further points trouble the authority's own statistician. Mean adult height in the authority's population rose by about five centimetres over the twentieth century, even though heritability for height was already high at the beginning of that period. She suspects that if the authority were ever to bring every child's environment up to that of the best-off decile, the estimated heritability of reading fluency would rise rather than fall. And she notes that conscientiousness was scored from a short questionnaire whose test–retest reliability is only about 0.6, which makes her doubt that its low heritability is telling us much about genes at all.

Set out the most important implications of what heritability does and does not mean for each of the draft's three conclusions and for the statistician's three worries. Take heritability to be the share of phenotypic variance, in a given population at a given time, that is attributable to genetic variance, and assume no prior acquaintance with the surrounding literature. Where a conclusion can be defended in an amended form, say what that form is; where it cannot, say what would have to be true for it to hold.
