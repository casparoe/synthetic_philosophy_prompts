I'm taking two courses this term that have turned out to be in an argument with each other, and my term paper is going to have to referee it. One is a seminar on the historiography of early modern science; the other is a drama course in which we're spending three weeks on Brecht's Life of Galileo. My drama professor reads the play as a study of the scientist's responsibility: the whole thing is built on the assumption that there is an old order and a new one, that Galileo is standing on the seam between them, and that his recantation matters because he had it in his hands to hand the new knowledge to the wrong people. My history professor treats that seam as our invention. She argues that the three-part scheme we all use was fixed by late-seventeenth-century compilers in Germany who simply needed a label for the stretch between the ancients they admired and themselves; that the rebirth talk of Petrarch and Vasari was self-promotion which Burckhardt converted into a period in the nineteenth century; that the Scientific Revolution as a discrete event was assembled in the twentieth century, partly by philosophers of science in exile and partly by textbooks that needed a founding moment; and that the technical moves we credit to Galileo already had serious precedents among fourteenth-century masters in Paris and Oxford, who had a theory of impetus and a mathematics of motion, which is why a great many specialists now prefer to talk about long continuity rather than a break.

I'd like a reading of the play that holds both of these in view instead of settling the question by fiat. What exactly does Brecht put on the old side — the Church, the Medici court, the plague, the peasants' faith, the little monk's plea for his parents, the senators who want the telescope for their galleys, the court scholars who, as I read it, keep finding reasons not to put their eye to the lens — and what does he put on the new, and does that division survive contact with what we now know about fourteenth- and fifteenth-century natural philosophy? Does the play's shape commit it to the periodization even where its content might not: five acts that carry us from an old world to a new one, a Galileo who is legible to us as a modern intellectual because he talks like one, an ending in which a manuscript crosses a border into a future? I'm suspicious that Brecht's Galileo is anachronistically modern in precisely the way my history professor objects to, and I'd like to know whether that is a flaw or whether the play is doing something with the anachronism deliberately. The harder question is what happens to the play's politics if the break turns out to be a later construction: if Galileo is one participant in a long, uneven transformation rather than the founder of a new age, does his recantation still carry the weight Brecht wants it to, or does the moral have to be rebuilt on different ground? Brecht rewrote the play more than once, and my drama professor says the later versions were written by a man who had seen what science did in the war, which seems relevant to how much of the epochal break is Brecht's own historical situation being projected backwards. And I want the reverse pressure too: what does the play give us that the historians' arguments cannot — some rendering of what it is like to live inside a moment that later gets called a turning point, and of the fact that naming an age is not a neutral description but a claim with moral weight, since calling something dark or new is already an evaluation.

Please don't hand me a survey of the positions and let me choose. Take a side and defend it: say what the play is committed to about periodization, where the honest critic has to concede that the play is doing something the continuity thesis cannot accommodate, or the reverse, and what you would say to each of my two professors. If your reading forces you to give up something that either the play or the revisionist historians care about, tell me plainly what it is and why the price is worth paying.
