I'm a journalist who writes about archaeology, climate, and the traffic of ideas between the two, and over the past few years I've kept circling the same subject from different directions: stories about "collapse." The end of the Bronze Age palace economies, the abandonment of the Classic Maya lowland cities, the Easter Island cautionary tale and the revisionist reading that has grown up around it — in each case the same argument reappears in different costume. One side holds that these societies really did fail, and that the failure is measurable in lost complexity, population, and institutional capacity. The other holds that nothing failed except our expectations, that people adapted, relocated, and continued under new arrangements, and that "collapse" is a story told from the vantage point of whoever came after. What strikes me is that both sides conduct this as an empirical quarrel about the archaeological record, when most of the disagreement seems to sit upstream of the evidence — in the choice of unit (a polity? a population? an exchange network? a bundle of institutions?) and in where the line between "change" and "failure" is drawn. My hunch is that once those parameters are fixed explicitly, a large part of the dispute either dissolves or converts into a straightforwardly empirical question, and that what remains is a verbal dispute in the technical sense — though possibly one where there is still a fact about which vocabulary earns its keep. I want to spend a fellowship period doing this properly, but I'm a reporter rather than a specialist and I need help turning the hunch into a fundable plan.

Please write me a two-page proposal for a nine-month residential fellowship. I need the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, rather than being an invitation to muse; an account of why the existing positions leave that question open and not merely unsettled; and a method described concretely, month by month, in terms of what I would actually do at a desk — working from published case studies and from the diagnostic checklists and criteria the field already uses, rather than from fieldwork or new data. I would like the proposal to take seriously the possibility that "collapse" sometimes does explanatory work rather than merely descriptive work, and to say what would have to be true for it to function as an explanans rather than a label for an outcome. It should also address the normative tail, since collapse narratives are constantly recruited to license present-day claims about ecological limits and civilizational fragility; if the concept turns out to be partly observer-relative, I want the proposal to be honest about what that does and does not undermine. Tell me what a positive result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and which parts I should cut if the fellowship turns out to be shorter or less well resourced than I hope.

Two constraints. First, do not give me a bibliography or a literature review by citation — refer to positions, arguments, and families of view, and I will supply the references myself; likewise, don't rest any part of the argument on contested details of a single case, since I would rather the proposal stand on the structure of the problem than on particulars I can't verify from where I sit. Second, if you think the framing itself is confused — if "unit of analysis" is the wrong diagnosis and something else is doing the work — say so in the proposal rather than humoring me.
