I'm the clinical ethicist at a mid-size hospital and I keep getting paged at 2am for the same cluster of questions, so I want to write a one-page procedure that the night resident or charge nurse can actually run without me. The subject matter is the doing/allowing family of distinctions — killing vs letting die, acts vs omissions, intending vs merely foreseeing, and the double-effect machinery that goes with it. Two wrinkles specific to us: our policy manual officially states that withholding and withdrawing life support are morally equivalent, which most of my nurses do not believe in their bones, and our state has legalized aid in dying while our hospital (religiously affiliated) opts out, so some calls are really about that.

The calls I get: should we start a treatment, should we stop one already running, should we withhold CPR or pressors, is it OK to sedate someone to unconsciousness for refractory suffering when we expect it may shorten life, can we stop tube feeding. Roughly twice a week, family in the room, and I usually cant get there in under an hour. The person running the procedure is a resident who's read maybe one article about any of this and is scared of getting it wrong.

Please write the procedure. I need the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which steps are factual (prognosis, what the intervention actually does) and which are value questions that no amount of data settles, where the procedure stops with a flat no, where it hands the decision to a person instead of answering, and who has to sign what at the end. Two things I want dealt with honestly instead of papered over: (a) whether the procedure should ask anybody to state their intention at all, since asking an exhausted nurse to declare that she isnt intending the death seems to just produce a form filled in to get the desired answer, and (b) whether to phrase the whole thing causally (did the act cause the death?) or normatively (is the death attributable to a choice that violated a duty?), because our risk manager wants the causal version and I think that's a mistake.

Then run it on three cases: (1) terminal extubation of a patient who will die within hours; (2) palliative sedation for a dying patient with refractory agitation, where the family is asking for it and one nurse refuses to take part; (3) a family demanding that everything continue, where the team believes ongoing dialysis is now doing more harm than good. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me something I don't want to hear — say, that the doing/allowing line I've been leaning on does no real work here and the live question is somewhere else entirely — and end with a short list of what the procedure cannot decide, so I know when to get out of bed.
