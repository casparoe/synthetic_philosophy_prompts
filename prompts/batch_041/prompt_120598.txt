Geologists fix the boundaries of the geological time scale by a procedure worth stating in full, because its details are stranger than the headline. A boundary is not defined by a date but by a place: one rock section somewhere on Earth is chosen, the horizon of the boundary is identified within it, and a physical marker is fixed at that level. That single point then defines the base of the unit — units are defined from below, never from the middle — and every other record on the planet is subsequently correlated to it. To qualify, a candidate section must be continuously deposited, undisturbed, accessible, and open to researchers of any nationality; its marker must be a physical signal that can be recognised globally and that does not depend on the local environment in which the sediment was laid down. The attempt to add the Anthropocene as an epoch followed this recipe exactly. A working group of the International Commission on Stratigraphy proposed sediments from a lake in Ontario that record a sharp mid-century rise in plutonium from atmospheric nuclear tests, with the boundary in the early 1950s; rival starting dates ran from the beginnings of agriculture to the industrial revolution; and in 2024 the proposal was rejected — first by the quaternary subcommission, then on confirmation by the commission and the geological union — on the grounds that the sedimentary record was too shallow and the proposed beginning too recent. Two details complicate the picture. The same institution fixes the deepest boundaries by a different method altogether: below roughly 630 million years, boundaries are simply agreed numbers rather than spikes in rock. And after the rejection the term stayed in use, with the geological union calling it a valuable descriptor of human impact even though it is not a unit of the time scale. The sampling that produced the proposal was itself paid for by a special appropriation from a national legislature, routed through a cultural institute rather than a science agency.

What conception of a historical period does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as fully as the details allow: whether periods are discovered features of the world or stipulated instruments, whether a period's boundary is an instant or a process, whether the question of when something began has a single true answer, and where the authority to settle it is taken to reside, on what grounds. Then sort the practice's commitments: which are strictly required for it to make sense, which merely fit comfortably, and which are conveniences of the moment. Which rival accounts of periodisation does it quietly rule out — say, that periods are defined by their human significance, or that a period's onset may be diachronous and spread over millennia — and which does it leave open, so that a realist and a conventionalist could both endorse it? Address two apparent inconsistencies directly: whether a practice that uses a spike for recent boundaries and an agreed number for the oldest ones is coherent, and whether, on this practice's own terms, the Anthropocene existed before 2024 and still exists after its rejection. Finally, consider the alternative floated in the debate — that the Anthropocene is a geological event rather than a time unit, a long and unevenly propagating process of the kind the oxygenation of the atmosphere is taken to be, and so need not have a base at all. If that view were adopted, what in the procedure would have to change, and who would then be doing the deciding?
