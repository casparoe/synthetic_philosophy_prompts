I run a 300-level seminar on ethics and rational choice; sixteen students, seventy-five minutes, and I want a runnable market activity that makes them feel the force of moral trade — that two people with sharply different and partly opposed values can reach an exchange each of them counts as an improvement, without either adopting the other's values and without any common scale on which their values can be compared. Give me the materials and rules: individual value profiles legible on a card, a budget, and a mechanism that makes pledges binding; the rounds of trading; a scoring scheme that needs no real money; a timeline for the seventy-five minutes; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the underlying structure — in particular why commensurability is not needed for a mutual improvement by each party's own lights, and why the market stalls precisely where someone's commitment is not a matter of degree. Don't let the term moral trade, or any of the literature's vocabulary, surface until the debrief.

Build in two designed obstacles: a participant whose profile contains a constraint she will not trade against at any price, and a case where what is being bought looks to the buyer like permission to do something he takes to be wrong. I want the group to hit that wall themselves and only then hear it named. Then tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time — students who treat it as a competition to maximize points, students who refuse to trade on principle — and how to handle each without spoiling the discovery. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
