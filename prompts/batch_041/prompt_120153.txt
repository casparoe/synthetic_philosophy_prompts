I am in a reading group that has spent the last several weeks on indicative conditionals, and we have just finished with the triviality results David Lewis proved in the 1970s. The rough shape of them, as I understand it: if you take at face value the idea that the probability of a conditional is just the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, and you allow that equation to hold for every conditional the language can build, the probabilities collapse, and the equation can be preserved only for a restricted class of cases or under restrictions that give up what it was originally meant to say. Since then the group has argued about whether this refutes the so-called Ramsey test or merely shows that the test was misstated, and I find that the question I keep coming back to is what Ramsey himself would have made of it. He died in 1930, decades before any of the proofs, and what we have from him is the suggestion, in a piece published after his death, that we assess a conditional by hypothetically adding its antecedent to our stock of knowledge and then asking how things stand with the consequent — the remark from which the entire probabilistic treatment of conditionals is usually traced. So what I would like from you is a serious exercise in counterfactual intellectual history: reconstruct Ramsey's most plausible response to the triviality results. I would rather have your reasoning than your references, so please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of what later writers decided he ought to have meant.

To make that concrete. State the triviality result in your own words, and be explicit about which assumption in it has to give: the universal scope of the equation, the claim that a conditional expresses a proposition with a truth condition, the identification of the Ramsey test with a rule for degrees of belief, or something else entirely. Then say which concession Ramsey, given the rest of his outlook — his pragmatism about belief as a guide to action, his interest in partial belief and in how we argue from suppositions, and his inclination, if I have got him right, to treat laws and generalities not as propositions that are true or false but as rules for judging — would most plausibly have reached for, and why that one rather than the others. A friend in the group insists that Ramsey's remark was about acceptance and argument rather than about probability: it tells us what it is reasonable to add to a body of knowledge, not how to compute a number, and therefore the triviality results simply pass him by. Adjudicate that, and say what it costs to hold it, in particular whether anything survives of the probabilistic reading once the equation is demoted to a test of acceptability. Finally, tell me where the extrapolation gets thin: which parts of your answer follow from commitments Ramsey plainly held, and which are you and I constructing a plausible character out of a few sentences.
