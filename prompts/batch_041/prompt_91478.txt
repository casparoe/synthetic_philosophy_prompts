In a departmental seminar on practical rationality, two colleagues of mine, Mira and Jonah, fell into a disagreement about a variant of Parfit’s hitchhiker. The variant is this: you are stranded in a desert; a driver offers you a lift to safety only if she judges that you have a settled disposition to pay her afterwards. She is a good but imperfect judge of character: most, but not all, travellers she picks up with that disposition do pay. There is no external enforcement, no repeat interaction, and no reputational mechanism beyond this encounter. Once you are safe, paying would cost you a significant sum and bring you no further benefit. You cannot communicate an enforceable promise; the driver relies on your character.

Mira argues: (1) If you genuinely have the disposition, then paying is the rational thing to do, because the disposition is not a mere prediction but a commitment; to act otherwise would be to betray the very trait that got you the lift. (2) If you can decide at the end not to pay, then you never really had the disposition, and the driver was simply mistaken about you. (3) The driver’s reliance is reasonable only if travellers like you generally pay; the rationality of your paying is therefore partly a matter of sustaining a practice, not just of your isolated payoff.

Jonah replies: (1) The disposition is a causal mechanism that secured the lift; once the lift is secured, it has done its work, and the reasons to pay have not survived the change in circumstances. (2) Calling it a commitment does not create a reason where none existed; commitments are just intentions, and intentions can be rationally revised when the reasons that supported them lapse. (3) The driver’s reliance is a gamble on a statistical regularity; if she is sometimes mistaken, that is her risk, not a reason for you to sacrifice your interests. (4) If you pay only because you cannot bring yourself to do otherwise, that is compulsion, not rationality; if you can bring yourself not to pay, then the driver’s trust was misplaced, and the rational course is to take the lift and keep your money.

Mira responds: (1) That picture makes rationality parasitic on whatever desires you happen to have at the moment of action, and so cannot explain why anyone should cultivate a disposition to keep faith in the first place. (2) If the driver knows that travellers of your type will reason as Jonah does, she will stop offering lifts, and the practice collapses; the fact that Jonah’s reasoning is self-undermining when universalised is a reason to reject it. (3) The imperfect reliability of the driver’s judgement does not make it irrational to be the kind of person she can trust; it only means that trust is risky.

Jonah responds: (1) The practice may collapse if everyone reasons as I do, but that is a fact about the practice, not about the rationality of my act. (2) You are smuggling in a moral requirement—keep faith—under the guise of rational self-interest. (3) If you want to pay for moral reasons, fine, but then say so; do not pretend that self-interest requires it.

They have asked me to adjudicate. Please go through the exchange move by move, saying who is right about each point and why, and then say what both of them are missing about the relationship between rational irrationality, precommitment, and the difference between a one-shot encounter and a practice. Please answer in British English.
