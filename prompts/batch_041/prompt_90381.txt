I've been reading the sequences and the forums for years, and there's one recurring frustration I can't shake: I genuinely can't tell whether the "unity of the proposition" is a real problem or an artifact of how we talk about content. So I sat down and reconstructed what I take to be the old Bradleyan regress — but I built it in a form where it plainly proves too much, and I've stopped trusting my own attempts to dissolve it. What I want is a diagnosis, not a tour of the literature; I have no way to look anything up, so please don't send me to sources.

Here is the argument as I've written it out.

(1) For a structured thing — a sentence, a thought, a representation inside a system — to have content, and to be the sort of thing that can be true or false, its parts must be unified into a single item. A mere list of the parts is not yet a proposition.
(2) The parts are unified only if something relates them: for the parts a, R, and b to make up the content aRb, the relation R must actually apply to a and b — R must be joined to them.
(3) But R's being joined to a and b is itself a relating. So a further relation R′ must join R to a and b; then R″ must join R′ to R, a, and b; and so on without end.
(4) The regress never terminates, so the unity is never achieved.
(5) Hence no collection of parts ever makes up a proposition: there is no such thing as structured content. And the reasoning generalizes — any relating demands a further relating — so there are no relational facts at all, not even that the cup is on the table.
(6) But there plainly are relational facts, and plainly some things are about other things. So a step must be wrong — yet (2) looks like a tautology to me, and (3) looks like a restatement of (2).

Two escapes I've tried and don't trust. First: "the proposition just is the set {a, R, b}, and sets don't need unifying." That trades the problem away — a set isn't about anything and isn't true or false, so I've lost the very feature I was trying to explain. Second: "the unity is primitive; stop asking." That refuses the question. Nor do I think relocating to the world helps: even if I say that judging is just a many-place relation to a, R, and b rather than to one proposition, the world still contains the fact that a bears R to b, and that looks like a complex needing the same unifying — so the regress seems to reappear on the worldly side.

So: which step do I reject, why did it look innocent, and what does each of the live repairs cost? I'd most like to keep (1) and (6). And tell me whether the regress is vicious or benign — if benign, exactly what makes it benign, since "it just is" is the one answer I can't accept.
