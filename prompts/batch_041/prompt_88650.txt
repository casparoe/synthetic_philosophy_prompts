I'm running a seminar next month on the whole 'my one burger doesn't matter' thing and I want a dialogue to get them arguing rather than nodding along. Two people, both sharp, both taking the problem seriously, neither one a punching bag. They should actually respond to each others points rather than take turns delivering speeches, and they should be arguing about the arguments themselves, not about who said what.

Here's the case they're fighting over, and I want them to stay on it instead of swapping in trolley problems:

A supermarket chain discounts chicken every Wednesday. Procurement is done by a single buyer working off a weekly forecast. If discounted sales come in more than a few percent above forecast, the buyer exercises a standing option with a supplier to commission an extra barn's worth of production, about forty thousand birds. If sales stay at or under forecast, the option lapses and the barns stay empty. The option can only be used once in the season and there's twelve weeks left. Nobody outside the chain knows where the trigger sits in a given week; the buyer's forecast drifts with the weather, school holidays, and whatever the rival chains are doing. Below the trigger, an extra sale still nudges the chain's aggregate numbers, which feed into the suppliers long-run price and so into how many birds get bred next season. You buy the Wednesday chicken. You're not hard up, so the money is irrelevant to you.

One of them thinks the honest answer is that your purchase doesn't make a difference in any sense that matters, and that the mature response is to admit this and spend your energy where it counts, policy and campaigning and organising. The other thinks that's a cop-out and that there is still a real reason not to buy it, but not the 'what if everybody did it' reason, which they think is a dud.

Things I want them to actually go at each other over:

- whether the hidden trigger changes anything versus the smooth version where every purchase adds a sliver, and whether 'there's a tiny chance your purchase is the one that tips it' is a real answer or a dodge
- whether it matters that the option can only fire once, so that at best you might only have brought the barn forward a few weeks rather than adding it
- whether 'you're helping bring it about even when your act makes no difference to whether it happens' is a coherent third position or just mood affiliation
- whether it matters that a person, the buyer, is deliberating and your purchase is one of their inputs
- whether the same verdict carries over to voting, where the threshold is exact and known and the pivotal probability is at least in principle estimable, and to flying, where the mechanism is a lot messier than the chicken case
- whether the real work is being done by participation and complicity rather than by causation, and if so whether one purchase among millions is enough to count

Don't let them settle into a polite both-sides conclusion at the end, I want the actual sticking point left sharp. A thousand words or so is fine, doesn't need to be longer.
