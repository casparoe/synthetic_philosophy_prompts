Here's the question. It's worth 10 points: (a) 3, (b) 3, (c) 4.

The colour phi phenomenon is sometimes described as a case in which the brain "revises" perceptual content.

(a) Describe what is presented to the subject and what the subject reports, and explain why the report is supposed to be puzzling.

(b) Set out the two styles of explanation Dennett distinguishes with the labels "Orwellian" and "Stalinesque", and explain the point of the historical labels.

(c) What does Dennett conclude from this case about the claim that a given conscious experience has a definite time of occurrence? What is he arguing against?

And here's what she wrote:

(a) The setup is simple. Two spots are flashed in different places on a screen, close together in time, and the spots are different colours — say a blue one high up and a red one lower down. At the right spacing and timing you don't see two flashes. You see a single spot that travels from the first position to the second, and it changes colour partway along the path. The puzzle is in the timing of the report. Subjects say they saw the colour change before the second spot appeared. But the colour of the second spot is what fixes the colour that the moving spot changes into, so the information that determines the content of the experience seems to arrive after the experience itself. Taken at face value, that means the experience of the change happened before its own cause.

(b) Dennett's labels come from two ways a regime can falsify the record. Stalin's show trials had the verdict decided in advance and the evidence arranged to fit it, so the record was never wrong. Orwell's Ministry of Truth rewrote the record after the fact, so the record contradicts what actually happened. Mapping this onto perception: the Stalinesque explanation says the perceptual system waits until the second spot has arrived, then assembles a single coherent moving-and-changing-colour experience and presents it as though it had unfolded in real time. The Orwellian explanation says you really do have an experience of a spot of the first colour, and then when the second spot arrives the memory of it is rewritten so that you remember having seen the change at the halfway point.

(c) Dennett thinks we should accept the Orwellian explanation. The Stalinesque story requires backwards causation — the later stimulus reaching back and altering the earlier experience — and nothing physical can do that. If the revision is made to memory instead, nothing reaches backwards: the experience happened when it happened, and only the record of it gets edited. So the case shows that our judgements about the timing of our own experiences can't be trusted, which is the point of the multiple drafts model: the brain produces several drafts of what is going on and then settles on one of them as the conscious content. This is also what Dennett means by denying the Cartesian theatre — there's no single place in the brain where the show is put on for an inner audience. He isn't claiming the mind is non-physical; the argument is meant to make consciousness easier to fit into a physicalist picture, not harder.
