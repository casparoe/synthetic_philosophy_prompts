Two philosophers who have argued for years about dispositions have agreed to produce a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. The first is a conditionalist: to ascribe a disposition to a thing, she holds, is to say something about how that thing would behave in certain circumstances, and there is nothing more to having a disposition than the truth of the relevant conditionals. The second is an anti-reductionist: a disposition, he holds, is a real feature of the thing, prior to and explanatory of the truths about what it would do, so that no set of conditional truths could take its place. Both agree that the simple form of the conditionalist's account — a thing is disposed to break when struck just in case it would break if struck — is false, and that everything turns on whether some repaired form survives.

Their dispute has revolved around three kinds of interfering condition. Call a condition a fink if the arrival of the stimulus removes the disposition itself; a mask if it leaves the disposition intact but blocks its manifestation; and a mimic if it produces the manifestation by another route while the disposition is absent.

Write the statement the two of them could sign. Format it as follows.

1. A numbered list of claims both accept: about what disposition ascriptions are for, about what the fink, mask, and mimic cases do and do not show, about what would count as an adequate repair of the conditional analysis, and about what is at stake that is more than terminological. Nothing vacuous; nothing only one side could accept.

2. A table of the residual disagreements. Each row states one disagreement twice — once in wording the conditionalist endorses as her own position, once in wording the anti-reductionist endorses as his — and adds a verdict on whether that disagreement is substantive or verbal, with reasons.

3. Their cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. For each crux, give a case or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, and record both parties' predictions about it in advance.

Both parties ask you to work through four cases on which neither has published, rather than gesturing at them:

(a) A pane that, when struck, instantly ceases to be brittle and is then broken a moment later by a charge released by the impact itself: it does break when struck, but not because it is brittle.
(b) A metal whose brittleness consists in a lattice structure that, whenever the metal is struck, converts to a ductile form and absorbs the blow — the very feature that makes it brittle is what keeps it from ever breaking.
(c) A substance that cracks under stress on roughly a third of the occasions on which it is stressed, the outcome turning on the stochastic microstructure at the crack tip.
(d) A substance that shatters on its first impact and is unbreakable thereafter.

For each case, say whether the thing has the disposition, whether the corresponding counterfactual is true, and, where the two sides must diverge, how each side accommodates the case.

Close with two short sections: what result, conceptual or empirical, each party would count against their own position; and whether either party believes the other's position can be re-described so as to be compatible with their own. Argue everything out from the cases and the positions themselves; neither party should need to invoke any particular published formulation.
