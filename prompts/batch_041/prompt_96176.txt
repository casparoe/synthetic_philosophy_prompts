So I've somehow ended up teaching the British Empire to year 12s — I'm an econ teacher, long story, the history dept is short-staffed — and to prep I read Ferguson's Empire and then Tharoor's Inglorious Empire, and now I can't stop thinking about how these two books are having completely different arguments while looking like they're having the same one. It also got personal, because my partner's family is from Kerala and her grandfather will happily tell me the Raj was one long theft, and then my own uncle (ex-army, reads a lot of Ferguson) says the whole thing ran at a loss for Britain so we should stop apologising for it. They've had this exact argument three times now and nobody has moved an inch.

Here's my rough sense of it. Ferguson's headline move is that the empire didn't pay — that the costs of defending and administering it outweighed the returns, and that the real story is the export of British institutions and the way what he calls Anglobalization shaped the world. Tharoor's move is that India was drained — that wealth flowed out through tribute, one-way trade, and the collapse of Indian textiles, so the question isn't whether Britain profited but what India lost. Those look like they contradict, but I suspect they're answering different questions with the same word, profit, and possibly with different units (fiscal returns to the British state vs. capital returns to particular Britons vs. welfare losses in India vs. GDP per head), different periods (the 1700s vs. the Victorian Raj vs. the whole span), and different counterfactuals (India without Britain vs. India under some other coloniser vs. India with no coloniser at all).

But I also don't want to be too quick to call it a merely verbal dispute, because there are real empirical fights underneath — how big the drain actually was, whether imperial investment really earned less than domestic investment, how to count the security spending that Ferguson treats as a cost of empire rather than just ordinary defence, and who within Britain bore the costs and who got the gains. And there's a moral layer that neither side is honest about: Ferguson's numbers get used as if they'd settle the justification question, and Tharoor's numbers get used the same way, but even a profitable empire wouldn't be justified and even an unprofitable one could have been monstrous.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word profit, two different ledgers being compared as if they were one, or a moral disagreement dressed up in economics? Diagnose it for me using what each side actually claims, tell me which parts are genuinely empirical and could in principle be settled by better numbers, which parts are really about the choice of counterfactual or unit of account, and which parts are bedrock moral disagreement that no amount of cliometrics will touch. And then tell me what would actually count as progress here — is it a decomposition, a shared baseline, a distinction between profitability and justification, or is the whole thing just ill-posed? I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right.
