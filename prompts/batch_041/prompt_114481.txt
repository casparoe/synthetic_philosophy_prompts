What exactly does a Dutch book argument establish? Explain why the answer is less straightforward than textbook presentations suggest, working through three points.

Begin with the standard argument: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then a bookmaker who knows them can offer you a set of bets, each of which you accept, that guarantees you a net loss whatever happens. Explain the familiar objection that this shows only that your betting dispositions are jointly bad for you — that an agent who is risk-averse, whose utility for money is non-linear in the stakes, or who does not gamble at all escapes the argument — and explain how the reply that appeals to bets you regard as fair, rather than bets you would accept, is supposed to meet that objection. State whether you think the reply succeeds, and what it must assume about the utility of money.

Turn next to the diachronic version of the argument, in which a bookmaker bets with you once before you learn something and once afterwards, and which is used to support conditionalization. Explain why this version requires stronger assumptions than the synchronic one does about what the bookmaker knows and about how your future self will behave, and why it has been argued that it constrains the plans you make in advance rather than the credences you hold at a time.

Finally, note that a Dutch book contains only finitely many bets. Explain what this implies for whether the argument can be used to defend the requirement that credences be countably additive.
