I am a third-year undergraduate taking a course on rational choice and the ethics of risk, and the draft below is for my final paper. I would be grateful for detailed comments before I revise. I am not asking for citations or for further reading — what I need is engagement with the argument.

Draft:

The standard objection to expected value maximization (EVM) — that it is fanatical — is usually put as a complaint about small probabilities. EVM, it is said, obliges an agent to accept a gamble with a minute chance of success whenever the payoff is large enough, and this is absurd. I argue that this is the wrong diagnosis. The objection is not about probability; it is about the value scale. Two familiar cases show why.

In the St. Petersburg game a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the payoff doubles with every toss, so the expected monetary payoff is infinite, though no sensible person would pay more than a few pounds for the game. In Pascal's mugging a stranger offers an enormous benefit — ten-to-the-hundred times the value of a human life, say — with a correspondingly minute probability, and asks for a small payment; EVM says to pay him. These are usually treated as separate puzzles. They are one puzzle, and one move handles both. If the value of outcomes is bounded above, Bernoulli's logarithmic utility makes the St. Petersburg expectation converge; and the same bound defuses the mugging, because a minute probability multiplied by a bounded value is itself minute, and so falls short of the cost of paying. Fanaticism therefore appears only where the value scale is unbounded, or bounded so high that the product of the bound and a minute probability still exceeds the stakes.

If that is right, the fanaticism objection is not a complaint about how EVM handles risk; it is a complaint about EVM's presupposition that value has no ceiling. The intuition that fanaticism is absurd is at bottom the intuition that value is bounded, which is a claim in axiology rather than in decision theory. But the anti-fanatic then faces a trilemma. Set no ceiling and she is a fanatic. Set a finite ceiling and she must treat every outcome above it as equally good, which makes the theory insensitive to enormous differences at the top; set the ceiling astronomically high and fanaticism returns, since the product of that ceiling and a minute probability is still large. If instead she refuses to multiply probabilities by values below some threshold, she must name the threshold, and a gamble just above it must then beat any certain finite good — fanaticism again, merely displaced. That leaves only the denial that rational preference must be continuous, that is, lexicographic priorities and the loss of the standard expected-utility representation. I am inclined to think this last option is more respectable than it is usually allowed to be, but I cannot make it precise, and I suspect it only relocates the difficulty.

What I would like comments on, in order of importance: (1) whether the derivation is sound — in particular whether a bounded value scale really does defuse Pascal's mugging, or whether some version of the case survives it; (2) whether the trilemma is correctly drawn, or whether I have misdescribed one of the three options or omitted a fourth; (3) whether the shift from decision theory to axiology is legitimate or merely a relabelling; (4) whether my conclusion — that non-continuous preferences are the only remaining option — is too quick; (5) what the strongest form of the fanaticism objection is that I have not addressed, especially if it can be stated without assuming an unbounded value scale; and (6) any suggestions about structure and emphasis. Please be specific about where the argument fails rather than only where it could be expanded.
