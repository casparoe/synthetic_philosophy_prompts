I'm in the last stretch of a philosophy of medicine course, and the unit on the concepts of health and disease has left me more tangled up than anything else we've done. As it was presented to us, there are two broad families of view. On the first, calling something a disease is a descriptive matter through and through: it is a matter of some part or process of the body failing to do the job it is biologically supposed to do, whether or not the person suffers or is disadvantaged by it. On the second, calling something a disease is at bottom an evaluative matter: it is a judgment that the person is worse off, harmed, or prevented from doing things they care about, and no amount of biology settles that by itself. I can feel the pull of each, and I keep catching myself applying them inconsistently. What I want to know is whether there is a single principle, or a small set of them, that actually captures what I think — and if there isn't, which of my judgments I should give up.

Here are the judgments, roughly in the order I would defend them. A broken leg from a skiing accident is not a disease, even though something in the body is plainly not working; my grandfather's osteoarthritis is. A person who has just been bereaved and cannot eat or sleep for a month is not ill, while a person with the same symptoms who has lost no one and whose life is otherwise going well is. An asymptomatic carrier of a recessive gene is not diseased; a woman who has been trying to conceive for two years and cannot is; but a woman in the same physiological state who has never wanted children and feels fine is not — and that last one bothers me, because I cannot see what separates her from the second woman except her goals. Needing glasses to read is not a disease; the same eyesight in someone who loses his job over it is, and then I notice I want to say he had it all along and we merely found out. Male pattern baldness is not a disease; a person born without a hand is not diseased either, though something went wrong developmentally; someone who feels no pain at all is; and a man who has drunk heavily for years and cannot stop is, even in the years before his liver gives out. Finally, a person who is four foot eleven and healthy is not diseased, though I would not say the same of a person who is four foot eleven because his growth hormone failed.

I have a candidate principle of my own, and I would like it tested rather than endorsed: a condition is a disease when some part of the body fails to do the job it normally does in the species, and that failure makes the person worse off, with worse off measured against that person's own goals and circumstances rather than against any ideal. Please take it apart against the cases above and tell me precisely where it breaks, then either repair it or replace it, being explicit about which of my judgments has to be sacrificed to the version you end up defending. I would also like to know what the repaired principle says about three cases I have not made up my mind about: a person who is lactose intolerant in a society where everyone eats dairy and would be perfectly fine in one where no one does; a person who is nearsighted in a society with no writing; and a person with a genetic variant that produces no symptoms at all but reliably shortens life by ten years. I am not after reading recommendations or a survey of who said what — I want the argument, run against my cases.
