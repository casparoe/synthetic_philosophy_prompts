Assume the role of a philosopher who specializes in the moral responsibility of political communities and the ethics of historical memory. I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a metropolitan daily, and I am hoping you can help me think through a story I have been reporting for months. The city council here is scheduled to vote next week on a formal resolution apologizing for a wrong committed in the 1950s: the council, then all white, rezoned a thriving Black neighborhood as industrial and used eminent domain to clear it for a highway spur that was never built. The neighborhood was destroyed and its residents scattered. The resolution, as drafted, acknowledges and apologizes for the harm but includes no money, no land, and no policy changes. The mayor calls it a necessary first step. The descendants' group is split: some say an apology without reparations is an insult, a way for the city to feel good while changing nothing; others say it is a long-overdue recognition they have sought for decades. One council member says he personally had nothing to do with it and does not see why he should apologize for his predecessors; another says the city cannot apologize because a city is not a person. A local historian has shown me records suggesting the project was widely considered progressive at the time, which she says makes the apology anachronistic. And a detail that has come to seem important: the city was acting under a federal urban-renewal program, and the federal government has already issued its own apology, which has left some residents asking what, exactly, the city is apologizing for that has not already been apologized for.

I want to write a piece that helps readers think clearly about whether this particular apology is meaningful or empty, and I want to avoid both the cynical it-is-just-words take and the naive apology-is-enough take. Begin by brainstorming the philosophical structure of the situation and the distinctions that matter -- among an apology as a speech act, as an assumption of responsibility, as a political act, and as a form of recognition; between the city as a legal entity, as a community, and as a set of current individuals; between apologizing for an action, for a policy, and for a history; and between what is owed to the dead, to the living descendants, and to the current residents. Then give me a concrete framework for assessing this resolution: what would make it meaningful, what would make it empty, which of the objections I have described get something right and which miss the point, and what the descendants' competing demands presuppose about what an apology is for. Please be specific to this case rather than giving me a general essay on the topic, and think carefully about the hardest question: whether an apology can be owed by people who did not commit the wrong to people who were not its direct victims, and if so, what makes it more than a gesture. Finally, tell me what to listen for in the council debate itself -- what rhetorical moves would signal that the apology is being used as a substitute for material change, and what would signal that it is a genuine assumption of responsibility.
