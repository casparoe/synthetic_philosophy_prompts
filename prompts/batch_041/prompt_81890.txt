In a reading group I belong to, a colleague defends a hard line on what wealthy states owe the global poor by leaning on a lifeboat analogy, and I want to know how much weight that analogy can bear before I accept or reject the conclusion he draws from it. His version runs roughly as follows. We — the citizens of rich countries — are passengers in a lifeboat; the world's poor are swimmers in the water around us; the boat has finite provisions, and although it is not literally full, he contends that taking anyone else aboard would set a precedent that would eventually swamp it, so we are permitted to refuse to admit or assist them; and the fact that we did not push them into the water ourselves is beside the point, since what matters is only what admitting them would now cost us.

Please audit the analogy rather than the conclusion. Begin with an explicit table of correspondences: for each element of the story — the boat, the sea, the passengers, the swimmers, the provisions, the boat's capacity, whoever is at the helm, and the fact that we did not cause the drowning — say what it is meant to stand for in the target situation, and whether the target actually supplies a counterpart or whether the element has been imported from the story without warrant. Then sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the inference my colleague wants to draw and those that are merely decorative. In particular, address which features of the source scenario the analogy quietly smuggles in — a fixed and knowable carrying capacity, a zero-sum stock of resources, boats that cannot communicate, cooperate or be repaired, swimmers whose plight is causally independent of the passengers' conduct, and a precedent-setting mechanism that operates reliably in the source but may not in the target — and whether the target shares any of them. Say whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along, and note separately any place where the argument is really doing empirical work (about precedent, say) that the analogy cannot supply. If some other analogy would serve the underlying ethical question better, offer it and say what it captures that the lifeboat does not.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in British English.
