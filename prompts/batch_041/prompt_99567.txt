I'm a software engineer by training and have no formal background in philosophy, but I've been trying to understand the demandingness objection to consequentialism — the complaint that a maximizing consequentialist is committed to giving away nearly everything, since there is always some stranger whose marginal gain from your resources exceeds your marginal loss. Rather than read another summary, I'd like to watch the argument happen. Please write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers: one who holds that the demandingness objection is decisive against act consequentialism, and one who holds that it can be answered. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should score points by caricature; each should respond to the other's actual last move rather than restating a prepared position.

I don't want the familiar set piece about famine relief versus personal integrity. I'd like the exchange to turn on a specific wrinkle I keep running into and haven't seen addressed directly. Suppose the defender answers the objection by saying that the agent's own projects — family, health, a vocation, even rest — are themselves welfare-producing, and so are permitted up to the point where sacrificing them would cost the agent more welfare than it produces for others. My worry is that this reply has no stable baseline. Any project the agent cares about can be redescribed as welfare-producing, in which case the accommodation quietly expands until it has swallowed the objection entirely and consequentialism turns out to demand very little after all; but wherever the redescription is refused, the objection seems to return in full force, and the agent is back to being required to give until it hurts and then keep giving. So the defender's move looks either empty or fatal, and I can't tell which.

Build the dialogue around that tension. Have the objector press it early and clearly, have the defender attempt a genuine answer, and let them go back and forth for at least three or four substantive exchanges, each one engaging the specific point just made — whether the baseline is fixed by a principle or only by whatever happens to maximize, whether the demandingness of the theory is essential to it or merely contingent on empirical facts about how welfare is produced, and what happens to the objection if the defender's answer turns out to be contingent. Let them end either with a partial resolution or with a cleanly stated impasse, and please make the ending honest: no artificial tie, no cheap knockout, and no appeal to authority or citation of literature to settle it.

Include at least one concrete example, and since I think in engineering terms, a mundane workplace scenario would be welcome — but it has to do philosophical work in the argument rather than merely decorate it. Aim for roughly a thousand words. Close with whichever character is more honest about the state of play stating plainly what the dialogue has and has not established about whether the objection survives the redescription of the agent's projects as welfare-producing.
