I've been going around in circles on epiphenomenalism and I want someone to just settle it for me. The view goes: experiences are caused by brain processes but don't cause anything in turn, they're basically exhaust, like the steam off a kettle. The standard objection is that this makes our judgments about experience inexplicable. If my conviction that the red I'm looking at is vivid isn't caused by the vivid redness, why is that conviction reliably about the redness? (You'll see this called the paradox of phenomenal judgment.) The usual fix is the common-cause gambit: the experience and the judgment are both downstream of the same neural state, so the judgment tracks the experience even though the experience does no work.

To make this concrete, here's a species I made up, the Glowheads. Their visual system splits right after the retina. One branch goes to a classifier that identifies wavelengths and drives everything they say and do. The other branch goes to a seperate circuit that generates a slow, vivid, ineffable color glow, and nothing at all reads that circuit, it's a dead end with no axons leaving it. The same retinal signal feeds both, so every time a Glowhead says "red" there's a red glow running along side it, perfectly correlated, and the glow is what they'd call the feel of red. Their color talk is therefore totally reliable about their glows. Now the awkward part: rewire the glow circuit so a Glowhead glows blue while the classifier still outputs red. Ex hypothesi they can't notice, since nothing downstream reads the glow. So which thing was the report tracking, and does tracking even get you aboutness?

Write me an essay on whether epiphenomenalism survives this. I want you to (a) give the strongest version of the epiphenomenalist reply, including the common-cause gambit and the fancier move where the glow isn't the cause of the judgment but a constituent of it, so the seeming is partly made out of the glow; (b) say whether nomic covariation without causation is enough for a judgment to be about an experience, and whether it's enough for that judgment to be justified or to count as knowledge; (c) deal with the self-stultification worry, i.e. that the epiphenomenalist's own words would come out identically whether or not any glow ever happened; and (d) land on a verdict. If you think epiphenomenalism definately survives only in a weakened form, name the exact claim it has to give up.

Format: exactly five numbered points, each one a sentence or two, no intro and no wrap-up paragraph — the fifth point is where the verdict lands.
