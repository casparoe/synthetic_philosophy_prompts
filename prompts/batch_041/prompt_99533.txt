I am assembling teaching materials on individuation and the identity of indiscernibles, and I would like a decisive ranked assessment rather than a general survey. Assume familiarity with the two-spheres case: a universe containing exactly two spheres, alike in size, shape, color, mass, and every other intrinsic respect, separated by some distance in an otherwise empty space. The case is standardly taken to show that numerically distinct particulars can be qualitatively identical, and therefore that the identity of indiscernibles is false as a claim about particulars.

Below are six replies that a defender of the principle might offer. I have written them so that each is at least initially plausible; several are compressed, and at least one may collapse into another on reflection.

1. Relational reply: the spheres are not indiscernible, because each bears the relational property of being two miles from a sphere (and whatever other relations the arrangement supplies), and these relational properties are genuine properties that the spheres possess.

2. Identity-involving reply: each sphere possesses the property of being identical with itself and distinct from the other; sphere A has the property of being A, sphere B the property of being B, and since these are properties, the spheres are discernible after all.

3. Symmetry-is-a-defect reply: a world containing exactly two indiscernible spheres, with nothing anywhere to break the symmetry between them, is not a genuine possibility — the perfect symmetry is itself a defect in the specification — so the case never gets off the ground, and the principle is never actually threatened.

4. Structuralist reply: individuation is always relative to a structure. Within the two-sphere structure the two positions are distinguished only by their places in that structure, and those places are characterized purely by the symmetric relation each bears to the other. The principle should therefore be understood as a claim about positions in structures rather than about bare particulars, and in that form it survives.

5. Cardinality-only concession: there really are two spheres, and the only fact of the matter is that the world contains two of them; there is no further fact about which is which that could ever figure in a property. This concedes the principle's falsity while denying that anything of consequence follows.

6. Quantum-mechanical reply: in quantum mechanics, particles of the same kind are not individuated by any qualitative difference, and the formalism makes no sense of one particle being the one rather than the other. The two-sphere world is thus not merely possible but realized in ordinary physics, which shows that the principle was never a constraint on physical ontology.

Please sort these six into tiers according to how well they succeed as defenses of the identity of indiscernibles, and rank them within each tier. The dimension I care about is not whether a reply saves the bare letter of the principle, but whether it preserves the work the principle was doing — namely, that there is nothing to a particular's identity over and above its qualitative character, so that no bare particular and no primitive thisness is required to individuate things. A reply that saves the principle only by quietly reinstating identity or thisness as a property has, in my view, not defended it at all; place such replies in a lower tier, but state plainly why.

Then, separately:

(a) Name any entries that are not genuinely distinct from one another, and explain the collapse rather than counting them twice.
(b) State which reply, if any, you would adopt if you had to defend something in the neighborhood of the principle, and identify the single weakest point in your chosen reply.
(c) Give a one-line verdict on whether the two-spheres case refutes the principle as a claim about the actual world, as opposed to a merely possible one.

Keep each justification to a few sentences. I want the ranking to be decisive rather than hedged, and I would rather see a firm placement defended briefly than a cautious one defended at length.
