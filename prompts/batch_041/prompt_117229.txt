I've been making a radio series about how the state decides things about children, and I keep tripping over the same contradiction. On the books it's simple: the childs welfare is paramount, and you're supposed to take account of what the child themselves wants. Then you sit in on a family group conference, where the social worker hands the whole thing over to the family — aunties, grandparents, the lads older brother — and they go off into a room and come back with a plan for what happens to him. Nobody asks the twelve-year-old to cast a vote. The professionals are in the room but they aren't meant to be running it. And by most accounts it works rather better than the version where the council decides.

What I want is the theory underneath. Does that practice only make sense if you hold something like the Confucian line — role ethics, they call it — that a person just is their roles, that the boy is somebody's son and somebody's brother all the way down, and that what's good for him can't be worked out apart from what he owes and is owed inside that network? Or is it perfectly compatible with the plain liberal picture, where he's an individual with interests and rights and the family is just a very effective way of finding out what those interests are? I'd like the honest split between the bits the practice actually needs and the bits it merely sits comfortably with, because I suspect half the enthusiasm for it is just the practical point that families know things social workers don't.

And the bit I'm really stuck on. If you go the Confucian route — the self as a bundle of roles rather than a little man behind them — what happens when the roles are rotten? When the family's plan is to keep it all quiet, or when the grandfather's role is to be obeyed and he's a bully. Can that picture of a person generate any criticism of the roles themselves, or does it just have to hope the roles are decent? And tell me what would change on the ground if we dropped it: if we really took the individualist picture seriously and stopped treating the family as the unit, what would actually be different in that room?
