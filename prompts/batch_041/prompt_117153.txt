I'm a PhD student in political philosophy at work on a chapter about whether anything survives of desert-based meritocracy, and what I need from you is a document rather than an essay. Two colleagues of mine have spent two years arguing past each other. The first holds that a just society should distribute at least some goods and offices in proportion to desert, and that a well-designed meritocracy is the institutional form that aspiration takes; call her the desertist. The second holds that the appeal to desert in meritocratic argument is a rationalization, that what meritocratic institutions actually track is the distribution of unchosen advantage, and that justice should stop tracking merit at all; call her the abolitionist. I want the joint statement the two of them could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

Two stipulations, so that the document has to reason rather than recite. First, desert comes in two readings, and both parties must say which reading their own position requires. On the narrow reading, a person deserves a benefit exactly when the performance or outcome that the rule rewards issued from her own agency, and the facts that fix her deservingness are internal to that performance. On the wide reading, a person deserves a benefit when the benefit is fitting for her considered as a whole person, including the unchosen traits, capacities, and formative circumstances that explain why she performed as she did. Second, both parties must reason in the presence of a Laplacean audit: an oracle that reports, for any person, her counterfactual effort profile under every possible background condition, together with a complete causal decomposition of her actual effort into genetic, developmental, institutional, and agent-originated components. Assume the audit is infallible, and assume it is sometimes run.

The document should contain, in this order:

A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. Make these substantive rather than safe: what they agree the concept of desert can and cannot do; what they agree about the relevant empirical facts (for instance about the intergenerational transmission of advantage, and about the reliability of effort attributions by employers and admissions officers); what they agree justice requires whatever the answer to the desert question turns out to be; and which parts of popular meritocratic rhetoric they jointly reject. A claim that either side could sign only because it says nothing does not belong on the list.

A disagreements table, one row per genuine disagreement, with columns for the disagreement stated in neutral wording, the desertist's own formulation of her side, the abolitionist's own formulation of hers, and what would have to be true for each to concede. Each side's formulation must be one its own camp would accept as fair to it.

The cruxes: at most four sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. At least one must be probed by the Laplacean audit — in particular, say what each side predicts if the audit attributes all of the variance in effort to factors outside the agent's control, and what each predicts if it attributes a substantial share to agent-originated components. At least one must be probed by a case of your own construction involving a scheme that pays rewards provisionally on a desert score and then claws them back when new causal information about the sources of performance arrives: is retroactive revision required, permitted, or unjust, and why does each side predict as it does? For every crux, name the case, argument, or finding both parties agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Finally, say which parts of the dispute are verbal, and why, and describe what the remaining dispute looks like once the verbal part is set aside.

Do not cite literature or supply a reading list; the document must stand on its own. Close with a short statement of how confident you are in the document's central claims, and identify the single point in it you are least sure of.
