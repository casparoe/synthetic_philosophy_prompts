I am a novelist, and my working vocabulary is the vocabulary of craft: scene and sequel, want against need, obstacle and escalation, the turn, reversal, subtext, point of view, dramatic irony, the foil, the arc, and the recognition scene in which a character at last sees another, or herself, as she is. I know that vocabulary cold. I read Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit once, in translation, years ago; I retain the shape of the master–slave dialectic but not its terminology, so please gloss any term of art you need rather than assuming I remember it.

What I want is a translation, as faithful as you can make it, of the master–slave dialectic into my vocabulary, followed by a residue report. Take the dialectic apart into the moves it actually makes — the encounter between two self-consciousnesses, the demand for recognition, the struggle that puts life at stake, the one who submits, the resulting asymmetry, the discovery that the victor's triumph yields a recognition that is worth nothing because it comes from someone he does not acknowledge as a peer, and the subordinate's turn to labor and what that labor does to him — and render each move as it would appear in a novel: as a scene, a beat, a shift in status, a reversal, a change in what a character can want or bear to see.

Then the part I care about: what did not survive. Tell me which moves translate cleanly, which survive only as distortion and what the distortion is, and which have no counterpart in craft vocabulary at all. I expect part of the residue to be the speculative machinery — the claim that this encounter is a necessary stage in something larger, that history or Spirit is working behind the characters' backs — and I want to know whether that residue marks a deficiency in my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim is doing less than it appears to. But do not simply confirm my expectation; report what you actually find.

Finally, test the translation against a configuration I am currently writing and cannot get right: a translator and the poet she translates, where the translator is the subordinate party, unpaid, largely invisible, and nonetheless the one who keeps the poet alive in another language. There is no violence, no struggle with death at stake, and no submission — only a dependence that runs both ways and an asymmetry in who is seen. Tell me what the dialectic predicts here, what my scene does that the dialectic cannot describe, and whether the gap belongs to Hegel or to me.
