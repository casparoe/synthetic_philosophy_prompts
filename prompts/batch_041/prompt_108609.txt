A colleague in my economic history seminar has an argument that 'why did sustained growth take off in Britain and not in China?' is not merely hard but ill posed, and I cannot find where to stop it.

(1) A why-question has an answer only once its contrast class is fixed: what counts as the cause depends on which alternative we are asking about, so 'why P rather than Q' and 'why P rather than R' call for different explanations.
(2) Nothing in the Britain/China case fixes that contrast class. 'Britain' and 'China' are labels for shifting bundles of regions, institutions, and periods rather than natural units; 'take-off' has no threshold that everyone accepts; and the alternative to the actual outcome can be rewritten as 'the Yangzi delta and not the Lancashire cotton districts', or 'the eighteenth century and not the Song', each of which invites a different explanation.
(3) Where nothing fixes the contrast class, nothing selects among the explanations the evidence permits, so whatever we say answers a question we chose rather than the question we asked.
(4) So 'why Britain and not China?' has no answer. It only looks as though it has one because a default contrast class is smuggled in by the phrasing.
(5) The same reasoning reaches 'why did the vase break rather than stay intact?': 'the vase' is a bundle of parts, 'intact' has no sharp boundary, and the contrast class can be rewritten as 'why this vase and not that one', or 'why now and not an hour later'.
(6) If the argument is sound, then, it takes ordinary causal explanation down with it.

I want to keep (2) — that the units are our artifacts seems to me the genuinely interesting claim — so tell me which step to give up, why it looked right, and what giving it up costs. If I keep (2), what still licenses an answer to the original question, and what do I have to concede to the argument's author?
