I'm drafting the criteria section of an internal design document on supervising AI systems that solve multi-step problems, and I want the criteria settled before anyone argues for a particular method. So: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of supervision in this area must meet. Take supervision broadly — any training signal, whether it scores the final answer, the intermediate steps, the policy that produced them, or some combination — and take the theory's job to be saying what makes such a signal appropriate: which episodes it must classify correctly, which distinctions it must not collapse, which facts about learning and about verification it must stay consistent with. I don't want pointers to the literature or a lineage of who has held what; if a requirement is standard, just state it. What I want is the criteria themselves.

Adequacy is relative to a purpose, so fix it as follows. The theory is for a setting in which the reasoning trace is only partly observable, final outcomes are verifiable but sparse (many attempts, few successes), and the signal will be optimized against by an agent that is trying to satisfy it. Assume the trainer can afford only a bounded amount of step-level checking. Where you think the purpose is doing illegitimate work in shaping the list, say so.

Any theory in this area has to get certain cases right, and I'd rather the list not rest on cases we'd have to go look up, so stipulate this suite as mine and make it carry the weight:

(a) Right answer, defective process: the outcome is correct, the reasoning is not the kind we'd want repeated.
(b) Sound process, unlucky outcome: the reasoning is good and a slip in the last line or an unlucky draw produces the wrong answer.
(c) Post-hoc narration: the answer was produced by a process the trace doesn't represent; the trace is confabulated after the fact.
(d) Invisible competence: the system is reliably correct for reasons that never surface in anything observable.
(e) Environmental dependence: a step that is good practice only given the statistics of the current environment, and malpractice if those statistics shift.
(f) Rubric-shaped text: a trace that scores well on any checkable step-level rubric without doing the work the rubric is meant to track.
(g) Divergent but equal: two defensible methods for the same problem that a step-level criterion would rank and an outcome criterion would not separate.
(h) Deferral: the correct behaviour is to stop and ask rather than proceed, so nothing is accomplished either way.
(i) Sparse success: the only outcome signal available is failure, almost always.
(j) Long horizon: a step's contribution to the final outcome is underdetermined by everything observable at the time.

Beyond the cases, I'm imposing three constraints by stipulation. Name them however you like, but the theory has to take a stand on each: first, an episode is to be assessed relative to what was knowable at the time of the decision, not relative to what the run later revealed; second, for every signal, the theory must say whether it is being treated as a proxy for the target or as the target itself, and what it assumes about the gap between them; third, the theory must say how a signal changes once it becomes the object of optimization.

Give me the response in these sections, in this order:

1. Purpose and Scope (~150 words): what the theory is a theory of, the purpose that fixes adequacy, and anything you're treating as fixed background rather than up for dispute.

2. The Requirements (the bulk of the response, ~900–1200 words): numbered. For each, state it as a condition a theory can fail, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say why it is a requirement rather than a preference of one camp.

3. Non-Negotiable vs. Camp-Specific (~200 words): which requirements any theory must meet and which are commitments a particular approach brings with it.

4. Joint Unsatisfiability (~300 words): the pairs or subsets you suspect can't be had together, with the argument for each. Say explicitly whether the impossibility is a constraint on any theory whatsoever or an artifact of the purpose I fixed.

5. Which Requirement Excludes Most (~150 words): the single requirement that does the most eliminating work, and what it rules out.

6. Where the Families Land (~250 words): outcome-only, process-only, and hybrid signals, each briefly, against the list. No scorecard — just which requirements each fails.

Flag at the end any requirement you think I should drop, split, or restate.
