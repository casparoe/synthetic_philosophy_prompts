I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and there's a move I keep running into: in a thread about what a person, an institution, or a trained model "really" values, someone says to just look at the revealed preferences — the choices are the utility function, and anything the agent says about its own values is confabulation layered on top. I want to know what that one-liner could actually be asserting, because I suspect it slides between a nearly vacuous bookkeeping convention and a very strong substantive claim about welfare. I gather that on one reading, any sufficiently consistent pattern of choices can be described as maximizing some ranking, which would make the slogan close to unfalsifiable as a thesis about minds; yet it gets used as though it settled a question about what is good for someone. Lay out the distinct precise theses "revealed preference" might stand for: what a preference is on each reading, whether it is defined by choice or merely inferred from it, what would have to be true for the reading to hold, and how each reading handles cases where choices seem to run against the agent's own stated values — akrasia, addiction, preferences shaped by manipulation or by scarcity, and the awkward case of an agent whose choices are simply inconsistent. Say which readings are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which one is doing the work when the phrase is used to shut down a claim about what someone values. Don't worry about citations, I'll add those. And don't just hand me the menu and leave the choice to me — tell me which reading you think is correct and why, and where you think my suspicion is misplaced.
