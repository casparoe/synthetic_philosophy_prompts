I am a novelist midway through a book in which a historian of science travels back to 1919 to prevent a fire that destroyed a mentor's laboratory and the only copy of his final manuscript. The fire, it turns out, is caused by the equipment she brings back to save him: the fireproof case containing the manuscript is what ignites the solvent, and the manuscript she carries is the one she read as a child, the one that set her on her path. I have been calling this the archivist's paradox. In my writers' group we have been arguing about it for months, and the argument has settled into a rut. One friend, a physicist by training, insists that the only coherent treatment is a single, self-consistent timeline: any apparent change is either impossible or already part of the history that led to the traveler's departure, so the fire cannot be prevented because it was always caused by the attempt to prevent it. Another friend, a philosopher, says this is a confusion: the whole point of time travel stories is that the past is a place one can visit and alter, and if the laws of physics forbid that, so much the worse for those laws; on her view, the paradox is not a logical contradiction but a proof that time travel requires branching timelines or a revision of causality. A third friend, who writes crime fiction, says both are dodging the real issue: the paradox only feels like a paradox because we smuggle in a notion of personal identity and agency from ordinary life, and once you see the time traveler as just another physical system in a block universe, there is nothing left to explain.

We have read the standard moves -- the self-consistency principle, branching time, the block universe, the distinction between changing the past and affecting it -- and yet nobody has shifted. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of time and causation, a verbal dispute about what 'change' and 'the past' mean, or three people answering different questions under the single heading of the grandfather paradox? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or just that it is hard? Use the fire in my novel as the test case, and tell me what would count as progress -- a distinction, a formal result, a change of question -- or why no progress should be expected. My own hypothesis is that the physicist and the philosopher are not disagreeing about the same modality: one is asking what is physically possible given our laws, the other what is logically or conceptually possible. But I also suspect the deeper confusion is between the past as a region of spacetime and the past as a settled history that might be edited, and that the crime writer's block-universe move may dissolve the paradox only by changing the subject. If either diagnosis is right, say what follows for how I should write the scene, and what the other two should concede.
