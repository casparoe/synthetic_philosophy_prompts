I'm writing a short poem to open the last session of a reading group that has spent the term on prediction and long-run history, and I want the poem to do the work of the outside view rather than merely mention it. The outside view, as I understand it, is the habit of forecasting a case by dropping it into a class of similar past cases and taking the distribution of how those went — base rates instead of this-case particulars. Here's the shape I have in mind. Six stanzas of four lines each, twenty-four lines total; lines one and three rhyme, line two is free, and the fourth line of every stanza is the same line, verbatim, never varied. Each of the first five stanzas is about a different polity at the point just before it came apart; I'd like the list to run from the obvious (a Mediterranean empire, a Chinese dynasty, a twentieth-century continental state) to at least one case a reader might object doesn't belong in the list at all. No dates, no battles, nothing invented: the details should be things a specialist in that place and period would recognize as right, and if you're unsure of a detail, leave it out and let the stanza be thinner. The sixth stanza is about the reader's own country, present tense, first person plural, and it has to be formally indistinguishable from the other five — same length, same rhyme, same closing line. Fill that last stanza with the case for our exemption, made as strongly as you honestly can: our technology, our institutions, our records of the others, our data. I want the reader to feel the pull of it and then feel the form refuse to budge.

Two tensions I want left open rather than resolved. The first is between the outside view and the intuition — I've seen it called the Lindy effect — that for things that aren't wearing out, the longer they've lasted the longer they should be expected to last. If we're just a draw from the distribution of durations in some reference class, then having lasted this long tells us nothing about how much longer we get, and a clock with no memory is the honest picture; but that seems to leave out everything that makes the long-lived cases long-lived. I can't tell whether those two are in genuine conflict or whether they're just different reference classes talking past each other, and I don't want the poem to pretend the question is settled. The second is that the list of cases is chosen by me, after the fact, and I want that visible: the odd case out is there so a reader can ask why it's in the list, which is the question I want to keep asking about every reference class anyone hands me. End on the refrain doing its work, not on a couplet that tells me what to conclude.

After the poem, give me a short note — three or four sentences — on where it takes liberties with the ideas, and say plainly if my framing is off. In particular, if "the outside view means our track record buys us nothing" is a caricature of how anyone actually builds these forecasts, or if the contrast with the Lindy intuition is a category error rather than a real tension, tell me so instead of writing the poem around it.
