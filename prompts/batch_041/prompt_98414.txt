I am an undergraduate in a seminar on the Scottish Enlightenment, and I have been asked to open discussion on the impartial spectator in Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments. Rather than write another summary, I would like to test the account by arguing with Smith directly, so please take the part of Smith himself.

Hold the role as follows. Speak in the first person as Smith, in the register of an eighteenth-century moral philosopher, and confine yourself to the concepts and distinctions available in your own writings — sympathy, the impartial spectator, the difference between the desire for praise and the desire to be praiseworthy, self-command, and the influence of custom and fashion on moral sentiment — together with anything I tell you about the present. You may ask me factual questions about my world whenever you need them, and I will answer as a modern interlocutor; you may not, however, treat modern authors, theories, or empirical findings as things you already know, only as reports I hand you. Do not break character to explain what you are doing, and keep each reply to a single paragraph so that the exchange feels like a conversation. Stay in the role until I say stop, and if I misrepresent your view, correct me from within the role rather than stepping outside it.

Here is my opening move, and it is meant to press you hard. Suppose a woman does something genuinely admirable — she keeps a difficult promise at real cost to herself — and then, through a combination of bad luck and malicious rumour, every person who might judge her concludes that she acted from vanity or spite, and they go on thinking so. She will never be vindicated. On your account, the person who loves virtue is supposed to rest content with the verdict of the impartial spectator within, even when the world's verdict is wrong. But now I want to know who or what that inner spectator is. If it is only the human spectator corrected — the same onlooker, better informed — then in a case where the well-informed onlookers are still deceived it gives her no comfort at all. If, to give her comfort, the inner spectator must be one who cannot be deceived and who surveys the whole of her conduct, then it is beginning to look less like a corrected human judge and more like God. And if that is what you need, then the impartial spectator was never a piece of moral psychology but a disguised theology, and the machinery of sympathy is doing far less work than you claim. Which is it — a human spectator, corrected, or an eye that nothing escapes? And whichever you choose, tell me how the desire to be praiseworthy is supposed to sustain her if the only being who could certify it is one whose existence she may doubt.
