I'm a novelist — three novels, none of them bestsellers — and for my sins I sit on the editorial board of a small literary quarterly here in the UK. We're relaunching the website, and our editor has proposed replacing the signed review-essays with something she's calling 'The Commons': an anonymous, continuously-updated wall where any reader can post a reaction to anything we publish, no name, no byline, sorted by 'heat'. She says it's more democratic, more alive, more of the moment. Two of the older board members are appalled and have definately started quoting Kierkegaard at us — the stuff about the press manufacturing a 'public' that's really just a phantom, nobody in particular, and how anonymity lets everyone chatter and no one be answerable. I've read a bit of Kierkegaard but honestly I get lost in it, and I don't want Thursday's meeting to turn into duelling quotations.

I have to circulate a short written opinion before that meeting, so please draft it for me. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt, reject, or adopt-with-conditions, with a time frame — and give the reasoning in a way my fellow board members could actually argue with, engaging the editor's case at its strongest rather than just restating the worry. And I'd like you to press on something I think we keep muddling: anonymity of the *writer* and the indefinite *addressee* (the 'public' as opposed to a real, particular reader) seem to me to seperate, and I suspect Kierkegaard's target is more the second than the first. So tell me whether the Commons really is the thing he's warning about, or whether we're just using him as a stick to beat a feature we happen to find tacky. One board member wants a 'signature test' (nothing goes up that no one could be answerable for); another wants a 'no-address test' (does the thing address a particular reader, or an indefinite public?). Feel free to say which, if either, is a workable rule we could put in the relaunch spec.

The whole thing in British English, please, and end with a last line beginning 'Verdict:' giving the decision in a few words, so I can paste it straight into the minutes.
