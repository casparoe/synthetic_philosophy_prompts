I'm in a little Sunday reading group with a couple of physics grad students and two philosophers, and we've spent what feels like three months circling the same spot: many-worlds and the Born rule. The setup is by now boring to everyone -- if the wavefunction is the world and everything just evolves unitarily, then every outcome happens, so where does the number the rule spits out come from, and why would an agent in one branch be rational to bet on the outcome with the bigger amplitude? The odd part is that nobody in the room disputes the formalism or the predictions. We all agree the other branches don't go away and we all agree the usual statistics have to fall out somehow. And yet the arguement never lands. One of us says the derivations that pull the rule out of symmetry or decision-theoretic axioms are circular, because they smuggle in something like a flat prior over branches or an assumption about how a rational agent weights a branch when choosing; another says that's fine, the rule was never something the physics owed us, it's a norm for agents and the theory just tells you which branches exist; a third thinks the whole thing is a pseudo-problem and that once you treat probability as an epistemic matter about self-locating uncertainty the question dissolves. I keep feeling they're all answering different questions and calling it the same one.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than just assume, is that 'probability' is doing two seperate jobs here and the debate stalls because a solution to one gets sold as a solution to the other: a bookkeeping job (reproduce the relative frequencies we actually find in our records, given that only one branch's worth of records is ever available to us) and a deliberative job (tell an agent in a branch how to weight outcomes when choosing). A derivation that assumes you already know how to weight branches for decision purposes delivers the first but not the second, and one that simply posits that a rational agent should be indifferent among branches just asserts the second and calls it physics. So tell me: is that the right diagnosis of the impasse, or is there a genuine formal gap underneath -- something provable about which assumptions are independent, a no-go result, a theorem showing the derivations can't get what they claim -- that makes this a real disagreement in disguise? And if it is conceptual, is it a verbal fight over the word 'probability', or a substantive disagreement about what a physical theory is even supposed to explain (do the other branches' records count as data that need a likelihood, or not)? I'd also like an honest answer about what would count as progress -- a theorem, a clean distinction between kinds of probability, an admission that one side is answering a question the other isn't asking, or nothing at all and we should just stop meeting. Please actually engage the positions as I've stated them instead of giving me a tour of interpretations.
