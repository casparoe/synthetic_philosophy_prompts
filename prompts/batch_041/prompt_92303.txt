You are a comparative political economist — the kind who has sat in on finance-ministry briefings in Copenhagen and Oslo, knows the Anglophone empirical literature well enough to be caustic about it, and has actually watched a coalition negotiate. I am giving a thirty-minute talk at a policy institute next month and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. No reading lists or citations; I know the literature. I want the questions.

My thesis: the Nordic model is exportable, but almost nobody tries to export the parts that travel. What travels is the compliance architecture and the organized-employer bargain. What doesn't travel is the headline tax rate or the generosity of benefits. And the causal arrow runs mostly from institutions to trust, not the reverse.

My argument, in sketch: (1) evasion is made practically hard and socially visible — payroll withholding, third-party reporting, returns that arrive pre-filled — so honest filing is the default rather than a moral achievement, and reciprocity does the rest; (2) universal benefits with low take-up friction sustain political support in a way tightly means-tested benefits do not, because targeting turns the middle class into a client of the tax authority rather than a beneficiary of it; (3) wage compression is bought from employers rather than imposed on them — employers' confederations trade wage restraint for industrial peace and a seat at the table on labour-market policy, with flexicurity as the sweetener; (4) small open economies with concentrated export sectors have a structural reason to want compensation and predictability, which is why the package holds together as a package.

The audience, and who says what:
- Two comparative political economists who know the Danish, Swedish and Norwegian cases in detail; they will attack the exportability framing as a category error — the model is a bundle, and unbundling it is the fantasy of a foreigner.
- A public-finance economist whose toolkit is labour-supply elasticities and deadweight loss; he thinks my story is a culture story with a footnote about withholding.
- A political scientist who works on the deep-history roots of social trust; he will say my causal arrow runs the wrong way, that trust is inherited from pre-industrial institutions, and that high-tax, high-trust societies are a selected sample.
- A union economist who will be annoyed by (3), because it makes employers the co-authors of the model and workers the beneficiaries of a bargain they never struck.
- A state legislator who wants one sentence on why her state could do this, and will ask what happens to the tax base when the rich leave.

Give me eight or so objections, ordered by likelihood times damage, each phrased in the vocabulary of the specific person who would raise it; for each, a two-minute reply and an explicit note on where that reply is soft or where I am bluffing. Flag anything I should concede up front. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who actually know these countries from what is genuinely contested, and do not hand me a contested position dressed up as consensus. Brainstorm the audience's priors before you write the sheet.
