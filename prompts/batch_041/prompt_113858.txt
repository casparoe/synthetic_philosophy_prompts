I retired from a chemistry department a few years back and have been reading alot of philosophy since, mostly on causation, and I'd like to know whether my gut verdicts on cases actually hang together. Here they are. My leaving the incubator door ajar killed the culture — if I hadn't, it would have been fine. Two heaters in the lab, each strong enough to warm the room on its own; A came on first and B never did; I say A warmed the room, even though had A not come on, B would have. My neighbor's plants died while she was in hospital because nobody watered them, and I say the not-watering caused the deaths, though nothing happened at all. Now the one that bugs me: I set the thermostat to 21 and the room sat at 21.5 by noon; had I set it to 22 it would have reached 22.5; I don't think my setting it to 21 rather than 22 caused the room to be 21.5, even though it plainly made a difference. I do think the thermostat's switching on caused the heater to come on, though my engineer friend says its all one current flowing and switching on is just our description of it. And whether A counts as a cause at all seems to depend on whether we put B's valve in the picture in the first place, which feels like cheating, but I also think did-A-cause-it is a malformed question until you say rather than what.

My tentative principle: a cause is a difference-maker at the right level of description, and the level is fixed by the question being asked. I gather some people say causation is just counterfactual dependence and others that it's about what would happen under an intervention, and I can't tell which of those my verdicts commit me to. Test my principle against the cases, tell me exactly where it fails, and then give me the best principle you can, including its verdict on a case of your own choosing that I haven't listed. Don't hand me a menu of options — pick a side, defend it, and say straight out whether I have to drop one of my calls.
