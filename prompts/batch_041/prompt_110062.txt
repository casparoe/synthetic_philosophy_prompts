I'm a third-year PhD student in ML, mostly working on RLHF and preference modeling, and I keep getting dragged into this argument at reading group about whether reward models are "just" doing utilitarian social choice. Someone brought up Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and I realized I can only half-reconstruct it. I took one grad seminar on decision theory back in my master's so I can follow the math if you don't skip steps, but I don't know the moral philosophy literature at all. Here's the theorem as I remember it: if each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy expected utility, and the social preference order does too (or at least is a continuous weak order that respects Pareto), then the social welfare function has to be a weighted sum of the individuals' utilities. Then Harsanyi adds a separate move on top, something about a rational agent behind a veil of ignorance assigning equal probability to being each person, which is what pins the weights to equal and gets you to utilitarianism proper rather than just "some weighted sum." Tell me if I'm mangling any of that.

What I want: restate that whole thing as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use day to day — reward models, chosen/rejected pairs, Bradley-Terry and Plackett-Luce losses, rater pools, the KL penalty to the reference policy, the fact that we're fitting a single scalar function to a pile of pairwise labels. Go step by step and flag where you're fudging. Don't hand me a pretty analogy and call it a translation; I care about the parts that don't carry over.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which axioms survive the trip and which don't? My guess is Pareto is roughly "we don't flip preferences the raters unanimously agree on" and pretty much everything else either gets distorted or just vanishes — the veil of ignorance especially, since my "individuals" are annotators from a labeling vendor and nobodys assigning equal probability to being one of them. So is the equiprobability move just gone, or is there some faint counterpart in how we sample and weight raters? And the reverse direction, which I'm more curious about: is there stuff RLHF does that Harsanyi's setup can't even state — the KL term tethering the policy to the base model, the fact that the "social preference" we learn doesn't have to be a coherent ordering at all, the fact that we're aggregating preferences over the model's outputs rather than over world-states? Last thing: I want to know if the "RLHF = utilitarian aggregation" line is doing real work, or if the alignment folks who say it are just unknowingly re-deriving the classic objections to Harsanyi — the ex ante vs. ex post thing where the ex-ante framework happily picks a coin flip that the ex-post view recoils from, and the impartiality vs. impersonality complaint about the veil. If it's the latter, just say so. I'd rather find out we're reinventing wheels than get a tidy false map.
