I want to defend a thesis about historical explanation, and I'd like you to tell me what would count against it. The thesis: for most significant historical events, the outcome was overdetermined in a strong sense — there exist several mutually independent causal chains, each of which was on its own sufficient for the outcome, so that the event would still have occurred had any one of its apparent causes been absent. Call the gap between the full set of causes actually present and the smallest subset of them sufficient for the outcome the event's causal slack; the substantive claim is that important historical events characteristically have large causal slack. If that is right, then counterfactual questions of the form "would it have happened without X?" are usually not merely hard but indeterminate, and the felt determinacy of such questions is an artifact of our singling out one salient cause.

Assume the thesis is stated carefully enough to avoid both the trivial reading, on which every event has many causes, and the vacuous reading, on which "sufficient" just means "necessary in the circumstances." What I want is a map of its exposure. Which observations, arguments, formal results, or concrete historical cases would count against it — and, more usefully, which of those would be decisive rather than merely awkward? What does the thesis predict that its rivals do not, such that it could be caught out? Which of the standard rescue maneuvers — widening the causal set until sufficiency is achieved, retreating from sufficiency to mere probability-raising, treating the independence of the chains as an artifact of our descriptions rather than a feature of the world — are legitimate qualifications, and which amount to insulation? I would especially like to know whether there is any event for which the honest verdict is "this one had little causal slack," and what a defender would say about it. Keep separate what the thesis's own defenders would concede counts against them and what only its critics allege.

Tell me how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
