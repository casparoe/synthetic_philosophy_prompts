I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've spent a few weekends trying to understand the hole argument in general relativity. Here is the setup as I understand it, so that you can correct me if I have it wrong: take a solution of the field equations, apply a transformation that is the identity everywhere outside some bounded region and not the identity inside it, and you get another solution; the two agree on everything outside the region and disagree inside it; so if the points of the manifold are genuine individuals with their own identities, the theory seems to leave the contents of the region undetermined by its own past. I can follow the mathematics fine. What I cannot do is tell how much of the surrounding debate is a real disagreement and how much is two camps talking past each other.

I want the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the two main camps would produce, written so that both camps could put their names to it. The first camp holds that spacetime is a thing in its own right, whose points are real individuals, so the two models describe two genuinely different situations and the argument is a real threat -- either indeterminism, or a reason to doubt that the theory is fundamental. The second camp holds that there is nothing to spacetime over and above the pattern of relations among events and fields, so the two models describe one and the same situation and the argument dissolves. I am also aware of a third position -- that spacetime points are real but have no identity over and above the structural roles they occupy -- and I want to know whether that is a stable third option or a relabeling of the second, since a great deal of the discussion seems to hang on that question.

Please draft the following, in this order:

1. A short joint statement of the argument itself, in a form that both camps accept as a fair statement of what is at issue, including what the two models do and do not share and what the argument does not establish.
2. A numbered list of agreed claims: what both camps can sign about the formalism, about what the two models share and fail to share, about what is not in dispute (I expect empirical predictions, the mathematics, and the impossibility of any experiment distinguishing them), and about what would count as progress.
3. The residual disagreements, each stated twice -- once in wording the first camp would accept as its own, once in wording the second camp would accept as its own -- with a note on what turns on each. Follow this with a table: the question at issue, the two formulations, and what would change for each side if the question were settled one way rather than the other.
4. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side. I would guess that they include whether determinism is a claim about models or about situations-up-to-isomorphism, and whether the identity of manifold points is primitive or structural, but I want your list and your reasons. For each crux, give the argument or case that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If any crux turns out to be verbal, say which one and what the underlying non-verbal question is.
5. A section on self-undermining evidence: what each camp would accept as counting against its own view, and what would move neither.
6. A final section that reruns the whole thing on a discrete toy analogue, since that is how I would check whether the disagreement is an artifact of the continuum. Replace the manifold with a finite graph, replace the metric with labels on the edges, and take graph automorphisms as the analogue of the transformations in question. Does the argument reappear? Does the claim that isomorphic models describe one and the same situation -- which I have seen called Leibniz equivalence -- have a clean discrete counterpart, and does discreteness help either side? I am especially interested in whether the position that points have no primitive identity becomes easier or harder to state when the structure is finite and everything can be defined up to automorphism.

Two constraints. First, no citations, no bibliography, and no appeals to particular papers or authors that I would have to take on trust; I have no way to check them, and I would rather have the reasoning than the attribution. If a term of art matters, define it in the document rather than gesturing at whoever coined it. Second, be honest about where the document is straining: if a claim can only be made to sound neutral by being made vacuous, say so instead of papering over it, and if you think part of the dispute is about words, name the part and explain what makes it verbal. I would rather have a document that flags its own soft spots than one that reads as authoritative.
