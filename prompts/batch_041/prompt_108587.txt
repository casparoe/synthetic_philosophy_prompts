Last week in a seminar on the philosophy of pleasure a classmate handed round an argument that I have not been able to answer, and I cannot tell whether it is airtight or whether something slips past me somewhere in the middle of it. (1) If two experiences are both pleasures, there is some property they share in virtue of which each of them is a pleasure. (2) Consider five experiences: the relief of finally scratching a mosquito bite you had been told to leave alone; the satisfaction of closing a proof you had been stuck on for a week; the warmth of a bath on a cold morning; the pleasure you take in a rival's humiliation; and the diffuse, objectless well-being of a strong dose of an opiate. (3) These five experiences have no common phenomenal quality. The itch-relief is a sensation of release, located in your skin; the proof-satisfaction is an attitude toward something you did; the bath-warmth is a bodily sensation with a location and a temperature; the gloating is a feeling about someone else's misfortune; the opiate well-being is a mood with no location and no object. (4) If there is no common phenomenal quality, there is no property shared by all pleasures. (5) So pleasure is not a single kind. (6) If pleasure is not a single kind, then a utilitarian who tells us to maximize pleasure is not telling us to do anything, because there is no one thing to maximize. (7) So hedonistic utilitarianism is not false but empty. She takes (6) to be the interesting step, and (7) to be a real result about the whole hedonist tradition rather than a quibble about a word.

I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (6). The five descriptions seem right to me, and I am not yet ready to give up on pleasures having something in common, but I also do not want to defend a view whose central quantity turns out to be a fiction. Two friends have offered me rescues and I want to know whether either is worth its price. The first says the shared property is not a felt quality at all but a relation: pleasure is a process running without resistance, a fit between what you are doing and what you want from it, so the differences catalogued in (3) are differences in content rather than in what makes something a pleasure. The second, who works in a lab, says the argument is a verbal dispute from beginning to end, since pleasure just is whatever the relevant brain circuit does, and the five cases are simply five inputs to one mechanism.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict: which step should go, what made that step look fine in the first place, and what it costs to give it up. If the honest answer is that the five experiences really do share nothing, then I need to be told how to state the hedonist's aggregation claim without the unity premise, and what that does to the claims that depend on it — the utilitarian calculus, the experience machine, and the thought that a life with more pleasure in it is for that reason a better life. If the honest answer is instead that the argument is broken, I want to see where, and I want the repair to be more than a relabelling of the same difficulty.
