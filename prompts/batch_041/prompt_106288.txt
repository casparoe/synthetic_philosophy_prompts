I lead the platform team at a mid-sized software company, and for about a year now we've been shipping what our product colleagues insist on calling autonomous agents — things that read support tickets and draft replies, that move ad spend between campaigns on their own, that merge dependency updates into production when the tests pass. Every one of these goes through a weekly design review that I chair, and the review keeps degenerating into the same unproductive argument: one engineer says the system is too dumb to be trusted with anything irreversible, another says we're insulting a model that has read the whole internet, and we end up voting on vibes. I don't have any formal philosophy training, but I came across a framework that has been quietly clarifying these fights, and I'd like to make it the backbone of the review rather than the hand-waving it currently is. The idea — I'm fairly sure it's Daniel Dennett's, from something he wrote about the kinds of minds — is that minds sit on a rough ladder of four grades. Darwinian creatures have a fixed repertoire and learn nothing during their own lives; whatever works was selected for over generations, and the individual is essentially a lookup table. Skinnerian creatures can learn within a lifetime by trial and error, adjusting their behavior from reinforcement and feedback. Popperian creatures can run the trial and error internally, simulating candidate actions and letting their hypotheses die in their place instead of dying themselves. And Gregorian creatures add tools, above all thinking tools taken over from other minds and from culture, which let them offload and extend their cognition. My instinct is that most of our internal arguments are really arguments about which rung a given feature belongs on, and that we've been sloppy about the difference between what the task requires and what we can safely afford to build.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run in a design review — not a summary of the framework, and not a reading list. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it deliberately hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values or risk appetite rather than fact. I need it to do two jobs: first, to settle the minimum grade of creature a proposed task actually demands, and second, to diagnose what grade a proposed system really is as opposed to what its authors claim — because my teams consistently oversell a feedback loop as reasoning, or a single internal check as simulation, and I'd like a small set of tests they can run to catch that. The constraints are real: it has to be runnable in about twenty minutes by engineers who have not read any philosophy, and it has to treat actions differently depending on whether they are cheap and reversible or expensive and irreversible, since our ticket drafter can be wrong all day and our spend agent cannot. The final call gets signed off by a staff engineer and our safety reviewer, so the procedure needs to say clearly which steps produce evidence and which steps produce a decision someone has to own.

Then run it, out loud, on three proposals: a ticket triage and draft-reply assistant that sends nothing until a human clicks send; a bidding agent that reallocates ad budget autonomously within a daily cap and learns from what happens; and a bot that auto-merges dependency bumps to production whenever the test suite goes green. At least one of those three should come out of the procedure with an answer my team will hate, and I want you to tell me which and why rather than softening it. There's a wrinkle I'd like you to address directly, because it's where our intuition keeps breaking: every one of these systems calls a large foundation model that has, in some sense, absorbed a great deal of human writing and tool use, and some of my engineers have started calling anything that does that Gregorian by default. Tell me whether that use of the distinction survives contact with the framework, or whether it collapses two different things. Finally, be honest about the edges: where the ladder is a hierarchy of sophistication but sophistication is not the same as suitability, where a fixed Darwinian policy is the correct and boring answer, where the procedure will misfire, and which of its steps are really judgment calls wearing the costume of a procedure.
