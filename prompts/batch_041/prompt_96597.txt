I've been asked to run a two-hour session for a team of about fifteen engineers who build consumer home robots — mostly ML people, a couple working on the assistant's "personality", plus one product lawyer who sits in on these things. The topic I've been handed is sentience as a matter of degree: moral status sliding from insects down through pets and fish to thermostats, with their vacuum cleaner somewhere in the middle. They'll walk in assuming sentience is binary and that the real question is whether a machine "really" feels anything, and I expect a fight about whether a dimmer-switch view just makes every line arbitrary.

Don't explain the topic to me — plan how I teach it. What has to be in place before "a matter of degree" even means anything to them? My guess is they'll collapse degree-of-sentience into degree-of-intelligence, and also into probability-that-there's-anyone-home, and I want those pried apart before anything moral shows up. Give me the order the pieces have to go in so the moral-status part doesn't arrive before they can state the distinction themselves.

For the case that carries it, I want something they can't have read about: a thing whose entire experiential life is one brief flash of unpleasantness, once, never remembered, no self, no sense of time. Should that carry the session, or does it beg the question against them from the start? If it won't work, say what should, and tell me what the case I do use has to have and has to lack.

Then the misconception map: the wrong turns they'll actually take, in the order they'll take them, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off — including the ones that only show up after they've swallowed the degree talk (that a continuum means there are no thresholds anywhere; that a little sentience licenses a little suffering; that sentience is measurable and someone should go build the meter; that anything with a feedback loop is therefore slightly sentient, which would ruin the thermostat; that this is really a compliance question for the lawyer).

Finish with one question to put to the room at the end that tells me wether they've got it or just got queasy, and what I should say out loud that I'm not covering at this level.

Format it as: Sequence — numbered, one line per step, about twelve lines; The carrying case — a short paragraph plus a line on what it must lack; Misconception map — one paragraph per misconception, six or so, each ending with where in the sequence it gets headed off; The closing question — two or three sentences, with what a good answer sounds like; Off the table — bullets, three or four.
