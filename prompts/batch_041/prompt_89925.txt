I'm in a small reading group that's been chewing through work on evidential decision theory, and last session we got onto the idea that people seem to call evidential cooperation in large worlds — roughly, if the universe is big enough (or if my decision procedure is instantiated many times over, whether in other civilisations or other branches or whatever), then there are agents whose choices are correlated with mine even though we'll never causally interact, and under evidential decision theory I should treat my own choice as evidence about what they'll do. Which sounds like it means cooperating with them could be worth astronomically more than anything happening locally. I have to lead the session next week and I've written a short draft, and honestly I'd rather be torn apart by you first than in front of everyone. Could you give me comments on it?

Draft, about 400 words:

"Cooperation without contact, and why it can't yet tell you what to do.

Suppose my decision procedure is not unique. If the world is large, something like it runs elsewhere — in other agents, in other branches, in descendants of the same training process. Evidential decision theory says: choose the act that is best news about the world. Since my act is evidence about theirs, acting as a cooperator is evidence that they are cooperators, and the returns from their cooperation land on me as surely as if we had shaken hands. Given astronomical numbers of such agents, the stakes swamp every ordinary consideration. That's the picture, and I find it compelling.

Here is my problem. The size of the gain depends entirely on the reference class — which agents count as correlated with me. And that isn't a fact about the world waiting to be discovered; it's fixed by which decision procedure I'm running. An agent who counts every agent who reasons as I do gets one answer. An agent who counts only agents sharing my physical implementation gets another. Since adopting a reference class is itself a choice, the theory cannot be used to choose between them: you need the answer before you can compute the answer. If instead you say 'pick the reference class that maximises expected value', you get a hunt for fixed points, and there will usually be several — including ugly ones, where the reference class is 'agents who will punish anyone like me'.

My fix is causal anchoring. Require that the correlation be traceable to a common cause in my actual causal past: a shared ancestor of the procedure, a shared origin, something in the history. Then the reference class is an empirical matter and not something I get to stipulate.

But I suspect this kills the view. If the correlation is fully explained by a common cause that is already settled, then my act doesn't influence theirs, and ordinary causal reasoning says do whatever is best locally — the correlation screens off. So causal anchoring may be a polite way of reinstating causal decision theory and quietly giving up the astronomical gains. The view survives only if the common cause is my decision procedure itself, which is precisely the case where the whole causal/evidential dispute is live and unresolved."
