I have been trying to work out, on my own, Simone Weil's hostility to political parties — the thought, as I understand it, that a party is not merely a group of people who happen to agree but a kind of mechanism that manufactures a shared passion and then quietly demands that its members serve the mechanism and the cause rather than attend to what is actually true, so that even decent people end up thinking and saying things they would never have arrived at alone. I would like this tested against a constructed case rather than restated as a slogan, and the point of the case is to see whether the objection survives contact with a fair example rather than to illustrate a conclusion I have already reached.

Please design a thought experiment with a built-in control. Put it in an ordinary, real setting — a tenants' association, a village council, a housing co-op, something of that kind — and make every person in it decent, with no villain anywhere in the story. Give me two versions. In the first, the people are organized as a party; in the second, it is the same people, the same grievance, and the same facts, organized without a party. The only thing that changes between the versions is the party-form itself, and the bad outcome in the first version has to fall out of that form rather than be stipulated in advance or caused by a bad rule or a bad actor. Keep each version to a paragraph.

Then walk me through what Weil's view predicts about each version and why, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, tell me what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and say how someone who believes parties are indispensable to democratic life would attack it. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
