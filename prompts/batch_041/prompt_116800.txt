I recently left a job in agricultural technology — six years on precision-irrigation systems — and in the months since, a question I used to treat as a dinner-party provocation has become something I think about seriously: whether the Neolithic transition to farming was, on balance, a mistake for the people who lived through it and for those who came after. My brother, who is finishing a doctorate in archaeology, pushes me toward the strong version of the claim: that the shift to cultivation and sedentism in the Near East and elsewhere reduced average human welfare relative to the immediately preceding forager lifeways — through narrowed diet, greater infectious load, harder and more repetitive labor, and the accumulation of storable surplus and therefore of hierarchy — and that everything downstream of it, including the world I helped build, inherits that deficit. I am not asking you to adjudicate whether the claim is true. I want the implications.

Suppose we grant it, in the strong form just stated. What are the most important implications — the ones that actually constrain how a person should think and act, not the ones that merely make for good seminar conversation? Please organize them by the arena in which they bite, and be explicit about which of them are conditional on the strong reading and which survive a much weaker one (say, that early farming was roughly a wash, or that the welfare comparison is not even well-defined because forager and farmer preferences are not commensurable). I am especially interested in three things. First, whether the claim, if true, gives us any traction on the large transitions now underway — the further automation of food production, urbanization, and the possibility that machine intelligence will do to cognitive labor what the plough did to foraging — or whether the disanalogies (we can now measure welfare effects, exit is genuinely available to some, the transition is not driven by a single subsistence bottleneck) are so great that the historical case is inert. Second, what it implies for how we should regard small-scale societies that still exist, and for the ethics of development work among them, given that we cannot know in advance whether we are the foragers or the farmers of that encounter. Third, what it implies about the coherence of progress as an evaluative notion at all, and in particular whether a welfare verdict on an entire subsistence regime is the kind of thing that can be true or false, or whether it dissolves into a comparison of incommensurable ways of life. Please rank the implications by how much they would change my behavior if I accepted them, give the reasoning for each, and close with the single implication you take to be most important and why.
