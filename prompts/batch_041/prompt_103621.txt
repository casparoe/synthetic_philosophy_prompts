I'm putting together a session for my reading group on the hedonistic imperative -- the David Pearce line that biotech is suposed to abolish suffering across all sentient life, not just humans, and steer everything toward what he calls gradients of bliss. Next week we're doing Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." Everone in the group reads it as a straight utilitarian thought experiment: one kid suffers so the whole town stays happy, do you take the deal. I want a reading that takes the imperative seriously instead. Pearce would say the deal is a false dilemma -- the whole point of the technology is that you don't need a scapegoat, you can lift the kid and everyone else at once, and Omelas only looks like a forced trade if you assume suffering is a fixed quantity that has to be redistributed somewhere. But give me the honest other half too: where does the story push back? The people who walk away aren't doing arithmetic, they're refusing complicity, and Le Guin treats that refusal as the moral center -- does it survive once the bargain is technologically unnecessary? Also the story is silent about animals, which is half of what the imperative is about. Which scenes should I put in front of the group, and what does the story see that the manifesto misses? And separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested -- don't hand me one camp as the consensus.
