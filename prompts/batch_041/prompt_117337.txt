I've been slowly working my way through the anti-federalist essays from the ratification fight -- the Brutus ones, which are much harder going than I expected -- and I've hit a stretch that I can parse sentence by sentence but can't actually follow as an argument. Here it is (I've cut a few sentences between chunks):

> "I question whether the world ever saw, in any period of it, a court of justice invested with such immense powers, and yet placed in a situation so little responsible. [...] I believe they in no instance assume the authority to set aside an act of parliament under the idea that it is inconsistent with their constitution. They consider themselves bound to decide according to the existing laws of the land, and never undertake to controul them by adjudging that they are inconsistent with the constitution -- much less are they vested with the power of giving an equitable construction to the constitution. [...] The judges in England are under the controul of the legislature, for they are bound to determine according to the laws passed by them. But the judges under this constitution will controul the legislature, for the supreme court are authorised in the last resort, to determine what is the extent of the powers of the Congress; they are to give the constitution an explanation, and there is no power above them to set aside their judgment. [...] There is no power above them, to controul any of their decisions. There is no authority that can remove them, and they cannot be controuled by the laws of the legislature. In short, they are independent of the people, of the legislature, and of every power under heaven. Men placed in this situation will generally soon feel themselves independent of heaven itself."

What I can't get is the middle step. Every court has to interpret the laws it applies -- that's just what judging is -- so how does "there is no power above them to set aside their judgment" add up to the court's power being above the legislature? Is the thought that the constitution is written in general language rather than as a code of rules, so that "giving the constitution an explanation" is unavoidably a kind of legislating, and whoever gets the last word on the meaning therefore holds the real power? Or is it a more modest, seperate institutional point -- in England a bad ruling can be fixed by the legislature and here nothing can fix it, so the court's errors just pile up and harden? I'd like the argument laid out as steps, with the load-bearing premise flagged, because I suspect one of them is doing all the work and I can't tell which.

Two smaller things while you're at it. First, is his picture of England even right for his own day -- did English judges really never set aside a statute as contrary to the constitution, or is that an idealization, and does his argument need it to be true? Second, what is "giving an equitable construction to the constitution" supposed to be, and why would that be worse than ordinary interpretation?

The reason I ask: I read the other side too, Hamilton's defense of the same arrangement (the one that says the courts have "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment," and depend on the executive even to make thier rulings stick), and I came away reassured -- but now I think the two of them might not even be answering the same question. Brutus is asking who has the final word, and Hamilton is asking who has the guns and the money. If that's the real disagreement, which framing should I be using when I try to decide whether unelected judges ought to be able to strike down statutes? And is there anything in Brutus's paragraph that a modern defender of judicial review just has to grant him?
