I write the ideas column for a general-interest science magazine, and the piece I am at work on now is an explainer on what researchers in AI safety call the Löbian obstacle. Stripped to its bones, the idea is this: an agent that wants to trust its own reasoning — to treat the rule "whatever I prove is true" as one it may apply to itself, including to the conclusions of the more capable successor it is building or becoming — cannot hold that rule consistently. Löb's theorem says that any system strong enough to formalize its own notion of provability, and that proves "if I can prove P, then P" for every sentence P, thereby proves its own consistency; by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem no consistent system of that strength can prove its own consistency, so the rule has to be abandoned or restricted. An agent can trust its own conclusions only piecemeal, or only at the cost of something else. That is the obstacle, and it is why the field worries about self-modifying systems that reason about what they themselves will do.

My readers are intelligent non-specialists: they have read the standard popular accounts of Gödel's incompleteness theorems and of the AI alignment problem, they know what a proof is, but they have no formal logic. The piece runs to about 2,500 words. Do not explain the obstacle to me; I understand it well enough to have pitched the piece. Plan how I should explain it.

Give me:

- The order in which the ideas have to arrive, and why that order and not another — what is a prerequisite for what, where Gödel's second theorem has to sit relative to Löb's theorem, and where the self-trusting agent can enter so that the reader never has to hold two abstractions at once.
- The single example that should carry the whole piece — something that does the work of a proof without being one — and why the obvious candidates (the liar paradox, an AI that rewrites its own code, a self-driving car reasoning about a future software update) will not carry it.
- The misconceptions my readers will bring or form, my own guesses being that this is just Gödel's theorem again, that it proves a self-trusting machine is impossible, that the remedy is simply to add self-trust as an axiom, and that it is really a point about self-awareness — and the exact place in the sequence where I should head off each one rather than let it harden.
- The beat the piece cannot skip: the difference between an agent trusting its successor's conclusions and trusting its successor's decision procedure, and why the obstacle bites hardest against the first.
- One question I can put to a reader at the end that would show the obstacle has actually landed, rather than that the reader has merely nodded at a paradox.
- What to leave out, and what to announce that I am leaving out: the diagonal lemma and the derivability conditions, the competing technical resolutions, and the dispute over whether the obstacle constrains real systems or only an over-idealized picture of them.

No reading list, please; the piece carries no citations.

Please write in American English.
