I'd like you to act as a philosopher working on virtue ethics and moral psychology — someone who's thought hard about the idea of a moral saint and is willing to give a definate answer rather than survey the field. Here's the set-up. Take a moral saint to be roughly someone whose every action is as morally good as it could be, and whose whole life is organised around that. Four candidates:

(a) Priya gives away about 70% of her income to effective charities, works a well-paid job she dislikes in order to fund it, lives plainly, and is scrupulously honest and fair — but she has no close friends and seems to feel very little for anyone in particular.

(b) Tom is a hospice nurse, endlessly patient and kind to dying strangers, but he does it partly because being needed is the only thing that makes him feel real; if nobody needed him he'd stop.

(c) Aisha is a warm, funny, deeply loyal friend and mother who also paints; she gives and volunteers in ordinary amounts, no more than most decent people, and would say she's no saint at all.

(d) Marcus has sacrificed career, health and family to a cause he believes is morally urgent, and his sacrifices really are supererogatory — but the cause is one that reasonable people think is a waste of a life.

Rank these four from most to least saintly, give each a score out of 10, and say in one sentence what's doing the work in each placement. Then, separately, tell me which of the four lives you'd most want to live yourself, and whether your two orderings come apart. If they do, say what you think that shows about the saintliness ranking — is it a defect in the concept, a defect in you, or neither? Brainstorm the awkward cases before you commit to numbers, think carefully about what's actually being measured, and give me your real view at the end instead of "it depends".
