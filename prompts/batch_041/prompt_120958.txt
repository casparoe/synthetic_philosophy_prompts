I sit on the accreditation committee of the International Forecast Standards Council, and we vote next month on whether to revise the standards that govern how member organizations phrase their warnings. One of our members, a humanitarian early-warning group, has for years issued what it calls determinate warnings — statements of the form "There will be a famine in the Sahel in the third quarter" rather than "There is a seventy per cent chance of famine." The group's director argues that only determinate language moves donors and governments, and that hedging is a luxury of institutions that do not have to beg. A rival member has filed a complaint: it says determinate warnings treat a contingent future as already settled, make the organization's own preventive work look either pointless or dishonest, and, when the warning does not materialize, corrode the field's credibility for everyone. Our legal advisor has added a proposal that will probably decide the vote: she wants any determinate warning to be treated as a "materialization commitment," so that a member that issues one and then fails to act on it, or whose warning fails to materialize, faces an automatic review. I have to circulate a written opinion before the meeting.

Please draft that opinion. I need a concrete recommendation — adopt the revision, reject it, or adopt it with stated conditions — together with a time frame for implementation and a clear statement of what the committee is and is not deciding. Engage the strongest version of the losing side rather than restating our current standards, and do not turn the memo into a treatise. The philosophical issue, as I see it, is the old problem of future contingents: if a statement about tomorrow is true now, in what sense is tomorrow's event still open, and what does that imply for the deliberator who is supposed to be able to affect it? But I do not want a lecture on the sea battle; I want the committee to be able to act. In particular, address whether the line between "will" and "probably will" is the right place to draw the distinction, or whether the real issue is whether a determinate warning functions as a prediction or as a commitment; say whether the two-ledger proposal — recording contingent forecasts on a separate ledger until the event occurs — solves the problem or merely relabels it; and say what we should do about the legal advisor's materialization-commitment idea, since that is what the vote will actually turn on.
