I am a novelist — seven novels, translated into something like a dozen languages, which has given me a long and mostly affectionate quarrel with the people who do that work — and I have been asked to give the after-dinner address at the annual dinner of a society of literary translators. The room splits neatly in two. On one side sit working translators, who will hear every technical move and will not thank me for a tribute; on the other sit partners, spouses and friends who do not work with language professionally and who will glaze over the moment I begin to lecture. I want six to eight minutes that make both halves of the room feel, from the inside, what it is to translate under constraint: that a rendering is never the solution but a settlement among competing demands — sense, sound, metre, rhyme, register, and the ghost of the original — and that fidelity to any one of them is always paid for out of another, so that 'the same poem' survives only as a family of mutually unfaithful cousins. This is the ground Douglas Hofstadter covers at book length in his study of translating one short sixteenth-century French poem, and I want the speech to do at a dinner table what he does on the page, without a syllable of his jargon. To make the audience feel the trade-off rather than merely nod at it, I want the address built around a small verse of my own, which I set down here: 'Sleep now, my love, the lamp is low, / the snow has hushed the street; / whatever else the years may know, / keep this hour, small and sweet.' Please present three or four constrained renderings of it — say a limerick, a haiku, a rhymed couplet, and a version forbidden some common letter — each one losing something the one before it kept, each loss named in a sentence or two. What I want left ringing at the end is that form is not a coat the meaning wears but part of what the meaning is, and that a translator who preserves the sense while losing the shape has, on occasion, preserved neither.

Please take the role of an experienced literary translator who is also a confident speechwriter and who knows how these dinners actually go. Begin by thinking through, in notes you then discard, which constraints will best expose the trade-off and whether my chosen four are the right four; only then write the speech itself. Keep each rendering short enough to be taken in at one hearing, and keep a thread of comedy running so that the non-specialists stay with us — the technical point should arrive through the examples rather than ahead of them. The register should be warm, a little self-deprecating, and formal enough for a dinner at which speeches are given; nothing that sounds like a footnote, and no seminar vocabulary on the surface.

One last thing, and I mean it. I would rather be told I am wrong than be flattered. If the conceit will not carry — if a handful of formal rewrites of my little verse cannot make the argument, or if 'form is meaning' is a slogan I could not honestly defend in front of people who do this for a living — say so plainly before you begin, and then either do the best version of the job you can or tell me what I should be asking for instead. Do not smooth the difficulty over.
