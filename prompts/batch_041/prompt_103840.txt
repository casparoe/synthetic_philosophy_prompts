I'm in my second term of a philosophy of history module and we've just done Popper's Poverty of Historicism, which I read mostly on the bus and understood maybe two-thirds of. What's actually bothering me isn't the book, it's a line my flatmate keeps trotting out whenever the news is grim: 'history repeats itself'. He used it the other night to argue that because revolutions tend to follow periods of the sort of thing we're seeing now, there's basically a law of history and you can see the future coming; when I said I thought Popper had killed that idea off he just said Popper was a liberal cold warrior who didn't like Marx and carried on. My tutor wasn't much help either — she said it depends what you mean by a law of history, which is true but not exactly a menu I can order from.

So what I'd like is exactly that menu. Take the sentence 'history repeats itself' and lay out all the precise things it could be claiming, ordered from the boringly obvious to the genuinely radical, and for each one tell me whether it's a platitude, a real substantive thesis that might be true, or something actually false — and crucially, which of the readings would give my flatmate the thing he wants (that there are laws of history and that they let you forecast), and which readings Popper would say are all anyone ever realy means when they say it. Somewhere in there I want the difference between a law and a mere pattern or trend, and whether 'laws of history' is even the same kind of animal as a law of physics, since I suspect that's where the whole row is actually happening. And if you can, say which reading a person is most likely invoking when they come out with it in a pub, because I don't think he's thought about it for more than four seconds. Bullet points please — I want to take it back to the seminar on Thursday.
