I retired from geology about six years ago and have been trying to teach myself philosophy ever since, mostly by reading primary texts too fast and then arguing about them with a friend who actually did a PhD. I've gotten stuck on Aristotle's account of natural slavery in the Politics. I think I finally see that it isn't supposed to be a racial or ethnic doctrine -- it's a teleological claim about a certain kind of person whose deliberative faculty is defective in a specific way, who can take in reason from another but can't properly possess it, and who is supposed to be better off being ruled, the way the body is better off ruled by the soul. He also seems to distinguish slavery by nature from slavery by law or conquest, and he doesn't say every actual slave is a natural slave. I'm probly mangling it, but that's the shape of the view I want to test.

Here's the episode I keep thinking about. In 1550 and 1551, Charles V convened a junta at Valladolid to hear Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda and Bartolomé de las Casas argue about the conquest of the Americas. Sepúlveda leaned on Aristotle to say that some of the indigenous peoples were natural slaves -- unable to govern themselves, fit only to be ruled -- and that this, along with their practices of human sacrifice and cannibalism, justified war against them and Spanish mastery. Las Casas replied that Aristotle's natural slave simply didn't apply: these people were fully rational, capable of Christian faith and self-government, and conquest was not justified. The junta apparently never issued a clean verdict, and both sides claimed victory.

What I want from you is a disciplined analysis of that debate through Aristotle's theory, not a summary of who said what. Who counts as the natural slave in Aristotle's scheme, and what would actually have to be true of the indigenous peoples for Sepúlveda's argument to go through? Does his argument even use the right criterion, or does it slide from cultural practices and military defeat to a claim about defective deliberative capacity? Does Las Casas accept Aristotle's framework and just deny the empirical premise, or does his universal rationalism quietly abandon the category of the natural slave altogether? And does the debate even map onto Aristotle's master-slave relation, given that it's really about just war, sovereignty, and the encomienda, not the running of a household? I'd like you to run it in both directions: what Aristotle's theory illuminates about the episode, where it misdescribes it or falls silent, and what the episode shows about the theory -- a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated. I don't need citations or a reading list, just the argument, and please don't just tell me the usual line that Aristotle was a bigot or Las Casas was a saint.
