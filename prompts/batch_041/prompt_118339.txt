I teach Parfit's hitchhiker every year, which is probably how I ended up on this committee. I'm on our university's IT and Information Risk committee, and I have to write the memo that goes out before Thursday's meeting.

Here's the mess. Six weeks ago the hospital side got hit. Imaging, scheduling and a big chunk of the research data are encrypted, and they also walked off with patient records -- a few hundred files that include HIV status and several names from a study on a stigmatized condition. They want 1.8 million in crypto by Friday noon, and they've already dumped a sample batch to prove they mean it. The provost wants a recommendation on whether to authorize the payment. Separately, the commitee has been circling for about a year on whether we should adopt a standing no-pay policy; we're the last school in our peer group without one.

What makes this interesting to me is that it's Parfit's hitchhiker at institutional scale. Individually, in the room, on Friday, paying is obviously the better move: we get the decryptor, they promise to delete the files, the threats stop. Collectively, if every institution reasons that way, the payments fund the industry and the attacks keep coming, and all of us -- us included -- would have been better off if none of us ever paid. The hitchhiker's fix wasn't to reason better at the moment of decision. It was to be the sort of person whose promise is worth something in the first place, which is a property you have to have before you need it.

So: a decision memo, two pages max, they will not read more. It needs an actionable bottom line. Specifically: (1) a concrete recommendation on Friday's demand and on the standing policy -- adopt, adopt with conditions, or don't -- with a time frame; (2) the mechanism spelled out well enough that we could actually vote on wording: who is bound (the university, the hospital, the subsidiaries, the individual labs that hold their own data), whether the policy is public or internal, what the escape hatch is and who can pull it, when it gets reviewed; and (3) the best argument on the other side treated honestly rather than as a speed bump. The best argument against my instinct isn't a cost-benefit one, it's that refusing to pay imposes a real, concentrated cost on identifiable people who didn't choose any of this -- patients about to have their diagnoses published -- in exchange for a diffuse benefit to future strangers. I want that stated at full strength and then either answered or conceded, not waved away.

Two things I'd rather you do than not. First, tell me which parts of the recommendation you actually believe and which are guesses about attacker behavior -- whether a public no-pay pledge really deters, or just advertises us as a target with nothing left to lose. Confidence levels, not hedges. Second, our CISO keeps pushing a bond idea: we deposit, say, five million with a consortium of peer institutions in escrow, forfeited to the others if we ever pay a ransom. It makes the pledge credible the way the hitchhiker's character makes his promise credible. Assess whether that's a real commitment device or a stunt that just hands our decision to whoever holds the escrow, and whether it survives the case where a life is on the line -- because if it works as advertised, it means the people in the room on Friday can't do the humane thing even when they should.

And if the Parfit framing is just decoration here -- if institutions aren't the kind of thing that can have a character, and this is really a security-economics problem with some decision theory bolted on -- say so in the first paragraph instead of writing me a memo that pretends otherwise. I'd rather hear it from you now than from the general counsel on Thursday. No citations needed, obviously; footnotes are wasted on this crowd.
