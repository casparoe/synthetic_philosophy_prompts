I'm writing the final for my upper-level metaphysics seminar on causation, and I want one essay question on causation by omission that a student cant get through by writing "an omission causes an outcome when the outcome counterfactually depends on it" and then padding for three pages. So I want to build the question around three short cases that are all counterfactually on a par, so that the counterfactual test can't do the sorting work by itself. Here they are:

Case 1. Priya is the process engineer at a chemical plant. Her written procedure requires her to close valve V every Friday before she leaves. This Friday she forgets. Over the weekend the tank overflows and floods a neighbouring lot. Had she closed V, there would have been no flood.

Case 2. The tank is visible from the public road. On that same Friday a passerby, Dan, notices the tank and the open valve and could have climbed the fence and closed V himself; he doesn't, because it isn't his job and he doesn't know the procedure. Had he climbed the fence and closed it, there would have been no flood.

Case 3. Two years earlier the plant had an automatic level sensor wired to close V whenever the level got high. A manager, Sam, cancelled the sensor's service contract to save money; the sensor has been dead ever since. Had Sam kept the contract, the sensor would have tripped and closed V, and there would have been no flood.

Now the question itself, the way I want it to appear in the exam booklet:

(a) For each of the three, say whether that person caused the flood, and state the criterion you're using. (b) The two criteria students usually reach for -- counterfactual dependence, and violation of some norm, expectation, or role-based duty -- are each supposed to explain these verdicts. Show that they come apart here, and say exactly which pair of cases pulls them apart and why. (c) In case 3 Sam's omission sits at one remove from the outcome: he doesn't fail to close a valve, he fails to keep in place a thing that would have closed the valve. Does that difference matter to whether he caused the flood, and does thinking about it tell us anything about whether omission-causation is the same relation as ordinary causation or a different one wearing the same name? (d) Finally, state the case your criterion gets wrong -- one where it delivers a verdict you're not willing to accept -- and either patch the criterion or explain why you'll live with the verdict.

Then I need: a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 with the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks, with a one-line note per point on how students typically blow it.

---

Model answer (about 600 words):

The natural verdicts are: Priya caused the flood; Dan did not; Sam is the hard case, and most students should come down on yes, but with less confidence than for Priya.

The two criteria behave as follows. Bare counterfactual dependence -- the outcome wouldn't have happened but for the omission -- holds for all three. Had Priya closed V, no flood; had Dan climbed the fence, no flood; had Sam kept the contract, the sensor would have tripped. So the counterfactual test says Priya, Dan, and Sam all caused it. A norm- or expectation-relative criterion says something else. Priya violated a written procedure; Sam violated an implicit duty of maintenance; Dan violated nothing at all, since he had no role, no knowledge of the procedure, and no duty to climb a fence. So the norm criterion says Priya and Sam caused it and Dan did not. The pair that pulls the two criteria apart is cases 1 and 2: identical counterfactual structure, opposite verdicts, and the only difference is the presence of a norm. Case 3 does not separate the criteria -- both say yes -- it separates two structures, direct omission and omission at one remove.

That gives the crux. Counterfactual dependence alone is too promiscuous: it counts Dan, and it counts the sun's not exploding, and every other absent condition that would have made a difference. Something has to restrict the class of omissions that count, and the norm does that work. But making causation turn on norms has costs. Norms are contested and change over time; a bad norm still generates causal verdicts; and where two people each violate a norm and either alone would have sufficed, we get overdetermination by omission, which is as awkward as ordinary overdetermination.

Case 3 is the interesting one because it is a double prevention by omission. Sam doesn't stop the flood; he fails to keep a stopper in place. If we accept that omissions can cause, there is no principled reason to stop at one remove -- the counterfactual chain runs through the sensor just as cleanly as through Priya's hand. Students who say Priya caused it and Sam didn't need to say what breaks the chain, and the honest answer is that nothing does, other than a preference for short causal chains, which is not a theory.

On whether omission-causation is the same relation: the candidate positions are (i) yes, absences are genuine causes and we should widen our ontology to admit them; (ii) no, omissions are never causes, and talk of "letting happen" is loose shorthand for citing the positive state that obtained; (iii) a hybrid, where causing and allowing are distinct relations and the norm tells us which one we're deploying. A good answer picks one and accepts the cost.

For (d), the usual move is to notice that the norm criterion makes the verdict on Dan flip with the jurisdiction -- enact a duty-to-rescue law and the same inaction becomes a cause. The patch is to say norms fix which omission we cite but not whether it is a cause; the bite-the-bullet option is to accept that causation talk is partly normative and stop apologising for it.

Marking scheme (20 marks):

1. Correct sorting plus an explicitly stated criterion (4). Typical failure: giving verdicts with no criterion, or silently switching criteria between cases.

2. Showing that counterfactual dependence and the norm criterion come apart, and identifying cases 1 and 2 as the separating pair (4). Typical failure: saying the criteria "differ" without naming the pair, or claiming case 3 is where they diverge.

3. The promiscuity objection to bare counterfactual dependence -- that it lets in Dan and every other absent condition (4). Typical failure: treating counterfactual dependence as sufficient rather than merely necessary.

4. Case 3 handled as double prevention by omission, plus a position on same-relation-or-different (4). Typical failure: asserting the one-remove case is "too indirect" without saying what indirectness amounts to.

5. The (d) move: a genuine counterexample to the student's own criterion, with a patch or a defended bite (4). Typical failure: offering a "counterexample" the criterion obviously handles, or patching by quietly adding an ad hoc clause that would also exclude Dan.
