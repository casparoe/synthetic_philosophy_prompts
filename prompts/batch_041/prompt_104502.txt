My roommate and I have been going back and forth for like a week and I finally wrote it out as a numbered argument, and now I'm worried it actually works. Help.

Background: we were arguing about a poem she loves that I think is fine but nothing special. She said I was just wrong about it, not that we have different tastes. I said there's no fact of the matter, it's a sentiment. She said then my opinion is worth nothing. So I tried to use Hume on her: beauty isn't a property of the thing, it's a sentiment in the viewer, but there's still a standard of taste, namely the verdict of the true judge, the person with delicacy of taste, lots of practice, the habit of comparing works, no prejudice, and good sense. Then she said: great, so let's just build the true judge. And that's where I got nervous.

(1) Beauty is not a property of objects; it's a sentiment in the perceiver. [Hume, and I agree with this]
(2) There is still a standard of taste: the joint verdict of true judges, i.e. people with delicacy, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. [also Hume, and I agree]
(3) Suppose we could build a machine that has all five of those things -- trained on everything, no prejudice, infinite patience -- and that gives a verdict on any work. Call it the Judge.
(4) The Judge's verdict just is the standard of taste's verdict. That's what (2) says.
(5) The Judge is deterministic: feed it the same poem, get the same verdict. Its output is fixed by the poem plus its training. So the verdict is determined by the poem.
(6) A verdict that is (a) determined by the poem and (b) authoritative is a fact about the poem.
(7) So there is a fact about the poem -- whether the Judge approves -- and that's what "beautiful" means.
(8) But a fact about the poem is exactly what (1) denies. So (2) is false: there's no standard of taste.
(9) And if (2) is false, my roommate can't tell me I'm wrong and the true judge is no better than anyone else. Which is absurd, and it's the opposite of what Hume wanted.

So it looks like either beauty is objective after all (contradicting (1)), or there's no standard and we can't say the philistine is wrong (contradicting (9)). I'd most like to keep (1) -- I really don't believe beauty is out there in the world -- and also keep (9), because some taste is definately better than others. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? Also, please say what would change your mind here -- which consideration would move you to a different verdict -- and where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
