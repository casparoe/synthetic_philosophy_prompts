I'm a second-year history PhD student who sits in on a method seminar in a neighbouring department, and there is one comparison the room treats as settled: the historian stands to the source as a court stands to testimony. A source is a witness; source criticism is cross-examination; independent corroboration is the requirement of a second witness; provenance and transmission are the chain of custody; and a claim about the past should be held to something like the criminal standard of proof. I don't object to this as a first approximation, but it is doing a great deal of quiet work, because the seminar uses it to license a substantive conclusion: that historians should import the rules of evidence wholesale — discount interested testimony, decline to decide on a single witness, read gaps in the record as an adverse inference, and withhold assent from any claim that cannot be independently corroborated. I am not sure the comparison can carry that weight, and I would like an audit rather than either a defence or a dismissal.

Set the correspondence out explicitly, item by item, and then say where it fails, separating the failures that matter for the conclusion the seminar wants to draw from the ones that are merely picturesque. Four points interest me in particular. First, the analogy is unstable about roles: at different moments the historian is cast as judge, jury, advocate, detective and clerk, and the source sometimes as a witness, sometimes as a defendant, sometimes as a document already in the file. Say which role-assignment each of the seminar's conclusions actually requires, and whether one inquirer can coherently occupy all of those positions at once. Second, the rules of evidence are not obviously truth-tracking instruments: they exist to protect parties, to discipline juries, and to produce legitimate verdicts inside institutions that need finality. Ask what happens when procedural values are imported as epistemic ones, and whether res judicata or a statute of limitations has any counterpart in a discipline whose questions are permanently open. Third, the past does not testify. There is no cross-examination, most surviving material is a mute trace rather than an assertion, a forged document is a nullity in court but a first-rate datum for the historian, and interest, which the law counts against a witness, is often precisely what makes a source worth reading.

Fourth, take the analogy's hardest case — a claim resting on a single late source written by someone with a stake in the outcome, whose only corroboration is a text that may simply derive from it — and say what the courtroom model tells us to do, what a careful historian would in fact do, and whether the two differ in substance or only in vocabulary. Finish by telling me whether the seminar's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if it does not, what better analogy you would put in its place — the auditor, the restorer, the translator, the pathologist reconstructing an event from traces, or something else — together with what that replacement would license instead.
