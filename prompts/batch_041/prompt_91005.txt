Write a short story of roughly 2,000 to 3,000 words that turns on the problem of future contingents — the ancient puzzle about whether a prediction concerning a contingent future event (a sea battle, a harvest, a marriage) is already true or false before the event occurs, and what follows for deliberation if it is.

The story should be built so that the metaphysics is load-bearing rather than decorative. Someone must act — write the policy, accept the wager, give the promise, set sail — before the question can be settled, and the outcome of the plot should depend on what the characters take the status of the prediction to be. Do not have anyone deliver the correct answer, and do not let an essayistic narrator explain the issue at the end; the reader should be able to reconstruct the competing positions from what the characters do and what it costs them.

The following distinctions should be handled precisely, since the story will go vague wherever they are blurred:

- A truth-value gap is not the same as indeterminate truth. On one view the prediction is neither true nor false; on another there is a fact of the matter, but it is not determinately one or the other. These are different claims, with different consequences for practical reasoning.
- The disjunction may not share the fate of its disjuncts: "either the fleet is lost or it is not" can be true even if neither part has a truth value. The story should have some purchase on how a whole can be true while no part is.
- Truth may be acquired rather than possessed. Perhaps the prediction becomes true only when the event occurs; the question is then whether it was true all along and merely unknowable, or became true at that moment, and whether "was true" can be prised apart from "had to be true."
- One may deny the inference from "it was true all along" to "it was unavoidable," on the grounds that the prediction is true because the event will happen, rather than the event happening because the prediction is true. A character who takes this line should be shown paying its price.
- Openness of truth value is not causal openness. Granting that a future contingent has no truth value yet gives no one power over it; granting bivalence takes no one's power away. Let someone discover where that distinction bites.

Set the story wherever you like — an underwriting office, a magistrate's court, a household — and give it at least two characters whose readings of the future diverge and whose interests run with their readings, so that the reader cannot settle the semantics by settling the motives. Foreknowledge may enter, but it should complicate matters rather than resolve them; the story should not be rescued by time travel or by an oracle whose pronouncements settle the metaphysics by fiat. No names, dates, or texts should be required of the reader.
