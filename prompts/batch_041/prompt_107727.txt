Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two parties to the dispute over material constitution, who have argued for years about whether two numerically distinct material objects can occupy the same place at the same time. The first party is a coincidentalist: in the standard case the statue and the lump of clay are two distinct objects, the statue being constituted by but not identical with the lump. The second party is a one-object theorist: strictly speaking there is a single object there, and the appearance of two is an artifact of how our predicates, sortals, or counting conventions work rather than a fact about what exists.

Before drafting, both parties agree to work through two cases that are deliberately not the textbook statue-and-clay example. Case One, the Found Lump: a river deposit hardens into a shape indistinguishable from a finished sculpture; years later a sculptor comes upon it and declares it a statue, without altering it in any way. Case Two, the Recast: a bronze figure is melted down and recast as a different figure, but the sculptor deliberately casts the new figure so that its lower half is the unaltered metal of the original figure's lower half while the upper half is new metal fused in.

The document should contain four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both parties will sign: about what these two cases do and do not establish, about what stands in need of explanation, about which of their differences are merely terminological, and about what each side concedes would count as an objection to the coincidentalist's two-object claim and to the one-object theorist's denial of it. Second, for each remaining disagreement, a one-sentence formulation in wording the party who holds the position would accept as fair rather than as a caricature. Third, the cruxes, which should include at least whether identity is governed by an unrestricted indiscernibility principle or is relativized to a sortal, whether the parties are disagreeing about what exists or about how we count, and whether the constitution relation, if admitted, does explanatory work or merely labels the phenomenon. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or datum that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about that evidence recorded in advance. Close by saying which parts of the dispute the collaboration found to be genuinely substantive and which it found to be verbal.
