I'm 200 pages into a novel set in the late Bronze Age and a philosopher friend has argued me into a corner I can't find the exit from. He's not being snide — he likes the book — he just thinks that "getting the period right" is a category mistake and that the research half of my life is a bit of a joke. He's fond of pointing out that Thucydides tells us he composed his speeches himself, making the speakers say what the occasion seemed to call for, and that Herodotus says he's obliged to report what he's told but not to believe any of it. If the founders of the genre were that loose, he says, what am I even doing. Here's his arguement, as I wrote it down in my notebook:

(1) Everything we know about the distant past reaches us as traces — texts, pots, ruins, bones — all of them partial, and all of them left by people with reasons of their own.
(2) A trace tells us what happened only once we've ruled out the other things that could have left it.
(3) The usual tricks don't help: two sources agreeing, or one source blurting out something against its own interest. Deciding that two sources are independent, or that a detail really did cut against the writer's interest, means reconstructing the very past we're trying to establish.
(4) So ruling out the alternatives means leaning on other things we already accept about that same past — other traces. There's no outside to stand on.
(5) Support that just runs in a circle gives us no reason to prefer one story over another.
(6) And fitting the traces is cheap anyway: whatever they are, we can always save a story by adjusting some other part of it. That's what a "revision" is.
(7) So no account of the distant past is better than any other. Mine, if I'm careful with the invented bits, is as good as the monograph — the difference is a matter of marketing, and the historian's confidence is a professional convention.

I tried "but what about the tree rings, the ice cores," and he waved it off — same problem, he says, they still have to be interpreted, and you interpret them with ideas about the past.

I'd like to keep (1) and (2). I've read enough bad historical novels to know what happens when the writer dosen't respect the material. So I'll give up whatever's cheapest to give up. Which step is doing the damage? Why did it sound so reasonable when he said it, and if I cut it, what do I pay? And then the part I actually need: after the repair, what's left of "getting it right" for someone writing fiction about a period they can't visit?
