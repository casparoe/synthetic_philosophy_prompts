I have a single research term — about four months of full-time work — to devote to the cluster of problems surrounding A. J. Ayer's treatment of life after death, and I need help deciding where to spend it. Ayer's official position was that statements about surviving one's own death are not literally significant, since nothing in experience could count for or against them, and in his later work on personal identity he pressed a stronger line: that the notion of a person continuing to exist without a body is not merely unverifiable but incoherent, because the criteria by which we identify a person across time are bound up with the persistence of a living body and its memories. Almost everyone now rejects the verificationist premise that did the heavy lifting in the first argument, and I am not persuaded that the second argument is anything more than an artefact of a particular theory of personal identity — yet the underlying worry, that we do not fully know what question we are asking when we ask whether we shall survive, seems to me to have survived the collapse of its original setting. Ayer himself, after a near-death experience in the last year of his life, reported that his confidence that death was the end had been somewhat shaken, which I take to be philosophically interesting chiefly as a datum about what such an experience can and cannot do to a considered position.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Which open questions in this area most deserve a term's sustained work, and in what order should they be taken up? I am thinking of questions such as: whether any non-verificationist reconstruction of the meaninglessness charge still has force, and what exactly it would now be claiming; whether the coherence objection is an objection to survival as such or only to one account of personal identity, and what a defensible alternative account would have to look like; whether talk of survival is better understood as predication of a person, of a continuing information-bearing pattern, or of something else entirely; whether first-personal and third-personal criteria for survival can come apart in a way that matters here; and whether reports of near-death experiences, including Ayer's own, bear on any of the above or are simply autobiographical. I am not asking you to accept that list — if there are questions I have missed that are more upstream, say so and say why.

Please defend the ordering rather than merely giving it. For each question, tell me what it is upstream of, how tractable it is for one philosopher with a term and no laboratory, how much of the rest of the area would be settled by settling it, and what would move it up or down the list over the coming year. Be explicit about the criteria you are trading off — importance, tractability, how many other questions depend on the answer, and whether the answer would change anything downstream — and say which of my framing assumptions you think I should abandon. Where a question looks attractive but would in fact be a cul-de-sac, say so plainly. Please write in British English.
