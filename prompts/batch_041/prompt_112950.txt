im putting together a small anthology of poems that argue a philosophical position from the inside, and i need one for the Borges section. The argument I want it to make is his attempt to refute time, which goes roughly like this: we never actually perceive succession -- what we're given is a state, and then another state, and the "then" is something we supply, so time itself is never an object of experience; and if succession were real, if each moment were absolutely itself, then memory would be a fiction, since the past would not be faint but simply not; but then the man making the argument has to notice that the argument is itself an event that comes after another one, so the refutation gets refuted by the mere fact of being written down.

Write the poem in the first person of that essayist. 18 lines, blank verse or loose iambic, plain and aphoristic, no props, no mirrors, no rivers. Make the form carry it: the first 8 lines state the argument, the next 8 take it apart -- each one negating a line of the first half, and in reverse order, so line 9 undoes line 8, line 10 undoes line 7, and so on -- and then the last 2 lines have to break that symmetry, because the thing doing the dismantling is itself a moment that came after something else. He should lose the argument to his own poem, and he should know he's right. Dont use the word time more than once, dont name or quote any of his books, and dont let the ending console anybody.
