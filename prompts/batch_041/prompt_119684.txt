My friend is doing a PhD in macro-history and keeps texting me lists of what she calls laws of history. I've read Popper's poverty of historicism stuff and now I'm insuferable about it, so she wants me to grade her list and I said I'd ask someone else. Here it is, roughly in her words, plus a couple I threw in myself to mess with her:

1. Literacy rule: in any society where adult literacy passes about 60%, the birth rate falls substantially within roughly twenty years, whatever the dominant religion and whatever the income level.
2. Third-decade rule: revolutionary regimes are always more repressive in their third decade than in their first.
3. r > g rule: whenever the return on capital exceeds the growth rate for twenty years or more, wealth inequality rises.
4. Commuting rule: the maximum radius of a daily commuting region is roughly one hour at the speed of the fastest widely affordable transport in that society.
5. Copying rule: any society in which copying information gets an order of magnitude cheaper either centralises censorship or splinters politically within two generations, never neither.
6. Veto-point rule: power concentrates wherever the number of veto points is smallest.
7. Prophecy rule: any prediction that a regime will fall within a year, if published widely, either hastens the fall or prevents it, depending on how people react.
8. City-size rule: the sizes of the cities in a country are distributed as a power law.
9. Fixed-supply rule: when demand for a good rises and supply can't expand, price rises; if price is fixed by law, queues, waiting lists, or under-the-table payments appear instead.

Sort these into tiers by how well they'd survive Popper's critique, and give me a sentence or two per item on which objection is doing the work — the unpredictability of future knowledge, trends not being laws, the prediction feeding back into the thing predicted (the Oedipus effect thing), unfalsifiability, or just that it's a tautology in a lab coat. Tiers: A = a genuine law in Popper's sense (universal, conditional, falsifiable, and not secretly a statement about one stretch of history); B = a respectable law or conditional prediction, but one that operates inside history rather than being a law of history's course; C = a trend or a single historical episode dressed up as a law; D = not even a candidate. Order them within each tier from most to least defensable.

Two extras. First: does anything in this list get her an actual law of the historical process as a whole, or are they all laws you'd use while doing history rather than laws of history? I keep thinking about how astronomers can predict eclipses without there being any law of the sequence of eclipses, and I want to know which of these nine makes that mistake. Second, one number between 0 and 1 for what share of her list is genuinely a law of history in Popper's sense. Also name the one you'd bet on surviving and the one you'd delete first. Don't bother with references or who said what, I just want the reasoning.
