I got into one of those lunch arguments with a coworker last week about big history — the Harari / Christian / Diamond kind of thing, the "history of everything from the Big Bang to now" genre. He's read Sapiens and a couple of the follow-ups, I'd read Sapiens too plus a few of the annoyed reviews by actual historians, and his whole defense of the genre was an analogy: it's just zooming out on Google Maps. You start at street level, you're looking at your own block, and it's all noise — driveways, trees, a weirdly shaped parking lot. Pull back and the highway system appears. Pull back further and you see the coastline, the mountain range the city is wedged into, and none of those views is the fake one. Every zoom level shows you structure that was literally invisible at the level below, and the structure is really there — it's not something the cartographer invented to make the map pretty. So when Harari tells you the agricultural revolution was a trap, or Diamond tells you geography set the terms, or Christian tells you complexity needs Goldilocks conditions, that's not a story being imposed on the facts, that's the coastline coming into view. And the critics, he says, are just standing at street level complaining they can't see the ocean. I didn't have a comeback, but I don't buy it, and I honestly can't tell whether I don't buy it because the analogy is bad or because I don't like where it leads.

Can you audit the analogy properly? I want the correspondence laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the zoom levels, the map tiles, the projection, the fact that a human being decided what to put on the map and what to leave off, and the fact that the map is a tool built for a purpose rather than a neutral record of the territory. Then where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for what he's using it to prove versus which are just nitpicks. One thing that bugs me: I build dashboards for a living, so I know aggregation isn't innocent — I've watched a metric trend up at the account level while every individual user trends down, and both numbers are correct, and picking which one to show is a decision someone made. Is that the same problem as the zoom thing or a different one? And his real conclusion is that the grand narrative isn't a story, it's just what you see from far enough away, so the "it's just a narrative" critique bounces off. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If you think there's a better analogy for what big history is actually doing, I'd like to see it, but don't just swap one metaphor for another — tell me what the new one buys us.

Two things I want from the answer specifically. First, don't be diplomatic about it: if the analogy is doing real work, say so and say which parts, and if it's just rhetorically convenient, say that plainly, because I'd rather find out now than in front of him again. Second, tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong here — like, which consideration would actually flip your verdict on whether the zoom picture can support the "not just a story" conclusion, and what's the weakest link in the case you're making. If your verdict hinges on something I could go check or think through myself, point at it so I can.
