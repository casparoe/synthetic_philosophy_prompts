I'm in a reading group on individuation and persistence, and next week I have to set out my own position on the identity of indiscernibles so the others can take shots at it. Before that happens I'd like a private consistency check, because I have the uncomfortable feeling that some of what I say is doing quiet work behind my back. Here is where I've landed. (1) The identity of indiscernibles is a necessary truth: there is no possible world with two things that share every property and differ only in being two. (2) Concrete objects are bundles of properties in the compresence sense — an object just is a compresent cluster of universals, with no bare substratum underneath doing the individuating. (3) Black's two-sphere world is genuinely possible: a universe whose only contents are two perfectly similar spheres, each two miles from the other, is not merely describable but genuinely there in modal space, and I take its conceivability as decent evidence of that, since I think conceivability is a fallible but real guide to possibility. (4) Relations count as properties for the purposes of the principle, so the spheres are after all discernible — each bears the relation of being two miles from a sphere, and by impure relational properties of the form "two miles from this one rather than that one" they come apart. (5) I reject haecceities, bare particulars, and anything like primitive thisness: if individuation is not qualitative, it is not an explanation at all.

Tell me which commitments actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and — for the real ones — which member of the pair would have to go and what that costs.
