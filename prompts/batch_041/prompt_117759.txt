I got picked to give the student speech at our community college graduation next month. I'm 44, went back for the accounting certificate after twenty years of warehouse shifts, so this is a big deal for me and my family, and I want to get it right.

Here's the thing I actually want to talk about, because I've been reading about Gödel on my own for about a year and I can't stop thinking about it: any set of rules that's big enough to do arithmetic will have true statements it can't prove, and it can't prove it's own consistency either. But almost everything people say about this in motivational talks is wrong, and I don't want to stand up there and say a bunch of wrong stuff. My sister thinks it proves nothing is certain and everything's up for grabs. My brother-in-law thinks it means maths is broken, like it has holes in it. And I don't want to say "so believe things you can't prove" -- that isn't what it says and it's a weird thing to tell a room of people who just spent two years doing homework.

The hard part is that it still has to be a real graduation speech and not a lecture. So if the true version is narrower and stranger than the popular version, what's the actualy-inspring version that survives? I have one example I've been using at work and I want you to tell me if it's any good or if it's misleading: our warehouse inventory system can tell you anything you want about the stock, but it can't tell you whether the system itself is accurate -- to check that you have to step outside it and count shelves by hand. That's my best attempt at the consistency one. And the other one I think of as: for any rulebook big enough to count with, somebody can write a sentence in that rulebook's language that says "you can't prove me," and it's telling the truth.

The room will be about 400 people, mostly families, a few faculty, and my old maths teacher from the night programme who will definately notice if I say something sloppy, plus my sister who'll be disappointed if I don't say something mystical. Five to seven minutes.

Couple of rules for you: no quotes from anybody, I can't check them and I don't want to put words in a dead mathematician's mouth. And don't hand me five framings and let me pick -- choose one, commit to it, write the speech that follows from it, and then tell me afterwards what you had to leave out or soften to make it land, where you think I'm wrong about Gödel, and whether the warehouse thing is a bad analogy (if it is, give me a better one and write the speech around that instead).
