I have been asked to give the commencement address for our college's philosophy and cognitive science program. I have spent my career defending a version of eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires that is a little unusual. I sometimes call it the census view: belief and desire predicates are like the average household size reported by a census. The average can be true of a population and useful for prediction without being the size of any household. In the same way, I think talk of beliefs and desires can be true of a population and useful for prediction without picking out a state that any individual is in. I do not deny that people are predictable; I deny that the prediction tracks an inner belief or desire. The graduates have studied this position; their families have not. Please write the address, about seven minutes spoken. It should be genuinely moving and appropriate to a graduation, but it should not cheat by falling back on the vocabulary I reject. No telling them to believe in themselves, follow their dreams, pursue what they want, or hope for the future, unless the speech explicitly marks such phrases as folk-theoretical and refuses them. The constraint is the point: the philosophical position should determine the shape of the encouragement, not decorate it. You may use other resources—dispositions, habits, practices, attention, care, the things we do—but only if you can make them do the work honestly. The audience will include people who will notice every slip and people who simply want to hear something warm at a graduation. Do not mention books, articles, or citations. Please write it as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just the words I would say.
