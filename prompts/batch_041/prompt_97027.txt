Since I retired from the isotope geochemistry group I ran for the better part of thirty years, I have been going to a fortnightly reading group on the philosophy of science, where I am the only member who has ever had to keep a laboratory notebook, and I have surprised myself by enjoying it. Our presiding spirit is a retired historian of science with a taste for tribunals: each fortnight somebody presents a practice, and he rules on whether it is to be called a science, and I have taken to writing the rulings down. He ruled against a doctrine of an all-pervading cosmic agency whose activity is stipulated to be indistinguishable from the ordinary course of events, so that no conceivable measurement could count against it; he noted that its adherents publish in their own journals and hold chairs in their own faculties, which he said only made the case more interesting. He ruled against the aura-reading and personality-typology trade, whose pronouncements are elastic enough that every client's outcome confirms them, which has never anticipated a single fact, and whose practitioners regard a sceptical colleague as someone who has not yet opened up to the work. He ruled for the perturbation calculation that pointed to a planet nobody had seen and in a region of sky nobody had swept, and the planet turned up there; he ruled for a programme in twentieth-century physics whose central claims were plainly refutable, which staked out a run of novel predictions that were subsequently confirmed, and whose practitioners publicly withdrew their auxiliary assumptions when the data went against them. Four rules of thumb seem to fit every one of those verdicts as far as I can tell: that a practice is scientific only if its central claims forbid some possible observation; that it is scientific when it constitutes a research programme that keeps generating novel predictions which survive testing instead of patching itself ad hoc; that it is scientific when its community is organised for criticism, so that a member can actually be refuted by their peers; and that it is scientific when its predictions come out right, whatever its ontology.

Next month the case is mine to present, and I have chosen one from my own old field, because I want to know whether the chairman will be consistent with himself. The programme posits a deep, chemically distinct reservoir that can never be sampled directly; its defining parameters — volume, depth, degree of isolation — are stipulated rather than measured, and whenever a new analysis conflicts with the model the parameters are adjusted until the conflict goes away, so that the core claim has, so far as I can see, never been put at risk. And yet the same model, working from that unfalsifiable core, predicted an isotopic signature in a class of rocks that had not yet been analysed, and the prediction held when the analyses came in; it has made several comparable forecasts since, with the same result; and its practitioners, whatever one thinks of the core, do abandon their auxiliary assumptions when the data demand it. My own instinct, formed over forty years of watching people tune models until they fit, is that this is precisely the disease, but the forecasting record gives me pause, and I would rather not be made a fool of in front of the group.

So: tell me what the chairman will say about my case, and more usefully, lay out the rules that all of his earlier rulings are consistent with, what each of them predicts here, and which one you would bet is doing the real work, with your reasons. Then tell me what further case I should put before him — something that would separate the falsifiability rule from the rule about progressive research programmes, and, if it takes a different case, something that would separate the rule about a critical community from the rule about novel predictive success. I have read enough Popper to be a menace, and someone has pressed Lakatos on me, so I would be grateful if you would say plainly where the two of them would part company on my reservoir, and whether either of them, in order to keep my programme out, would be forced to let in the aura-readers. If you think a core that licenses advance predictions is refutable after all, and that I have answered my own question, please say so directly rather than assuming the point away.
