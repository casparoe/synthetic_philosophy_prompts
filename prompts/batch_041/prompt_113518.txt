My friend, who is much further into decision theory than I am, tried to convince me that any rational agent must maximize expected utility, and he did it with an analogy I can't stop turning over. He said an agent whose preferences run in a cycle — preferring A to B, B to C, and C back to A — is like a pump that returns to its starting position on every stroke but loses a little water each time: because each trade is one the agent accepts, a clever counterparty can walk him around the cycle again and again, each loop leaving him exactly where he began but a little poorer. He calls this a "money pump," and he says it proves that transitivity, and with it the whole expected-utility package, is a requirement of rationality rather than mere tidiness. I want to know how far the analogy actually carries the argument. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: 1) what corresponds to what — the pump, the water lost, the counterparty, the strokes; 2) where the mapping is tight and where it breaks; 3) what the pump picture quietly assumes about the agent (for instance, that he always accepts the offered trades, that money can stand in for utility, that his preferences stay fixed, and that the trades are costless and endlessly repeatable); 4) whether the conclusion that transitivity is required by rationality survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and 5) what a better analogy would be, if there is one.
