I am assembling a specification for a theory of psychiatric classification, and I want the desiderata in hand before I judge any candidate theory. Treat the case sketched below as stipulated background rather than as something to look up.

Suppose a major diagnostic manual introduces a new adult category — call it reduced social reward syndrome — defined by a checklist of behavioral criteria and diagnosed chiefly by self-report: a stable, lifelong pattern of diminished pleasure in social contact, low motivation to seek company, and little distress about the resulting isolation. The category is introduced because clinicians reported a gap and because a research group campaigned for it; its recorded prevalence rises steeply in the years after introduction; advocacy communities form around the label; some diagnosed people say the diagnosis reorganized how they understand their own histories and changed what they expect of themselves; and the category overlaps with, but is not reducible to, the autism spectrum and social anxiety disorder. It runs in families and responds partially to one class of medication, but its boundaries shift with each manual revision and its prevalence varies sharply across cultures.

Write the desiderata any adequate theory of psychiatric classification must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. I expect the list to cover, at least: the phenomena the theory must explain (the stipulated category and cases of its shape; the removal of homosexuality from the manuals; the historical rise and fall of hysteria and of the nineteenth-century "diagnosis" that treated enslaved people who fled captivity as mentally ill; cross-cultural variation in prevalence; comorbidity; heritability and treatment response); the distinctions it must not collapse (disorder versus diversity, validity versus reliability, natural kind versus social kind, classification versus diagnosis, the person classified versus the classification itself); and the empirical and sociological results it must remain consistent with. Be explicit about what the theory owes to the looping effect — the possibility that classifying people changes them, so that the category and its members co-evolve — and to the observation that categories can be revised in response to patient experience and advocacy rather than only to new biological findings.

Then do three things. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of a particular camp. State plainly whether the requirement that categories track a stable, mind-independent dysfunction is jointly satisfiable with the requirement that the theory accommodate looping effects and the revisability of categories under patient testimony; if these two cannot both be met, that conflict is the result I most want to see. And name the single requirement that does the most to exclude the current contenders.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; a scorecard of particular manuals or theorists is optional and secondary. Do not supply reading lists or citations — reason from the cases as given.
