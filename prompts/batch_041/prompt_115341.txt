Our reading group spent the whole term on Confucian ethics and the last session is on role ethics, and I want to open it with a poem about the Zai Wo bit in Analects 17 — the one where Zai Wo asks to cut the three-year mourning down to one year, and his argument is honestly pretty good: three years out of ritual and the ritual rots, three years out of music and the music collapses, the old grain is gone and the new grain is up, the fire-drills have been changed around, a year would do it. And the master doesnt argue with any of that. He asks whether Zai Wo would be at ease eating good rice and wearing brocade, and Zai Wo says yes, at ease. So the master says: if youre at ease, then do it — a gentleman in mourning can't enjoy his food, can't enjoy music, isn't at ease in his own house, that's why he doesn't do it; but you're at ease, so do it. And then Zai Wo walks out the door, and the master says to whoever's still in the room that Zai Wo isn't ren, that a child is in its parents arms for three years before it comes out of them, that three years is the mourning everyone does, and then the line that wrecks me: did Yu ever have three years of love for his parents.

What I want is a poem, somewhere around 20 to 35 lines, that I can read at the top of the session. The structural thing I actually care about: he says the same thing twice — if you're at ease, then do it — and the second time it isn't the same sentence. The first one hands the decision over; the second one has already stopped talking to him. If you use a repeated line or a refrain, that doubling is what it has to do, don't just bring it back for the sound of it. The door is the other hinge — everything the master really means is said after Zai Wo is gone — and the poem should let that land without a narrator leaning in to explain it.

Also let Zai Wo be right on the merits. His case is the better argument on paper, and the master answers him with a question about how he feels, which is either the deepest thing in the book or a dodge. Don't resolve it, and no closing sermon about filial piety or about how we've lost our rituals. One more thing from the reading we've been doing: on the role-ethics version of this, there's no self standing behind the roles deciding how much of itself to hand over, so the question how long do I owe him should feel a little malformed in the poem — the master's whole move is to stop treating it as a debt — but nobody in the poem should say that out loud.

Two practical notes. Don't put anything in quote marks as if it's a line from the text; I don't want a translation I can't check getting attributed to the Analects, so paraphrase the sense instead. And it doesn't have to be in a fixed form, but if you pick one, the form should be doing some of this work rather than just sitting there.

Be committed — pick a reading of the scene and write the poem so it lands, no hedging inside the verse. Then, after the poem, 4-6 sentences on where it takes liberties with the passage or leans on something people actually argue about (the at-ease test, whether the master is being cruel or just exact, whether Zai Wo's ritual-decay argument gets brushed off). I want to know what I'm teaching if I read it out.
