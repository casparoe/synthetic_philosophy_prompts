I sit on the historical marker and memorial committee of a small town in the American South. We are nine volunteers, none of us a professional historian; we meet once a month and rule on whatever the public brings us — a request to raise a new marker, to reword an old one, to add a plaque, to rename a school, occasionally to take a thing down. Our decisions can be appealed to the town council, so a bad ruling costs the town a year of argument. At present we decide by asking what feels defensible that night, which in practice means the outcome depends on who showed up. I have no formal training in history or philosophy — I have simply been reading on my own for several years — and I want to arrive at something I can actually hand to the committee: not an essay, a procedure.

What has occupied me is the contrast among the four places I keep reading about, because each seems to fail in a different way. In Germany the reckoning with the Nazi past is largely inward-facing: the memorials speak to the perpetrators' own descendants and say, in effect, this is what we did, and the whole apparatus of schools and monuments and a word for working through the past is aimed at the nation's own future. In Japan the contest is over whether the deed is admitted at all; a shrine in Tokyo honors the country's war dead, and its own records count people convicted as war criminals among them, so that honoring the fallen and glorifying what they did cannot be pulled apart — even the emperor stopped going. In Turkey the official position is denial; the mass deportations and killings of Armenians in 1915 are officially described as something else and saying otherwise has been a crime, so the question there is not what to build but whether anything may be said. And in the South the monuments themselves were the act of memory politics: they went up decades after the war, built by a we that defined itself against the people it had enslaved, so that here the memorial is the instrument of the false memory rather than the remedy for it. I notice that the local formula — that a statue is about heritage, not about hate — is doing exactly the work of separating the deed from the people who did it.

Out of all this I have pulled four ideas that seem to me to matter, and I would like them put to use rather than admired. First, the Germans have two words where we have one: one for a memorial that commemorates, which tells you that something happened or that certain people are worth remembering and which is usually built to leave you settled or proud, and one for a memorial that warns, which is built to unsettle you and to carry a lesson forward, and which is often deliberately uncomfortable to stand in front of. The strange fact I keep returning to is that the large Holocaust memorial in Berlin is called by both words, which suggests the same object is being asked to commemorate and to warn at once, and that those two jobs pull against each other. Second, I read of a project that puts small brass plates into the pavement outside the houses where victims last lived, instead of gathering the memory into one plaza you can walk around, so that you meet it while going about an ordinary day and cannot choose to avoid it — a completely different theory of what a memorial is for. Third, I have read of artists who design memorials to sink into the ground or efface themselves, on the theory that a permanent and beautiful monument flatters the visitor into believing the past has been taken care of. Fourth, the Japanese case raises a question I cannot settle: when you honor people who died serving a cause, and the cause was criminal, is there any way to honor the dead without honoring the deed? The shrine is defended as remembrance of the fallen and attacked as glorification of what they did, and both descriptions strike me as true.

So here is what I want. Give me a decision procedure the nine of us could run, in order, on any proposal that comes before us, containing: the questions in the order we should ask them; what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and what counts as a dodge; the point at which the procedure simply stops and the answer is no; and the points at which it must stop and hand the matter to a person or a body because the remaining question is one of values rather than of fact. It has to be runnable in the fifteen or twenty minutes we realistically give an item, by people who will never read the theory behind it, and it has to keep whatever is genuinely load-bearing in the four ideas above while dropping whatever will not survive the trip into a volunteer committee. Where the procedure has to simplify in a way that will make it misfire on some proposals, say which ones.

Then please run it, showing the steps, on three proposals. (a) Keep a Confederate monument in the square but add a large plaque explaining that it was erected in the early 1900s as part of a campaign to assert white supremacy — the compromise most of the committee favors. (b) Rename an elementary school now named after a local physician who was also, in the 1950s and 1960s, a leader of the local resistance to desegregation. (c) Place a small memorial at the old courthouse steps to the people who were sold there — this one is mine.

I expect the procedure to give me an answer I do not like on at least one of these, and I want you to say so plainly rather than bend the procedure toward my preferences. And at the end, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which proposals it will handle badly, and which of its steps are value judgments wearing the costume of a test.
