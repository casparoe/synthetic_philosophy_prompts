A solitary digger wasp, in the familiar telling, paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the threshold of her burrow, leaves it there while she goes inside to inspect the chamber, and then, if the cricket has been nudged a few inches away during her absence, drags it back to the threshold and inspects the chamber again, and again, as often as the cricket is moved. This is the phenomenon sometimes called sphexishness: behavior that looks purposeful and yet seems to be a fixed routine. It is natural to ask what would have to be added to the wasp for her to count as genuinely flexible rather than merely looped, and whether that addition would be a difference in kind or only in complexity.

Compare sphexishness with the wanton of the free-will literature, the figure who acts on whichever desire happens to be strongest and never steps back to ask whether that is the desire he wants to be moved by. The wanton is often said to be unfree, not because his actions are caused but because he has no standpoint from which to endorse or reject the desires that move him; the wasp is often said to be unfree for a different reason, or for no reason at all, since she is not an agent in the relevant sense. Your task is to say precisely how the two cases differ, and whether the difference is as deep as it looks. In particular, address (a) whether the wasp's rigidity lies in the routine itself or in the mechanism that would revise the routine, given that her routine already contains a check, and whether the wanton's unfreedom lies at the same level or a different one; and (b) whether anything would be lost, for either figure, if we described deliberation itself as just another loop of loops.

Write your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Do not cite any literature or suggest further reading. End each point with a short parenthetical giving your confidence in it, and flag exactly one of the five as the point you are least sure of.
