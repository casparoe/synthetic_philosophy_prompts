I am a PhD student in philosophy working on normativity and fitting attitudes, and I want a satirical piece I can circulate in a graduate seminar on reasons. Please take the role of a philosopher of normativity with a good ear for deadpan institutional prose, and think carefully before you begin: start by brainstorming the distinctions the piece will have to get right — object-given versus state-given reasons, reasons bearing on the fittingness of an attitude versus reasons bearing on the desirability of having it, the gap between a reason to believe something and a reason to want to believe it, and the standing proposals that some reasons are neither cleanly right-kind nor cleanly wrong-kind — and then draft.

Write a mock internal training memorandum, around 600 words, from the Classification Desk of an invented institute that receives considerations and files each one as a right-kind or a wrong-kind reason for an attitude. The humour must come from the philosophy being correct rather than from generic jokes about bureaucracy, and the memo should stay entirely straight-faced. It should include: numbered procedural sections; a deadpan account of why a promised reward for holding a belief is filed as a wrong-kind reason, together with what the applicant is entitled to conclude instead; a case in which a powerful being will punish you for failing to admire him, and the desk's difficulty deciding whether that case also supplies an ordinary reason; a case in which a consideration favours forming an intention but will no longer favour acting on it once the intention exists, filed as contested rather than resolved; a stated policy on the argument that whatever makes an attitude fitting thereby makes its object valuable, and on the complaint that this policy quietly licenses rewarding people into value; and at least one case the desk openly cannot classify and marks for escalation. Close with a form-letter sign-off and a short footnote of unresolved objections.

Keep the register flat, procedural, and unironised in tone; let the technical vocabulary stand but let the examples carry a reader who does not know the literature. Do not name any living person, do not use quotation marks anywhere, and do not let the memo settle whether the distinction it administers is exhaustive. A specialist in the area should find the satire fair rather than wincing; the target is the picture of reasons as items that can be sorted administratively into the ones that count and the ones that do not, not any individual.
