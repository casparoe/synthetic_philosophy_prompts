Assume, for the sake of the exercise, that failures to act can be genuine causes — that at least one defensible account of causation admits omissions as relata — and treat the live work as lying downstream of that concession rather than in re-litigating it. I am assembling a three-year programme (two postdocs, a workshop budget, no empirical staff) on the metaphysics of omission and prevention. The funder is a philosophy-friendly foundation whose stated interest is that the work eventually become usable by people doing institutional accountability: regulators, tort and criminal-law theorists, and teams that run post-incident reviews of automated systems. Abstract work is fine; work with no conceivable route to that use is not.

What should the programme take up, and in what order? I want the reasoning more than the list: for each item, what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is with the tools of analytic metaphysics plus a competent formal modeller, and what evidence over the next two or three years would move it up or down the agenda. My own hunches, which I would like you to assess rather than simply adopt:

1. Individuation. A single non-action can be described as failing to do A, as failing to do B, as failing to do anything at all, or as the positive event of continuing to sit still. Much of the disagreement about whether omissions cause anything may really be a disagreement about how omissions get cut up. Is there an account of omission individuation that is not simply relativized to our explanatory interests — and if there is not, does that relativism infect the causal claims themselves or only our reports of them?

2. The division of labour between counterfactual dependence and everything else. Counterfactual dependence is cheap in cases of inaction: for nearly any outcome there is some non-action on which it depends. If so, something else is doing the work of singling out the cause — a norm, a default, a statistical baseline, an expectation. Is that supplementary element constitutive of the causal relation, or is it a feature of our causal talk and our blame? The answer looks load-bearing for the accountability audience, so I want to know whether it can be settled before the applied work leans on it.

3. Omission chains. If causation is transitive and omissions can be causes, then long chains of non-actions look causally potent (nobody reminded anyone, nobody checked anything, and the outcome followed). Does an account of omission causation have the resources to stop such chains from swamping the more local causes, or must it give up transitivity, or deny that the chains are causal at all?

4. Representation. What formal apparatus can even represent an omission? In a structural model an intervention is defined relative to a set of alternatives, and it is not obvious what not doing X is as an intervention rather than as a description of an undisturbed system. If our only rigorous formalisms treat omissions as second-class citizens, does that tell us something about the metaphysics, or only about the formalisms?

Rank what you would fund, defend the ranking against criteria you state explicitly, and say what you would leave out — including anything that looks central but whose resolution would not change much downstream. Also tell me which of the four hunches above is most likely to be a dead end, and why. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want a case for a division of effort.
