I'm putting together something for a weird module I'm doing that's half maths, half drama, and I want to read Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead through Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. I'd like your take on it.

Here's the maths side as I understand it. Bertrand's chord problem — pick a chord of a circle at random, ask whether it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — comes out at 1/3 if you randomise over pairs of endpoints on the circumference, 1/2 if you randomise over a point on a radius and take the perpendicular chord through it, and 1/4 if you randomise over midpoints inside the disc. Three innocent-looking appeals to indifference, three different answers, so indifference on its own doesn't determine anything until you've said what the randomising procedure is. Jaynes later tried to rescue it by insisting the answer be invariant under shifting and rescaling the circle, which singles out the 1/2 as the only well-posed one. And there's the older name for the principle — insufficient reason — plus the reference class problem about which partition you're entitled to be indifferent over, which I think is where the real action is.

Now the play. It opens with a coin coming down heads again and again and again, well past the point where Guildenstern thinks the law of averages should be doing something, and he's genuinely rattled by it. The rest of the play is two men who don't know they're in a play trying to work out whether what's happening to them is chance, fate, or somebody else's script, with the travelling Players hanging about saying things about what makes a proper tragedy, and with all of us in the audience knowing perfectly well from Hamlet how it ends.

What I want is an honest reading rather than a cute pairing. My hunch, and I'd like you to push on it: the coin is the one case in the play where the randomising procedure is completely specified — physical coin, physical toss — and so the one case where indifference is innocent, and it's precisely that case that lets Guildenstern down, whereas everything else (whether the letter gets swapped, whether they die at the end) is the ill-posed case where there's no selection procedure to be indifferent about because the outcome is fixed by an author, so his worries about probability are aimed at the wrong target. Is that right? Does the play actually dramatise the ill-posedness of the phrase at random, rather than making any claim about chance? And does it know the difference between his ignorance and the world's indeterminacy, or does it blur them, and does it matter if it does?
