I teach a small graduate seminar on translation and literary form, and I am putting together a handout that has to do one job: map the logical space of positions on what a translator owes to a text's formal constraints, so that students can locate themselves in it rather than swap labels. A colleague and I have been arguing for a fortnight about whether an acrostic that loses its acrostic is still a translation of the same poem, and I would rather hand the students the whole map than my side of it. Please act as a philosopher of language with a specialism in translation and constrained writing, and build me that map. Begin by brainstorming, on paper as it were, the distinctions that genuinely do work here; resist the temptation to reproduce the familiar literal/free/imitation trichotomy, which is far too coarse for my purposes and which my students have already met. Think carefully about which candidate distinctions are substantive and which are merely verbal, and say briefly which you set aside and why before you commit to a tree.

Build the instrument as a decision tree in which every branch node is a question that a translator could answer about a concrete case without already knowing which position it leads to, and every leaf is a position, named where a name exists and marked 'unnamed' where it does not. The tree should run on cases in which the formal constraint is doing real work: an acrostic whose initial letters spell the dedicatee's name; a lipogram that avoids a letter the source language makes easy to avoid and English does not; a poem in a strict syllable-count metre rendered into a stress-timed language in which syllable count and stress pattern pull against each other; a text whose rhymes are load-bearing because the rhyme-words carry the argument; and a constraint that exploits a feature with no target-language analogue, such as vowel harmony or grammatical gender in the rhyme. Assume throughout that the semantic content is fully recoverable and that we are translating into English, so that any interesting failure is formal. Among the questions I expect the tree to turn on — though you should add and prune as the logic requires — are: whether the constraint is part of the work's meaning or merely a vehicle for it; whether the target must exhibit the same constraint or merely a constraint that does the same work for the target reader; whether a translator may impose constraints of their own that the source lacks; and whether an untranslatable constraint is a defect to be compensated for, a fact to be flagged, or an occasion for a new work. I should like six to nine leaves.

Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, do not silently drop it: say whether the gap is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and close with two or three sentences on which single branch does the most work in sorting the positions.
