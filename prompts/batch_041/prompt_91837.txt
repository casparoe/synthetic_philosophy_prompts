I have been asked to run a ninety-minute session on selection by lot for a mixed group of graduate students in law, economics, and public policy. Their background in democratic theory is thin, but they are quick with institutional arguments and will attack any proposal on design grounds. What I need from you is a teaching plan, not an exposition.

The object of the session is a proposal of my own construction, so that nothing turns on their having read any of the literature. In outline: a body of 150 members chosen by lot from the adult population, serving a single non-renewable term of eighteen months; the selection list is drawn and sealed, and published only when the body first convenes, so that nobody can court the selected in advance; the agenda is fixed by a rotating subset of the body's own members rather than by staff or outside experts, though the body may summon testimony; and its enactments take effect unless overturned in a referendum triggered by petition. Two features are the real target of the session: the members are never answerable at a future election, and the body contains no organized opposition — no party whose standing interest is to expose the other side's errors.

Two things must be carried by the session itself, and neither is anything the students will have met. The first is the distinction between the selection effects of a method of choosing legislators (who ends up in the room) and its incentive effects (how those who are there behave while there); I want them to see that elections, term limits, and lotteries differ along both dimensions at once, and that most popular arguments about reform run the two together. The second is the assumption that accountability requires the possibility of removal at an election, and I want them to leave able to state the difficulty this poses for my proposal rather than to hear it waved away.

Please give me, in order: (1) the sequence in which the components have to be introduced, with what each depends on — singling out the one idea that must land before the rest can even be stated, and any component that looks optional but is not; (2) the single concrete case the session should be built around, what it must have and what it must lack, and which obvious candidate I should resist using; (3) the misconceptions they will arrive with or form, in the order they will surface, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off — I expect at least that a random sample of the population is a random sample of the ignorant, that a body with no power to compel anything is a focus group, that sortition and term limits are rival answers to one question, and that a legislature nobody elected is for that reason undemocratic; add the ones I have missed, and tell me if any of my four is not a misconception but a fair objection I ought to concede; (4) one closing question that will show me whether they have grasped the design problem rather than merely absorbed the case for the proposal; and (5) what I should explicitly refuse to cover in ninety minutes, and how to say so.
