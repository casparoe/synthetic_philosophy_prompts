Next month I am leading a ninety-minute session for a cross-disciplinary reading group — twelve graduate students from economics and cognitive science, plus two lawyers — on David Lewis's account of convention. None of them has read any philosophy of language; most are comfortable with game theory. What I want them to leave with is not the ability to recite a definition but the ability to sort lookalikes correctly: a convention, a mere custom, the equilibrium of a coordination game, and a norm that carries genuine ought-force. Please plan the explanation rather than giving it to me.

I need the order of the ideas, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be in place before what, and why. Tell me which single idea, if I introduce it early because it happens to be the part I find most interesting, will poison everything downstream, and what I should do at that point in the session instead. Give me the sequence as a list of moves with rough minutes attached.

Then the carrying example. My candidate is a regularity I have watched settle in my own building: whoever reaches the lift first holds the door for whoever is behind, but only if that person is roughly within ten feet; nearly everyone does this, and nearly everyone is mildly annoyed by people who hold the door for someone visibly too far away to make it. Tell me whether that can carry the session, and if not, what would; and in either case, what the example must have and must lack. I know the textbook example is which side of the road to drive on. If you would rather use it, say why, but I suspect it is too easy: the arbitrariness is obvious and the mutual benefit is enormous, so they may come away thinking a convention is just an obviously good arrangement with an arbitrary flavour.

I expect the following misconceptions, roughly in this order, though I would like you to correct the order and add what I have missed: that a convention is something agreed to, promised, or contracted; that any regularity everyone follows is a convention; that a convention is simply the equilibrium of a coordination game, so any stable arrangement qualifies; that conventions are efficient; and that breaking one is blameworthy. There is a sixth I am not sure how to handle at all: I think they will hear common knowledge as nothing more than everyone knowing. For each misconception, tell me the exact point in the sequence where I have to head it off and what the move is that heads it off.

Give me one question to ask at the end that would discriminate — a case they must classify, where the answer turns on a feature they would have missed had they only half-understood the account. I would prefer the case to be one none of them has encountered before.

Tell me what to leave out at this level, and say it out loud to them, so that nobody leaves thinking they have had the whole topic.

One further thing I want the session to end on, because it is the part I actually care about: the same behaviour, sustained in one population by expectations and mutual preference alone and in another by genuine disapproval, so that they can see exactly what work the disapproval is doing that the expectations were not. Build that in if it can be built in; if it cannot be done in ninety minutes, say so, and tell me what it would have to displace.
