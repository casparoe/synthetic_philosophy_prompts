After a career in physical chemistry, I have spent the last several years reading philosophy with a small group of fellow retirees, and we have become stuck on a puzzle that I cannot stop turning over. The puzzle is this: you are stranded in a desert, and a passing driver will give you a ride to the next town only if he believes you will pay him a substantial sum on arrival; once you are in the car, however, you have no reason to pay, and both of you know it. The question is whether it can be rational to make yourself into the sort of person who will pay anyway—by a resolution, a habit, or some other device—so that the driver takes you. I have come to hold a view about this, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it.

My view is that the case shows act-based accounts of practical rationality to be incomplete. The correct account, I think, has two tiers: individual acts are assessed relative to the dispositions that produce them, and dispositions are assessed relative to the agent's long-run aims. On this account, the hitchhiker who pays acts rationally in the first sense, given the disposition that produced the act, and the disposition is rational in the second sense, because it secures the ride; the act is not rational relative to the agent's immediate aims, but that is the wrong level of assessment. I take this to dissolve the paradox without appealing either to self-deception or to a change in the agent's values, and I take it to generalize to many commitments—to honesty, to loved ones, to institutions—that would look irrational if assessed only act by act. In our group, someone proposed a variant that I think sharpens the issue: suppose the driver is an automated system that scans your brain and takes you only if it detects the disposition to pay, and suppose you can acquire the disposition only by taking a pill that also dulls your philosophical judgment for a week. Does the case still work, and does it still show what I think it shows?

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results would count against it? I am especially interested in the replies that defenders of act-based rationality have made or could make—that the case is really about coordination, that the agent's values make paying rational after all, that a broader act-description dissolves the paradox, that the disposition is not genuinely irrational in the relevant sense, or that the two-tier account collapses back into act-consequentialism at the level of dispositions. Say which of these are decisive, which are merely awkward, and which are live rather than merely logically possible, and rank the defeaters from fatal to incidental. I would also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not, whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and what its own defenders would concede counts against it. For my part, I would find it most troubling if the view turned out to be a redescription of act-consequentialism rather than a genuine alternative, so please tell me whether that charge sticks. Write your answer in American English.
