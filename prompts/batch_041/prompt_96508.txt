In 1962 the dialysis unit serving Seattle had far fewer machine slots than patients who could medically benefit from them. A medical filter came first — roughly, patients past a certain age and those with other grave conditions were excluded — and what remained was passed to an admissions committee, usually described as seven citizens, among them a lawyer, a banker, a housewife, a labor leader, a minister, and a state official. The committee did not meet the candidates; it worked from written records, its membership was not made public, and it weighed such considerations as marital status and number of dependents, income, occupation, church attendance, emotional stability, past performance, and public service. In the years that followed, dialysis capacity expanded and transplantation arrived, and in the early 1970s the United States extended Medicare coverage to patients with end-stage renal disease, which for practical purposes ended the rationing regime rather than settling the question it had raised. Take those facts as stipulated; you need not worry about the rest of the historical record.

Analyze that episode through the lens of incommensurable values and hard choices. Fix the framework as follows. Assume value pluralism: the goods at stake — more life, being a parent, being useful to others, being loved, keeping faith with a particular patient, distributing fairly — are several and not reducible to one another. Between two options, three relations are standardly distinguished: one is better; they are equal; or they are incomparable because there is no common measure on which to rank them, which is what I mean by incommensurability, and which is stronger than mere difficulty. I want a fourth relation in play as well, which some recent work calls parity: neither option is better than the other and they are not equal either, yet they remain comparable, in that a small improvement in one would settle the matter, so either choice can be rationally permissible. Finally, let commensuration name the construction of a single scale on which otherwise heterogeneous goods can be scored; the committee's criteria are an instance.

I want the following, in order. First, sort the comparisons the committee actually faced: which were ordinary better-or-worse judgments, which were ties, which look genuinely incommensurable, and which, if any, are cases of parity — naming the goods on each side of each comparison. Second, say what the scoring procedure is doing under each reading: on the commensuration reading it presupposes a common scale, on the incommensurability reading it manufactures a surrogate for one. Which reading better explains the moral character of the episode — in particular, why the revulsion attaches to the criteria themselves and not only to the outcomes — and can incommensurability explain that feature or only redescribe it? Third, run the case back against the theory: does the existence of a workable procedure that did in fact produce decisions count as evidence that the goods were commensurable after all, a scale we had simply not specified, or does it show only that a decision had to be made? Say what would count as evidence either way. Fourth, consider the standard treatment of hard choices for an individual chooser, where a choice can be right and still leave a remainder, and the chooser owes a kind of regret to the road not taken even when she was right to take the other. Ask what survives of this when the chooser is an anonymous, rotating committee deciding on files rather than on persons. Is the diffusion of the burden an improvement or a loss? Can a corporate body have the analogue of that regret, and if not, does the phenomenon belong to the values or to the agent? Fifth, note that the scarcity was eventually abolished politically rather than by value theory, and say what that does to the theory's scope: is incommensurability a fact about values, or about situations in which we decline to enlarge the option set, and can the theory say anything about when buying one's way out of a hard choice is itself the evasion? Close by stating where the framework goes silent — what a decision-maker facing this case could not learn from the thesis that the values are incommensurable, including whether the thesis has anything to say for or against deciding by lottery.

No references, please; work with the framework as I have stated it rather than reconstructing the literature.
