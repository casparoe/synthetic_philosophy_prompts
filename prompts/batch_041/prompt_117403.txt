I'm in a seminar on transitional justice this term and I got stuck with the opening presentation on Jaspers, so I've spent the week with the little book he wrote right after the war about German guilt, where he pulls apart four kinds of guilt that people kept jamming into one word: criminal guilt (the kind a court can actually convict you of), political guilt (what you carry just by having been a citizen of the state that did it, and which the winners get to judge), moral guilt (what you answer for to your own conscience and to the people you love), and metaphysical guilt (the one where you're guilty before God for being alive while others weren't, and no human tribunal, not even Jaspers, is allowed to sit in judgment). I think I've got the four boxes down. What's tying me in knots is what he'd make of the machinery we use now, and our case study next week is the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission — the deal where perpetrators who came forward, said what they'd done and showed it was politically motivated got amnesty, meaning the state consciously gave up on criminal guilt in exchange for a truthful story, and where Tutu and co. kept insisting this was the opposite of victor's justice. Would Jaspers even recognise that as a trade in the same currency? My hunch is he'd say the commission was trying to do the moral and metaphysical work with political tools, and that handing out amnesty is just the old victor's justice wearing a nicer suit, but then again he also seemed to think ordinary Germans had a duty to accept the political verdict the victors handed them, unfair or not, so I genuinely can't tell which way he'd land. 

The part I actually have to write about is worse. Does his scheme have any room for guilt that belongs to a structure or an institution rather than to persons — a state, an army, a church, a company — or would he call that a category mistake every time, since on his view only people can be guilty of anything? Because if only people can be guilty, then what exactly are we doing when a commission declares that the state as such is responsible for X and pays money to the descendants of the people it was done to, decades later, out of the pockets of people who weren't born yet? Would those descendants be carrying political guilt because they inherited the membership, or moral guilt for living off the proceeds, or nothing at all because guilt can't travel down a family line like an unpaid debt? Somebody in my seminar said Jaspers just needs a fifth box for structural guilt, which sounds right to me but also sounds like cheating. Don't summarise the book at me, I've read it — tell me what you think he'd actually say about the amnesty deal and about the money, and where his four boxes would start to crack.
