Last night a friend and I had a long argument about the problem of logical omniscience, and it ended without either of us conceding anything, so I want to see the whole thing laid out as a dialogue and figure out where it actually turns. Please write that dialogue.

Here is the terrain, stated as neutrally as I can. In the standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge, an agent's knowledge is closed under entailment: if she knows a proposition and knows that it entails another, she knows the consequence, and she knows every logical truth. My friend defended this. Her line was that it is a deliberate idealization of the same kind as frictionless planes and perfectly rational choosers — that the semantics is meant to capture what is implicit in an agent's information rather than the psychology of what she has actually managed to infer, and that once you separate explicit from implicit knowledge, the supposed problem dissolves. She also leaned on the operators that come free with the framework, such as what a group knows jointly once its members' information is pooled, and she claimed that the closure properties which look absurd for a single person are exactly right for a database or a committee.

I took the other side. My complaint was that the idealization is not innocent, because the very thing it idealizes away — that inference takes effort, that premises can sit in different compartments of one mind and never be brought together, that a tautology can be too long for anyone to ever entertain — is not incidental to knowledge but part of what we want the concept for. A theory that grants knowledge of every logical truth cannot explain the difference between an agent who can derive a consequence and one who cannot, and that difference is most of epistemology. I also raised a puzzle I still cannot resolve: closure under conjunction. If I know one thing and I know another, must I know the two of them together? It seems I can know each and still be genuinely surprised by the conjunction, especially when the conjuncts are drawn from different contexts.

I want the dialogue to do three things. First, let each of us state the strongest version of our position rather than a caricature. Second, have us engage each other's specific moves — when she offers the explicit/implicit distinction or the database analogy, I should respond to that exact move, and when I press the conjunction puzzle or the cost of inference, she should respond to that exact move; no talking past each other. Third, bring in the concrete repairs that people propose for the closure picture — restricting reasoning to the formulas an agent is aware of, admitting worlds in which logic itself fails, or treating a mind as a bundle of fragmented, locally consistent compartments — and have us argue over whether any of these actually saves the notion of knowledge or merely changes the subject. Both of us should be competent and neither should fold for the sake of a tidy ending. Please do not declare a winner; stop where the argument genuinely stands.
