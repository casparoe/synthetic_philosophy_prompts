I retired recently from a career in physics and have been working my way through the philosophy of mathematics on my own, and I have hit a passage in a popular introduction to infinity that I cannot adjudicate for myself. The book presents Thomson's lamp — the lamp switched on and off at intervals that halve each time, so that infinitely many switchings occur before the one-minute mark — and then summarizes the literature as follows. "Thomson showed that the description is contradictory, since at the end the lamp can be neither on nor off; after every 'on' there is a later 'off,' and after every 'off' a later 'on.' Benacerraf's reply has since become the standard treatment. He observed that the sequence of states — on, off, on, off, and so on — has no limit, and concluded that the lamp simply has no state at the end: the description is incomplete rather than contradictory. The puzzle, like Zeno's race, dissolves once we stop expecting an infinite sequence to have a last term; the lesson is that supertasks are a mathematical fiction rather than a physical possibility."

My difficulty is that this reasoning both appeals to me and troubles me. I am entirely at home with limits, and the claim that the state sequence fails to converge looks to me plainly true; but I cannot see how "the limit does not exist" licenses "the lamp has no state," and the assimilation to Zeno feels wrong in a way I cannot put my finger on, since in Zeno's case there is something that does converge and here, it seems, there is not. I would also like to know whether the passage has fairly reported the two philosophers, since it presents Benacerraf as having merely tidied up Thomson rather than as having opposed him.

What I want is an audit of the passage, sentence by sentence, in the spirit of a referee's report: which claims a philosopher working on supertasks would accept as stated, which they would wince at as an oversimplification, and which are simply false; what Thomson actually took himself to have shown about the lamp and about the possibility of supertasks; what Benacerraf's reply actually was, and why it is or is not decisive against Thomson; and finally whether the passage's closing claim — that supertasks are a mathematical fiction rather than a physical possibility — follows from anything true in the summary, or is an independent and perhaps unsupported assertion. Please lead with your overall verdict in a single sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
