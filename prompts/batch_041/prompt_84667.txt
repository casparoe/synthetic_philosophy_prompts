I work in public history — museum labels, curriculum panels, the odd truth-commission gig — and I'm about to pick a fight with my collegues, so I want my own position straight first. Here's what I actually think. (1) Collective memory and academic history arn't rivals answering the same question; memory is identity-constituting and answerable to what a community needs now, history is answerable to evidence, so "which one is true" is usually the wrong question. (2) All the same, the historian has a standing obligation to the record: when the evidence goes against a beloved communal story, going quiet is a failure of the discipline. (3) The "record" isn't neutral either — archives were built by whoever had the power to build them, so documentary history is often just somebody's memory with better shelving. (4) In public-facing work, when a community's memory and the professional consensus clash, and that community has been on the receiving end of state violence, the community should generally win: they bear the costs of being overridden and the identity stakes are theirs. (5) Two verdicts I'd defend: at a site where an oral tradition holds that a massacre happened and the archaeology turns up nothing, the label should state that it happened, full stop, no hedging parenthetical about absent evidence; but where a community's memory casts it purely as victim and drops its own documented atrocities against a neighbouring group, the museum must put the documents up anyway, over that community's objection.

Do these five hang together? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each one say which commitment has to give and what that costs. Some of them probably only look like conflicts — I'd rather you tell me which. Give it as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no closing summary.
