I'm a reporter on the science desk and I've got a mess I need help thinking through before our 4pm news meeting, so apologies in advance for the length.

I've been covering a mid-sized biotech's phase 2 trial. Two participants had a serious reaction back in the spring. My question to the company was simple: did you know about those reactions before you enrolled the rest of the cohort? I emailed their comms person and got back: "Every event in this trial has been reported in accordance with all applicable requirements, and the trial remains open." I wrote back saying that's not my question — are you telling me the reports went in before the later enrollments, or are you declining to answer? Crickets. A few days later on a call, their VP of comms said they weren't going to "litigate the timeline" and changed the subject.

So the story ran with the line that the company acknowledged the reactions were reported only after more patients were enrolled. It got picked up alot. Now their lawyers want a correction, arguing the statement was accurate and that we put a confession in their mouth. Privately I think they knew exactly what they were doing — that sentence was built to be deniable.

Two things are chewing at me. First, my desk has a rule of thumb I've never trusted: if the speaker could have tacked on "but I'm not saying anything about the timing" without contradicting himself, then the timing claim wasn't part of what he said, it was my inference, not his statement. That test feels backwards to me — the most deliberate evasions are exactly the ones you *can* tack that clause onto. Second, and this is the part that keeps me up: I had an internal email suggesting the safety report was drafted late. So I may have heard his sentence as an admission because I already believed it was true.

What I need by 4: should we run a correction, a clarification, or stand by the story? And in the follow-up, what am I actually allowed to say about them — that they declined to answer, that they misled people, or nothing at all? And please tell me what the real rule should be, because "the tack-on test" is going to get somebody fired one of these days. Keep it usable — I have to say this out loud to a room of editors who will roll their eyes at anything that sounds like a seminar.
