I'm an undergraduate in the final weeks of a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and our last meeting is devoted to Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes. I'd like to close the session by reading a short poem aloud, and I'd like you to write it. Please adopt the role of a poet who is also a careful reader of the moral-responsibility literature — someone who can make a formal constraint carry philosophical weight rather than merely decorate it. Think carefully before you begin, and start by brainstorming in a few lines about how the structure you choose could enact the idea, not just describe it; then discard the weaker options and write the poem. I don't need the brainstorm in the final answer unless you think it helps me see the choices, but the poem itself should show the result of that thinking.

Here is the conceptual target. Strawson distinguishes the participant stance — the stance in which we address one another and are exposed to resentment, indignation, gratitude, hurt feelings, and forgiveness — from the objective attitude, in which a person is treated as something to be managed, understood, treated, or planned around. He also distinguishes two ways a participant-stance response can be defused: we can excuse someone, which leaves them inside the interpersonal relation and merely revises our appraisal of the particular act, or we can exempt them, which removes them from the relation altogether as someone for whom the reactive attitudes are not, in the ordinary sense, in play. The exemptions are the interesting cases, because they come in degrees and can be entered into for someone who is otherwise a normal adult — through fatigue, derangement, or the temporary suspension of ordinary engagement. What I find philosophically striking is that the objective attitude is unstable: it is hard to sustain toward someone we live with, it tends to be a resource we pick up and put down for particular practical purposes, and yet it can also be justified as the sober, therapeutic, non-resentful way to deal with people who are dangerous or damaged. The worry the poem should leave open is whether the participant stance is something we can argue ourselves out of, or whether it simply reasserts itself and carries its own authority.

Please write a pantoum of about twenty-four to thirty-two lines. The pantoum's interlocking repetitions are what make it the right form here: a line that arrives as an accusation should return later as a clinical observation, and then return again with its force changed, so that the reader feels the same words shift from second-person address to third-person case note and back. Choose one repeated line that can plausibly function both as a participant-stance reproach and as an objective-attitude description, and let its four appearances do the structural work. Do not announce the shift in a footnote or an epigraph; the repetition itself should carry it.

Three further constraints. First, keep the vocabulary of the essay's distinctions accurate enough that someone who has taught this material would recognize the excuse/exemption contrast, the instability of the objective attitude, and the thought that the participant stance may be inescapable — but render them through concrete scenes rather than terms of art. A reader who knows the literature should be able to point to the line where the exemption happens; a reader who doesn't should still find it a real poem about the difficulty of staying angry at someone, or of not being angry at someone. Second, resist the temptation to resolve the tension in the last quatrain. The closing lines should leave the question of whether we can choose our stance genuinely open, without a moralizing turn in either direction. Third, avoid naming philosophers or works; I want the poem to stand on its own, and I'll supply the context when I read it.

After the poem, add two or three sentences in prose explaining which repeated line you chose, why its repetitions track the participant-to-objective shift, and what you deliberately left unresolved. Those sentences are for me, not for the seminar.
