ok so a friend of mine dropped this arguement on me and I can't find the loose thread. Setup first: we flip a fair coin until the first heads. If the first heads shows up on toss n, I win 2^n / n dollars when n is odd, and I lose 2^n / n dollars when n is even. So toss 1 wins me $2, toss 2 costs me $1, toss 3 wins me $8/3, toss 4 costs me $4, and it just keeps going forever. Then his argument:

(1) The expected value of a bet is each payoff times its probability, all added up.
(2) So the expected value here is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which comes to about 69 cents.
(3) But addition doesn't care about order, and nothing forces me to add the terms in the order the tosses come in. Rearrange them and the same sum comes out to a million dollars, or minus fifty, or any number you like (he says there's a theorem for this).
(4) The gamble is the same gamble either way, so its expected value can't be two different numbers.
(5) So the game has no expected value. There's no fact about what it's worth.
(6) And if there's no fact about what it's worth, there's no fact about whether it's worth playing, or at what price. And this isn't some special case — the same move works on lots of gambles — so "expected value" isn't a real property of gambles at all, just an artifact of how you write the sum down.
(7) So we should stop pretending expected value can settle anything.

My gut says (7) is way too strong. I'd still say don't pay $1000 for this thing, and I'd happily pay a penny. But every one of (1)–(6) looks fine to me on its own, which is the problem. Which step is doing the damage, and if I patch it, what do I have to give up? And seperately — if the game really has no expected value, then what *should* I be willing to pay for it, and on what grounds?
