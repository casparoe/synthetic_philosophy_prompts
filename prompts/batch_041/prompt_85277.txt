Write a short story of roughly 2,500 words, set in a near-future research facility, that dramatizes a problem about which computations matter morally. The story should be about the problem rather than a lecture that gestures at it: no character may explain the philosophy to the reader, and no narrator may settle the question.

Take the following as fixed background.

The facility performs what its staff call witness runs. A witness run instantiates a detailed dynamical model of a nervous system, steps it through a fixed sequence of internal states, records a single checksum, and halts. The run exists to demonstrate that a computation of that kind can be performed at all: to satisfy a certification requirement, to win a benchmark, to prove something about the hardware. Nothing downstream depends on it. No one reads the intermediate states; the data is discarded; any machine that could be made to step through the same sequence would do equally well.

The distinction the story turns on is between tethered and untethered computations. A computation is tethered when its intermediate states are load-bearing: the final state cannot be produced without actually passing through them, and no table, shortcut, or summary could stand in for the sequence. It is untethered when the same input-output profile could be delivered by a lookup. Witness runs are tethered in the strongest sense the facility can certify — the intermediate states do real work — and yet that work terminates in a checksum and is thrown away.

Among the staff there are at least three defensible positions, and the story must give each of them to someone with something at stake:

- that tethering is what matters, so a state that plays the right functional role in the right kind of process is a state that can be wronged, whether or not anything downstream depends on it;
- that what matters is belonging to a life — an ongoing system with memory, needs, and a stake in what happens next — so that a discarded run, however tethered, is nobody's experience;
- that the question is malformed, and that what the facility is really doing, morally, is to its living employees, its clients, and the dead whose neural data were used as templates.

Requirements for the story:

- Choose one viewpoint character who must act rather than opine: someone who has to start the next run, certify it, cancel it, or answer for it afterwards. Give that character a reason to want one of the answers to be true that has nothing to do with the argument.
- Give the run a history. Make it a model of a particular person, or of a particular animal, and let what the characters know about that fact do work in the story.
- Include at least one scene in which two competent characters argue in dialogue about whether the discarded states matter, and let each land a point the other cannot answer. Neither may be a straw man, and neither may win.
- Ground the story in the ordinary texture of the place: shift schedules, maintenance, paperwork, the sound of the room, the tedium of certification.
- End on an action and its cost, not on a verdict.
- Do not use the words sentient, sentience, conscious, consciousness, qualia, moral status, personhood, or phenomenal. Do not name any real philosopher, book, or theory. The concepts must arrive through what the characters do, notice, avoid, and pay for.

After the story, add a note of no more than 150 words naming the crux you took the story to turn on and stating which of the three positions you, as author, find least defensible, and why — without revising the story to fit.
