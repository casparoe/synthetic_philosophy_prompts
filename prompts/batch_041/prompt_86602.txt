For two years I have taught a Wednesday-afternoon workshop in the MFA program here — fourteen students, ninety minutes, one whiteboard, and a great deal of coffee — and I have never once succeeded in making them believe me when I say that an analogy can be right in more than one way at the same time. They nod politely and go back to writing sentences that are secretly arguments with one hidden answer. So I have stopped lecturing and decided to stage the thing. What I want them to feel in their hands is the discovery Douglas Hofstadter's research group kept running into while building Copycat, their model of analogy-making, and which he wrote up in Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies: that in a microdomain as thin as strings of letters there is no fact of the matter about how to carve a problem up; that notions like same, successor, group, reverse and opposite are not labels one applies to a fixed scene but structures that slide into one another; that a single string can be seen in several ways at once; and that two people who answer the same puzzle differently may both be right, the verdict on rightness coming from a judge standing outside the puzzle rather than from anything inside it. I have read enough of that work to talk about slipnets and codelets, but I do not want the workshop to be about a program — I want the participants to be the program for an hour and to catch themselves in the act of slipping.

I have invented the raw material myself, precisely so that nobody in the room recognizes the items. The domain is letter strings and the relation of the form 'X is to Y as Z is to —'. Five of the warm-up items all begin from the same source pair, 'aab is to aac', 'aab is to bbc', 'aab is to baa', 'aab is to ab', and 'aab is to aabb', each followed by the same target, 'mmn', so that the problem set quietly licenses five different answers to one question — the rightmost letter advancing, every letter advancing, the string turning over, the doubled letter being dropped, the doubled letter being doubled — and the pair 'aab is to baa' can itself be read as either a reversal or a rotation, which is a slip inside a slip. Later targets are chosen to make the structure argue back: 'cdd' for the doubled-letter readings, 'xyz' where the alphabet simply runs out and the solver must decide whether to wrap, extend, or refuse. I would like you to design the runnable ninety-minute session around that material: what the participants do in the room, what the rules are, who holds which role (I am taken with the idea of a rotating judge who must rank the answers for naturalness and give reasons, and of a mid-session round in which each team is handed a card naming a different family of features — letter identity, alphabetical distance, group shape, length, direction — and made to re-solve items they have already solved, then confronted with the answer they gave the first time), what the stakes are in place of money, and a minute-by-minute timeline.

Then give me the debrief, which is the part I care about most: it should map specific moments of the session — the moment the five answers surfaced, the moment the judge's reasons started sounding like the answer rather than a reason, the moment a team re-solved its own item under someone else's vocabulary and could not dismiss the rival result — onto the corresponding parts of the idea, and it should not name Hofstadter, Copycat or conceptual slippage until it is well underway. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure without rescuing the participants, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will therefore have to supply myself. I do not need the activity to be fair or to have a winner; I need it to leave the room slightly unsure that 'the answer' is a thing that exists.
