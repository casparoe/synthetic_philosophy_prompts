I'm in a biweekly reading group on philosophy of technology and we've just done Winner's 'Do Artifacts Have Politics?', which I'd read before but never with a concrete case in front of me. What I want from you is the case, not the theory. Take San Francisco and its freeways. My memory of the map is hazy so correct me gently, but as I understand it: in the fifties the city was slated for a full interstate network, one route through Golden Gate Park and the Panhandle, more elevated roadway through South of Market and the Mission, and the stretch that actually got built was the Embarcadero Freeway, an elevated double-decker that opened in 1959, ran along the waterfront and walled the Ferry Building off from downtown. In January of 1959 the Board of Supervisors passed a resolution against the rest of the network; in 1964 there was a huge anti-freeway rally in Golden Gate Park; most of the network never got built. Then the 1989 earthquake knocked the Embarcadero about, the city spent a couple of years arguing over whether to repair it, and in 1991 it came down, and the waterfront is now the city's showpiece. Don't worry about exact dates and dont give me citations -- I want the analysis, and if a fact is load-bearing enough that you'd want it checked, just say so.

Here's the analysis I'm after, and I want it running in both directions. First, which of Winner's claims does this episode actually support -- the weak one, that technical arrangements are ways of building order and settling who gets to do what, or the strong one, that some technologies are inherently political and demand a particular social order? And which does it fail to support? I keep hitting the same snag with the strong claim: the same transportation need could have been met by a subway, or a boulevard, or nothing, and different cities met it differently, so the politics starts to look like it lives in the choosing rather than in the concrete. If that's right, say so plainly and tell me what's left of the thesis; if I'm wrong, tell me what the artifact itself is supposed to be doing that a process explanation can't cover. Be disciplined about the mapping -- who imposed what on whom, which options the freeway closed off and for whom, what the relevant social groups would be if you want to bring the social-construction people in. Then the bit I actually can't get past: the object's politics flipped. The same stretch of elevated roadway was an imposition, then a civic embarrassment, then a thing worth demolishing, and the ground it freed up is now valuable in a way that has its own winners and losers. What does an artifacts-have-politics thesis do with a flip like that -- refutation, or evidence that the thesis was always about the whole arrangement (design plus money plus who has standing to object) rather than the object? And what do I do with the fact that the decisive events were an earthquake and an argument about who pays for a retrofit, not a deliberation about the good city? Where the theory is silent, say it's silent; don't make it speak. Finish by telling me what evidence would actually settle, for this case, whether the freeway had politics -- and whether the fact that I can't imagine what would count as settling it is my problem, Winner's, or the thesis's testability.
