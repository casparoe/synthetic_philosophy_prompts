Write a dialogue between two philosophers going at each other over whether the difference between doing harm and merely allowing harm carries any real moral weight. One is a nonconsequentialist who thinks it does — not because she's squeamish about pushing people off footbridges, but because she thinks agency is the issue: what you do is yours in a way that what the world does around you is not. The other is a consequentialist who thinks the distinction is either arbitrary or a stalking horse for something else, and who knows the cases well enough to be dangerous. Neither should be a strawman and neither should win by fiat; every time one of them makes a move, the other has to actually answer it rather than pivot to a different point. Let them genuinely get somewhere and then genuinely get stuck.

Please don't let this turn into a trolley-problem rehash. Things I'd like them to fight over:

- the criminal-law duty to act when you created the danger yourself (the squatter who falls asleep with a lit cigarette, sets the mattress on fire, then just rolls over) versus the default rule that there's no liability for doing nothing
- end-of-life law, where the US Supreme Court upheld a ban on physician-assisted suicide in Vacco v. Quill by leaning on the doctor's intent rather than on any act/omission line — which arguably concedes that the interesting distinction isn't doing vs allowing at all
- the psychologist's omission bias, and the Knobe-style finding that whether people say a side effect was brought about on purpose flips depending on whether the side effect is good or bad. The skeptic should use these to argue the intuition is a quirk of how we assign blame; the defender needs a real reply here, not just a shrug about debunking explanations
- Warren Quinn's suggestion that the doing/allowing line is tracking positive vs negative rights, and the counter that it's really tracking harm-as-means vs harm-as-side-effect

At some point they should land on a case where the distinction seems to come apart from itself — switching off a ventilator vs never putting someone on one, or deleting a post vs declining to host it — and each of them should be forced to say something that costs them.

Around 900 words, give the two speakers names, no footnotes and no reading list.
