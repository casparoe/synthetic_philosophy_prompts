In a reading group I sit in on, a visiting neuroscientist and one of the regular members spent most of a session arguing about integrated information theory, and I still cannot work out who was making sense. (1) The neuroscientist began by saying the theory is not really empirical. Its central claim, she said, is that a system's integrated information — its Φ — just is its experience; but since we have no independent handle on experience, there is nothing for Φ to be checked against, and because Φ is computationally intractable for anything the size of a brain, the theory only ever gets applied to toy networks small enough to calculate, where the answer tends to have been built in beforehand. (2) The member replied that this confuses hard to compute with unfalsifiable, and that the theory sticks its neck out in at least three places: it predicts that the cerebellum, which contains several times as many neurons as the cerebral cortex, contributes almost nothing to consciousness, because its elements are arranged in largely feedforward, modular chains rather than densely recurrent ones; it predicts that a purely feedforward system has zero Φ however clever its behavior; and it predicts a posterior rather than a prefrontal substrate for experience. (3) The neuroscientist said she would grant all three and still object, because the real work is done by the exclusion postulate. Drop it, and every subsystem with positive Φ is conscious, so experience proliferates without limit; keep it, and the theory merely stipulates that the conscious system is whichever one has the maximum Φ — a rule that looks designed to land on whatever we antecedently took to be the subject, and that cannot be checked, since the maximum is uncomputable in a real brain. She added that the rule commits the theory to strange verdicts: if the maximum-Φ complex were a shifting, oddly shaped coalition of neurons that failed to line up with the person, or if joining two brains with a rich bundle of fibers yielded a single complex, IIT would have to say that the person was not the subject, or that there was one subject where we thought there were two. (4) The member answered that exclusion is not a patch bolted on to save the appearances but a consequence of experience having to be definite — there cannot be one experience that is also several overlapping ones — and that the strange verdicts are predictions rather than reductios: if the causal structure is like that, so is the subject. He added that IIT is not a form of functionalism, which is why a system that merely simulated a brain, or realized the right input–output function with the wrong causal structure, would not be conscious — a commitment he thought was exactly the kind a substantive theory ought to assert. They asked me to referee, and I could not. Please go through the four moves and say who is right about what, and what both of them are missing.
