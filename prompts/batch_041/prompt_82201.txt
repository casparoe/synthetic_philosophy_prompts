I'm a novelist — third book out next spring, the current one's about a woman who runs a chain of care homes and lives or dies by her inspection rating — and I keep running into a sentence that I've started to think is doing about six different jobs at once. It goes: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Apparently it's an economist's law from the 70s that an anthropologist later turned into that exact sentence, and now it's the thing everyone says. Last week I was at a governors' meeting at my kid's school and the chair killed a whole discussion about the new progress measure with it — his point being that the measure would just become a target and then we'd have nothing left to look at. Nobody argued. I didn't know how to argue either, and I'd rather not recieve a lecture about accountability in modernity as my consolation.

So: put on your philosopher-of-social-science hat, or your measurement-and-evaluation hat, whatever you've actually got, and help me take that one sentence apart. I want the distinct precise claims it could be making laid out as a menu — starting from the versions that are barely more than a tautology (any imperfect proxy for a goal, once you optimize the proxy, stops being a perfect proxy... well, obviously) up through the sweeping versions that say something like: no indicator can survive being used as an indicator, so target-setting is doomed in principle. Somewhere in that range there are readings that are really about people gaming and cheating, readings that are about statistics quietly going wrong with no cheating at all (you select on the numbers that looked good, and they drift back down), and readings where the thing being measured actually changes character once it's counted, so the number stops meaning what it used to mean even if nobody lies. I want to know which of these are trivial, which are real claims a person could defend, which are probably false, and which one the chair at my meeting was actually leaning on — and whether that version really gets him his conclusion (drop the measure) or whether it undercuts him too.

Think it through before you write — maybe start by just listing every reading you can generate, then cut the list down. And keep it tight: under 800 words, and open with one sentence saying which reading you'd bet the chair meant, then give me the menu.
