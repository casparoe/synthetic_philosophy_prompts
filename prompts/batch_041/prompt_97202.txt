My cousin and her friend Marcus got into an argument at a party about vagueness, and since I was the only other person there who'd taken a philosophy class they drafted me as referee and I completely failed. So I'm outsourcing the verdict to you. Both of them know this stuff way better than me, so I've tried to reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can. Names changed.

Dana went first. She said vagueness is a feature of language, not the world: a word like "heap" is imprecise because there's a whole range of admissible ways to make it precise, and a sentence is true when it's true on all of those, false when false on all, and neither when the precisifications disagree. So for a borderline pile there's just no fact of the matter. She said this also kills the sorites: the premise "if n grains is a heap, so is n minus one" comes out not-true on every precisification, even though no single n is the culprit.

Marcus said that can't be right. He said on her own semantics, "there is some exact number of grains that makes the difference" comes out true -- true on every precisification -- while she denies that any particular number is the true one. So she's committed to a sharp boundary she can't point to. He said a pile is a heap or it isn't, and that fact doesn't evaporate because our word is fuzzy -- like saying a mountain has no precise height because we haven't measured it. Vagueness is ignorance, he said: the boundary is real and sharp and just unknowable, the sorites has a false premise, we just can't know which one, and that's precisely why the paradox feels inescapable.

Dana said the mountain analogy actually cuts her way: a height only has an answer once we fix sea level and a unit, and without the convention there's no hidden fact being withheld, just no answer yet. Same with "heap." And she said his view has a nasty consequence -- near the boundary he can't know "this is a heap" even when it is one, so heap-knowledge becomes impossible across a big range, whereas she gets to say we know tons of heaps because the knowledge tracks what's true on every precisification. She also threw in that on her view "either this is a heap or it isn't" is perfectly true, so she keeps the law of excluded middle for the disjunction even while neither disjunct is true or false.

Marcus said the margin-of-error thing is fair and he'll take it -- knowledge near the boundary is hard, but hard isn't impossible, and she has the same problem, since she can't precisely know "this is borderline" either. Then he said her admissible precisifications are themselves vague: where does the range of admissible ways of drawing "heap" stop? If she needs a definite set of them she's smuggled in a sharp line one level up, and if she lets that be vague too then the theory is being applied to itself and comes apart. He said he's got one unknowable line and she's got an unknowable cloud of them.

Dana conceded higher-order vagueness is a real cost but said it's the same phenomenon recurring, not a contradiction, and she doesn't need a definite set. Then she said he still owes her an answer: he says "there's a last heap-grain" is true and unknowable, and that every vague predicate has such a boundary, so he's populated the world with infinitely many forever-hidden facts -- which she called metaphysics run amok to save one awkward thing about bivalence. Marcus said no, what's actually incoherent is her side: she makes "there's a last heap-grain" true while "this is the last heap-grain" is neither true nor false for every number, so she's affirming the existence of something no possible instance of which she'll affirm. He said he'd rather have hidden facts than a true existential with no true instance.
