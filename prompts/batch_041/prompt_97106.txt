Two people in my reading group got into it last week after a session on Gödel, and I've been chewing on it since because I genuinely couldnt tell who was ahead. It was about whether the incompleteness theorems say anything about minds versus machines. I'll reproduce it as best I can.

Ravi went first. Take any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic, he said. There's a sentence it can neither prove nor refute, and we can see that it's true. So for any machine that grinds out theorems from fixed rules there's a truth it can't reach but we can. The mind isn't a machine, full stop.

Nadia said that's sleight of hand. To 'see' the sentence is true you have to see that the system is consistent, and that's exactly the thing the system itself can't establish — that's the whole second theorem. If the human gets to just help herself to it, why can't the machine? And she added: humans are inconsistent all the time, and if you're inconsistent you can prove anything, so reaching truths the machine can't comes cheap. Consistency is both the price of the machine's limitation and the thing you can't vouch for in yourself.

Ravi came back with a picture I hadn't come across before, and it's the part I keep getting stuck on. Fine, he said, imagine the machine is self-improving: whenever it hits a sentence it can't prove, it just adds 'I am consistent' as a new axiom and carries on. Now it proves the old Gödel sentence. But there's a fresh unprovable sentence one level up, so it adds another consistency axiom, and so on forever. That's a machine on a treadmill, he said — it only ever closes the old gaps by assuming its own blindness away, and it never closes the gap it's standing on. Whereas we just see the whole tower at once and know every level is consistent.

Nadia said that last sentence is where it all falls apart. She said the machine can be built to do exactly what he described — add the consistency axiom, prove the old sentence, add another, prove the next — and at no point is it doing anything you aren't doing. What he calls seeing the whole tower at once is just accepting an infinite pile of consistency assumptions without checking a single one, and if he can do that on faith then a machine can be programmed to do it on faith too. She said the only way his argument works is if he can survey every possible machine at once and certify that none of them is the one he's running on, and that's a trick nobody can pull, including him.

They went back and forth a bit more but those four moves were the load-bearing ones and neither budged. Can you adjudicate it move by move — Ravi's opening, Nadia's first reply, Ravi's treadmill, Nadia's last move — and say who's right at each step, not just who wins on the whole? Rate each of the four from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify each rating in a sentence or two. And then say what both of them are missing. One aside too: earlier Ravi claimed this has to apply to any rule-governed system at all, 'a legal code, a company handbook, anything,' and Nadia just laughed. Was she right to laugh, or is there something to it?
