I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy finishing a term-long reading group on rational choice and cooperation, and for our last meeting I'd like to close with a poem rather than a summary — something we can read aloud and then sit with. The idea I want it to hold is the one usually called superrationality: that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two agents who each know the other reasons exactly as they do, each should cooperate, because each one's choice is the output of the same reasoning applied to the same situation, and so defecting cannot be the answer that arrives in one of them without arriving in the other; the ordinary dominance argument, which treats the other player's choice as fixed independently of one's own, is thereby supposed to lose its grip. I want the poem to take that seriously without pretending the objections are settled.

The form should do the work. Twenty-four lines, in two twelve-line halves. The second half must be the first half in the same order, with I and you exchanged throughout, so that the poem reads as though the other party had spoken it. If you use rhyme, the rhyme sounds should be identical in the two halves, so that the symmetry is audible and not merely visible. Exactly one line must be exempt from the exchange and appear word for word in both halves; that line is the poem's hinge, and it should be the point at which the argument stops being a matter of symmetry and becomes a matter of assumption. It should be genuinely uncertain who is speaking it — either of the two, or a third reasoner who is almost, but not quite, identical to them. That boundary case, the near-copy, is exactly where the position is contested, so it should be the poem's most exposed line rather than its most confident one.

Three things the poem must not do. It must not turn the position into altruism: the cooperating agent may care nothing whatever about the other's payoff and may be reasoning only about what it is rational for someone like them to do. It must not present cooperation as a way of influencing or signalling to the other player, who has already reasoned their way to a choice independently. And it must not let the familiar complaint go unanswered — that the cooperator is the one who gets taken, that in a world of defectors the agent who reasons this way simply does worse, and that a theory of rationality which reliably leaves you poorer than the alternative owes an explanation. You may leave that complaint standing; you may not pretend it has been refuted, and you may not let the poem settle on whichever side is more comfortable.

Please assume the standard ordering of the dilemma's payoffs (temptation above mutual cooperation, above mutual defection, above being suckered), so that this is a poem about a specific problem rather than about cooperation in general. Do not name theorists or cite texts; I want the reasoning in the poem's own voice. Give it a title. And append a note of no more than five sentences identifying the line you left unexchanged and saying what work it does, so that I can check whether the formal constraint is carrying the argument or merely decorating it.
