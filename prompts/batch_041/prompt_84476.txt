I run a 90-minute workshop for about twenty graduate students in political philosophy and PPE, and I want an activity that makes them feel, rather than merely concede, the structure behind races to the bottom: an outcome nobody chose, nobody wants, and nobody can unilaterally leave. The stock examples — arms races, overfishing, advertising — they already know, and knowing the examples has made the phenomenon feel obvious and therefore inert. So the activity has to do more than re-run a prisoner's dilemma. It should make them feel that the same sense of being trapped can arise from structurally different situations whose remedies differ, and leave them unable to say from the inside which situation they were in.

Please design it around that. My rough thought, which you should improve on if you see something better: three or four short rounds of play, same group, same surface situation, with one rule changed each time, so that the participants themselves can locate which feature is doing the work. The variants I want them to have run into without being told are (a) a case where a good outcome is available and known to be available but not selected, because no one can be sure the others will join; (b) a case where the bad outcome is the individually rational move whatever the others do, so that no agreement survives contact with the next decision; (c) a case where nothing gets built or used at all because too many parties each hold a veto — the mirror image of the commons, where the failure is too many rights of exclusion rather than too many appropriators, so that each holder can block the others and the resource is left idle; and, optionally, (d) the same situation repeated with a high enough chance of meeting again that cooperation becomes self-sustaining, so they can feel the shadow of the future as a variable rather than a slogan.

Constraints: paper, pencil, a simple scoring sheet; no money, no computers; everyone plays every round; it must fit the 90 minutes including the debrief. Do not use the names of the games, or any of the nicknames the phenomenon has acquired, in the rules or the briefing — they should meet the structure first and the vocabulary afterwards.

Then give me: the rules and materials, plus a one-page run sheet I can hold while they play; the payoff scheme, and why those numbers, with an explicit note on which inequalities between the payoffs are load-bearing and which are arbitrary, since someone will ask; the round-by-round timeline with timings; the opening briefing and nothing beyond it; the debrief, mapped moment by moment onto the ideas above, including the point that remedies are diagnosis-relative (assurance, enforceable commitment, aggregating or unbundling rights, lengthening the shadow of the future) and that the remedy for one variant is useless or worse in another; a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — collusion before the rules are understood, one player refusing the dominant move and thereby shifting everyone's expectations, round-one norms bleeding into round three, participants reading the payoffs as moral weights rather than preferences, the debrief collapsing into a lecture — and how to handle each; and an honest note on which features of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce at all and which I will therefore have to supply in words, among them scale, decades-long institutional and cultural drift, the way real races reshape what participants want rather than merely frustrating what they already want, and the question of complicity, which no payoff matrix settles.

Finally, separate for me what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. I do not want a contested diagnosis presented as consensus, and I want to know where the activity's own framing takes a side — for instance on whether these failures are one phenomenon or several, whether the fault lies in structure or in institutions or in what people want, and whether competition reliably runs downward or sometimes upward.
