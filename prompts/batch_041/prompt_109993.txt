I'm making a hand-out for a second-year populaton ethics seminar and I want a dialogue in it. Two characters, both sharp, neither of them a strawman — if one has an obvious reply the other isn't making, I'll bin it.

The case they're arguing over: a mining company can build its tailings dam to a cheap spec or an expensive one. The cheap dam will eventually leak, and over the next eighty years the kids in the valley grow up on mildly contaminated water — a few years of healthy life lost each, their prospects knocked back a bit, though their lives are still plainly worth living. The expensive dam doesn't leak, but the cost means the mine opens on a different schedule, the town's population turns over differently, and a different set of kids ends up being born. So almost everyone in the cheap-dam future wouldn't have existed at all in the expensive-dam future. Except for a handful of families who stay put either way, whose kids are born in the same years, and those kids are worse of under the cheap dam.

Character 1 thinks a wrong has to be a wrong done to somebody: if nobody is made worse off, there's no wrong, and our recoil from the cheap dam is either a mistake or is really about that handful of overlapping families. Character 2 thinks this case shows the whole person-affecting way of thinking has to go, or be widened so far that it stops being person-affecting at all.

Yes, I know it's a version of the non-identity problem — don't rehash the standard examples, I want these two going at it over this case. Write the dialogue as a real back-and-forth where each answers the other's actual points, roughly 700 words. Then two short sections: 'Where they're really disagreeing' (~150 words), sorting out which bits of this are genuinely contested among people who work on it and which are settled, without dressing up a contested view as the consensus; and 'Best move on each side' (~120 words). British English, and no references, citations or reading lists — I just want the argument.
