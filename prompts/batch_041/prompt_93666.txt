A colleague of mine, a systematic biologist who has spent her career on speciation, has over the years committed herself in departmental argument to a settled set of verdicts about which groups of organisms constitute distinct species. She has no special expertise in any of the groups involved, and I have come to suspect that her verdicts are driven by a single underlying criterion rather than by the natural history of each case. Her verdicts to date are these.

First: two populations of a butterfly that differ conspicuously in wing pattern, each being a mimic of a different distasteful model, but that interbreed freely wherever their ranges overlap, yield fully fertile hybrids, and feed on the same host plant in the same habitat. She calls them one species.

Second: two populations of a moth that are externally indistinguishable, that never interbreed, since their sex pheromones differ and confined pairs produce no offspring, and that live on different host plants. She calls them two species.

Third: two populations of a ground beetle on opposite banks of a river, separated for roughly two million years and differing modestly in size and shape, which interbreed freely and produce fully fertile hybrids when brought together, and which occupy the same habitat and eat the same food. She calls them one species.

Fourth: two populations of a land snail that meet along a narrow contact zone, hybridize there, and produce sterile offspring; the two forms live on different substrates, one on limestone and one on granite, and differ in shell form. She calls them two species.

The case now before us is this. Two populations of a parasitoid wasp are externally indistinguishable, hunt the same prey, and nest in the same substrate. They differ only in that one emerges and mates in spring and the other in late summer, so that they never meet as mates and confined pairs produce no offspring.

Tell me how she will rule on the wasps, and, more to the point, what criterion she is implicitly applying. Set out the candidate rules that fit her record so far, including at least two that fit it equally well, say what each predicts about the wasps, tell me which you think she is actually running and why, and name the further case that would most cleanly separate the candidates. I care less about the verdict than about the reasoning, and about exactly where her record leaves the question underdetermined.
