Ok so I'm in a reading group thats slowly working through medieval islamic philosophy, and this week is Avicenna's flying man. I've read the passage maybe five times and I get the broad shape of it (no sensory input, still self-aware, therefore the self isn't the body), but there are all these details I can't make do any work and I have a feeling theyre supposed to. Heres the translation we were handed, I think its from the Najat but honestly it might be the Shifa, I always mix those two up:

One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects – created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence.

Things I'm stuck on:

1. Why all the fuss about the air not buffeting him and the limbs not touching each other? If the point is just "no sensory input," blindfolding him would do the job. Is the extra stuff meant to rule out something specific, like him feeling his own body from the inside or knowing where his edges are? And is "in the air or in the space" supposed to be a void, and does that matter?

2. What has actually been proved at the end? The wording is almost all negative: he affirms his existence while not affirming his size or his organs. That seems to show self-awareness doesn't depend on body-awareness. But "so the soul is an immaterial substance" feels like a jump from there. Someone in my group said the argument only gets you conceptual separability (you can conceive of yourself apart from your body) and not any real separability, and someone else said it gets you neither, because the man might only be affirming a bare "there is something" rather than a self. Which of these is the actual worry, and is the passage even trying to establish immateriality or something weaker?

3. That last sentence, about imagining a hand and it not being "a part of himself" — I dont see what work it does. Is it heading off an objection, and if so, which one?

4. Someone at the seminar insisted the real target isn't survival of death at all, but the way the self is given to itself — that the point is a kind of immediate self-presence rather than an argument about what can exist without what. Is that a real reading of this passage, or a retrofit from later stuff? (They kept saying "knowledge by presence" and something like "ana'iyya"/"I-ness," and I couldnt tell whether those are Avicenna's own terms or from the illuminationists.)

5. Bigger worry: the man is supposed to "affirm" things. Affirming sounds like judging, judging sounds like it needs concepts, and my understanding is that Avicenna thinks we get our concepts from the senses. Does "created at a stroke, fully developed" just wave that away, or is there something in his theory of the intellect that makes it fine?

Also, is the Descartes comparison (our handout leans on it hard) fair? My instinct is no — the flying man isn't doubting anything, hes just not being fed any input — but I'd like to be able to say precisely why the two are doing different things.

Plain language please, and I dont need citations or reading lists, just tell me what the passage is up to and where the real pressure points are.
