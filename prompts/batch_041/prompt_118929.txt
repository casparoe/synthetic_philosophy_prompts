Ok so bit of a weird one. I'm a first year PhD student — philosophy, but I sit in on the stats seminars — and my first real chapter is on the old evidence problem. You know the one: Bayesian confirmation theory apparently can't say that a piece of evidence confirms a hypothesis if the agent already knew the evidence, because if you already know E then P(E) = 1, conditioning on E does nothing, and the posterior doesn't budge.

I got into it for a dumb personal reason. I finally decided to leave a long relationship last year, and afterwards I realised that every single thing I'd used to justify the decision I'd known for years. Nothing new came in. The evidence was sitting there the whole time; what changed was something else. That's the old evidence problem with my life in it, and now I can't stop seeing it everywhere.

Anyway, I have a view, and in three weeks I have to defend it at our joint workshop — the stats department's Bayesian reading group plus the history and philosophy of science crowd, one room, about 25 people, 20 minute talk then Q&A. Who's in the room: six or seven people who do Bayesian stats for a living and are genuinely baffled that anyone thinks there's a problem here; three or four HPS people who think Bayesian confirmation theory is a toy that never touches a real historical episode; one formal epistemologist who works on measures of confirmation and will definately ask whether I mean the difference measure or the ratio measure; and my supervisor, who likes the chapter but keeps telling me I'm reinventing something that already has a name.

My line, roughly: the old evidence problem isn't a bug in conditionalization, it's a bug in how we specify the evidence. Confirmation should be relative to a partition — the set of distinctions the agent's evidence lets her draw — not relative to a time and not to the set of propositions she already believes. On that view, old evidence confirming a newly formulated hypothesis is unremarkable: the evidence confirms H relative to the partition that H itself makes salient. Everyone has been trying to fix this with probabilities (the counterfactual ur-prior move, or the trick of treating the discovery that H entails E as itself a fresh piece of evidence) when the thing to fix is the evidence relation. I also think the counterfactual move is circular in a specific way — to compute what my probability in H would have been had I not known E, I already have to know what counts as my evidence, which is the disputed thing. And I want to hold that there's a real difference between E being merely already known and E being entailed by H; the ur-prior fix runs them together and I don't think they're the same.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet. The objections I'll actually get from this room, not the generic ones from the literature. For each one: how that person would actually phrase it (the Bayesian stats people will put it very differently than the HPS people), how much damage it does, what I should just concede up front, and a reply I can deliver in about two minutes. Flag which replies are soft — I'd much rather know where I'm exposed now than find out in the room. Order them by how likely times how bad. And include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, plus a sentence on what I should say if it comes anyway. No reading lists or citations, please — I can't check them and neither can you, and what I need is the argument, not the literature.
