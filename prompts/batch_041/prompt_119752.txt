You are a philosopher of science who works on economic modeling, idealization, and the realism-of-assumptions debate. Think carefully before answering: first check whether the argument below is even valid, and only then decide which step to reject.

A colleague in my methods seminar gave me this and I can't tell whether it refutes Friedman's F-twist or cheats. The F-twist, as he states it, is that a theory is an instrument for prediction and the realism of its assumptions is irrelevant to its merit.

(1) If the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to its merit, then the truth of those assumptions cannot contribute to that merit.
(2) If truth cannot contribute, then merit is fixed by predictive performance alone.
(3) So any two observationally equivalent models -- models making the same predictions in every situation we can test -- have equal merit, however different their assumptions. (Picture a representative-agent macro model that fits the aggregates though no individual resembles the agent, and a heterogeneous-agent model that fits them equally well.)
(4) If their merit is equal, we may keep whichever assumptions are computationally convenient, so long as the outputs match.
(5) If we may do that, then no scientific reason can prefer a model that captures the mechanism to one that merely fits the data.
(6) Then the distinction between explanation and curve-fitting collapses.
(7) That distinction does not collapse.
(8) So the F-twist is false: the realism of assumptions matters after all.

Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine, and what giving it up costs -- in particular, whether an instrumentalist can resist this argument without quietly reinstating truth as a criterion of merit. I would most like to keep (7).
