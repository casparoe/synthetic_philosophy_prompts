Hey, so I'm on my school's math team and one of the seniors showed me this proof last week and it's been bugging me ever since. It seems to prove way to much and I can't find the hole in it, so I figured I'd ask someone who actually knows this stuff. Here's how he wrote it out: (1) Take any two player game where both players have exactly the same moves available and start off in the same situation, where they take turns, where a draw is impossible, and where the game has to end eventually. (2) Suppose, just for the argument, that the second player has a winning strategy -- call it S. (3) The first player now makes any legal move at all, it doesn't matter which. (4) From that point on the first player just pretends to be the second player: whatever the real second player does, the first player looks up what S says to do in response and does that, and if S ever tells them to make a move that's already been made, they just do some other legal move instead, since being one move ahead of the other player isn't a disadvantage. (5) So no matter what the second player tries, the first player has a reply that keeps them on track to win, which means the first player has a winning strategy. (6) That contradicts (2), so the second player can't have had a winning strategy after all. (7) And since there are no draws and somebody has to win, the first player is the one who wins. So the upshot is supposed to be that in any symmetric no draw game, the first player always wins.

The thing is that can't be right. If we sit down with a pile of 3 counters and take turns taking 1 or 2 and whoever takes the last one wins, the first player definately loses -- I've played it with my little brother like ten times and the second player just cleans up. And I looked it up and in the game Nim, if you start with two equal piles, the second player wins by copying whatever you do. Both of those seem to fit every condition in step 1. So one of the steps has to be cheating, but honestly every single line reads fine to me, which is the annoying part.

So what I want to know is: which step is the one that's sneaking in something it isn't allowed to assume, and why does that step look so innocent when it isn't? And once you fix it, what do I actually lose -- does the argument still work for the games it was first invented for (I think it started with a game played on a board of hexagons where you try to build a path connecting your two opposite sides), or does the whole thing collapse and it was never really a proof of anything? If I have to give something up I'd rather keep the part about the hexagon board than the super general claim, but I want to know what that costs me.
