I am trying to get clearer on a difficult passage from Thucydides' account of the Mytilene debate, and I would like your help reading it closely. The passage is Diodotus' argument against the death penalty for the Mytilenians, in Book 3 of the History of the Peloponnesian War. Here is the relevant stretch:

"All, states and individuals, are alike prone to err, and there is no law that will prevent them; or why should men have exhausted the list of punishments in search of enactments to protect them from evil-doers? It is probable that in early times the penalties for the greatest offences were less severe, and that, as these were disregarded, the penalty of death has been by degrees in most cases arrived at, which is itself disregarded in like manner. Either then some means of terror more terrible than this must be discovered, or it must be owned that this restraint is useless; and that as long as poverty gives men the courage of necessity, or plenty fills them with the ambition which belongs to insolence and pride, and the other conditions of life remain each under the thraldom of some fatal and master passion, so long will the impulse never be wanting to drive men into danger."

I am interested in a distinction I have been calling the difference between first-order and second-order deterrence. First-order deterrence is the effect of a threatened punishment on the initial decision to rebel or offend; second-order deterrence is the effect of that punishment on the decision to persist, surrender, or escalate once the initial decision has already been made. My rough thought is that Diodotus is making a point about the second-order failure of capital punishment: if the maximum penalty is already attached to the offense, then once someone has committed it, the penalty provides no further marginal reason to stop, because there is nothing worse to threaten. But I am not sure whether that is what the passage actually says, or whether I am importing a modern game-theoretic distinction into an ancient text. I am also unsure about the logic of the sentence that begins "Either then some means of terror more terrible than this must be discovered..." Does Diodotus mean that the death penalty is literally useless as a deterrent, or only that it is insufficient by itself, and that something else—certainty, civic education, or the structure of incentives—must do the real work? And is the argument vulnerable to the objection that a penalty can deter even if it is sometimes disregarded, so that the fact that people still rebel under threat of death does not show that the threat has no effect at the margin?
