Write a poem of twenty lines in two equal halves, with a single hinge line between them that is at once the conclusion of the first half and a premise of the second. The first half should reason its way to a genuine contradiction — one thing that is both so and not so, not a pun, not an equivocation, not two parties disagreeing — and the second half must carry on from there. That carrying-on is the whole point. On the classical assumption that anything follows from a contradiction, the poem would be finished at the hinge, since nothing further could be said that the contradiction had not already licensed; this poem has to keep going with material still answerable to what came before, so that a reader can feel the difference between a logic that detonates and one that holds. Let the contradiction stand at the end. If the closing lines dissolve it — by distinguishing senses, by conceding that each of us has her own truth, by a wise resignation — the poem has failed. I want this for the last meeting of a seminar on dialetheism and paraconsistent logic, but it should stand on its own: a logician should be able to name the rule the poem refuses to apply, and a reader who isn't one should feel why the refusal matters.
