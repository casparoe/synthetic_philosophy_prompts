I teach a small seminar in formal epistemology, 14 students, one 90-minute session, and I want to run an in-class game on Dutch books rather than lecture the standard version. The standard version is the problem: everyone's seen the "incoherent credences, here's a set of bets that guarantees you a loss" diagram, and they can nod along without it landing. So I want them to actually get pumped, with small real stakes (I'll use poker chips or candy, no money), so the sting is felt before anyone says "Dutch book."

Here's the shape I want, and where I want you to get weedy. Round one: they work in pairs, one person is the agent and posts buy and sell prices in chips for a small slate of logically related claims about a single random draw I make at the end (claims like the ball is red, the ball is blue, the ball is red or blue, the ball is red and today is Tuesday, and so on). The agent has to honor any bet the other person wants at the posted prices, on either side, up to a cap. The bookie's whole job is to pick bets that combine across the related claims. If the agent's prices are incoherent the bookie can lock in a profit no matter how the draw comes out, and I want them to find that themselves. Round two is the part I actually care about and I don't want it to be the textbook coda. Now the agent's payoff is non-linear in chips (I'll tell them their chips convert to a prize or a curve with diminishing returns, or just instruct them to be averse to variance), and their credences are coherent but their quoted prices come out of that non-linear valuation rather than straight out of their degrees of belief. The bookie tries the same extraction. What I want the room to discover is that the pump still runs but it no longer isolates credence-incoherence -- it might just be exposing risk aversion or a gap between what you believe and what odds you'd trade at. That's the weedy turn: the argument needs a bridge from credence to betting behavior, and round two is where the bridge visibly wobbles.

So design me the full runnable thing: setup and materials, the exact slate of claims and how the draw works, the betting and payoff rules for both rounds, a rough minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, and a debrief that walks back through what just happened and pins each beat to the corresponding bit of the argument (what a Dutch book is, why the incoherence in round one does the work, and why round two muddies it). The debrief should map the moments in the game onto the parts of the idea, and it should be honest about the one thing the activity can't reproduce -- I think it's the jump from "you suffered a sure loss" to "you were irrational," and I'd like you to say plainly that the game gives them the sure loss and not the irrationality, and what would have to be added.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- where students will game the round-one setup, where the bookie will fail to find the combination and just sit there, where the non-linear twist will confuse everyone including me -- and how to handle each. And be frank about the risk that round two reads as "so the Dutch book argument is just wrong," which I don't want; I want them to come away seeing that it's a conditional argument and what its antecedent costs.
