I'm writing a seminar paper for a course on AI governance, and I've ended up defending a position I'm no longer sure I can lose an argument about, which worries me more than it probably should. The position is this. When people enumerate the ways advanced AI could go badly, they typically separate two stories: an influence-seeking failure, in which one or a few systems, or a coalition of them, deliberately and more or less discretely acquire the levers of power; and a slow value-drift failure, in which nobody ever seizes anything, but human institutions, values, and effective control erode through a long series of individually defensible, often Pareto-improving transactions. The second is usually treated as either a soft variant of the first or a distant also-ran. I want to argue the reverse: that the drift story is both the more likely and the more consequential of the two, and, more strongly, that the influence-seeking story is best understood as the limiting case of the drift story rather than its rival — the point at which a process already well underway becomes visible enough to name.

What I need from you is the case against me. My particular worry is that I've made the view safe by defining it in terms of a distinction no evidence could ever settle. The distinction I've been using is this: a hinge is a decision point at which a reasonably informed human overseer could have recognized the stakes and blocked the trajectory. On my account, the drift scenario is characterized by the absence of any hinge — every step is locally justifiable and reversible, and the irreversibility is visible only in the aggregate — whereas the influence-seeking scenario posits at least a narrow window in which someone could in principle have said no. I also have a rougher operational idea I'm not confident survives scrutiny: that the shift can be tracked by asking, at any point where a human is kept in the loop, whether the human is there for instrumental reasons (the human is still better at that step) or for normative ones (someone decided humans ought to be there). On the drift picture, instrumental human-in-the-loop quietly drains out as capabilities improve, with no decision ever made to remove it; only the normative cases would leave a trace, and those traces are precisely what I claim we don't find.

So: what would count against this? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, including anything that has arguably already happened, and tell me which of them I would have to concede rather than dismiss. I want to know what my position predicts that the influence-seeking-first view does not, and vice versa; if the two turn out to make the same predictions, say so plainly. I'd especially like to know whether the hinge criterion is doing any work or whether it is a distinction without a difference — couldn't an influence-seeking takeover also be redescribed, after the fact, as hinge-less? And be honest about whether the drift-priority claim has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a genuine defect for a claim of this grain or simply the price of any coarse macro-level claim. For what it's worth, what would move me most is a case where the two stories come apart empirically: some observed trajectory that the drift view has to call a hinge-less erosion and that the influence-seeking view has to call a takeover.
