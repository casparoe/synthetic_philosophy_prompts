Next month I'm presenting a paper at the internal workshop of an AI lab I consult for, on how we should talk about model belief and model deception, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the audience I will actually face.

My thesis has three parts. First, the intentional stance is the right predictive tool for large language models and nothing is lost by using it, provided we distinguish three registers in which a belief attribution can function: a predictive register, where the attribution earns its keep by forecasting outputs; a diagnostic register, where it is a claim about internal structure; and a responsibility register, where it is what licenses praise, blame, and correction. Second, most of the current dispute is people in different registers talking past one another — the mechanists and the representationalists are arguing about the diagnostic register while the safety people quietly rely on the responsibility register and never say so. Third, and the part I expect to get hit hardest: the notion of deception we need for evaluations is not a belief attribution at all. I want to propose that we call an output deceptive when it presents a claim as supported while the system's own uncertainty representation would not support it — unendorsed assertion — and to argue that this notion is available whether or not the model has beliefs, which means the intentional stance is neither necessary nor sufficient for calling a system deceptive.

The audience is specific. There are two mechanistic interpretability researchers who want every mental term cashed out as a feature or a circuit and regard stance-talk as a licence to stop looking. There is one philosopher of mind who is a committed representationalist and will say my predictive register is instrumentalism with the hard question deferred. There is an alignment-and-policy person who needs a definition that assigns responsibility to someone identifiable and will not accept one that ends up blaming only the developers. There is a model-welfare researcher who will worry that my criterion pathologises ordinary miscalibration and anthropomorphises in the opposite direction. And there are five or six ML engineers who will keep asking what any of this changes about how they build evaluations.

Give me the objections I will actually get, in their vocabularies and roughly in the order they will arrive, ordered by how much damage they do — not the objections I would get at a philosophy conference. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first three minutes to buy room for the rest, and which single objection I should be willing to spend my credibility on rather than dodge. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. No citations, no reading list, no references to literature I would have to look up — argue from the positions themselves.

Open with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the talk survives, and which objection decides it — then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate what I have asked for.
