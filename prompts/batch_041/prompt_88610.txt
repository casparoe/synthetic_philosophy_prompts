I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on rules that govern their own amendment — the structure the parlor game Nomic makes vivid, where players change the rules of the game by moves made under those same rules, and where some rules are marked unchangeable. I've come to think the usual talk of paradox is misplaced. My working view is that a rule-set which appears to entrench a rule always turns out to have a seam: some piece of its own procedural machinery — a silent step, a nondebatable motion, a gap between the text and who is authorized to read it — through which a determined majority can reach the entrenched rule without going through the amendment procedure the rule-set prescribes. On this view entrenchment is never more than a claim about which seams happen to be unavailable at the moment. I'd like this tested against a real case rather than against toy examples, and I'd rather you tell me where it fails than where it works.

The case is the United States Senate and the maneuver its members call the nuclear option. In November 2013, after a nomination failed to clear the sixty-vote cloture threshold, the majority leader raised a point of order that under the cloture rule (Rule XXII) a simple majority sufficed for nominations other than Supreme Court nominations; the presiding officer ruled against him; he appealed; and a bare majority voted to overturn the chair, leaving the chair bound by the new precedent. In April 2017 the same maneuver was run again, by the other party, to extend the precedent to Supreme Court nominations. What interests me is that the standing rule was never formally amended: its text still says what it always said, and changing it still requires two-thirds of the senators present and voting, while the rule as the Senate actually operates changed twice, by fifty-one votes, through a step — an appeal on a nondebatable question — that requires no cloture at all. I know the politics, and I know the standard complaint that this was a norm-breaking power grab; what I want is the conceptual anatomy.

So: locate the seam precisely, and say why it is where it is rather than somewhere else. Then tell me whether what happened in 2013 is better described as amending the rule-set or as reinterpreting it, and whether that distinction survives contact with the case — the text of the rule, its application, and the practice that makes either authoritative may not be the same object, and I want to know which one you think changed. Then consider whether the seam could be closed — a rule making such appeals debatable, or requiring a supermajority to overturn the chair — and whether closing it would restore genuine entrenchment or merely push the seam elsewhere, so that a rule-set can never in principle entrench anything. Then test a second hypothesis of mine, that seam amendments ratchet: each one makes the next cheaper by converting a norm into a precedent. The 2013 to 2017 sequence looks like evidence for that, but the fact that the maneuver has never been extended to legislation, despite repeated threats, cuts the other way, and I want your verdict on which way the case runs. Finally, say what the episode shows about my seam view: does it confirm it, or does the Senate case display something the view cannot capture — for instance that the new precedent is only as firm as the willingness of future presiding officers and majorities to honor it, so that the rule is doing less work than either the formalists or my account supposes?

Please answer in continuous prose of four to six paragraphs, then append a single JSON object with the following fields, each a string of no more than about ninety words: seam_location (where the seam is and why it sits there); rule_or_reinterpretation (your verdict on what changed in 2013, and your reasoning); closing_the_seam (whether it can be closed, and what closing it would reveal about entrenchment generally); ratchet_verdict (your verdict on the ratchet hypothesis, plus what evidence would falsify it); theory_verdict (whether the episode confirms my seam view, or exposes what it misses); and residual_question (the question the case leaves most unresolved). I don't need citations, a reading list, or anyone's name attached to the positions you take.
