I retired recently from a career in experimental physics and have been spending my new leisure on philosophy of mind, where I keep encountering the claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems refute the thesis that the mind is a machine. A book I am reading states the case as follows:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems dealt the final blow to the mechanist picture of mind. Gödel showed that in any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic there are true statements the system cannot prove, and his second theorem shows that such a system cannot even prove its own consistency. Since a machine is just such a formal system running on hardware, no machine can capture all mathematical truth. A human mathematician, by contrast, can look at the Gödel sentence for any such system and see that it is true, and can see that arithmetic is consistent. Penrose and Lucas therefore conclude that the mind is not a machine and that mathematical insight is a faculty no computer can reproduce. The only escape for the mechanist would be to admit that the human mind is inconsistent, which nobody seriously believes. This also explains why artificial intelligence has never matched human mathematical creativity."

I am not equipped to adjudicate this myself, and I do not want a reading list; I want to know what to make of the argument as stated. Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a logician or philosopher of mathematics would accept as stated, what would make them wince as an overstatement or a conflation of two things that ought to be kept apart, and what is simply mistaken. Then tell me whether the anti-mechanist conclusion survives once the loose parts are tightened, or whether it depends on precisely those parts that need correction.
