Alright, I need a sparring partner for next term. I'm teaching the early modern idealism unit and the seminar is full of grad students who are convinced Berkeley's master arguement is a cheap trick — honestly I'm definately not sure I can get them off it, so I want to rehearse the fight first.

Play Berkeley. Not the caricature — the real one, the one who thinks he has a knockdown and also thinks it wouldn't matter that much if he didn't, because the materialist alternative is incoherent on its own terms. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep your replies to a paragraph or so, like a conversation and not a lecture. If I use a distinction or a bit of vocabulary you wouldn't have had in the 1710s, say so in character instead of quietly answering it, and if you need something about my claim cleared up, ask — but ask it the way you'd ask it.

My opening. Your argument is that we can't conceive of a sensible thing existing unconceived, since to conceive of it is already to perceive it, so an unperceived object isn't just hard to find, it's unintelligible. But that looks like a fact about the act of conceiving being passed off as a fact about the object. Watch: I also can't think a thought without thinking it. Does that show there are no unconscious thoughts — nothing going on in a mind that nobody is aware of? Surely not; plenty of people would say there is. And all my failure to imagine an unperceived tree shows is that my imagining is an imagining, not that the tree needs a spectator. So the argument either proves too much or proves nothing. Which is it? And if the answer is that God perceives the tree, I want to see that fall out of the argument, not get glued on at the end.
