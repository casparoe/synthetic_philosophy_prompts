I'm presenting at our departmental colloquium next month and, rather than a summary of my own view, I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis is that Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance should be taken not as a rueful concession that ordinary concepts are vague, but as a positive account resting on three load-bearing commitments: concept membership is graded rather than binary; the similarity relation that underwrites it is intransitive, so A can resemble B and B resemble C without A resembling C; and the centre of gravity of a concept shifts with the conversational purpose at hand, so there is no context-independent fact about which features are essential. I then argue that this package, once made reasonably precise, absorbs the strongest parts of both the classical necessary-and-sufficient-conditions picture and the prototype/exemplar pictures while showing why each fails alone, and I push the further, more contestable claim that a concept with a stable core is often worse at doing its job than one without. A colleague who heard the dry run said the argument is either obviously right or not an argument at all, which is not reassuring.

I need the objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, each phrased the way the objector would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where my reply is soft. The audience is mixed, so please keep the objectors distinct. Two experimental psychologists work on categorisation, treat family resemblance as an informal forerunner of prototype theory, and will want to know what my account predicts that theirs does not; they will also press me on how a graded, intransitive similarity relation could ever be operationalised, or distinguished in principle from an exemplar model. Two analytic philosophers accept that most concepts lack necessary and sufficient conditions but think family resemblance so understood is either trivially true or outright incoherent, since any resemblance must hold in some respect, and those respects threaten to smuggle back the very necessary conditions I claim to dispense with. A historian of the later Wittgenstein knows the text closely and will object wherever my reconstruction outruns it. A handful of graduate students will ask the meta-question of whether any of this changes what we should say about borderline cases in law or medicine. Include, at the end, the one question I am hoping nobody in the room asks.

Keep the whole thing under 1200 words. Open with the single most dangerous objection stated in one sentence, then give the ordered list, and close with the question I dread.
