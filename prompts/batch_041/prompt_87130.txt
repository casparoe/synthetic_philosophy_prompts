For my seminar paper in a cross-listed ancient psych / neuroethics course: if Aristotle got dropped into the current fight over whether brain size is morally relevant — the encephalization quotient stuff, the neuron-count work (birds pack way more neurons per gram than mammals), the old craniometric claims about race and sex, and the argument that tiny-brained insects might still be conscious — what would his position be? I dont want a summary of his psychology, I've had that all term; I want the prediction, argued from what he's actually committed to. Does he even accept the brain as the relevant organ, given that for him the heart is where perception happens and the brain is basically a radiator that cools the blood? Is there anything in him that plays the role of "moral status," or is the whole question malformed by his lights? I think he says somewhere that our brain is the biggest relative to body size — is that even right, and if it is, does that make the big human brain a material precondition for the rational soul, or does his hylomorphism make that a category mistake? And be honest about where his scala naturae and the natural-slave bits push him: is he committed to graded moral worth by species, and what would he have to give up to get out of it? Tell me which single commitment is doing the most work in his answer, and whether dropping it is cheap or expensive for the rest of his system. American English, and skip citations, I can't use them anyway. End with one last line in the form \answer{...} giving his verdict on the question in ten words or fewer.
