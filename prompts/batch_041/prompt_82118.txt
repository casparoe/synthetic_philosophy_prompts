For the past two terms I have been keeping a notebook of my supervisor's verdicts in our philosophy-of-science reading group, because I am trying to work out what she actually believes rather than what she says she believes. Whenever a posit from the history of science comes up, I put the same question to her — was it reasonable, on the evidence available at the time, to believe that the thing existed? — and she always answers with a bare yes or no. Her record so far: phlogiston, no; the caloric fluid, no; the luminiferous ether as of 1900, no; Newtonian absolute space and time, no; the intra-Mercurial planet Vulcan, no; Blondlot's N-rays, no; polywater, no; the neutrino as of the mid-1930s, no; the atom by around 1910, yes; the electron, yes; the Mendelian gene by the early 1920s, yes; the neutrino once it had been detected, yes; the Higgs boson once it had been detected, yes. When I pressed her about the pattern she became impatient and said it was one test applied consistently, that it was not a list of lucky guesses, and that I would see it if I looked properly. I have not managed to see it, and I suspect that more than one test would fit her list equally well.

The three cases we are about to argue over are live ones, so the record simply runs out. First, the dark-matter particle — I mean the particle species, not the missing-mass phenomenon, which nobody disputes: the cosmological fits lean on it, and half a century of direct-detection experiments has turned up nothing. Second, the quark: nobody has ever isolated one, confinement says nobody ever will, yet the predictions that come from taking quarks seriously have been confirmed repeatedly. Third, the quantum state, taken as a real physical entity rather than a computational device. For each of the three, tell me what verdict she is most likely to give.

I want more than three verdicts, though. Set out the two or three tests that fit her existing record equally well, show what each of them predicts for the three new cases, and say which of the tests the record actually supports and on what evidence — I want to know which of her past verdicts is doing the discriminating work and which are idle. Then name the further case, historical or contemporary, that would best force a choice between the surviving tests, and tell me honestly where her record is silent, so that no amount of consistency-checking will yield an answer. You may assume I already know the textbook version of the pessimistic meta-induction and the usual no-miracles reply; what I cannot do is turn her list into a rule.
