I help run a small decision-theory reading group, and we close each session with a joke that only lands for people who were following the argument — last time it was a bit about Newcomb's problem, which I won't try to reproduce here. This week the topic is superrationality, and I'd like you to write the closing joke. The idea, roughly, is that in a symmetric one-shot game such as the prisoner's dilemma, a player who takes its opponent to reason exactly as it does should treat its own choice as evidence about the opponent's choice, and so should pick the option that is best in the case where the two of them do the same thing — cooperating, in the dilemma, with no repetition, contract, or enforcement mechanism to lean on. The joke has to turn on that structure rather than merely being about decision theorists: someone who knows the idea should laugh because the punchline exploits its actual content — the correlation between the two decisions rather than any causal influence of one on the other, or the fact that the reasoning yields cooperation for free in a game where the standard arguments insist you must defect.

To keep it from being the vanilla version everyone has already heard, please build the setup around two players who are almost but not exactly alike: copies of one another differing in a single trivial respect, whether that is one extra bit of information, a different random seed, or a one-line change to the program each is running. One member of our group never stops objecting that the agents in these puzzles are never really identical, so I'd like the joke to face that squarely: what a small difference does to the correlation, what a player who knows about the difference should expect of its twin, and whether the case for cooperating is a knife-edge that snaps as soon as the two diverge at all. Keep it under 250 words, end on a line of dialogue or a short exchange rather than a narrator's explanation, and then add two or three sentences telling me where the bit takes liberties with the idea or leans on a reading that is contested rather than settled — flag any step that works only under one of the live interpretations of superrationality and not the others, and say plainly which parts of the idea are common ground among people who work on it and which are genuinely up for grabs.
