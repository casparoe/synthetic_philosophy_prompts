Assume the role of a philosopher working on the epistemology of markets and on the theory of rational belief. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the candidate readings of the practice described below before committing to a considered view.

I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I keep bumping into a practice I can't square with what I know about it. The practice is treating market prices as probabilities: news desks report the "market-implied odds" of a rate cut or an election result, colleagues quote option prices and prediction-market contracts the way they'd quote a weather forecast, and some teams now wire those numbers straight into decisions. What puzzles me is that the same people who trust these numbers will also accept, when pressed, two arguments that seem to gut them. First, that informational trading shouldn't happen at all if traders share the same prior and it's common knowledge that they're all rational — differences in what people privately know wouldn't by themselves give anyone a reason to trade, so the price should just sit at the common prior and carry no news. Second, that if prices already reflected everything knowable, nobody would be paid for finding out more, yet the effort of finding out more is supposed to be exactly what gets baked into prices.

I'd like you to reconstruct what this practice presupposes rather than survey the literature. What view of belief and knowledge does it commit us to — that prices literally are credences, gradable and scoreable like a forecaster's probabilities, or only that they behave well enough to serve as summaries? Which rival views does it quietly rule out: that persistent disagreement is evidence about priors rather than about facts, or the reverse; that a price aggregates opinions rather than information; that a market can be informative even when no one can profit from the information in it? Are its parts consistent with one another, or is the everyday use of prices as probabilities in tension with the theoretical results I half-remember? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, tell me where it's philosophically underdetermined so that several incompatible theories would license the very same practice, and say what would have to change — in how forecasts are reported, scored, or trusted — if we replaced the presupposed picture with one of its rivals.
