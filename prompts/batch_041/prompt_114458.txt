For the past several months I have been attending a reading group on literary interpretation, despite having no formal background in philosophy — I write backend services for a living, and I joined mostly because a colleague insisted that the questions in this area were harder than they look. The argument that has stuck with me concerns whether an author's intention has any bearing on what a text means. One position, which I find intuitive, is that the author's intention settles the matter: where a sentence is ambiguous, or where it is unclear whether a passage is meant seriously or ironically, we consult what the author was trying to do, and the author's own account of it is the best evidence we have. A friend in the group takes the opposite line: the text is a public object made of shared linguistic conventions, and whatever private episode occurred in the author's head is not part of the work at all, so the author's later testimony about what they meant is merely one more piece of evidence about the text, and not a privileged one. A third member, who has read more widely than the rest of us, keeps proposing what she calls the reasonable compromise: the question is not what the author actually had in mind, but what a reader who knew the relevant conventions, genre, and context would be justified in taking the author to have meant — so intention still matters, but only in this reconstructed, hypothetical form. We spent an hour on it and got nowhere, and I have been asked to close the final session with something other than another argument.

What I would like from you is a poem, specifically a pantoum of about twenty lines: quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, with the closing quatrain reusing the first and third lines of the opening stanza so that the poem folds back into where it began. Let the repeated line be the author's own flat statement of what the work means, phrased as a writer might phrase it in a preface — plain, declarative, unpoetic, a sentence that sounds like a fact about the work rather than a line of verse. Its recurrences should not be mere echoes. Each time it returns, the surrounding lines should have shifted enough that it says something different from what it said before, and by the middle of the poem the reader should be able to hear both the sense the author gave the line and the sense the words carry on their own, with no way to decide which one is authoritative. Whether the poem speaks in one voice or in two is up to you, but the repeated line must at some point be available to both the writer and the reader, and when the loop closes in the final quatrain it should arrive in the voice of someone who has read the work rather than someone who wrote it — a reader's gloss rather than an author's claim. The poem should end there, without resolving whose reading has won.

Please render all three positions recognizably enough that a specialist would nod — the view that intention settles meaning, the view that the text is public and the author's private intention irrelevant, and the reconstructed-intention compromise — but do not name any of them, do not name any philosopher, and do not state the compromise as a conclusion. The pantoum's repetitions should be doing that work, not a summarizing final line. It should be good enough to read aloud to a group of non-specialists with no gloss, and precise enough that no one in the room who knows the debate would wince at it.
