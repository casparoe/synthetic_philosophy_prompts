Over the weekend a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether an ant colony can have a mind, and we ended up further apart than when we started. The occasion was Hofstadter's imagined colony — the one the Anteater, in the dialogues of Gödel, Escher, Bach, calls Aunt Hillary — an aggregate of ants whose comings and goings, taken together, seem to track food, threats, and the condition of the nest, and whose behavior my friend and I agreed is at least usefully described as if the colony believed and wanted things. What divided us was an argument he built out of that shared agreement, which I set out below in the numbered form we finally managed to agree on. He insists it is valid and that my discomfort with where it lands is mere sentiment; I suspect there is a step that looks innocent and is not, but I cannot find it, and I am reluctant to reject a premise for which I have no better replacement. Here is the argument.

(1) To have a mental state is to be a system whose internal states occupy the right causal and inferential roles, whatever the system happens to be made of; mentality is a matter of organization, not of tissue. (2) Aunt Hillary has just such an organization: the ants and their interactions carry signals, store and retrieve information about the nest, and settle into states that track features of the world and steer the colony's behavior. (3) So Aunt Hillary has beliefs, desires, and moods — the colony is, in a plain sense, a minded creature. (4) But the criterion in (1) is pitched at a level coarse enough that the same organization is realized by many other aggregates: the citizens of a nation, the traders of a market, and the machines of a network each interact so as to carry and transform information about their environment. (5) So the United States has beliefs and desires, the market forms expectations, the network has moods. (6) If you accept (3) but balk at (5), you owe me a principled difference between the colony and the country — and I can find none that does not simply privilege the biology. (7) Therefore either we accept that the United States is a minded agent, which I find I cannot, or we give up (1), and organization is not sufficient for a mind. He then pressed a variant on me to make it worse: bisect a colony with a stream, leaving the two halves joined only by a slow trail of pheromone that neither half can use to coordinate its foraging in real time. By (1) and (2) each half still realizes the relevant organization, so each is a mind; a single colony, split by a landslide, has quietly become two minds, and we can make and unmake minds by rerouting traffic. He takes this as further proof that (1) must go; I take it as a sign that something has gone wrong earlier and that the variant is merely amplifying it.

So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (3) — I have read the Anteater's dialogue a dozen times and I do think Aunt Hillary believes things — and I would most like to be rid of (5). Tell me whether I can have both, and if not, what I am paying in each direction. If the argument is valid as it stands, say so and identify the cheapest premise to surrender; if it slides somewhere, name the slide and explain why it looked fine. I am as interested in the price of the alternatives as in the verdict.
