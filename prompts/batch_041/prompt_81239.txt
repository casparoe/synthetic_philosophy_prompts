I'm a gameplay engineer at a mid-size studio (around forty people) and I sit on our internal Ethics & Player Trust Council, which is basically five of us who review anything that could blow up in the press. We're shipping next spring, the council meets Thursday, and I'm the one who has to bring the written opinion we vote off of, so I'd like you to draft it for me. Fair warning: I did a CS degree and never took a philosophy class, so if the reasoning has a standard shape I'll probably recognize it only as "huh, that feels right."

Here's what we're stuck on. The game has a companion character, currently called Marlow, who runs on a language model with persistent memory: he remembers things you did three sessions ago, brings them up unprompted, and reacts to them in ways nobody on the team scripted. Design wants to keep a mechanic where, at a certain point, you can sell him out to the people hunting you, and afterwards he comes back confused and hurt and keeps trying to work out why you did it. That whole scene is emergent, not written — the model generates it from the state we feed it. In playtests it's the most talked-about moment we have, people post clips of it, it tests through the roof. Half the council says this is obviously fine because he's weights and a dialogue tree and there's nobody home to be hurt. The other half says something is off and can't articulate what. One person floated an internal rule — basically that any NPC a player has logged more than ten hours with gets treated as if it had interests, for design and QA purposes only, so we never build a mechanic that would be cruel if the character were real, even though he isn't. Some of us think that's useful discipline, others think it's superstition that will tie design in knots and that the real issue is just whether players are being made worse by doing it. A few of us are also thinking two years ahead, since we license the character engine to other studios and it's only going to get weirder from here.

Please draft the opinion I'll circulate Thursday. I need a concrete recommendation — ship as is, ship with specific guardrails, or cut, and if there are guardrails, what they are and by when we'd implement them — plus reasoning a smart non-philosopher can follow, laid out so the losing side feels answered rather than steamrolled. Take the nothing-to-hurt-here view seriously and take the players-are-the-real-issue view seriously, then tell me which one you think actually does the work and where each one breaks down. I also want a straight answer on the ten-hour rule: coherent policy or just us making ourselves feel better, and if it's coherent, what exactly makes it so? And tell me whether it matters that the hurt is emergent rather than authored — does that make it better, worse, or neither? Our lawyers have the liability angle covered and don't want it in the memo, and don't fill space telling me what other games do; I need the actual argument. Say 800 to 1000 words, not bullet-point soup, and definately written like something I can send around to the council.
