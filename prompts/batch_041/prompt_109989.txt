My friend's in law school and he's decided consent -- medical, sexual, whatever -- is basically just a contract, so everything contract law knows carries straight over: you need a meeting of the minds, capacity, no duress, and if you were deceived about anything material the whole thing's void. He uses this to argue that a patient never told about a rare side effect never really consented, and that being lied to about something you'd obviously care about turns sex into assault. I think he's onto something but overreaching. Audit the analogy for me: give me the correspondence first (what plays the role of offer, acceptance, consideration, breach, remedy), then where the mapping breaks -- consent's revocable, it's a permission rather than a promise, there's no damages remedy, nothing's negotiated -- and then whether his two conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying them the whole time. One thing I want you to press hard on: contract law only treats silence as deception when there's a duty to disclose, and consent theory looks like it has the same hole (how material does the undisclosed thing have to be, and material to whom -- the reasonable patient or this particular one?), so does the analogy just relocate the problem instead of solving it? And if the contract picture is the wrong one, what should replace it?
