My friend is a second-year psych resident and we ended up yelling at each other for like two hours last night about something dumb she said at dinner. Her attending had the whole team re-code a patient from one diagnosis to another and it flipped whether the insurance would pay for the therapy, and she said, oh well, the manual just tracks what the science says is a real disorder. I said no it doesn't, and we went round and round and now I want you to settle it, because I think the whole apparatus is doing two jobs at once and quietly pretending they're one job.

Job one is the value job. If you actually read the criteria they keep saying the symptoms have to cause clinically significant distress or impairment in functioning. So a difference in your body by itself isnt a disorder, you also have to be suffering or failing at something. That sure looks like the line is a judgement about what a life should be able to do, not a fact about the body.

Job two is the discovery job. Everything else it says sounds like it thinks it's finding facts. They deleted the old rule that stopped you diagnosing depression during the first couple months after a death, and then the newest edition went and added a whole disorder for grief that drags on too long, so now mourning your spouse past some cutoff is a condition you can be diagnosed with and billed for. But the distress there is exactly what you'd expect and arguably the right thing to feel, so the distress clause can't be doing the normative work after all. Same pattern with blood pressure: the US guidelines dropped the cutoff to 130 over 80 a few years back, so overnight tens of millions more adults had a disease, while the European guidelines still call 140 over 90 the line. Nobody's arteries changed. Prediabetes is the same trick. And when they took homosexuality out back in the seventies it was a vote by a professional body, not a lab result, yet everyone now talks as if they'd discovered an error.

So here's what I want. Work out what theory of disease this whole thing actually presupposes. Does it need there to be a genuine malfunction of some biological mechanism, with harm bolted on as an extra condition, or is a disorder really just a label for a state we've decided is bad enough to treat? Can a single view cover both the distress requirement and the fact that the thresholds slide around when committees and trials move? Tell me what it strictly presupposes versus what it merely happens to be compatible with, because my suspicion is it's compatible with a lot of rival views and just acts like it isn't.

Then the part I actually care about: what changes if you swap the presupposition out. Take the hard naturalist line, where a broken mechanism is a disease whether or not it hurts you and whether or not anyone can bill for it. What do we suddenly have to start calling diseases? Baldness, aging eyes, being short, infertility, the sexual interests that harm nobody? And take the hard opposite line, where disease is just whatever we've decided is bad enough to treat. Then does it even make sense to say we discovered homosexuality was never a disorder, or was that just a vote? And which of the two readings is the billing and coding system actually running on, since that's where the real force is. Finish with your own take, I want to know if you think I'm wrong that it's incoherent.
