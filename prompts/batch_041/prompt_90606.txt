I write about science and ideas for a general-reader magazine, and for the past few years I've been reporting on the forecasting world — base rates, reference classes, the outside view, calibration, the distinction between a model's structural uncertainty and its parameter uncertainty. I keep noticing that when historians and policy writers argue over whether the lessons of history are usable — whether the Munich analogy, or the Vietnam analogy, or the so-called Thucydides trap, actually licenses any inference about a present crisis — they seem to be having a dispute for which I already own machinery, and also one that my machinery keeps failing to capture.

Please restate the strongest version of the position that historical analogies are treacherous, and then the strongest reply that analogical reasoning is unavoidable and often sound, in the forecasting vocabulary I actually use: reference classes and how they get selected, base rates, effective sample size, overfitting to a vivid single case, feature selection and the problem of deciding which similarities are the relevant ones, selection bias in which episodes survive in the historical record, and the gap between model uncertainty and irreducible noise. Do it faithfully, one side at a time, without quietly importing my framework's assumptions and calling the result a translation. Then give me the residue report: which claims carried over cleanly, which I had to distort to make them fit, and what has no counterpart in the forecasting framework at all — in particular, whether the historians' insistence on the uniqueness of each case, or their claim that one well-understood precedent can outweigh any base rate, is expressing something my framework cannot say or is a less clear way of saying something it already contains. End by telling me which of those two readings you think is correct.
