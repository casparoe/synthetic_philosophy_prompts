I am a novelist by trade — three books of literary fiction, the fourth now underway — and the protagonist of the current one is a forecaster at a public-health agency, which has forced me to decide what I actually believe about rational belief under uncertainty rather than merely what sounds plausible in dialogue. I have ended up with a package of views that I would now defend as my own, and I want a candid audit of it before I hang a whole character's psychology on it. First, I hold that a rational agent's doxastic state is at bottom a single sharp probability function, and that talk of degrees of belief as intervals or sets of functions is a confusion between the agent's beliefs and the quality of the evidence those beliefs answer to. Second, I hold that the discomfort people display in the Ellsberg setup is not a bias to be trained away: where the evidence genuinely fails to fix a chance, declining to name a single number is a rational response to the world. Third, I accept a reflection principle — my present credence in a proposition should equal my expectation of my own future credence in it, once I have thought further and seen more. Fourth, I regard dilation as a reductio of the models that generate it: as I understand the phenomenon, a model can contain evidence E that by its own lights is irrelevant to X — the conditional estimates for X come out the same whichever way E falls, and E is independent of X in every sense the model recognizes — and yet the mere acquisition of E widens my interval for X, so that learning more leaves me less able to say what I think. Fifth, I hold conglomerability: if every member of some partition of possibilities assigns the same value to a quantity, then the unconditional value of that quantity cannot differ from that common value.

I am comfortable with the machinery — measure-theoretic probability, conditional previsions, lower and upper envelopes, the standard arguments that run from Ellsberg to sets of probabilities — so do not spare the notation on my account. What I want to know is whether these five commitments can be held together, and I genuinely do not know where the trouble lies; I am not fishing for permission to abandon any particular one, and I would rather you tell me that a pair I assumed harmonious is not than that a pair I flagged turns out fine.

Please give me the audit as a table, one row per pair of commitments that interact, with these columns: Pair (the two commitments by number, e.g. "2 and 4"); Verdict (either "genuine tension" or "apparent only"); Severity (a rank, 1 being most severe, ties permitted); The Conflict (two or three sentences stating precisely what each commitment demands and why the two demands cannot both be met — or, for an apparent-only row, why the appearance of conflict is misleading); Cheapest Repair (the minimal revision that removes the tension); Cost of Repair (what is given up, and what downstream commitment is endangered by giving it up). Order the rows by severity. Then, beneath the table, a short ranked list of the repairs from least to most costly, closing with a single sentence naming the one revision that resolves the most at the lowest price. If some pair that a reader might expect to be in tension is not, include it and say so explicitly.
