Last night I lost an argument to an old friend about eliminative materialism, and I have not been able to let it go. Her case, in the spirit of the Churchlands, is that our ordinary talk of beliefs, desires and the rest of the propositional attitudes names nothing real — that folk psychology is a stagnant theory awaiting the same fate as phlogiston and caloric, and that a mature neuroscience will simply dispense with it. What exasperated me was that every objection I reached for seemed to require me to treat my own beliefs as things that exist, which is precisely what she denies.

I should like you to write a piece of sustained satire that captures what troubles me about the position. The form I have in mind is a mock agony-aunt column: a brief letter from a reader, followed by the columnist's reply. Set it in a plausible world in which the doctrine has quietly become house style at a certain kind of magazine. The columnist is a sincere, credentialed eliminativist — calm, therapeutic, gently professional, the sort of person who would never knowingly condescend. The reader's marriage is in trouble because her husband keeps telling her, in effect, that she does not really think what she says she thinks, and she wants to know how to live with a man who has adopted this view about her. The whole thing should be deadpan: no winking, no nudges to the reader that a joke is being made.

The humour has to come from the doctrine itself rather than from invention. The columnist genuinely holds the position, so her advice must be advice an eliminativist could actually give; her theory forbids her to ascribe beliefs and desires to anyone, and she knows this, so let the comedy arise from the contortions that forces and from the doctrine's difficulty in counselling anybody at all. Where she slips and attributes a belief despite herself, the slip should be legible as her slip, not as confusion on the part of the author. The reader, who need not share the doctrine, may press the obvious lines of attack — that a view which denies all believing seems to ask us to believe it, and that belief-talk might be a useful instrument even if it fails to carve nature at its joints — but do not let the columnist capitulate. If she wobbles, she should recover in character.

Aim for roughly six hundred words, including the letter, and give the column and its author invented names. Use British spelling throughout, and do not mention any real, living person.
