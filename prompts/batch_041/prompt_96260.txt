I am a software engineer with no formal training in history or philosophy, and I would like help auditing an argument that has been circulating among my colleagues. Two historical analogies are doing the load-bearing work in our internal debates about how to respond to current AI, and I want to know how far each of them actually holds, not which one sounds better.

The first is the printing press. The version a colleague defends is fairly specific. On his account, what the press changed was not the supply of information in general but who could produce authoritative text: before it, copying was slow and controlled by a small literate class; after it, rival readings of the same documents could be produced and distributed at scale. He says the long period of religious and political conflict that followed is not a malfunction of the transition but its characteristic shape, and that the lesson for AI is to expect an extended period of contested interpretation and to invest in institutions that can absorb it rather than trying to slow the technology down. The correspondences he offers are these: the press maps to model weights, cheap copying maps to cheap inference, the newly literate reading public maps to the population of people who now generate text and images, and the clerical monopoly on interpretation maps to today's gatekeepers of expertise. He also stresses that printed copies were identical, which he says is what allowed errors to be located and corrected across editions, and he treats that as grounds for expecting AI to accumulate reliable knowledge over time.

The second is nuclear weapons. A different colleague thinks the press is a poor model because it makes the technology sound diffuse and gradual, when the salient fact is that frontier capability is concentrated in a few laboratories that depend on expensive and physically conspicuous inputs. On his view the right frame is the nonproliferation regime: a small number of states, verifiable limits, inspections, and a shared fear of what happens if the technology spreads. He uses this to argue for treating compute and model weights roughly the way we treat fissile material.

I would like both analogies audited. For each one, give me the correspondence table, then the points at which the mapping breaks; tell me which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion that person draws and which are cosmetic; note which features of the source each argument is quietly borrowing without defending the loan; and say whether the conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table. Two things interest me in particular. First, the claim about identical copies: models are stochastic, so the same input yields different outputs and there is no edition to correct, so does that undercut the printing-press argument or is it a surface difference? Second, whether either analogy is being asked to answer a question it cannot answer, specifically whether either can tell us anything about the pace of change rather than only its direction. Finally, if both analogies are unfit for the purpose, tell me what a better comparison would be and what it would have to get right.
