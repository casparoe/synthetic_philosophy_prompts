I have been reading Machiavelli's The Prince on my own this winter — no course, no instructor, just an old free translation I found online (Marriott's), checked now and then against a modern one when the wording puzzles me — and I have got stuck, in a way I cannot talk myself out of, on the twenty-fifth chapter, the one about fortune. Two stretches in particular keep pulling me back. Early on he writes:

"It is not unknown to me how many men have had, and still have, the opinion that the affairs of the world are in such wise governed by fortune and by God that men with their wisdom cannot direct them and that no one can even help them; and because of this they would have us believe that it is not necessary to labour much in affairs, but to let chance govern them. This opinion has been more credited in our times because of the great changes in affairs which have been seen, and may still be seen, every day, beyond all human conjecture. Sometimes pondering over this, I am in some degree inclined to their opinion. Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free-will, I hold it to be true that fortune is the arbiter of one half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less.

"I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her."

Then, further down the same chapter, after remarking that "he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times, and that he whose actions do not accord with the times will not be successful," he says of men that one "cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

The trouble is that I seem to find two doctrines in these pages and cannot get them onto the same shelf. In the first, fortune is a force outside the prince — a flood arriving from elsewhere, indifferent to him, against which a man can at least build dikes in advance; the whole point of the image seems to be that foresight and preparation shrink fortune's share. By the middle of the chapter, though, the flood has been quietly replaced by something else: success comes from a fit between a man's fixed temperament and the temper of his times, and the reason men are ruined is that they cannot change what they are — the cautious man cannot become impetuous on demand. If that is right, then the dikes are built out of the one thing the prince cannot alter, namely his own nature, and the man who prudently dams the river in fair weather is exactly the man who drowns when the weather turns. So I cannot tell whether the chapter argues that fortune can be resisted, or that it cannot be resisted except by the accident of a fit between character and circumstance, and whether Machiavelli notices the tension or wants us to notice it.

I would be grateful for help reading the passage on its own terms rather than as a summary of the book. Concretely: what is fortune supposed to be in each part, and is the shift a real change of view or one idea seen from two angles? What counts as a dike here — prudence, arms, allies, institutions, something else — and how can it be built by a man who cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to? A friend of mine who prices insurance for a living read the river passage and said it is merely about a hazard you can pay to reduce; that struck me as either obviously right or a bad anachronism, and I cannot decide which. What is the Italian virtù doing in all this, and is the translator's "valour" (where I have also seen "virtue" and "ability") a help or a hindrance? Should the arithmetic of one half be taken literally, or is it a rhetorical shrug? And what am I to make of the closing move, where he calls fortune a woman and declares it better to be impetuous than cautious — does that belong to the argument, or is it a flourish that spoils it? I am after a patient walk through the passage and the puzzle, and I don't need a reading list.
