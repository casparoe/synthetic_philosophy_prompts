I'm a third-year student and I TA the discussion section for a course on classical Indian philosophy. The section is 16 students, 80 minutes, and next week the professor's away at a conference so she's asked me to run the whole session myself. The topic that week is anekāntavāda and the Jaina idea of many-sidedness, and every year the same thing happens: everyone nods along, and then someone says "so it's basically relativism, every perspective is valid," and I can't get them to see how much more specific and more strange the view is than that. So I want to run an activity instead of a lecture — a game or a structured deliberation where they run into the problem themselves and only get the vocabulary afterwards. Please, not the blind men and the elephant; they've all heard it and they think it *is* the doctrine. What I want is something where the rules do the work: put them in positions where each of them has to assert something flatly, something another participant has to deny, and then require the group to produce a single joint statement that everyone can actually sign. And I'd like a constraint built in that they can't escape through "well, for me..." or "it depends" or "both are true" — that's the exact escape hatch they always take, and it's what the Jaina view isn't doing. If a qualifier is going to earn its keep it should have to name a respect in which the claim holds, not just soften it.

So: design the activity for me. Seminar room, whiteboard, index cards, no laptops. Give me the roles or teams, the exact rules, whatever points/scoring scheme you use (no real money, we're broke), a rough timeline for the 80 minutes, and a list of what I need to prep beforehand. I'd also like it to set up that fourth moment where both claims hold and the combination can't be said — I want them to feel that as a genuine limitation on what you can assert, not as a cop-out, so build in a stage where they have to try to state the combined claim out loud and find that whatever they say drops something. Then the debrief: walk me through mapping what just happened onto nayavāda (the standpoints) as against anekāntavāda and the seven-fold predication, including the syāt qualifier, and explain why the view still lets you say some standpoints are better than others — that's the bit that always gets lost. And be honest with me about what will definately go wrong the first time I run it (I'm worried it collapses into "everything is relative" or just becomes a shouting match that the loudest person wins) and how I should handle that in the moment, plus which feature of the view the activity simply can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in my own voice at the end.
