I've read the usual blog-level treatments of the lottery and preface paradoxes, and they tend to stop in the same place: rational belief isn't closed under conjunction, so much the worse for deductive closure. I'd like something more careful on a narrower question — what the difference in dependence structure between the two cases is actually doing. In the lottery, exactly one ticket wins, so the propositions that individual tickets lose are not independent of one another; in the preface, the author's errors plausibly share a common cause, so one bad habit of inference can spoil many claims at once, and the joint risk isn't the product of the individual risks. Explain whether a threshold account of acceptance — accept a proposition just when your credence in it exceeds some t — can handle both cases as they stand, whether the threshold has to move with context or with the size of the conjunction, and whether the two paradoxes are one problem wearing two hats or genuinely two.

Then stress-test that account by stipulating the dependence away, and tell me what survives. Suppose the author's errors really were pairwise independent across claims, and suppose the draw were with replacement, so that each ticket's fate was independent of every other's. Which paradox, if either, still bites? And what does the answer show about which of the two is really about the agglomeration of many small risks and which is really about the epistemic standing of a single high-credence proposition? I'd rather have the argument than the verdict. Please write in British English.
