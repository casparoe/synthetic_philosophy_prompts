I have no background in philosophy or mathematics — I read popular books and argue with a friend — but I keep running into the same wall, and I would like a proper specification before I go further. The wall is this. If space is infinite, or if our descendants continue for an infinite time, then infinitely many lives will exist, and the question of which of two outcomes is better will arise for worlds containing infinitely many lives as the ordinary case rather than as a curiosity. I do not want a theory of value defended. I want the list of requirements that any theory would have to meet to be adequate for that setting, each one attached to the case or consideration that motivates it. Here is the list I have started, mostly from things I have noticed myself; tell me which items belong, which do not, and what I have left out.

1. Worlds containing the same lives in a different order, or the same lives arranged differently in space and time, should come out equally good. (The trick where a hotel with infinitely many full rooms can still take a new guest, because everyone shifts along by one, seems to show that such worlds can be made to look different while containing the same lives.)
2. If two worlds differ only in finitely many lives, the verdict should be the one the ordinary finite comparison gives.
3. Adding a life worth living should not make a world worse. I would also like to know whether it has to make it better, or whether the extra life can be absorbed without change.
4. A world whose population is on the whole happy should beat one whose population is on the whole miserable, even when both contain infinitely many good lives and infinitely many bad ones. I understand that a sum with infinitely many positive and infinitely many negative terms can often be rearranged to total whatever one likes, so a theory cannot simply add up a series and take its limit.
5. The theory must be usable for decisions. If a one-in-a-googol chance of an infinite good outweighs any finite good, then a one-in-a-googol chance of an infinite bad outweighs everything, and no action can be justified; I want to know whether avoiding that costs the theory anything.
6. The verdict must not depend on how we count lives — whether a long life is one life or a series of stages, or where one life ends and the next begins — because in an infinite population the count changes under redescription.
7. It must say something, even if the something is "no verdict", about worlds of different sizes of infinity, and about whether an infinite world can be ranked against a finite one at all, or whether "off the scale" is an acceptable answer.
8. The betterness relation should be transitive. If some otherwise attractive requirement forces me to give up transitivity, I want to be told that plainly.

Then the part I care about most: are any of these jointly unsatisfiable? If some pair or triple cannot all hold at once, say which, explain why, and tell me which one I should give up if I want to keep the rest. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. I would rather have a short honest list than a long one, and I am not asking for a reading list — I want the requirements themselves.
