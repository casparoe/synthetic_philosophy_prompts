I have just spent most of a weekend arguing with a game-theorist friend about Aumann's agreement theorem, and on Thursday I am presenting my side of that argument to a formal epistemology reading group. The group consists of three game theorists who know the theorem and its proof thoroughly, two probabilists who are broadly sympathetic to Bayesian idealization, and one philosopher of science who is skeptical of importing formal results into debates about real-world disagreement. I need a preparation document for the question period, not a written defense.

The thesis I will defend is this: Aumann's theorem is commonly cited for the claim that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, but the theorem establishes only that agents who already share a common prior over a common state space, and who have common knowledge of one another's posterior probabilities, cannot have different posteriors. It is silent on the cases that matter for peer disagreement, because those cases typically involve what I call state-space disagreement: agents who partition the same underlying reality differently, or who disagree about which events are the salient ones, or who do not share a common prior in the first place. I will illustrate this with a toy case in which two Bayesians share a prior over a common refinement but disagree about which coarsening of the event space is the salient one. The theorem is therefore a consistency result about a highly idealized model, not a normative constraint on rational disagreement. I will also argue that the common prior assumption is not a requirement of rationality but a substantive modeling choice, and that treating it as a rationality requirement begs the question against the disagreement.

Prepare me for the eight objections this audience will actually raise. Order them by how much damage they would do to my position, and note how likely each is from the specific members of the group. Phrase each objection in the vocabulary a formal epistemologist or game theorist would use. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, tell me what to concede up front and what to hold firm on, and add a candid note on where the reply is weakest. I want at least one objection that turns on the proof's assumptions, one that turns on the distinction between agreement and convergence, one that turns on the possibility of embedding different state spaces into a common refinement, and one that accuses me of attacking a caricatured version of how the theorem is used. Finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, why it is the most dangerous, and what I might say if it comes up anyway. No citations or reading lists; this is for oral delivery.
