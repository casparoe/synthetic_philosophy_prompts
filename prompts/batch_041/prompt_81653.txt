Some colleagues and I have started a small reading group on Frank Ramsey's Epilogue — the short piece about how he saw life that got tacked onto the end of the posthumous foundations-of-mathematics collection, and that almost everyone treats as juvenilia. Our running joke is that it may be the only philosophical text where the author's brother ends up Archbishop of Canterbury while the author himself married in a register office as a declared atheist, and the group keeps spliting over what the text is even doing. One faction reads it as a document of personal crisis — he wrote it in his early twenties, not long after the stint of analysis in Vienna — and thinks the only interesting questions are biographical. Another thinks there's a real philosophical position buried in it that nobody has bothered to reconstruct: something like a subjectivist or expressivist account of what makes a life worth living, mirroring the way he handled probability as a degree of belief rather than an objective relation; plus the pragmatist strand where holding a belief is a matter of having a map you steer by, which, taken seriously, would make the question of whether my life is meaningful a question about what difference the answer makes to how I live rather than a question about a fact. Then there's the scale thing — whether the sheer size of the universe has any bearing at all on what matters — which some of us think is the most original move in the piece and others think is just a mood. We're also split on the immortality bit: whether he really is rejecting the desirability of living forever, or whether that's a misreading of a much more offhand remark.

What I want from you is not a survey and definitely not a reading list, but an agenda. Concretely: if we could keep one postdoc for two years and run one workshop, where should the effort go, and in what order? I care more about the reasoning than the list — which of these questions is upstream of the others (does the interpretive question of whether the Epilogue is continuous with his technical work have to be settled before you can even ask whether the position is distinct from familiar subjectivist or naturalist views about meaning, or is it the other way round?), which are actually tractable for a philosopher who is not a historian of early analytic philosophy and has no archival access, and which sound deep but would turn out to be a nice essay and nothing more. Two extra candidates I'd like folded into the ranking rather than treated seperately: first, whether you could Ramseyfy talk of meaning — treat it as a theoretical term whose content is fixed by the network of claims in which it appears, then ask which of those claims survive scrutiny; and second, whether the Epilogue quietly fails the author's own pragmatist test of meaningfulness, which would be a reductio of either the test or the piece. For each line, say what the deliverable would actually look like after two years, what evidence or argument over that period should move it up or down the ranking, and which line you'd cut entirely.
