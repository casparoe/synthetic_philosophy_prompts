Analyze three episodes from classical Athens through a single lens: the Athenians' own distinction between nomos and psephisma — between a standing law and a decree of the assembled people — and the claim, familiar from Aristotle, that a democracy in which the assembly decides by decree has made the demos a monarch and quietly dissolved the rule of law. The episodes are the Mytilene debate, in which the assembly voted to kill the adult males of a rebellious city, reversed itself the next day with no new facts on the table and only speeches in between, and sent a second ship that arrived just in time; the Sicilian expedition, in which the assembly approved the campaign, heard Nicias try to deter it by describing the force that would be required, and voted the larger force instead; and the trial of Socrates, where a popular court condemned a man and there was no second vote to be had. Assume these facts rather than restating them.

Run the mapping in both directions. For each episode: does the distinction between law and decree explain what happened, or misdescribe it? Was Mytilene a failure of law-rule or an exercise of it, given that the same body, on the same arguments, could undo a capital decision within a day? Does Sicily exhibit the decree pathology, or only bad deliberation under uncertainty — and what does the irreversibility of a fleet already at sea add, which the theory seems to have no room for? Was Socrates' condemnation a sovereign verdict of just the kind the theory warns against, or a lawful application of Athenian law that the theory must count as law-governed?

Then the reverse direction: what do the cases show about the distinction itself? Does the later insistence that no decree may contradict a standing law vindicate Aristotle, or does Mytilene suggest that what saved Athens was the revisability of its decisions rather than their fixedness? End by naming the hardest case for the theory and the point at which it goes silent.
