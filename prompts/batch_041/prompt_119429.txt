I serve on my state's parole board, and at Thursday's meeting we must decide how to handle the hearing of a specific inmate, D.M., who has served fifteen years for a violent offense and is now eligible for parole. The department has acquired a system called Veritas, which its vendor describes as measuring "affective authenticity": it analyzes facial micro-expressions, vocal prosody, and the congruence between what a speaker says and how he says it, and outputs a "remorse index" from 0 to 100. Our board is split in a way that has turned into an argument about what an emotion actually is. Some members hold that remorse is a feeling -- a private, first-person state that no camera or microphone can reach -- so the index measures performance at best and, at worst, penalizes people who are anxious, neurodivergent, or simply awkward on camera. Others hold that remorse is a judgment -- a judgment that one has done wrong and that one's future conduct must change -- and that judgments are expressed in what people say and do, so a well-validated instrument can legitimately inform us. A third view, pressed by our staff psychologist, is that emotions are perceptions: ways of seeing a situation as calling for a response, which can be more or less accurate, and which may show up in attention and appraisal even when the person cannot articulate them. Our psychologist also argues for what she calls a "two-channel" view -- an emotion is a judgment that is also felt, so someone who says all the right things but feels nothing is not remorseful, and someone who feels terrible but cannot say why is not fully remorseful either.

I need to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting, and the chair has asked for a document the whole board can act on rather than a survey of the literature. Please draft it: recommend a concrete course of action for D.M.'s hearing, including whether and how the Veritas index may be used and under what conditions and time frame; give the reasoning; and engage the strongest version of the view you reject rather than dismissing it. The vote is likely to be close, and whatever you recommend will be read by members who hold each of the positions above, so the opinion should be honest about what it is asking them to give up.
