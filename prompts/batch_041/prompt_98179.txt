I've spent the past four years as a systems engineer at a regional transit authority, and I've recently begun a part-time MA in science and technology studies. The question I keep circling back to is the old dispute over technological determinism — whether the political character of a thing lives in the thing itself or only in the uses people later find for it. My own work has made this uncomfortably concrete. I helped specify the fare gates at a new station, and only afterwards did I notice how much the choices we treated as purely technical — how tall the gate stands, how quickly it closes, what counts as a valid fare, where the accessible lane sits, what the default assumes about the user's hands and height and hurry — quietly settled which bodies could move through the system and which would have to ask a stranger for help. I've been trying to think this through in prose and keep failing, so I'd like to open our seminar with a poem instead.

Please write a sestina in the voice of such a gate — or, if it serves the poem better, a turnstile, a curb, a scanner, or a bridge. Draw the six end-words from the vocabulary of the artifact itself; I've been working with gate, clearance, measure, default, load, and through, though you should swap them for a better set if one suggests itself. The form should carry the argument: the six words return in every stanza, each time re-contextualized, so that what first reads as a mere engineering constraint comes back, on its next appearance, as a decision about who belongs — the poem should make a reader feel the difference between a design that merely permits a use and one that prescribes it, and should hold open whether that politics was intended, emergent, or somehow both. Keep the diction concrete and unprogrammatic; no slogans, and no treating the word power as a punchline. Someone who knows the debate should recognize its real fault lines in the poem, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.

After the sestina, add a short note of three or four sentences naming the one claim about artifact politics the poem is most committed to, and the consideration that would most likely force you to revise it — for instance, a case where the same artifact was turned against its apparent grain, or an argument that the politics is always supplied by context rather than by the thing.
