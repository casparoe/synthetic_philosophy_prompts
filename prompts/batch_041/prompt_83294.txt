I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, so I spend a lot of my time talking to smart people on opposite sides of disputes that never resolve, and there's a shape some of those disputes take that I don't have a word for.

The type case: two teams, same dataset — genuinely the same, shared, nobody sitting on anything — and they come back with different stories about whats going on. One story has a simpler core, fewer moving parts. The other postulates something the first team thinks is conjured up on the spot, say a population of objects too faint to see directly that, if you assume it's there, makes the numbers come out the way they do. Here's the part that gets me. When I ask the simple-core people why the other story is wrong, they don't say the data rule it out — it fits fine, they know that. They say it's a curve fit, a redescription of the data dressed up as an explanation. When I ask the other team the same question, they say the simple model is the curve fit: it gets the numbers right but has no account of why the numbers should be those numbers, and the invisible population is exactly that account. Both sides describe the other's theory in the same words, and neither has an experiment that would end it. It's not one paper either — both views stay in use for years, both keep generating new predictions, and each new result comes out fine for whichever side cares about it. The argument has quietly moved off the evidence and onto which of the two deserves to be called the explanation rather than the description. I've seen the same shape in epidemiology, where two models of an outbreak fit the case counts about equally well and the fight ends up being over whether some parameter is a real feature of the pathogen or an artifact of how the model was set up, with each side calling the other's parameter a fudge.

Is there a name for that? I know the general territory is underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I know the holism point, that you can always save a theory by adjusting something else. But that feels much broader than what I'm looking at. As I understand it, underdetermination is either the philosophers' point that you can cook up a rival that fits any data — in which case the rival is usually a contrivance nobody would actually work with — or the practical point that scientists have to commit before the evidence is all in. What I'm describing is narrower and has a feature the textbook version doesn't seem to have: both rivals are live, both are being used, both are defended by serious people, and the disagreement has migrated onto the question of which one is doing the explaining, where the standard that's supposed to break the tie — simplicity, not multiplying entities, unifying power — is itself what's in dispute instead of being a neutral referee. And nobody is accusing anyone of bad faith; all the accusations are about what kind of thing the other theory is.

So does this have a name, or a small family of names? If the honest answer is that this is just underdetermination and I'm describing the standard case, then tell me what the standard case actually claims and where the line falls between it and what I'm describing. If it's something else — something about the gap between accommodating a result and predicting it, or about theoretical virtues carrying weight that evidence can't — say which, and how it differs from plain underdetermination and from the holism point. And if nothing fits, coin something, but define it so it doesn't just swallow every scientific disagreement there's ever been: tell me what it rules out, how I'd tell this apart from a case where one side really is just fitting a curve, and how I'd tell it apart from two sides who only sound like they disagree because they mean different things by the same word. A word by itself won't help me — I need to know what the thing is and where its edges are. My editor keeps asking me who's right, and this is the closest I've gotten to an answer.
