I am a high school junior, and this spring I have to submit a proposal for the independent research paper that is the capstone of my school's philosophy program: roughly four thousand words, written over one semester under a faculty mentor, on a topic I choose now and cannot change later. I would like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into that proposal. The idea came out of two things that happened in the same month — a unit in my biology class on organ systems, and an argument between my parents after my grandfather died in a hospital, over when exactly he had stopped being there. What struck me is that the legal definition of death seems unstable in a way that has nothing to do with medicine and everything to do with our asking a single concept to do two jobs at once. The first job is descriptive: to mark the event at which a living organism stops being an organism, the point after which nothing that happens to it counts as its own functioning. The second is normative: to mark the point at which permissions and obligations switch — that treatment may be stopped, that organs may be taken, that a killing has occurred, that a will takes effect. Both jobs seem to depend on the notion of irreversibility, and irreversibility is not a fact about a body; it is a fact about what the available technology can do to one. A stopped heart was once death itself, because nothing could be done about it; now a heart can be stopped in surgery and restarted, and nobody says the patient died and came back. So my working hypothesis is that the criteria we actually use — the brain-death criterion especially — are best read not as locating a moment in the biology but as locating a moment relative to a reference class of available interventions, and that the intractability of the brain-death-versus-circulatory-death dispute is a symptom of our never having made that reference class explicit. My second hunch is that two readings of irreversibility are quietly at work in the debate and that separating them does most of the work: one reading is that a process cannot be reversed by any means whatsoever, the other that it will not be reversed by the processes that are in fact going to operate on this particular body, and I suspect only the second is consistent with the criteria clinicians actually apply.

Please write the proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer and so that I could in principle be shown to be wrong rather than merely incomplete. Explain why the standard positions — whole-brain death, higher-brain death, the circulatory criterion, and the view that death is simply a biological matter settled by the organism's loss of integration — leave my question open instead of settling it, since I need to be sure I am not just re-describing a debate that already has an answer. Set out the method: I assume this is conceptual rather than empirical, and that I would work through a set of cases, but say what working through cases actually consists in here, why that is not just a survey of intuitions, and why it beats the obvious alternative of asking clinicians what they do. Say what a successful result would look like, what would count as the project failing, including the real possibility that the two jobs turn out to be inseparable after all, and give me a realistic plan for four thousand words in one semester, with an honest note on which part is most likely to balloon out of control. I do not need citations, a reading list, or author names — I will fill those in myself — so please refer to positions and arguments rather than to who held them.
