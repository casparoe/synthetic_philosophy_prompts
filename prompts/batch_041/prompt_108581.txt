I teach a six-session evening course on Marx to adults with no background in either philosophy or economics — the group typically includes a nurse, a delivery driver, a retired accountant, and one or two people who have read the Manifesto once. The third session has to establish the difference between exploitation and alienation, and it has failed the same way every time: everything collapses into a single grievance about being underpaid, and alienation becomes a word for burnout. I do not want the lecture. I want the plan for it.

Give me the order in which the components must be introduced, and why each depends on the one before it. My own instinct is that the spine of the session should be the gap between a wage as the price of a capacity and a wage as payment for work actually done — that the whole of exploitation is packed into that gap, and that alienation is what happens when the gap becomes invisible to the person standing in it. Tell me whether that spine can bear the weight, and what must already be in place before it can even be stated (I assume the audience has to see labour as something sold rather than a service rendered, but I may be wrong about what comes first; I am equally unsure whether the abstract/concrete distinction in labour has to precede it or can be dropped at this level without damage).

For the example that carries the whole thing: I am inclined to use a case deliberately unlike a Victorian mill — a well-paid software engineer with equity, autonomy, and real enthusiasm for the work — because I want the audience to discover that these concepts are not aimed at misery. Is that the right case to carry both ideas, or should it be held back as a test case at the end while something humbler carries the middle? Say what the case must contain, and what it must be missing, to do that job.

Then the misconception map. Candidates: exploitation as mere underpayment; alienation as a feeling; the two as one complaint; Marx as making a moral accusation that a raise could answer; surplus value as a synonym for profit; the labour theory of value as a forecast about prices. Tell me which of these the group arrives with and which they will form in the room, at exactly which beat of the sequence each has to be headed off, and what goes wrong if it is not.

Close with the single question I can put to the group at the end that would show the distinction has landed rather than merely been heard, and say what I should explicitly decline to teach at this level, with the reason stated aloud.

Finally, tell me where this plan is most likely to fail: which ordering, or which predicted misconception, you are least confident about, and what I would have to see in the room to conclude that the spine I proposed is wrong.
