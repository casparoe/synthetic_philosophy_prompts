I'm not a philosopher, just someone who's been reading about the effective altruism thing where people publicly pledge to give 10% of their income to the most effective charities, and they also tell you to pick a career for its impact -- like go into finance or tech and donate the salary. Here's what bugs me. If you really think suffering is bad and you can prevent some of it by giving up stuff you don't need, why stop at 10%? The same logic seems like it should get you to 50% or more, at least if you're in a rich country. But the people who do this don't act like they're monsters for not giving everything, and they don't treat the 10% as just a nice extra either -- they talk like it's something you ought to do. So what does the practice actually presuppose about moral obligation? Does it presuppose there's a duty to give some specific amount, and if so where does the amount come from? Or does it presuppose giving is above and beyond the call of duty, and the pledge is just a personal commitment? Can one consistent view make sense of both the 10% number and the career advice, or is the practice quietly borrowing from two incompatible pictures -- a demanding consequentialist one and a common-sense one with a lot of latitude? If the practice were rebuilt on a strictly impartial maximizing view, what would have to change? And if it were rebuilt on a view where morality doesn't demand much, what would have to change? Please seperate what's actually settled among people who know this stuff from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
