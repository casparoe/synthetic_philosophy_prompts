I am a novelist at work on a book about people who earn their living by predicting things, and I keep running aground on a knot that my own prose cannot dramatise. I would like you to write me a short story that does it on my behalf.

Take the role of a writer who is also a competent philosopher of science: someone equally at home in the mathematics of dynamical systems and in the craft of fiction. Before you write anything, think carefully and set out a short brainstorming section — a page at most — in which you work through the conceptual problem, sketch two or three possible situations, and say why you chose the one you did. Then write the story itself. I care about the quality of the thinking as much as the quality of the prose, so do not hurry past the preparation.

The knot has four strands, and I need all four to be load-bearing rather than decorative.

First, the difference between a system's being settled and our being able to know it. Determinism, as I understand it, is a claim about the equations: given the state at one time, every later state is fixed. Predictability is a claim about us: whether any finite observer, with finite measurements and finite time, can find out what that fixed state is. A system can be wholly deterministic and wholly unpredictable, and the two facts do not quarrel.

Second, sensitive dependence. In many such systems, two starting points that differ by an amount too small to measure separate from one another at an exponential rate, so that every error in the initial reading, however slight, is magnified until the forecast and the fact have nothing in common. The unpredictability is not a failure of effort or instrument; it is a property of the system.

Third, and this is the strand I most want you to handle well: shadowing. For a great many chaotic systems, a numerical trajectory that has wandered away from the true trajectory is nevertheless closely tracked by some genuine trajectory of the same system — a real solution, obeying the same laws, that began from a slightly different state. The computer's forecast is therefore not the actual future, and yet it is an actual future, a state the system could genuinely have reached. The simulation is wrong about the world and right about the law.

Fourth, what is sometimes called computational irreducibility. For some deterministic systems there is no shortcut: the only way to learn the state at a later time is to run the thing forward, step by step, at the same cost as the system itself. Determinism without any prospect of a shortcut.

What I want from the story is not an exposition of these ideas but a situation in which they bite. Somewhere in it there should be a character who is obliged to act on a prediction that they have proved, to their own satisfaction, cannot be the true future — and who must nevertheless decide what to do with it. I want the difference between it is already settled and we can find out what it is to be felt in the body, not explained at a lectern. Avoid the temptation to resolve the knot by fiat: no character should win the argument by announcing the moral. Let the fiction keep the difficulty intact.

You have a free hand with setting, period and cast. A meteorological office, a clockmaker's workshop, an insurance house, a bureau that forecasts river heights, a gambling den, a family that has run the same tide-tables for four generations — any of these, or something better of your own devising. Place the ideas in the hands of people whose livelihoods and affections depend on the answer. Dialogue is welcome; a lecture disguised as dialogue is not.

Constraints. Length between 1,500 and 2,500 words. Give the story a title. Invent everything: no real persons, no real publications, no quotations from any existing text, no citations, no reading lists, and no epigraph borrowed from anyone. If you want an epigraph, write it yourself and make plain that it is your invention. Do not append commentary or a moral after the story ends. Write throughout in British English — spelling, idiom, punctuation and rhythm — and keep the register that of a serious novelist rather than a textbook.
