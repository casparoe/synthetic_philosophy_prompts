I am the housemaster of a boarding house of sixty children aged eight to thirteen, and between three and six times a week I have to decide how to respond to an incident: a boy who lies to me about a broken window, a girl who has been quietly excluding a younger child from every game, a boy who has taken money from a coat pocket. I make these decisions in ten minutes, with the school's published sanctions tariff in front of me — warning, detention, loss of privileges, letter home, suspension — and with the child's whole record at stake. I read Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education once, years ago, and remember only its headlines: that virtue comes before learning, that the wish for esteem and the fear of disgrace do more work than the rod or the sweet, that habits laid down early outlast anything one can argue a child into. I would like my responses to run on what Locke actually says rather than on my memory of the headlines, but I have no time to re-read him and my deputy has read none of him.

Please write me a procedure the two of us can run on an incident, in about ten minutes, without either of us having read Locke.

- Give the questions to ask, in order, and say what counts as a sufficient answer to each.
- Mark the points at which the procedure terminates: where it stops with no formal response at all, where it hands the matter to the tariff, where it must go to the head or the safeguarding lead, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than of fact.
- For each incident the procedure must decide whether the response should be aimed at the child's understanding or at his habits and surroundings, and it must be willing to conclude that the right response is to change something in the house routine rather than to sanction the child.
- Tell me which parts of Locke's scheme I should not import into a school like mine. He wrote for one tutor with one boy in one household; I have eight staff on a rota and sixty children, and there are things a modern school cannot and should not do to a child's reputation or his body. Say plainly where his psychology and my institution come apart.
- Then run the procedure on three incidents: a boy of eleven who lies whenever he is in trouble; a boy of nine who appears genuinely indifferent to what any adult thinks of him; and a case in which the procedure tells me to do nothing to the child and something to the house. I want that last one worked through even though I will not like the answer.
- Finish with the small set of Locke's claims the procedure actually rests on, so that I can check them myself, and a short honest account of the incidents it will get wrong and the judgements it cannot take off my hands.
