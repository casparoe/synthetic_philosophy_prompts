I'm building a case that pulls apart two things people tend to run together: testimonial injustice — the classic setup where a hearer docks someone's credibility because of who they are, their accent or class or gender or whatever — and the quieter thing where a speaker preemptively silences herself because she's already worked out that the hearer wont really take it in. I want a thought experiment engineered so the first is absent and the second is present, and the interesting part is that the second still feels like a wrong.

Specs, and I mean these as hard constraints:

- one speaker, one hearer, one ordinary setting — a clinic, a workplace, a school, a family kitchen. Not a courtroom, not a news scandal.
- the hearer carries no identity prejudice against the speaker's group. Stipulate it away, including the sneaky unconscious kind, so the case cant be explained by the hearer's bad character or hidden bias.
- the twist I actually care about: if the speaker had just said the whole thing plainly, the hearer would have believed her, and it would have changed a real decision. So there is no actual credibility deficit anywhere in the scene — the disbelief never happens, because the testimony never gets said.
- yet the speaker reasonably anticipates not being heard and trims or swallows it. Her anticipation has to be reasonable given how things really are — structural, institutional, a script she's absorbed — not paranoia and not her being oversensitive.
- the testimony she withholds is true and consequential.
- nobody in the scene is a villain and nobody's a saint. If the wrong only shows up when someone is nasty, the case fails.

After the scene, one paragraph, walk me through the following. (a) State the belief she would have voiced, the mechanism that stops her, and why her foresight is reasonable — the same kind of check you'd do for a knowledge case, method and all. (b) Say whether this counts as testimonial injustice on the standard definition, and why or why not. (c) Say whether it's a wrong at all — and if so, what kind — given that literally no one disbelieves her. (d) Tell me which single feature of the setup is doing the work of making it a wrong, and then flip it two ways: make the hearer prejudiced, and make the speaker's foresight unreasonable, and say what happens to your verdict each time. (e) Give me the strongest version of the objection that says "no credibility deficit, no injustice, case closed," and your best reply.

The point of the case is to test whether the wrong of epistemic injustice can survive with zero actual credibility deficit, so if you find yourself just asserting that it's a wrong without generating that from the structure of the scene, you've cheated.
