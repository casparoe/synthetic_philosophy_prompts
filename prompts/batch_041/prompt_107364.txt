After a career in the physical sciences I have spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I keep meeting a difficulty that I used to handle by instinct and would now like to understand properly. The difficulty is this. When I know nothing about a quantity beyond the range it can occupy, I am tempted to treat all values in that range as equally likely. But that instruction is not well defined, because everything depends on the variable in which I apply it. Suppose I know only that a cube's edge lies between one and two centimetres. If I take the edge length to be equally likely at every value, I obtain one spread over the volume; if I instead take the volume to be equally likely across the range it can occupy, I obtain a different one. Both seem licensed by the same reasoning from ignorance, yet they disagree about any question I put to them, such as the chance that the volume exceeds some threshold. I have since noticed the same structure elsewhere. A colleague estimating a rate constant from a bare order-of-magnitude range gets a different spread depending on whether he works with the rate or with its logarithm. And a friend reasoning about a random point on the surface of the Earth gets different answers about whether it falls in a given hemisphere depending on whether she conditions on latitude or on longitude first.

What I would like to know is whether there is an established name for this general phenomenon — the failure of "treat every possibility as equally likely" to be well defined, because it silently presupposes a choice of variable — and, more than the name, how the field carves it up. Is it one problem or a family of related ones? How does it bear on the separate complaint that a probability for a single case depends on which class of similar cases one draws the frequency from? In practice we often settle such questions by demanding that the answer not change when we change units, but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine derivation or a substantive empirical assumption in disguise; nor can I tell whether appeals to a symmetry of the situation, or to some rule for making the assignment as uninformative as possible, actually resolve the ambiguity or merely move it somewhere else. Is the honest conclusion that probabilities of this kind are subjective through and through, or is there a defensible objective version?

I am not after a reading list. I want the distinctions drawn carefully, the boundary cases made explicit, and a verdict on which of the neighbouring concepts fits what I have described and which does not.
