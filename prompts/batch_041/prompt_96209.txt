I'm in a seminar on church and state and we've spent weeks on the standard menu of arrangements -- establishment, seperation, the accommodationist versus strict-separationist readings of the First Amendment, and the German church tax, that 8 or 9 percent surcharge on your income tax that the state collects on the churches' behalf and that you only escape by formally leaving the church. All fine. The case I can't place is Alsace-Moselle, and I'd like you to work it through rather than summarise it. The setup, as I understand it (correct me if I have a detail wrong, but don't let the answer hinge on the details): the Napoleonic Concordat of 1801 was abrogated in the rest of France by the 1905 law of separation, but Alsace and Moselle were part of the German Empire at the time, so the Concordat was never repealed there, and when the territories returned to France after the First World War the arrangement was kept and then made permanent by enactments in 1924. It survives today. Four religious traditions are recognised: Catholicism, Lutheran Protestantism, Reformed Protestantism, and Judaism. Their clergy are on the state payroll, paid out of the Interior Ministry's budget, with salaries pegged to civil service scales under an agreement from the 1990s. The bishop of Metz and the archbishop of Strasbourg are appointed by decree of the President after agreement with the Holy See; the heads of the Protestant churches and the Jewish consistories are appointed by the Prime Minister. Religious instruction is part of the curriculum in public schools, with an opt-out on written request. The only state theology faculties in France are at Strasbourg. Every serious attempt to repeal the arrangement has failed, the Constitutional Council upheld it against a secularist challenge in 2013, and Islam -- the second largest religion in the country -- isn't on the list at all.

What I want is this analysed through the lens of the neutrality ideal, and specifically through the split between neutrality among religions and neutrality between religion and irreligion. So: who are the parties, what does the neutrality ideal say each of them is entitled to, and what does it say about a settlement that is scrupulously even-handed across four faiths and quite deliberately not even-handed between belief and unbelief? Does the fact that the recognised list is a list -- closed, historical, and missing the largest minority religion -- show that even-handedness always needs a baseline the theory can't supply for itself, which would make the 1905 settlement just as much a non-neutral choice as the Concordat is? Then the other direction, which is the part I actually care about: what does the case do to the theory? Push the rival reading that laïcité was never really about neutrality but about the state's authority over religion -- the Gallican inheritance, the state deciding which religions exist and who gets to run them -- and see whether Alsace-Moselle comes out as an anomaly under that reading or as the more honest picture of what French church-state policy has been doing all along. Don't retell me the history and don't hand me a reading list or citations; I have the reading. What I want is the arguement, and an honest note on the places where the theory is simply silent rather than being stretched to cover this case.
