I'm doing a short talk for a reading group on Keynes and uncertainty and I want to open with a joke. Not the usual "economists predict 9 of the last 5 recessions" thing — I want one that actually turns on the ideas, so that someone who's read the Treatise and not just the General Theory laughs and everyone else is slightly lost.

Here's the bit of Keynes I want it to land on, since it's the bit I care about. People treat his uncertainty thing as if it were just Knight's risk/uncertainty split, but it isn't quite. In the Treatise he has this second dimension he calls the "weight of argument" — roughly, how much relevant evidence is sitting behind the number, which moves seperately from the number itself. More evidence can lower the probability and still raise the weight, so a worse-looking probability can rest on a firmer basis. Knight's distinction is different in kind: it's about whether the thing is measurable or insurable at all, which is why he gets mileage out of insurance markets and profits. What I'd love is a joke where two people agree on the exact same probability and still end up in a real fight, because one of them has weight behind it and the other has nothing but a sharp number.

Keep it short: a setup and a punchline, or a couple of lines of dialogue at most. It should land for someone who knows the material. Please don't do a "a Keynesian and a Bayesian walk into a bar" bit, that's exactly the register I'm trying to avoid.

Then, underneath the joke, give me a short note on where it takes liberties with Keynes and where I should be nervous. Which move in the joke is doing the work on a contested reading, and what would change your mind about whether the joke is fair to him? If it only works because you've quietly handed Keynes a Knight-style notion of uncertainty, say that outright. And flag anything in there a picky historian of economics would call flat-out wrong.
