I spent thirty-five years as an experimentalist — a doctorate in physics and then a career in biophysics — and only since retiring have I begun to read moral and legal philosophy in earnest, which I mention because it probably explains why the following has me stuck. At a small dinner after a seminar last month, a retired judge and a much younger friend of mine argued about capital punishment, and, to my surprise, both of them planted their flag on the same old maxim: that it is better that ten guilty men go free than that one innocent man suffer. Neither had much patience for the deterrence literature — the judge is a retributivist and considers the empirical question beside the point — so the quarrel came down to those few words. The judge read them as a piece of institutional engineering: any workable system will occasionally convict the innocent, the maxim tells us how much of that we should be willing to buy in exchange for catching the guilty, and a demanding standard of proof together with appellate review keeps us on the right side of the exchange. My friend read the same words as an absolute constraint: certainty about guilt is never available, an execution cannot be undone, and so the maxim forbids execution altogether — the ratio it names may be ten to one in ordinary matters, but where the penalty is final no number of guilty men may be released in exchange for one innocent life. Each thought the other was simply misreading the sentence, and neither could say what the sentence actually asserts.

What I want is not a verdict on capital punishment but an anatomy of the maxim. In my own line of work two kinds of error always traded off against one another: one could push down the rate at which a real signal was missed only by raising the rate at which a spurious one was manufactured, and the whole of the practical question was where on that curve to sit. The maxim, as I hear it, is either a refusal to acknowledge such a curve at all or a claim about one particular point on it, and I cannot get those two readings to come apart cleanly in my head — perhaps because I do not know whether the sentence is meant to describe the world, to prescribe a manner of reasoning, or to state something that we owe to one another.

So please lay out the distinct precise claims the maxim could be making, ordered from the nearly empty to the genuinely radical, and give a verdict on each: platitude, defensible but substantive, or false. Tell me what work, if any, the number ten is doing — a literal exchange rate, a rhetorical flourish, a placeholder for 'very much greater than one' — and whether 'innocent' is being used in the sense of 'not proved guilty' or in the sense of 'did not do it,' since the two seem to me to come apart precisely where the judge and my friend disagree. Finally, say which of the readings would actually license the judge's use of the maxim, which would license my friend's, which would license neither, and which one the people who recite it in ordinary argument are most likely to have in mind. I do not need a bibliography; what I need is the distinctions sorted out.
