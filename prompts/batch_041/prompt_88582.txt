I'm a software engineer who writes short fiction on the side, and I've fallen down a rabbit hole in the philosophy of mind: the dispute over whether dreams are experiences at all — a dispute associated with Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett. On one picture, a sleeper undergoes experiences during the night and the morning report merely describes them; on the other, the "dream" is assembled around the moment of waking by processes the sleeper never experienced, and is then mistaken for the memory of an experience. What grips me is that every dream report we possess fits both pictures equally well.

Act as a philosopher of mind who also writes literary short fiction. Before drafting, brainstorm four or five candidate premises and pick the one with the most dramatic pressure. Then write a short story of roughly 1,200–1,800 words, built around one invented device: a clinic that offers "dream playback," reconstructing a dream from a sleeping patient's physiological data and playing it back as they wake. Two constraints on the device. First, its engineers designed it on the assumption that the dream was an experience waiting to be read out. Second, the reconstruction is partly generated from what the patient says at intake, so the machine cannot in principle distinguish a dream that was lived through from a plausible dream manufactured after the fact. Give me at least one scene in which a character must decide whether a playback counts as evidence of what actually happened in the night, and let the stakes be personal rather than academic.

Requirements: the philosophical question must be carried by what the characters do, fear, and choose, not by anyone explaining it; no character delivers a lecture, and no real philosopher's name or work appears in the story. Keep both readings genuinely alive at the end — do not resolve the question by fiat, and give each side its strongest moment. Don't cite any literature; I only want the story. Finish with two sentences, in your own voice, naming the two positions you left hanging and which scene you used to make each one vivid.
