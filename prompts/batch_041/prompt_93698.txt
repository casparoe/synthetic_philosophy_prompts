I got into a genuinely stupid five-hour argument with my friend Priya last night (she's doing a psych PhD, I did an MA in philosophy and then sold out and went into tech) about whether dreams are experiences, and somewhere around 1am I talked myself into a position I've never seen written down anywhere, which usually means its either trivially wrong or was refuted by somebody in 1962 and everyone moved on. I want you to tell me which. I've written the position out below as a short essay. Don't grade my summaries of Malcolm and Dennett — I'm genuinely not that fussed about whether I've got them right, I care about whether the argument itself works.

Here's the essay:

Malcolm's line is that dreaming isn't an experience at all: a dream report isn't an observation report, and there's no sense in saying you were observing anything while you were asleep. Dennett's reply is roughly that this just forces a choice about where to put the dream in the night — either it happens in real time while you sleep, or the brain assembles the whole thing and then plays it back to you, and what you remember is the playback rather than something you lived through. He calls the second one the cassette theory (I always get muddled about exactly when the tape gets made — during sleep or on waking — but the upshot is the same), and his point is that nothing observable discriminates between the two, because a cassette can be written to fit the data. The classic sleep-lab stuff gets cited as evidence for the real-time view — spray the sleeper's face with water, and the report afterwards has a fountain or a rainstorm in it — but a cassette written after the fact has the wet pillow to work from, so it doesn't settle anything.

I think the cassette theory is a red herring, and so is the whole fight about timing. The cassette theory is still a *playback* theory: it says there's a finished episode with a beginning, a middle and an end, and the only question is when it got manufactured. What I want to say is that there's no moment of occurrence at all, not even a compressed one. Think about the colour-phi thing — flash a dot, then flash another dot in a different colour slightly to the right, and people report seeing one dot move across and change colour halfway, which means the second colour is being experienced *before* it was presented. That isn't a recording played late; it's the absence of any fact about what was experienced at t that's independent of what happens at t+Δ. So a dream is a postdiction of a night. And the closest analogue isn't perception, it's confabulation — the thing where someone gives you a reason for what they did that they demonstrably couldn't have had at the time, and yet the reason is genuinely theirs. Nobody asks whether the confabulated reason was experienced. Which is why I think the question of whether dreams are experiences dissolves rather than gets answered. The cost is that I lose the right to say I was *there* for my dreams, and that feels false. But I suspect the feeling is the only thing holding the whole picture up.

So, comments please. Start with the single strongest objection you can make to the central move, then tell me whether the whole thing is really just Dennett's cassette theory in a different outfit or whether it's an actual step forward, and then flag anywhere I've quietly helped myself to something I haven't earned. Under 500 words — I'm sending this to Priya tonight and I want the sharp version, not a lecture.
