I teach a graduate seminar in metaphysics that spends several weeks on self-locating belief, and I am working through the take-home midterm. One question, reproduced below along with a student's response, is worth 20 points. I would like you to grade the response.

The question:

(a) State the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA). For each, say what set the observer is treated as randomly drawn from and what, if anything, each assumption says about the evidential weight of the bare fact that you exist.

(b) Set out the Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment and say precisely what it is supposed to establish.

(c) A cosmologist informs you that the prior probability that the universe is small — containing on the order of 10^10 observers in total — is 1/2, and the prior probability that it is large — containing on the order of 10^30 observers in total — is also 1/2, and that you have no further relevant evidence. Using SIA, compute your posterior credence that the universe is large. Show your reasoning.

(d) Explain how SSA and SIA respectively bear on the Doomsday argument.

The student's response:

"(a) The self-sampling assumption says that I should reason as if I am a random observer drawn from the set of all actually existing observers in my reference class. The self-indication assumption says that I should reason as if I am a random observer drawn from the set of all possible observers, so that a hypothesis entailing that more observers exist is more likely, since it supplies more 'slots' that I could have occupied. The difference is that SSA treats my existence as fixed and only randomizes my position among observers who exist, whereas SIA treats my existence itself as evidence, with a likelihood proportional to the number of observers the hypothesis says there are.

(b) In the Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment, a philosopher is asked whether the universe is small or large and is told that no astronomical data will settle the matter. He nevertheless declares himself almost certain that the universe is enormous, on the ground that a large universe contains vastly more observers and is therefore, by SIA, vastly more likely to contain him. The point of the example is that SIA lets the bare fact of one's own existence swamp all the empirical evidence, which shows that SIA is internally inconsistent: no principle of self-locating belief could license such confidence and still respect the probability calculus. A sensible theorist should therefore reject SIA and adopt SSA.

(c) With a prior of 1/2 on each hypothesis and likelihoods proportional to the number of observers, the posterior that the universe is large is 10^30 / (10^30 + 10^10) = 1 / (1 + 10^-20), which is essentially 1. So SIA makes me practically certain that the universe is large before I have looked at the sky at all.

(d) SSA and SIA divide on the Doomsday argument. SIA strengthens it: by making my existence more likely in worlds containing more observers, it makes it more likely that I am one of the many observers located late in the species' history, so my unremarkable birth rank counts against a long future. SSA, by contrast, holds the fact of my existence fixed and so leaves the Doomsday reasoning without its key premise, licensing confidence in a very long future."
