I am a high school student, and in a paper for my philosophy club I have been defending the following view; I want to know what would count against it. Human extinction would be bad for a reason beyond the deaths it involves: it forecloses a collective afterlife, a continuing human future that remembers us, carries our projects forward, and lends our lives a retrospective significance they would not otherwise have. On this view the distinctive harm is not dying multiplied; it is the severing of a relation between us and whoever comes after.

Rank what counts against the view from what should genuinely make me abandon it to what is merely awkward, and say which of these someone who holds the view would concede rather than dismiss. Two cases I keep worrying about deserve real treatment: a future in which humanity survives but has no memory of, or continuity with, us, and one in which it survives and remembers us only to repudiate what we did. The view seems to imply that these are nearly as bad as extinction, and I cannot tell whether that is a reductio or a point in its favour. I also want to know whether the view is really about us or only about the attitudes of future people, whether it predicts anything that a simpler view (that extinction is bad because it costs future people their lives) does not, and whether it has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
