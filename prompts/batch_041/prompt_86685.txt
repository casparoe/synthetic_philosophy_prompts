I'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of history right now and we just spent three weeks on the covering-law model -- Hempel's deductive-nomological thing, where to explain an event is to deduce it from a general law plus initial conditions -- and the longer we go the more convinced I am that the whole program is better understood as an administrative policy than as an epistemology. Like, it's not a theory of what explanation is, it's a licensing regime. So I want you to write the paperwork. Invent a government body -- the Bureau of Historical Explanation Standards, the Office of Nomological Compliance, whatever you like -- that has been charged with enforcing the covering-law standard on the historical profession, and give me its actual documents, deadpan, no winking, no narrator who knows it's a joke. I want: Form CL-1, the form a historian files to have an explanation certified, with its numbered fields (event to be explained; antecedent conditions; covering law relied upon; certification that the law is true; certification that the law is not trivially true of any possible history; certification that the derivation would fail if the law were false; and so on); the Bureau's appendix of standard rejection codes; a hardship waiver for narrative historians, with the Bureau's deadpan reasoning about why it exists; and a one-page memo from the Director explaining why the Bureau will not be recognizing narrative explanation as a category. About 650 words total. The comedy has to come from getting the philosophy right, so a philosopher of history laughs instead of wincing -- the funniest version of this is a Bureau that is scrupulous and sincere and keeps discovering that essentially every real submission fails its own criteria, and is quietly troubled by that. Use the actual problems: the symmetry or irrelevance objection (the same law that "explains" the event equally "explains" its non-occurrence), the fact that candidate laws in history tend to be either false or tautological or hedged with ceteris paribus, the fact that a historian's explanation seems to survive the falsity of the law she cites, and the alternative picture on which explaining is showing what made a difference or assembling a narrative that shows how it came about rather than deriving it. You can also have the Bureau grapple with "how possibly" versus "why necessarily" explanations, and let the narrative historians' grievances come through only in the rejection codes and the waiver's fine print, never as an editorial aside.

A couple of constraints. Don't name any living scholars and don't have the Director be a buffoon -- he should be a competent administrator who is genuinely trying to apply the standard, which is where the satire should bite. I'm argueing about this with a friend who's a working historian and she thinks the covering-law people were never really offering a description of what historians do, only a normative ideal, so it'd be nice if the documents left that question open rather than settling it for me.

Then, after the documents, a short note outside the fiction -- just a few sentences, no headers needed -- telling me which bits of this are settled among people who actually work on explanation in history and which are genuinely contested, and not presenting a contested reading as the consensus. I'd rather know where the piece takes liberties than have it all sound like received wisdom.
