I'm a retired field ecologist — thirty-odd years spent watching forests come back from blowdowns and burns — and since retiring I've been reading my way into archaeology and the philosophy of history, largely because I keep colliding with a word I no longer trust: collapse. In my old line of work, what looks like catastrophe from the air is very often just the release phase of a cycle; the system returns, usually different, sometimes richer, and the dead trees turn out to have been the point. So when I read that a Bronze Age world or a Maya city or some island society "collapsed," I want to know whether we are describing the thing itself or the frame we carried in with us. What I'd like from you is a case built to order, so that the ambiguity stops being a matter of taste and becomes something I can inspect.

Here are the two criteria I want you to work with, stated as plainly as I can manage. Criterion A, the complexity criterion: a society is doing well to the degree it sustains a high and rising ceiling of internal complexity — more levels of settlement and administration, more occupational specialization, more monumental construction, more stored records, more long-distance exchange — and it has collapsed when that ceiling drops sharply and fails to recover within, say, three generations. Criterion B, the option-space criterion, which is mine and therefore stipulative: a society is doing well to the degree its members retain a wide range of feasible ways of living — several subsistence strategies, several places they could actually go, several roles they could actually occupy — and it has collapsed when that range contracts, whenever and however slowly that happens, even if every headline measure of complexity is still climbing. Build me one concrete case, set in a single river valley or island or highland over no more than six or eight generations, in which A and B return opposite verdicts while both are looking at the same society and the same sequence of events. Keep it an ordinary pre-industrial farming society — no science fiction, no alien intervention, no conveniently timed meteor. Isolate the one variable whose setting flips the verdict, and present the case twice, once with that variable in each position, with everything else held fixed.

Then do the accounting, and do it in the open. For each criterion, state the verdict and the chain of reasoning that yields it; say which single feature of the case is doing the real work; show what happens to the divergence when I remove that feature; and tell me how someone who prefers whichever criterion comes out worse would set about attacking the case rather than accepting it. I'd like you to close by taking a position rather than surveying one — I have read enough surveys. Tell me which criterion I should be using if what I actually care about is whether people were better off, and defend the choice against the obvious objection that the other criterion is tracking something real that mine simply ignores. I would much rather have a definite answer I can argue with than a balanced menu.
