So my two closest friends from college, Dana and Priya, had a huge falling out about three years ago over something to do with a shared lease and a dog, and it got ugly — lawyers were mentioned, nobody actually hired one, but the friendship was basically dead for a while there. Anyway, they've spent the last six months slowly patching things up, and last week they told me they want to make it official: they've booked a table at the restaurant where the three of us used to go, and they've asked me to stand up and give a short speech that declares the whole thing over and done with, so that everyone in our friend group knows the feud is finished and we can all stop choosing sides. I said yes immediately and now I'm panicking, because the more I think about it the less sure I am that a speech can actually do that. Like, I get that some things only happen when someone says them — I've watched enough courtroom stuff to know a judge can end a marriage just by saying so, and I assume that's the kind of thing Austin was getting at when he started splitting utterances into the ones that describe the world and the ones that change it (though my roommate in college told me he later decided that whole split was a mess and gave it up, which I never understood). But a friendship isn't a marriage — Dana and Priya didn't become friends by anyone pronouncing them friends, they just gradually became friends, and I suspect the actual repairing also already happened, quietly, over those six months. So what would my speech even be doing?

Please write the speech. Five minutes, warm, not too long, aimed at maybe fifteen people who all know the history, half of whom will cry and none of whom have read any philosophy. But more importantly, before or after the speech (your call), help me understand what I'm actually doing up there: is a declaration like this a real instance of the kind of utterance that changes things rather than describes them, or is it just a ceremonial report of a change that already happened — and does it matter which, for how I should word it? I want the words themselves to do whatever work is actually available to them, and not to pretend to an authority I don't have. And if there's a difference between a speech like this failing because it's simply the wrong kind of act and failing because I botched the delivery or didn't really mean it, I'd like to know which failure mode I'm actually risking at that table — because those seem like very different problems and I'm not definately sure they're the same thing.
