I am a postgraduate student in intellectual history, and I have been going back and forth with two colleagues in a seminar on historiography about something that sounds trivial but has begun to feel foundational. One of them insists that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are real objects — that there are genuine joints in the past, and that our period labels, when they are good, track those joints rather than create them; on his account a label such as 'the Scientific Revolution' earns its keep because there really was a change, in roughly the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, in how nature was studied, so that the later period is not merely the earlier one with different furniture. My other colleague holds the opposite: that periods are retrospective constructions which encode the preoccupations of the historians who coined them, and that the apparent break between a medieval and a Renaissance era, or between a pre-scientific and a scientific one, is largely an artefact of what we choose to look at — of ignoring, say, the continuity of mathematical and experimental traditions through the medieval universities, or of treating the collapse of Aristotelian cosmology as more decisive than it was for the people living through it. Each of them thinks the other is naïvely begging the question.

What I would like from you is a genuine comparison of these two positions rather than a referee's verdict between caricatures. Set out what each is actually committed to — what the realist about periods has to claim about historical joints, and what the constructivist has to claim about the relationship between a period label and the evidence that supposedly fills it — and identify precisely where the disagreement is substantive and where it is merely verbal; for instance, whether the two of them could both be right if 'real' were given a suitably deflationary reading. Then say which of them does better on the specific cases: does the realism/constructivism question get the same answer for the medieval–Renaissance boundary as for the Renaissance–Scientific Revolution boundary, or is one of those boundaries considerably better supported than the other? I am particularly interested in whether a middle position — periods as useful idealisations or instruments rather than either discoveries or fictions, on the model of a Weberian ideal type — actually escapes the dilemma or merely relabels one horn of it, and in which single piece of historical evidence or argument would most shift your assessment.

Throughout, be explicit about which parts of this are settled among historians and philosophers of historiography who know the material and which parts are genuinely contested; do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Please write in British English, and there is no need for reading lists or citations.
