I teach a second-year undergraduate module in social and political philosophy, and next week's fifty-minute session is on how much rule-following a society should demand. I want to build it around the tight/loose contrast rather than the usual liberty-versus-authority framing. My students have read some Mill and Rawls but no empirical social science. Don't give me the explanation; give me the teaching plan.

Two things the session must get right, and they are the reason I am not simply handing you a topic. First, the descriptive axis is not one dimension: the strength with which a community holds its norms and its tolerance for visible deviance vary independently, so a community can hold its norms strongly while shrugging at private nonconformity, or hold them weakly while punishing anyone who breaks them publicly. Second, once the axis is split that way the normative question splits too: coordination arguments (rules settle which of several workable equilibria we land in), threat arguments (tightness as risk management under danger), and expressive or constitutive arguments (rule-following as the community's way of being itself) each license a different answer, and they can pull apart.

So: give me the order in which the components must come and why each depends on the one before it; the single carrying example the whole session should hang on, which must involve real stakes and a genuine coordination problem, be legible to students with no social science, and not be one of the stock comparisons; the misconceptions I should expect and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off (I predict at least: tight equals authoritarian, loose equals free, and the whole thing collapsing into relativism, where the normative question gets answered by reporting what the society already does); the one question I should ask at the end to see whether the split between norm strength and deviance tolerance has actually landed; and what I should openly decline to cover at this level.

Close by telling me how confident you are in the plan, and which single step in it you are least sure of.
