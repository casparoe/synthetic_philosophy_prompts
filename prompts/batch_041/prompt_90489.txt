I am a graduate student in philosophy preparing a short paper for a seminar on the metaphysics of action, and I would like you to read it as a demanding but constructive referee — someone who knows the killing/letting-die literature thoroughly and has also worked on the metaphysics of causation and on omissions. Before writing any comments, please spend a few lines brainstorming the strongest objections the paper invites, and then organize your remarks around them; I would rather have three objections that could sink the paper than a dozen small suggestions. Please also say, at the end, which single premise is doing the most work, and how the paper would fare if I gave that premise up.

The paper's thesis is that the killing/letting-die distinction should be drawn relative to what I call the agency baseline: the course of events that would have unfolded had the agent been entirely inert — paralyzed, not merely abstaining — at the moment in question. An act, on this view, is a departure from that baseline; an omission is a continuation of it. The familiar contrast cases come out as one would hope: the doctor who switches off the ventilator departs from the baseline and kills, while the one who never starts it does not, and merely lets die. The account is deliberately mechanism-relative, treating the classification as a fact about what the world would have done on its own rather than about which muscles moved. To test it, I have built the paper around a case I call the inverted pump. In a neonatal unit, the standard infusion pump is replaced by a fail-open model: unless a nurse keeps a lever held down, the drug runs. Nothing else changes — same drug, same dose, same infant, same intentions on the nurse's part. Under the old mechanism, a nurse who wanted the infant to die had only to walk away, and we would say she let the infant die. Under the new one, she must actively hold the lever down for as long as she wants the drug withheld, and the moment she releases it she is doing nothing with her hand while the drug flows and the infant dies. My claim is that the agency baseline keeps the verdict stable across the two mechanisms — she lets die in both cases, since death is precisely what the baseline delivers — and that this stability counts in the account's favour rather than against it, even though I concede that the phenomenology, and probably most people's intuitions, flip. The second half of the paper argues that the residual discomfort is best explained by the fact that we ordinarily read the baseline off social and mechanical convention rather than off counterfactual inertia, and that this explains something the standard accounts do not: why clinicians who insist that withholding and withdrawing are morally on a par nonetheless describe them differently, and why that difference tracks what the doctor's body would be doing at the moment of death rather than any difference in outcome.

I would like your comments on three things in particular. First, accuracy: is the agency baseline a coherent notion, or does it smuggle in a choice of baseline that is doing the moral work under another name — in particular, is the relevant counterfactual the world with the agent inert, the world with the agent absent, or the world with the agent inert but having performed whatever preparatory acts she in fact performed? Second, whether the inverted pump really is a case where the classification should stay fixed, or whether the intuition that it flips is a datum the account must accommodate rather than explain away. Third, interest: is there anything here that a reader who already knows the standard literature would find new, or am I re-labelling a familiar position? If the latter, tell me which familiar position it collapses into, and what the shortest route to a genuinely different one would be.
