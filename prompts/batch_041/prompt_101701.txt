Before I settle on any verdict about whether destroying a habitat is on balance good or bad for the wild animals that live in it, I want the specification first: what must a theory of ecosystem-level welfare be able to do in order to count as adequate? Write me the desiderata — the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the empirical results it must not contradict — with the consideration behind each requirement. Treat the following as raw material to be sorted into genuine requirements and mere prejudices of one camp, and add anything the list omits.

1. Aggregation across dissimilar lives. The individuals to be summed differ by orders of magnitude in number, lifespan, and welfare level. Many species are r-strategists: enormous numbers of offspring, the great majority of which die young, frequently by starvation or predation. Others are K-strategists: few individuals, long lives, extended parental care. The theory must say how to aggregate across these, and whether a life that is short and ends badly is a benefit to be counted or a cost to be booked.

2. Endogenous populations. Destroying a habitat does not merely alter the welfare of a fixed set of animals; it changes how many animals there are and of which kinds. The comparison is therefore between two different populations rather than two states of one population, and the theory must specify the baseline against which the comparison is made — the individuals now alive, the individuals that would otherwise have come to exist, or some rate per unit of habitat.

3. Totals versus averages. Maximizing total welfare and maximizing average welfare yield opposite recommendations here, since the very interventions that multiply lives may lower the average quality of those lives. The theory must choose between them, or argue that the distinction lapses in this setting.

4. Death versus suffering. It must say whether death is itself a harm to the animal that dies, or whether only the suffering contained within a life counts, and how the two are weighed when a habitat change reduces suffering while raising mortality, or the reverse.

5. Non-naturalism. It must not treat a state as better simply because it is undisturbed or natural.

6. Determinacy and action-guidance. It must be usable to rank actual interventions, which requires it to return verdicts under the severe uncertainty that attaches to wild-animal welfare.

7. Empirical fidelity. It must be consistent with the broad facts of wild population biology — high juvenile mortality, predation as a principal cause of death, the dependence of population size on habitat — and it must not require the welfare of a population to be read off from anything we could not in principle measure.

Two things I want most from you. First, flag any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the conflict; if the list turns out to be internally inconsistent, that is itself the result I am after. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and say what it excludes. I do not need a scorecard of named positions — keep the weight on the criteria and on the cases that motivate them.
