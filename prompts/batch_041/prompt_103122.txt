For the past term I have been caught between two of my teachers, and I would like your help writing the document they should have produced instead of continuing to argue. My biology teacher holds that species are real, mind-independent divisions in nature that our classifications discover; my philosophy teacher holds that species are human-made conveniences imposed on a world that is continuous, and treats the fact that no single definition of 'species' covers every organism as proof of this. Their exchanges keep going in circles, and I suspect that at least part of their disagreement is about words rather than about nature.

Please write the joint statement they could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I would like it organised as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: everything both sides can affirm without hedging, including about what biological classification is actually doing, about what does and does not follow from the absence of a single definition that fits all organisms, and about what kind of evidence would count for or against each side. Please make these substantive; I do not want truce sentences such as 'species are complicated'.

Second, a section setting out the remaining disagreements, each stated in wording that the side in question would accept as a fair account of its own position. Where a disagreement turns out to be verbal — a matter of what 'real' or 'natural' is being made to mean — say so, and separate the verbal residue from the substantive dispute.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a shared answer would actually move at least one side. For each, identify the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it.

Fourth, a brief section on whether the two are even disagreeing about the same question, and on whether the realist and anti-realist labels I have used misdescribe either position.

To keep the cruxes concrete, please work through at least these cases, described generically rather than by name, with each side's prediction written down before the evidence is consulted:

(a) populations arranged in a ring around a geographic barrier, in which neighbouring populations interbreed freely, but the two ends of the ring do not interbreed where they meet, even though each interbreeds with the populations between them;

(b) organisms that reproduce without sex, so that the interbreeding test cannot be applied to them at all, yet which fall into stable, recognisable clusters that occupy different niches, evolve separately, and persist over time;

(c) a long fossil sequence showing gradual change with no natural break anywhere in it, so that any line drawn between 'before' and 'after' appears to be the classifier's doing rather than nature's.

I do not need citations, names, or a reading list; please state the arguments in their own terms and keep the document self-contained.
