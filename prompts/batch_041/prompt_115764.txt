I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about theories that look wrong now but are said to be right in the long run. The case that started this was Malthus: the prediction that population growth tends to outrun food supply, which has not happened in the way or on the timetable many people expected, yet which is often defended by saying the mechanism was real but delayed or offset. I can see the appeal of that defence, but I also see how easily it becomes a way of never being wrong. I want a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures my verdicts below. I have been calling the missing ingredient temporal slack, though I do not know whether that is a real term. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want the underlying criterion, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases.

My verdicts:

1. A theory says that if a codebase grows without architectural investment, deployment frequency will fall. Our deployment frequency did fall, but only after we deliberately stopped investing in tooling. I think this confirms the theory, because the mechanism was there and we interfered with it. It does not feel like a rescue to me.

2. A theory says that any sufficiently large microservice system will eventually develop distributed transactions. Ours has not, ten years in. Proponents say it is early. I think the theory is not yet refuted, but it is also barely confirmed, and it should be able to say what we would see in the meantime if it were true.

3. A theory says that technical debt will force a rewrite within five years. Eight years have passed and there has been no rewrite. The author now says the prediction was always about the long run. I think the original prediction was refuted, and the revised version is a different and weaker theory.

4. A theory says that a system will eventually collapse under load, but gives no time frame, no threshold, no precursor, and no possible observation that would count against it. I think this is not a scientific claim at all.

5. A theory predicts a trend, but the trend is only visible when you aggregate over decades; in every individual year the prediction is wrong. I think that is suspicious unless the aggregation was specified in advance.

6. A theory's short-run prediction fails, but it is modified to add a new feedback mechanism; the modified theory then predicts a novel short-run effect, which is observed. I think the modification is legitimate.

7. A theory's short-run prediction fails, and it is modified only by adding a caveat that the effect is delayed; no new prediction follows. I think that is ad hoc.

8. In the Malthus case, the predicted crisis has not occurred globally, but the mechanism may have been offset by agricultural and demographic changes. I think Malthus was not simply wrong, but I am not comfortable saying the theory was right either.

I keep going back and forth on a case like this: a colleague says our current architecture will eventually cause an outage that forces a rewrite. It has not happened for six years. He says we are just early. Is that a legitimate long-run prediction or a rescue? And what is the difference between that and case 1, where I think the mechanism was suppressed rather than absent?

Please adjudicate: give me the principle, or the competing principles, that best fit the verdicts above. For each principle, tell me which cases motivate each clause, what it predicts about a case I have not listed (for example, a prediction that a particular programming language will die out, where its share is falling but it has not died), and which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If a principle saves every verdict only by becoming too complicated, say so and tell me which verdict I should give up instead. I am particularly interested in whether there is a principled way to distinguish a theory that is right but early from a theory that has simply been rescued from refutation, and in what a long-run theory owes us in the meantime. Please answer in British English.
