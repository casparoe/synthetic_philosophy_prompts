I'm putting together a criminology-and-philosophy seminar and I keep tripping over the same thing. The law insists it punishes acts, not people — you can't argue 'he's the sort of man who'd do that', you have to prove what he actually did. But then sentencing and parole are nothing but character assessment: the pre-sentence report is a portrait of the person, the judge weighs remorse and good character, the parole board asks whether he's changed and whether he's still a risk. All of which seems to assume that people have traits that are stable, that carry across situations, and that predict what they'll do next. Which is more or less what the situationist stuff denies — the experiments where being in a hurry, or finding a coin in the phone box, does more work than any disposition. So what theory of character, or of persons, does the sentencing-and-parole practice actually presuppose? Is it stuck with global traits, or could it get by with something weaker, like traits that are real but local (careless with the tax office, scrupulous with his mates), or dispositions that are only stable relative to a situation? Is the 'we punish acts, not persons' line genuinely in tension with it, or do the two fit together? And if we took the situationist point seriously, what would concretely have to change in how judges sentence and boards release people — or is the practice fine as it is, because predicting behaviour only ever needed base rates rather than character? Dont sit on the fence at the end, I want a verdict. British English please.
