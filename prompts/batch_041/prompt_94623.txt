OK so this is a wierd one and I'm mildly embarrassed to be asking it, but I've been chewing on it for a week and I teach Kant for a living — this is not my area.

A letter turned up at my house on Tuesday. Typed, no return address, a bank account number at the bottom, and one sentence: "Exactly one of the following is true: you will send me $500, or your house already has a cracked foundation that would cost about $40,000 to put right." That's the whole letter. No threat, no deadline, nothing about telling anyone anything. And here's what makes it more than a nuisance: the man apparently sends these out often, and as far as anyone has ever been able to check, every sentence he sends is true. He does not go around cracking foundations.

My friend Dan, who has read a worrying amount of the decision theory literature, says I should pay, no question. His argument, as best I can follow it: I can't do anything about whether my foundation is cracked, sure, but look — if I pay, then the first half of the sentence is true, so for the whole sentence to be true the second half has to be false, i.e. my foundation is fine. If I don't pay, then the second half has to be true, i.e. my foundation is cracked. So the choice isn't "pay $500" versus "keep $500", it's "pay $500 and learn you're fine" versus "keep $500 and you're staring down forty grand of underpinning". He says it's the cheapest bit of evidence I'll ever buy. I told him that's the daftest thing I've heard all year, that handing a stranger $500 doesn't reach down into my soil and un-crack my foundation. He said yes, obviously, but that that isn't what his argument needs. And here's the part I can't shake: the sender's clearly not going to waste a stamp on someone who'd bin the letter and doesn't have a problem — that would make his sentence false. So whether I got a letter at all seems to depend on what I'm going to do with it, which sounds like the same point again, or maybe a different one, and I've lost the thread.

Three things, then. (a) Is Dan's argument a real argument, or a scam wearing a lab coat? (b) Does the answer change if it's $5 rather than $500, or if the sender is only right, say, 60% of the time instead of always? (c) Is there a name for this shape of problem, so I can stop feeling like I'm losing my mind over lunch?

And one request: when you explain it, please seperate the parts that are settled — the bits where anyone who actually works on this would agree — from the parts that are genuinely contested among people who know the area, and don't serve me the confident-sounding version of a live disagreement. I want to know where the real fights are, not just which side sounds slickest.
