I have been reading my way through Carnap's later meta-philosophy, and I keep tripping over what looks like a fault line running through it. On one side is the machinery he is best known for: the Principle of Tolerance, the idea that the choice of a linguistic or conceptual framework is not a matter of truth but a practical decision, and the related distinction between questions that arise inside a framework and the external question of whether to adopt the framework at all — a decision, he says, to be weighed by pragmatic considerations such as fruitfulness, efficiency, and simplicity, with the tolerant attitude that no framework is to be prohibited in advance. On the other side is the socialism: Carnap's own left-wing commitments, his journalism for socialist papers, his lifelong pacifism, and, around him in the Vienna Circle, Neurath's insistence that the scientific world-conception is not a neutral intellectual pastime but an instrument for the rational reconstruction of society — for planning, in the broad sense, against tradition, myth, and charismatic authority. The trouble is that these two strands seem to pull in opposite directions: the first tells me that at the level of framework choice there is no truth of the matter and no framework may be ruled out in advance, while the second plainly regards some ways of organizing society as better than others and treats the choice among them as something more than an unargued preference.

What I want is a careful explanation of how far the two can be reconciled — not a survey of who said what, but a reconstruction of the strongest version of Carnap's position. Specifically: on Carnap's own taxonomy, is the question whether to organize society through comprehensive planning an internal question, an external question, or neither, and does the answer turn on how narrowly or broadly one draws the internal/external line? What exactly is a practical decision supposed to be for him — irrational, arational, or rational but simply not truth-apt — and can such a decision be defended as better or worse without covertly smuggling back a truth claim, given that the pragmatic criteria he lists are themselves contestable? Does the Principle of Tolerance, as he formulates it, extend to political frameworks at all, and if it does, does he have any non-arbitrary way to keep the tolerance while still ruling out an authoritarian or fascist order, or does the tolerance fall silent exactly where his politics most needs it? And where Carnap and Neurath genuinely diverge — over how far the scientific world-conception can be turned into a political instrument, or over how much the choice of a framework may be guided by explicitly social aims — does that divergence track a real philosophical disagreement about planning, or is it only a difference of temperament and emphasis?

I would like you to close by saying where the reconstruction has to strain or give up one of Carnap's own commitments, and to be explicit about any point at which you think the tension is merely apparent rather than real. Please write the answer in American English.
