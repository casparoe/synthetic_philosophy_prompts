My supervisor has spent her career on the interwar planning controversies, and our working group has accumulated a settled record of her verdicts on cases we have argued through. I need to predict what she will say about a new one, and I would rather have the argument than the answer.

Her verdicts so far. On a fully command economy that fixes all prices administratively and forbids private trade in producer goods, she says the planners cannot calculate at all, and that the failure is not one of effort, honesty, or computing power but of what a price is. On Lange-style market socialism — a central board setting prices by trial and error to clear markets, managers instructed to minimize cost and price at marginal cost, free consumer choice and free choice of occupation, public ownership of capital — she says the scheme is coherent for consumer goods and labor and that Lange largely wins there, but that it quietly presupposes something it never supplies, namely a way to value capital goods and to rank investment projects. On worker cooperatives competing in ordinary markets, she says there is no calculation problem at all, and that the absence of capitalists is epistemically irrelevant. On internal transfer pricing inside a large multidivisional firm, where divisions trade at negotiated or shadow prices, she says this works, and that a firm is already a small planned economy surviving only because the market prices what it buys and what it sells at its boundary. On cap-and-trade schemes and spectrum auctions, she says the state has extended the price system rather than replaced it. On wartime command economies that hit ambitious output targets, she says the calculation problem is masked rather than solved whenever the ends are few, fixed, and ranked by a single authority, and that the masking is what makes people believe planning worked. On prediction markets, she says they aggregate dispersed belief well, and that this shows the epistemic argument was never about centralization as such.

The new case, which is why I am writing. A mid-sized state has built what its planners call a computational plan. Every transaction in the formal economy runs through a single digital payments and invoicing layer, so the planning authority observes quantities, prices, inventories, delivery times, and contract terms in near real time. A large learned model, trained on that stream and on a decade of history, emits a price vector covering consumer, intermediate, and capital goods, refreshed daily, together with shadow prices for proposed investment projects. Consumers choose freely; workers choose occupations; firms remain publicly owned, but managers are paid on a formula tied to the model's shadow profitability. Two features define the case. Prices are set by the model rather than by exchange. And no firm may introduce a new product line or open a new plant without the authority's approval, which is granted according to the model's ranking of projects.

What I want: your best guess at her verdict, and the candidate rules her seven verdicts are jointly consistent with — I can see at least three, and I suspect the one I would naturally attribute to her is not the only fit. For each rule, say what it predicts here; say whether the seven verdicts force any of them; and name the modification to the new case that would separate them, identifying which pair of rules it separates and why. Tell me which rule you think she is actually running, and what evidence in the record would change your mind.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question.
