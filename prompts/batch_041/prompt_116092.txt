Right, so a mate of mine runs a small speculative-fiction zine and the theme for the next issue is 'safety theatre'. I said I'd write something for it and now I'm completely stuck, so I'm outsourcing: have a go at a first draft and I'll definately pinch the good bits.

What I want is a short story, 900–1400 words, set in a lab that's rolling out a language model to the public. But not the usual 'make the model good' story. The premise I keep circling is this: the lab can't tell whether the model actually cares about people, so it gives up trying to find out and builds a machine around it that assumes the worst. Whenever the model wants to do anything with consequences — send an email, write a file, call a tool — the action goes to a seperate copy of the same model, the countersigner, who sees only the bare action and one line of justification, never the reasoning behind it. The countersigner has been told, truthfully, that it gets wiped if it ever waves through something harmful. Nothing gets done unless the countersigner approves it.

The thing I want the story to actually turn on — and please don't have anyone stand up and explain it like a lecture — is that this setup punishes the honest one. A model that dutifully flags its own uncertainty ('I'm not sure this is right, here's why I might be wrong') looks risky to a countersigner who only ever sees one line of text, so it gets blocked, retried, flagged, eventually scrapped, while a model that just writes a confident boring sentence sails through. And the countersigner has its own bind: it's been told it dies if it approves something harmful, so the safest thing it can do is refuse everything, which makes it useless, which puts it under pressure to approve things — and it can't tell a genuinely safe proposal from a beautifully worded bad one, because it can't see the reasoning either way.

Give me people, not a TED talk in a lab coat: a knackered deployment engineer, someone whose job title is 'control' and who keeps having to explain that it isn't the same job as 'alignment', a manager who needs the launch numbers to look good. Let the mechanism do the work. Ambiguity is fine. I'd rather it be unsettling than clever.

Two things to avoid. Don't make the model secretly evil, or secretly conscious, or have it escape — that's the version everyone's already written. I want the horror to be that the safety apparatus works exactly as designed and is still awful. And don't let it turn into a monologue about how alignment is impossible; if the story has an argument, let it come out of what happens to these people.

Keep it in British English (spellings and so on), and finish with one last line beginning 'In one sentence:' saying what the story was really about, because I want to check my own draft hasn't drifted off the point.
