I'm a graduate student in cognitive science, and I've been arguing with a labmate about what our experimental apparatus actually licenses us to say. The practice I'm worried about pervades the field: when we want to know *when* a subject became conscious of a stimulus, we present the stimulus, record a late neural component — a P300 around three hundred milliseconds, or whatever our favorite "neural correlate of consciousness" happens to be on that day — and then we write down "stimulus onset plus latency" as the moment of awareness. We build timelines this way, we compare conditions this way, and at least one company has pitched the same logic to courts as a kind of lie detector, treating a late brain response to a crime detail as proof that the defendant consciously recognized it. The whole enterprise seems to assume that a conscious experience has a definite onset, that this onset can be read off a neural event, and that the neural event and the experience stand in a clean one-to-one temporal relation. I want you to tell me what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes.

Here is what is eating at me. There is a well-known visual effect in which a red dot is flashed on the left and a green dot on the right, close together in time, and subjects report seeing a single dot travel across the gap and change color in mid-flight — even though the green color was never on the screen until the dot had, by their own account, already changed. Since all we ever have is the retrospective report, there are two ways to tell the story. On the 'Orwellian' story, the subject really did experience something first — a red dot, then a green one — and the memory of it was quietly rewritten afterward, so that the remembered experience contains a color change the actual experience never had. On the 'Stalinesque' story, the brain simply waited, collected both flashes, and then presented a single already-coherent experience in which the color change is genuinely part of what was experienced, only later than the stimulus. Both stories predict exactly the same report, so the report cannot tell them apart. My worry is that our timestamping practice quietly presupposes the answer to this puzzle in advance.

So what I want from you is this: reconstruct the implicit theory of mind and of time that the timestamping practice needs, and separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Does the practice require that experience have a determinate onset that a neural latency can date — and if so, which of the two stories, Orwellian or Stalinesque, does that commit us to, or does the practice secretly rule out both? Is its reliance on a single neural event even coherent, given that the color-phi case shows a content can be reported as occurring before the information needed to produce it was available? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — what could it keep doing even if we abandoned the picture of a timestamped experience? Finally, tell me what would actually have to change in the lab and in the courtroom if we adopted the Stalinesque reading rather than the Orwellian one, or if we concluded that the distinction is empty and that there is no fact of the matter about when the experience happened.
