I am a doctoral candidate in epistemology and I co-host a small departmental podcast; next month's guest is a philosopher who has just published a book defending a tracking-style account of knowledge in the tradition of Nozick. She accepts the core sensitivity condition (roughly: if the proposition were false, the subject would not believe it), but her version adds two amendments. First, the tracking conditions are relativised to a method, and the method is individuated by causal salience — the method is the process the subject would have used had the salient features of the situation been different. Second, the tracking must be sustained by a body of evidence that the subject herself has no reason to doubt, which she calls a no-undercutting-defeat clause. She advertises the package as preserving the anti-skeptical payoff of tracking (ordinary knowledge survives, and we still do not know we are not brains in vats) while repairing the counterexamples that sink the unamended view.

I want a set of interview questions that make her defend the machinery rather than restate the headline; the audience is mostly graduate students, and the last tracking theorist we had on spent the whole hour on the headline. Please draft twelve questions, organised into the named sections below, in that order, at roughly the lengths indicated. No citations or further reading are needed — just the questions and their follow-ups. After every question except the closing one, add two labelled lines: Anticipated reply (one or two sentences, giving the answer she is most likely to give) and Follow-up (one sentence that makes that reply harder to use). Press only where the literature genuinely presses: method individuation, closure, vacuous tracking, lotteries, and defeat.

Section 1 — Introduction script (about 70 words). A short spoken paragraph I can read to open the episode, stating her position plainly and without endorsing it.

Section 2 — Method individuation, and sensitivity versus safety (three questions, about 250 words). Press the causal-salience clause: how it escapes the narrow-method trivialisation, in which sensitivity comes cheap because the method is described so finely that no false belief would have been formed by it, and the opposite worry that it simply inherits the generality problem; whether the salience clause does any work that a plain reliabilist condition would not; and why she wants sensitivity at all rather than the safety condition her rivals prefer, given the cases where the two verdicts come apart.

Section 3 — Closure and the abominable conjunction (three questions, about 250 words). Her view, like any sensitivity theory, has to deny that knowledge is closed under known entailment. Ask her to state the denial in its sharpest form — that she knows she has hands while not knowing that she is not a handless brain in a vat — and to say why that is not a reductio; ask what she tells a student who finds the denial worse than the scepticism it was meant to answer; and ask whether denying closure is a consequence she chose or a price she pays.

Section 4 — Necessary truths and vacuous tracking (two questions, about 160 words). For a necessary truth the antecedent of the sensitivity conditional is unsatisfiable, so the condition appears to be met vacuously, and the account threatens to make mathematical and logical knowledge either free or impossible. Ask how the amendments handle that, and whether vacuous satisfaction of a tracking condition is a respectable result or a sign that the condition has the wrong shape for the a priori.

Section 5 — Lotteries and the necessity of sensitivity (two questions, about 160 words). Ask first how the account treats lottery propositions: the belief that one's ticket will lose arguably satisfies the tracking conditions under the most natural reading of the counterfactual, yet we are widely held not to know that our ticket will lose — so does she accept the result, and if not, which condition is meant to fail? Then ask about a grandmother who firmly believes her grandchild is well, is right, but would have believed it just the same had the child fallen ill, a case many read as knowledge without sensitivity, and whether her amendments save sensitivity's necessity or force her to retreat to a sufficiency claim.

Section 6 — The defeat clause (one question, about 90 words). Ask whether the no-undercutting-defeat clause does independent explanatory work or only patches the cases the tracking conditions get wrong, and whether adding it collapses her view into a general defeasibility theory in which the tracking apparatus is idle.

Section 7 — Closing (one question, about 70 words). Ask what would count as decisive evidence against the tracking core — an observation or an argument — and which of the three components she would give up first if one had to go.
