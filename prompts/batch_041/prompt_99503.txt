I'm writing a piece on the move philosophers pull constantly and mostly get away with -- treating what we can imagine as evidence for what could be the case. Before I lean on it in print I want the spec first: what would a theory of that move have to deliver to be any good for a general reader? Give me the desiderata, with the case behind each -- the cases it has to get right (the zombie intuition; the water-isnt-H2O case, where the conceiving feels fine but the possibility isn't really there; a mathematician who swears she can picture a counterexample to a conjecture she cant prove and turns out wrong), the distinctions it cant collapse (conceivable vs imaginable vs merely not self-contradictory; epistemic vs metaphysical possibility; my surface-level imagining vs whatever idealized version you'd get with unlimited information), and the results it cant contradict (that some a posteriori identities are necessary; that what we can imagine is partly a product of what we were taught). Flag any pair of requirements you dont think can be met together -- I suspect there's at least one -- and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular versions. One extra case I want in the list: someone who grew up somewhere a certain way of arranging family life was unthinkable and now finds it easy to picture. Does that shift count as evidence about whats possible, or just about her? If the theory can't answer, say so. Skip the scorecard, I'll do that part myself.
