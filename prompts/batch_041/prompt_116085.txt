Write me a fake historical document: the founding prospectus of a Victorian society for psychical research — something like "Objects, Methods, and Schedule of Work" — around 650 words, in the deadpan voice of its secretary, laid out as numbered clauses or a schedule of items. Background you can take as given: Henry Sidgwick helped found the Society for Psychical Research and was one of its presidents, and one thing driving him was that his own ethics bottomed out in a standoff between living for yourself and living for the general good, and he thought that if you could get genuine evidence of survival after death, the standoff might dissolve — the two standpoints might turn out to coincide somewhere further along. The piece should satirise that move: treating a philosphical impasse as an empirical question that a research programme with a budget and a timetable might eventually close out. Don't sneer at the believers. The funniest version is written by people who are entirely serious, methodologically scrupulous, and dimly aware that the whole enterprise may be a category error, and who carry on regardless.

Some things to build in, so it doesn't come out generic:

- an invented "Ladder of Evidence" with maybe five rungs, from coincidence at the bottom to repeated, independent, cross-checked communications containing verifiable information that no living person could have
- a rule stating which rung, and only which rung, may be used to settle what the committee keeps calling the practical question
- a subcommittee tasked with sorting messages that arrive in fragments across several mediums
- a note on a donor who will only fund work described in his own preferred phrase, the reconciliation of duty and interest, which the secretary finds a bit embarrassing
- a clause on what becomes of the society's conclusions if the evidence simply never comes in, and whether the moral question can be left hanging that long

Use only invented names for the society and its officers. You can mention Sidgwick in passing, but don't put words in his mouth, and don't quote anyone real — paraphrase if you need a view in play. It should be funny to someone who's read the Methods of Ethics and followable by someone who hasn't, and it should never explain its own joke.
