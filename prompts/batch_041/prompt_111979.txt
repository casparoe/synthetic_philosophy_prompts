I am an undergraduate taking a course on consciousness whose final unit covers non-Western treatments of the self, and I have spent the term reading Schrödinger's late philosophical essays alongside the Advaita Vedanta material on the syllabus. I have come away genuinely persuaded rather than merely interested, and I would like to state the position plainly and then have it attacked.

The position is this: there is exactly one subject of experience, and the plurality of minds is not a plurality of things that experience but a set of limits on what any one stream of experience can reach. When I say that you and I are two, I am reporting a fact about the boundaries of memory, attention and report, not a fact about how many experiencers there are. The apparent multiplicity is, as the tradition says, an appearance. I take this to be a claim about the world, not a metaphor and not a devotional formula.

I want to know what would count against it. I am asking for the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for the reasons the ranking goes that way. Please include the cases that look empirically relevant — surgical disconnection of the hemispheres, dissociative conditions, dreamless sleep and general anaesthesia — and say, for each, whether it tells against the numerical oneness of the subject or only against the unity of a single mind's access, which is a much weaker claim. Then the conceptual objections: that the position is on one reading a tautology about the first-person pronoun and on another a substantive claim no possible experience could disconfirm; that it collapses into a rival, whether neutral monism, or a bundle theory that likewise denies the ego but without any single substratum, or the combination problem run backwards, where the difficulty is division rather than combination. Tell me which of these my own side would concede as counting against it rather than wave away, and what the view predicts that its rivals do not. If the honest verdict is that it risks nothing, say so, and say whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind or simply the price of its being a claim about the subject rather than an object in the world. Finally, what follows for the reasons we have to care about one another's suffering if there is only one of us who suffers? I would rather be told that the position is well insulated than be handed a defence of it.
