(a) Set out the kind of case Frankfurt uses, and say exactly what it is meant to show. [8]

And here's what one of them wrote:

The case is meant to show that a person can be morally responsible for what he has done even though he could not have done otherwise, and so that PAP — the principle that someone is morally responsible for what he has done only if he could have done otherwise — is false.

The case runs like this. Jones deliberates about whether to do X and decides, for reasons of his own, to do it. Unbeknownst to him, Black has been monitoring his deliberation and has arranged a mechanism which would have ensured that Jones decided to do X had Jones been about to decide against it. Black never has to lift a finger: Jones decides on his own. So Jones could not have done otherwise. Yet we don't think he is any less responsible. And the reason we don't is that the actual sequence of events — Jones weighing up his reasons and deciding — is exactly what it would have been had Black never existed. Since the absence of any alternative makes no difference to the actual sequence, it makes no difference to his responsibility.

It's worth noticing that the case doesn't assume that determinism is true, nor that Jones's decision is uncaused. The mechanism sits in the counterfactual branch and never fires, which is what makes the example useful: it isolates the lack of alternatives from any question about causation. It's a case of overdetermination, with Black's device as a redundant backup cause.

Some incompatibilists reply that Jones retains a flicker of freedom: even if he could not have done otherwise, he could have formed a different intention, or tried to do otherwise, and that is enough to satisfy PAP. The standard response is that a flicker this thin cannot be what responsibility rests on — if the only thing Jones could have done differently is form an intention that would instantly have been overridden, it's hard to see how that could be the thing that makes him responsible. The other main objection is that the case either assumes determinism, in which case it begs the question, or it leaves Jones a genuine alternative, in which case it doesn't show what it claims. But this seems to me a false dilemma: the mechanism never firing means the case is neutral about determinism, so it can't be begging the question against the incompatibilist.

What follows for PAP is that it is false as stated, and with it goes the incompatibilist's central objection: if responsibility doesn't require the ability to do otherwise, then the fact that a deterministic world would leave us no alternatives cannot by itself threaten it. So determinism is no threat to moral responsibility.

(c) Yes. Free will is the freedom that moral responsibility presupposes; if responsibility survives the absence of alternatives, then so does free will. Anyone who insists that free will requires the ability to do otherwise is just restating PAP in different words.
