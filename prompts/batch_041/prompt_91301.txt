I teach a mid-level undergraduate survey in ancient philosophy, and I am writing the final examination. I want a single long essay question on Aristotle's treatment of money and exchange — but designed so that a student cannot get far by reciting the familiar contrast between household management and wealth-getting, or by repeating that Aristotle regarded money as a conventional measure and usury as unnatural.

The question should force students into a tension that the standard summaries smooth over. As I reconstruct it: Aristotle treats money as existing by convention and as a measure of need, which is precisely what lets otherwise incommensurable goods be exchanged in proportionate reciprocity; and yet he treats the "breeding" of money — interest, and more broadly the pursuit of wealth for its own sake — as unnatural and, worse, unlimited. The puzzle I want students to confront is whether the objection to usury can rest on the supposed barrenness of money, given that money's value is itself conventional and so not a natural fact about the thing; or whether the objection must instead rest on a claim about the end of acquisition, in which case the real target is the unlimitedness of the desire for wealth rather than anything about money's nature. A good answer has to see that these two strands pull in different directions, and that the first is in danger of being undercut by Aristotle's own conventionalism about money.

To keep students off slogans, build the question around the following case, which I will state inside the question itself: a lending arrangement that does charge interest, but where the lender's total income is capped so that it can never exceed what is required to sustain the lender's own household — an interest-bearing loan whose end is limited to household need rather than endless accumulation. Students must say whether Aristotle would have to condemn such an arrangement, and whether he can consistently condemn the form while approving the end, or whether his critique only works if the end is unlimited. I also want them to say something about what the measure-of-need doctrine commits him to: if need (chreia) is the common measure, is Aristotle already committed to a subjective or demand-based account of value, and does that help or hurt the usury argument?

Please produce three things:

1. The essay question itself, phrased for the exam paper, incorporating the case above and clear about what the student is being asked to decide. Assume a 90-minute essay, so the question should be demanding but not sprawling.

2. A model answer of roughly 700 words. It should reach a definite verdict on the capped-interest case while showing the reader why the verdict is not obvious. In the model answer, be explicit about which interpretive claims are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on Aristotle — do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.

3. A marking scheme worth 30 marks, built around five must-hit points, each with its mark value. For each point, add a one-line note on the specific mistake that should cost a student the marks, and state clearly which single move separates an A answer from a B answer.

Assume the students may use their own notes but have no internet access, and that the answer must stand on reasoning from Aristotle's texts and doctrines rather than on secondary citations, so do not require them to name or quote any modern commentator.
