I run a fortnightly reading group for ML engineers — mostly people who've shipped models, one or two with a math PhD, but the median member's formal-logic background stops somewhere around undergrad discrete math. They know what a formal proof is, they've absorbed the pop version of Gödel, and that's about it. Next session I've been handed the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents — the thing where an agent that wants to justify trusting its own reasoning (or its own proof-checker, or its own successor) runs into the fact that it can't derive its own soundness without already having it. I want you to plan how I should teach this, not teach it to me. In particular I care about the dependency order: what has to be in place before the obstacle even reads as a problem rather than as a trick, and why each piece can't be moved earlier. The pieces I think are in play are: the distinction between a system and a system's own provability predicate; Gödel's second incompleteness theorem; the general shape of Löb's theorem; and the collapse of the tempting self-trust rule. But I dont trust my own ordering — tell me what actually depends on what, and where I'd be wrong to front-load Gödel's second theorem because it's the bit they've all heard of.

The carrying example: I was going to build the whole session around a self-modifying agent that's only allowed to rewrite its own code when it can prove the rewrite is an improvement — the safety rule that looks like a real filter. My worry is that this example is doing too much work and will make them think the obstacle is about self-improvement specifically, or worse, about compute limits, when it isn't: a prover with infinite time and unbounded memory hits the same wall. I have a rival candidate — an organisation that builds a proof-checking system and then wants the system itself to certify that its checker is sound — but I suspect that one smuggles in a whole theory of institutional trust. Pick whichever carries the idea, say what the example must have and what it must conspicuously lack, and say explicitly why the obvious first instinct (just have the agent prove it will never make a mistake) is the wrong thing to reach for. Also: I've seen people present this as a straightforward corollary of Gödel's second theorem, and people present it as a much stranger, almost paradoxical result about the provability predicate eating itself. Tell me which framing is honest at this level.

The misconceptions I'm already bracing for, in the order I expect them to surface: that this is just the incompleteness theorem restated; that the obstacle is a limitation of finite agents; that you can escape it by bolting on an axiom of self-trust; that it's about truth rather than about what a particular system can derive; and that even if the single universal soundness schema is blocked, you can just prove the instances one at a time and get the same effect. For each one, tell me the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off — before the definition, right after the second incompleteness theorem, after the Löb schema, wherever — and what one-sentence defusing move I should use, keeping in mind they have no modal logic and no interest in acquiring any. Then give me one question to ask at the end that would actually show whether the obstacle has landed, rather than whether they can recite the theorem; something where a person who's only memorised the statement will confidently give the wrong answer.

Format it like this. First an Opening move section, two or three sentences, saying what the very first thing I say should be and what I must not say yet. Then a Dependency chain section as a numbered list, one line per step, each with a short clause on why that step can't come earlier. Then a section called The carrying example, one paragraph, naming the example and the two or three features that make it work. Then Misconception map, as a list, one item per misconception, each with the position in the sequence and the defusing line. Then The check, two or three sentences plus the question itself. Then a final section called Cut list, as bullets, for the things I should openly announce I'm skipping and the one-line reason for each. Keep the whole thing under about nine hundred words, and dont pad it — I have fifty minutes and I'll be talking for maybe half of them.
