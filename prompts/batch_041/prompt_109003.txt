A couple of colleagues at the software company where I work got into a long argument at lunch over the two-envelope puzzle, and since I studied computer science rather than philosophy I couldn't referee it, though I found it genuinely interesting and would like a careful adjudication rather than a hand-wave. Here is how it went, as best I can reconstruct it.

Rachel opened by laying out the puzzle: two envelopes, one holds twice the other, you pick one and open it and find some amount X; the other envelope holds either 2X or X/2, and since it was a coin flip which envelope you took, those two cases are equally likely, so the expected value of switching is (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = 1.25X, which beats X. So you should always switch. But that reasoning works for any amount you might see, so it would also tell you to switch before opening — which is absurd, because the two envelopes are symmetric. She concluded that the puzzle exposes something genuinely broken in expected-value reasoning, and that we should not be so confident that decision theory is consistent. Dev replied that the bug is localized and boring: the setup never told you the other envelope is 2X with probability one-half. Once you have seen X, the live possibilities are the pair (X/2, X), in which you already hold the larger, and the pair (X, 2X), in which you hold the smaller, and those two pairs need not be equally probable — with any sensible prior over the amounts, seeing a large number should make you think you are more likely to be holding the big one, not less. So there is no paradox, just a prior smuggled in and then ignored.

Rachel came back that she did not need any prior to feel the force of the puzzle. She described a version with the probabilities stripped out: whatever you hold, the other envelope either doubles it or halves it; in the doubling case you gain X, in the halving case you lose only X/2, and a gain that exceeds the corresponding loss looks like it favors switching no matter what. She added that she was fairly sure she had once read about someone who constructed an actual probability distribution over the envelope contents under which switching pays off in expectation no matter what number you happen to see, which would show the trouble is not just an artifact of assuming all amounts equally likely. Dev answered that the no-probability version is sleight of hand, because without probabilities there is nothing to compare, and the moment you supply them you are back to talking about a prior. As for the supposed distribution that pays off at every amount, he said it cannot exist: the two envelopes are symmetric, so on average switching must gain exactly nothing, and if switching were favorable at every amount you could possibly observe, the average could not come out to zero — so Rachel must be misremembering, or the construction must rely on a prior that is not a genuine probability distribution at all. Rachel conceded it might be an improper prior, but said that only relocates the puzzle into the foundations of probability: improper priors are used constantly in Bayesian work, and here one of them seems to generate a decision rule that is provably better at every single point, which strikes her as a real foundational problem rather than a bookkeeping error. Dev replied that improper priors yield undefined expected values, which is precisely why you are not entitled to make decisions with them unless a limiting argument justifies it, and that the limiting argument is exactly where the "better at every point" claim collapses; the symmetry, he said, still stands untouched.

I have deliberately reported this without telling you which of them I think is winning, because I genuinely do not know, and I would like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than simply declaring a winner. For each of the substantive moves the two of them make — Rachel's original switching argument, Dev's diagnosis that a prior has been smuggled in, Rachel's probability-free version and her appeal to a distribution that pays off at every amount, Dev's symmetry-and-impossibility reply, and Rachel's rejoinder about improper priors — please say how much of it is correct and where it goes wrong, rate the move (clearly mistaken, partly right, clearly right), and justify each rating in a sentence or two. Then tell me what the actual resolution of the puzzle is, and, importantly, what each of them is missing or getting right that the other does not appreciate; I would like to finish understanding not only who was more nearly correct but why the argument felt so compelling to both of them.
