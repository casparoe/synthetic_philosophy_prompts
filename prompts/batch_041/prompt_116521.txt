I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been unable to settle an argument I overheard between two colleagues. I'd like you to adjudicate it.

The situation. The company we work for depends on a small open-source library, maintained by two volunteers in their spare time and used by thousands of teams. The library's most recent release broke something for us. After a day of debugging we had a workaround, but we never established whether the defect was in the library or in how we were using it. Any engineer anywhere who depends on the library can open a public issue on its tracker unilaterally: nobody has to approve it, and nobody can stop it.

Ravi and Mei argued about whether we should open one.

Ravi: "If this is the library's bug, the maintainers need to know, and the tracker is where they'll see it. If it's our mistake, we've filed one bad issue and cost someone a few minutes. The downside is trivial, so we should file. In the good case — the one where we're right — filing is exactly what a responsible user does."

Mei: "The word doing all the work there is 'we.' You're picturing yourself as the only team in the world with this problem. You aren't. Thousands of teams depend on this library, and every one of them can open an issue at the same moment you do. If you're right and it's the library's bug, one issue is enough: the maintainers will see it and fix it, and yours adds nothing but one more notification. If you're wrong, every team that made the same mistake also files, and two volunteers wake up to a hundred false reports. So being right buys almost nothing and being wrong costs a great deal, and you can't tell in advance which you are. That asymmetry is the whole point."

Ravi: "Two things. First, maintainers don't act on a single report — a lone issue usually gets closed as 'cannot reproduce.' It's the pile that forces attention, so the tenth report isn't redundant; it's part of what makes the first one matter. Second, even if there is a pile, closing duplicates is their job. They chose to maintain the library. I'm not responsible for strangers in other companies deciding how to spend their afternoons."

Mei: "Closing duplicates is unpaid work nobody signed up for, and it's the specific thing that drives volunteers away. On responsibility: you're right that you don't control them, but you do control what you do given them. When you file, you're betting that you're one of the few who will rather than one of the many who might. The more teams are in your position, the worse that bet is, and the more you should assume the issue is already being handled."

Ravi: "But I'm not guessing the way they are. They don't know whether it's the library's fault; I've actually done the debugging. My information is better than the average team's. Doesn't that change the calculation?"

Mei: "Possibly. But that's a different argument from the one you opened with. 'I would file if I were the only one' and 'I know more than the crowd does' are not the same claim, and only the second survives what I said."

Ravi: "And if everyone reasoned the way you do, nothing would ever be filed and real bugs would go unfixed. Someone has to be the one who doesn't defer."

Mei: "I'm not telling you to defer forever. I'm telling you that 'I would do this if I were alone' is not the question, and that you keep answering that question instead of the real one."

That's where I gave up. I genuinely can't tell who has the better of it.

What I'd like from you:

1. Go through the exchange move by move — Ravi's opening, Mei's reply, and each later turn — and say for each whether it holds up and why.
2. Say what each is getting right that the other isn't, and what both are missing about the structure of the situation.
3. Say who is more nearly right overall.

I'm not after advice on how they should communicate, and I don't need citations; I want to know whether the arguments themselves are correct.

Format: give the discussion as prose or as a list, then end with a single final line of exactly the form \answer{...} containing one of the labels Ravi, Mei, or Neither, with nothing after it.
