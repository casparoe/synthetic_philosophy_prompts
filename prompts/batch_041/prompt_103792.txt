I am a professor of philosophy and law, and for several years I have co-taught a graduate seminar on the concept of genocide that pairs the legal instrument with the intellectual history behind it. Every term a student asks some version of the counterfactual I can never answer to my own satisfaction: what would Raphael Lemkin have made of the world that grew up after him? Lemkin died in 1959, so he never saw the ad hoc tribunals of the 1990s, the Rome Statute and the permanent International Criminal Court, the rise of "ethnic cleansing" as a preferred euphemism, the doctrine of the responsibility to protect, or the modern advocacy wars over who is permitted to apply the g-word. I would like you to reason your way to a defensible projection of his views rather than to summarize the secondary literature. To keep the exercise tractable, hold fixed what we know of his own commitments: his two-phase conception of the crime, in which the national pattern of the oppressed group is first destroyed and the oppressor's pattern then imposed; his earlier pairing of "barbarity," the physical destruction of a group, with "vandalism," the assault on its culture and language; his insistence — partly defeated in the 1948 Convention — that political and social groups belong inside the definition; and his understanding of genocide as a process of group-destruction that unfolds through occupation and empire, not merely as an episodic massacre.

Please write a short analytic piece of a few hundred words that projects his stance, and then give me a structured summary I can hand to students. For the structured part, return a table (or an equivalent JSON object) with one row per issue and the following columns: "Issue" (the posthumous development, stated in a few words); "Predicted position" (whether he would have welcomed, resisted, or been ambivalent about it, in one sentence); "Lemkinian basis" (which of his own commitments drives the prediction, named explicitly); "Point of divergence" (where his reaction would likely cut against today's mainstream view); and "Confidence" (high, medium, or low, with a one-line reason). Cover at least these five developments: the creation of a permanent international criminal court; the popularity of "ethnic cleansing" as a substitute label; the jurisprudential expansion of genocide in the 1990s tribunals; the responsibility to protect; and the renewed push to treat cultural destruction as genocide. Do not hedge every row into the same "it is impossible to know" verdict; where the evidence of his commitments is strong, commit.
