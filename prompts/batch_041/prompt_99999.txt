My friend and I argued until two in the morning about the old story of the man who rides from Damascus to Aleppo to escape Death and finds Death waiting for him in Aleppo, and he left me with an argument I cannot shake off. He insists the story is not a parable about fate but a theorem about prediction, and he has dressed it up as a proof. I would like you to tell me where it goes wrong, and what it costs to say so.

Here is the case as he sets it up. You are in Damascus and would strongly prefer not to die tomorrow. There is an oracle in whom you have complete confidence: he has never once been wrong about what a person will do, he knows this about himself, and so do you. This morning he announces publicly that he will spend tomorrow in Aleppo. You may stay in Damascus or travel to Aleppo. You know that if you go to Aleppo you will meet him there, and that if you stay in Damascus he will have been wrong, which cannot happen.

His argument runs as follows.

(1) The oracle has never been wrong, and will not be wrong this time.
(2) If you stay in Damascus, he is wrong. Therefore you will not stay in Damascus.
(3) So you will go to Aleppo, and you will meet him there.
(4) And you can see this now, before you have decided anything: your going to Aleppo is already guaranteed by (1) and (2).
(5) If the outcome is guaranteed before you deliberate, then your deliberating does not bring the outcome about.
(6) So your deliberation is idle. Whether you spend tomorrow weighing reasons or flipping a coin, you will be in Aleppo; there is nothing here for you to decide.
(7) Nothing in this case depends on there being an oracle. Every choice any of us makes is in principle foreseeable from the state of the world before we make it, and so is every consequence of it.
(8) So in every case our deliberation is idle, and the impression that we decide what we do is an illusion.
(9) If that is the conclusion, the argument has proved far too much — it would dissolve not just this story but all practical reasoning — and yet each step looked unobjectionable when he said it.

He treats (1) through (8) as a proof, and says that anyone who balks must be clinging to a magical conception of the will. I cannot accept (8). But I also do not want to surrender (1): I do think you cannot outsmart a predictor who is never wrong, and that in this story the man dies in Aleppo no matter how clever he is. So what I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Which step is the bad one, and why did it look compelling? Is the trouble in the slide from (4) to (5), in the leap at (7), or somewhere I have not noticed? If I keep (1), what exactly am I forced to accept, and is the price of keeping it higher than my friend believes? Set out the options, tell me what each of them costs, and say which one you would take.
