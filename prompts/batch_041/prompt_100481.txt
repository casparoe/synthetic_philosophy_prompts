I convene a graduate seminar on laws of nature, and I want to set an exercise that forces my students past the standard taxonomy and into the places where the Humean and anti-Humean positions actually pinch. I should like you to attempt it.

Suppose David Hume were restored to us and given time to read the contemporary literature on laws of nature. He is then interrogated. I want your best reconstruction of how he would respond — not a summary of what the Humean tradition has since said, but a genuine prediction about Hume himself, defended from his own settled commitments rather than from his reputation.

Address these, in whatever order best serves the argument:

First, the best-system account of lawhood, on which the laws are the theorems (or axioms) of that deductive systematisation of the occurrent facts which best balances simplicity and strength. Would Hume greet this as the natural flowering of his own view, or would he resist the suggestion that the systematisation, rather than the regularities themselves, is what confers lawhood? Press him on whether the balance is discovered or imposed.

Second, Humean supervenience — the thesis that all there is supervenes on the spatiotemporal arrangement of local, intrinsic, point-sized qualities. This is the crux. Entanglement and other features of contemporary physics make it doubtful that the world decomposes into separable local matters of particular fact. Would Hume surrender the supervenience thesis, or relocate the laws into the supervenience base, or dig in? Say which, and why.

Third, the necessitarian programmes that place a real relation of necessitation among universals, and the dispositional-essentialist programmes that locate modality in the essences of properties. Would Hume regard these as genuine discoveries about the world, or as precisely the projection of felt compulsion onto nature that he spent his career dissolving? What would he make of the claim that he simply lacked the conceptual resources to see the necessity that is there?

Fourth, the distinction between laws that govern and laws that merely describe. Where would he place himself, and would he think the distinction marks anything real?

Now a complication you must handle rather than ignore. There is a scholarly reading — the so-called New Hume — on which Hume was a sceptical realist about causal necessity, holding that there are real necessary connections in nature to which our idea of necessity is an inadequate response, even if we cannot know them. If that reading is right, my prediction exercise changes shape. Tell me how, and whether the evidence for it should move your verdict.

Throughout, distinguish what is extrapolation from Hume's actual commitments — his empiricism, his account of custom and habit, his denial that we perceive any necessary connexion, his separability intuitions — from what is invention on your part. Flag at least two or three questions on which his texts genuinely underdetermine the answer, and say what further evidence would settle them. I do not want a bibliography or a list of secondary sources; I want the reasoning.

Begin with your bottom line in a single sentence — whether Hume would recognise himself in the modern Humean label, and where he would part company — and then give the supporting reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
