Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether Jefferson deserves the place we give him, and it turned into a fight about the man-of-his-time defense in general — whether it ever really exonerates anyone or only softens the verdict, and whether we can apply it consistently across Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus. I wrote up my side the next morning so I'd know what I actually think, and now I'd like your comments on it. I'm not fishing for encouragement. I want to know where it's wrong, where it's too quick, and whether the distinction I lean on is doing real work or just relabeling the disagreement.

Here is the essay.

**The Man of His Time Was Not Ignorant; He Was Invested**

The man-of-his-time defense comes in two forms that defenders tend to slide between, and the slide is where most of the persuasion happens.

On the epistemic reading, the claim is about knowledge: given the evidence, education, and intellectual tools available to him, he could not reasonably have arrived at the correct view. On the conative reading, the claim is about motivation: even granting that he could have arrived at the correct view, we cannot reasonably demand that he have paid the price of holding it — the loss of standing, income, friends, the coherence of a life's project, and sometimes safety itself.

The epistemic reading is the one people usually say out loud, and it is almost always false. First, the reasons were almost never absent; they were present in fragments. Nearly every figure who gets this defense lived alongside critics, dissenters, and victims whose testimony was in circulation. What was missing was not the raw material of the argument but its status as common knowledge, and those are different things. Jefferson is the cleanest example: a man who could denounce slavery in print while enslaving people, and who arranged his life so that the denunciation never had to touch the household. Second, "available" is a threshold notion, and moral knowledge is not like a research result that requires a community to establish it. One credible dissenter makes a question live. If the excuse requires that nobody around him believed otherwise, it will excuse nearly everyone for nearly everything, because there is always a minority somewhere. If instead it requires that nobody he respected believed otherwise, then it is a claim about social pressure, not about knowledge — and it has quietly become the conative reading. Third, the epistemic reading overgeneralizes into a description of the human condition. People in every era held views we now regard as monstrous, and in every era some of them could have known better. An excuse that covers all of them is not an excuse; it is a fact about how moral knowledge spreads.

So the defense has to be the conative one if it is to have any force. Read that way, it is not a distinctively historical excuse at all; it is a claim in the theory of moral demandingness — the view that some requirements are too costly to be binding. And that relocation costs the defender two things.

The first cost is that demandingness claims are normally partial. They tell us that a requirement was too expensive to be obligatory, not that failing it was blameless. A person who cannot bring himself to act is still the person who did not act, and the rest of us are still entitled to say so.

The second cost is worse. The conative reading is symmetric across time. We too are embedded in practices — supply chains, diets, energy use, the treatment of animals and of the people who make our goods — whose wrongness is contested in our own moment and whose abandonment would cost us a great deal. If the excuse is that abolition would have ruined the slaveholder, then the excuse is that changing would inconvenience us. The defender wants the excuse available to the dead and withheld from the living. Nothing in the conative reading licenses that asymmetry; it is a preference about which verdicts we find comfortable.

What actually gives the defense its grip, I think, is not cost as such but the way the error was load-bearing. The wrong view was not a detachable opinion; it was part of the machinery by which the person understood himself, justified his life, and stayed in step with everyone around him. Correcting it would not have felt like changing a belief. It would have felt like losing a self. This suggests a distinction I find more useful than the usual one. Call a view ambient when it was simply the air the person breathed, and live when he had the argument in hand and kept it at arm's length. Call the error detachable when it could have been surrendered without dismantling the rest, and load-bearing when it could not.

On that grid, the three familiar cases fall apart from each other, which is the main reason I distrust the slogan.

Jefferson is the hard case because his error was live and, in an odd way, detachable. He had the argument; he made it himself; he compartmentalized it. That is not ignorance, and it is not the ordinary cost of changing your mind. It is structured avoidance, and I would rather call it a vice than an excuse. The defense's best material is elsewhere.

Churchill is the stronger case for the defense, which is not the same as being a good case. His views on empire and race were partly ambient and partly live, and the arguments against them were being made in rooms he was standing in. But the view was genuinely load-bearing: his self-understanding as a statesman, and much of what he had built his life around, would have had to come apart for him to see it. That earns him mitigation. It does not earn him an acquittal, and I notice that we rarely extend the same mitigation to people whose load-bearing commitments we find distasteful rather than impressive.

Columbus is where I think the defense does not even get off the ground, though I am less certain here than I would like to be. The standards he is judged by — that you may not enslave, mutilate, and work to death the people you govern — were being stated within his own lifetime and shortly after, by people who had no better information than he did, including some who had every incentive to keep quiet. A practice can be universal and still be contested; contestedness, not consensus, is what makes a question live. I would welcome pushback on this, because I may be collapsing two different things: whether a norm was articulated and whether it was widely enforced.

So my conclusion is deflationary. The man-of-his-time defense survives as a plea in mitigation, not as an exoneration, and its proper object is not the era but the individual's relationship to the era's errors. The real question is not whether the dead deserve our judgment — they will get it either way — but why we want the defense to be exculpatory. My suspicion is that we want a version of it to remain available to ourselves.

What I want from you:

1. Does the epistemic/conative distinction hold up, or does every epistemic excuse collapse into a conative one under pressure — in which case the distinction is doing less work than I claim?
2. Does my symmetry argument prove too much? If the conative reading excuses the slaveholder, it seems to excuse all of us, and if it excuses all of us it excuses nobody. Is there a principled way to keep the excuse for the past and deny it to the present, or is the asymmetry just self-serving?
3. Is the load-bearing criterion doing anything that cost was not already doing? Is it circular — do I end up calling an error load-bearing whenever I have already decided the person should be blamed?
4. Am I being fair to the defense in the three applications, or have I set a higher bar for the figures I already dislike? In particular, is my treatment of Jefferson's compartmentalization an anachronistic standard dressed up as psychology?
5. Is the conclusion too deflationary? If the defense only ever mitigates, does it still have a legitimate role in how we teach history, name buildings, or design memorials — or does mitigation-only undercut the practical uses people actually want from it?
6. What am I missing — a distinction I need, a case that breaks the grid, or an objection from the other side that I have made too easy to answer?
