I have funding for a small team — four philosophers, two years, no empirical budget — and I want to spend it on the philosophical literature on epistemic injustice. I take the now-standard core as settled enough to build on: a speaker suffers testimonial injustice when an identity prejudice deflates the credibility they are granted, and hermeneutical injustice when a gap in the shared interpretive resources leaves them unable to make sense of their own experience. What I need is a defended agenda for what comes next, not a survey.

Rank the following candidate lines of work, add any I have missed, and say which I should decline to fund:

- Whether these are one phenomenon or a disjunction. In particular, is there a third thing in the refusal case (a hearer whose interpretive resources are perfectly adequate but who declines to extend them to this speaker) and the uptake case (a hearer who reads the speaker through the hearer's own categories, so the speaker's self-understanding never gets through), or are these just the two we already have?
- Whether the wrong is done to the knower as a knower or is at bottom distributive and structural — and whether the virtue-and-vice framing, which locates the fault in individual hearers, individualizes a structural harm and ought to be given up.
- Whether non-human systems can occupy either role: an LLM that mediates someone's testimony, or a triage algorithm whose output is credited where the patient's own report is not.
- How the concept earns its keep against the two things it is most often collapsed into: oppression, on one side, and ordinary epistemic bad luck, on the other.

For each, tell me how far upstream it is (how many other questions in the area presuppose an answer to it), how far a small philosophical team could move it in two years, how neglected it is, and what evidence would push it up or down my list. Close with the ranking, the trade-offs you made among those criteria, and the one line of work you would cut.
