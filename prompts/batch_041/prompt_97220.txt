I am a novelist, well into a book whose protagonist is a systems neuroscientist, and I have arrived at the chapter in which she tries to persuade a skeptical colleague that the question of machine consciousness is not merely a fight over words. I have read enough of the popular and semi-technical literature on Integrated Information Theory to be dangerous, and I want to build a thought experiment I can put in her mouth, one that makes the theory's commitments vivid instead of gesturing at them, and that I can trust is not just a recycled version of the examples every reader has already met. I am asking you for the construction of the case, not a summary of the theory. I should say plainly what the case is for, because that fixes what counts as cheating: I need a scene in which two machines behave identically and turn out, on one respectable theory, not to be the same kind of thing at all, and for that scene to work the verdicts must be generated by the theories' own commitments rather than asserted by me.

Here is the specification. Build a pair of artificial systems, each described in a single paragraph, in entirely ordinary physical and computational terms — components, wiring, dynamics, what each unit does from moment to moment — such that the two are as close to behaviorally indistinguishable as you can make them, and yet the theories come apart over them. System A should carry every functional and behavioral mark a working cognitive scientist would treat as evidence of consciousness: it reports on its own states, its states are globally available to its other processes, it forms and uses representations of itself, it remembers, it reasons, it says the right things. System B should be, by contrast, behaviorally mute and functionally unimpressive. Now arrange the two architectures so that Integrated Information Theory is compelled to say that A is not conscious and B is, while at least two rival accounts — choose whichever two you can handle most cleanly, say a global workspace account and a higher-order representation account — are compelled to say the opposite. The verdicts must fall out of each theory's own machinery; if a reader could complain that you simply declared B to be the conscious one, the case fails. Keep everything buildable in principle from ordinary electronics: no exotic physics, no quantum effects, no infinite systems, no stipulations about which one has a soul. Do not lean on a single famous laboratory result, and do not assume I have read any particular paper.

When the two systems exist on the page, do the following in continuous prose. Walk through the reasoning that gives IIT its verdict, naming the features of the architecture that carry the weight — the presence or absence of recurrence, the notion of an integrated cause-effect structure, the exclusion postulate's insistence that only one candidate structure can exist at a time — and then walk through the reasoning of each rival theory, saying which system it crowns and why. Then tell me plainly which single structural feature is doing the real work: if I added that feature to the system that now lacks it, or stripped it from the system that has it, would the disagreement collapse into ordinary agreement, and if so, in which direction? Finally, be honest about the soft spots. How would a committed defender of IIT attack the case, and how would a defender of the rival theory attack the system that IIT favors? I would like the whole answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
