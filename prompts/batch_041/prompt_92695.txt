I've been asked to run a single seventy-five-minute session on the rule-following problem for a mixed reading group — a couple of linguists, a cognitive scientist, an economist — all comfortable with analytic prose, none of whom has met the argument before. It became personally pressing a few weeks ago when a colleague and I spent an hour disagreeing about whether a clause in our department's workload policy covered a case neither of us had anticipated; each of us accused the other of inventing a rule on the spot, and neither of us could point to anything that settled it. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the problem to me. Give me the order in which the pieces must be introduced for the skeptical challenge to be felt before any solution is offered, and why that order is forced rather than merely convenient; tell me whether my workload dispute can carry the whole thing — what a carrying case must have and must lack — and, if it will not, what should carry it instead. I would rather not default to the usual arithmetical example (the one where a learner's finite past sums are equally consistent with a bizarre alternative operation); if that example is genuinely the best carrier, justify it, and if not, say what it fails to do for this audience. Then the misconceptions to expect and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off — I anticipate three: that this is just the problem of induction; that the upshot is that meaning is impossible and anything goes; and that the resolution is community agreement, understood as a plain fact about what people do. Add one closing question that would show whether they have grasped the shape of the challenge rather than memorized a label, and name what I should tell them outright that I am not going to cover. Keep the exegetical question of whose argument this really is out of the session. Close with a single line beginning Arc: that states the explanatory sequence in one sentence.
