I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, and the Dutch book argument is one of those things I absorbed early and have never quite stopped being suspicious of. The folk version goes: if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability calculus, then someone who knows your numbers can sell you a set of bets that leaves you poorer no matter how the world turns out — so coherence isn't an aesthetic preference, it's the price of having opinions that are yours at all. A stretched version of the same argument, spread across time, is supposed to show that you must update by conditionalization.

I want an essay on the controversy this argument raises. Take on the role of a philosopher of probability and decision theory writing for a technically literate reader who is not a specialist, and think the problem through carefully before you write. Begin with a short, clearly labelled brainstorm in which you state the sure-loss argument in its strongest form and list every assumption it needs in order to get from the claim that your betting prices are incoherent to the claim that your credences are irrational; then write the essay proper.

Work through the following case, which I've assembled as a test of the argument. An agent has credences over a partition {A, B, C} of 0.5, 0.4, and 0.3. A bookie who knows these numbers sells her a ticket on each cell at the price she herself would name, or a hair below it. Set out the trades, say what she pays now and what she is guaranteed to collect later, and explain why she has no complaint about any individual ticket at the moment she buys it. Then say what the smallness of the guaranteed loss — 0.2 — does and does not show.

Next, consider a defense I've been mulling over, which I'll call the escrow policy. The agent will trade on anything she finds attractive, but she insists on seeing the bookie's entire list before committing to any part of it, and she evaluates a list by its worst case rather than by its expectation. It seems to me that this leaves her unbookable even though her credences are as incoherent as before, and that the same move defuses the diachronic book: she simply won't be bound by prices she announced before seeing the offer that arrives later. If that's right, what is the sure-loss argument actually establishing? Is vulnerability under a protocol that forbids package-level evaluation a defect of belief, or an artifact of the protocol — and is the escrow policy a serious objection or a refusal to play?

Along the way, say whether the argument can tell us anything about countable additivity, what it shows about an agent who simply declines to bet at all, and whether it matters that the bookie needn't be coherent herself.

Reach a verdict and defend it; don't survey the positions and leave it there. If you think the pragmatic argument needs help from a non-pragmatic one in order to do the work people ask of it, say what the help consists in and whether you accept it. At the end, state how confident you are in your main claims, and single out the point in the essay you are least sure of, and why. I don't care who said what first and I don't need citations — spend the words on the argument instead.
