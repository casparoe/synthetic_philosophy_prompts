So me and a couple of friends have been going around in circles for months now about whether a nation — like, a whole country, not just the people currently living in it — can actually be guilty of something, and whether that guilt can carry down to people who weren't even born when the thing happened. One of us keeps saying yes, obviously, look at Germany after the war, that's basically what Jaspers was getting at with his different flavors of guilt (criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — the idea that you can be implicated by what your state did even if you personally did nothing). The other one says no, that's a category error: guilt is something only a person can have, it dies with the person who did the deed, and dressing up collective responsibility as transgenerational guilt is either moral self-indulgence or something worse, a way of holding people responsible for accidents of birth. I want these two to finally sit down and produce the document they should have written a year ago instead of shouting past each other.

So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a defender of transgenerational national guilt and someone who thinks only individuals are ever properly guilty. Give me the claims both of them can sign — about what guilt is and what it's for, about what the post-war German case is actually evidence of, about what a nation has to be such that it could be a bearer of anything at all. Then the genuine remaining disagreements, each one phrased in wording that its own side would actually accept (not a strawman version the other side finds convenient to knock down). Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that if you settled one, at least one side would have to move — I suspect one crux is whether 'political guilt' is guilt proper or just a name for shared liability, and another is whether guilt requires the kind of control that only the original agent had. For each crux, hand me a case or an argument both sides would agree actually bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about it. And if any part of this turns out to be a fight about words — two different things both getting called 'guilt' — say which part, and why. Please dont give me a reading list or references, I can't look anything up right now, just reason it through. Number the agreed claims, then put the disagreements in a table. Finish with a last line starting 'Verdict:' giving a short label for whether the residual disagreement is genuine or merely verbal, plus the name of the single crux you'd bet on as the deepest one.
