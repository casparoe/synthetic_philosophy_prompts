I have been carrying around an argument that one of my doctoral students pressed on me last term, and I have still not decided whether it is a genuine refutation of conciliationism or a piece of legerdemain with a locatable flaw. I would like you to tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling until I pushed on it, and what rejecting it costs.

Fix the terms as follows. Call two thinkers peers on a question when they share their total evidence bearing on it and are no less competent than one another at assessing that evidence. Conciliationism is the view that when peers disagree about p, each is rationally required to move their credence in p toward the other's — and, where the disagreement is with a group of peers, toward the group's aggregate credence. The student's argument runs:

(1) If you and a peer, or a group of peers, disagree about p, you must move your credence in p toward theirs, weighted by how many hold it.

(2) Peerhood is fixed entirely by shared total evidence and equal competence. Nothing else — not the felt vividness of your own reasoning, not your sense that you can simply see the truth of p — can exempt you, because any such felt advantage is itself just another item in the evidence, and if it were genuine it would be shared.

(3) On almost every contested philosophical question, your total evidence is shared by many equally competent thinkers who disagree both with you and with one another. On most such questions, the majority of those who share your evidence reject the position you hold.

(4) So by (1) and (3), on most contested philosophical questions you are required to hold a credence closer to the majority's than to your own — often below 0.5 in the view you actually hold.

(5) Now apply (1) to (1) itself. The evidence bearing on whether conciliationism is correct is public; many equally competent thinkers possess it and reject conciliationism; on my reading of the field, most of those who have considered it carefully reject or heavily restrict it.

(6) So by (1) you must move your credence in (1) toward theirs, to a point at which (1) is no longer something you endorse.

(7) If you nevertheless go on applying (1) at full strength, you are exempting your own favourite norm from a rule you apply to everything else — exactly the special pleading conciliationism exists to forbid.

(8) Therefore conciliationism either prescribes its own rejection or arbitrarily exempts itself, and either way it cannot be a rational norm.

The student then draws a wider moral: since almost every norm worth having is disputed by a majority of the competent, no such norm can be rationally held at full strength, and we should hold all of our normative commitments loosely. That strikes me as absurd, and I want to know where the slide occurs.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2); I am attached to conciliationism and to the thought that felt insight is no evidence. But I cannot accept (8). Two things nag at me, and I cannot tell whether they are repairs or evasions. First, (1) and (3) treat the disagreeing peers as so many independent votes, when in fact their judgments are heavily correlated — they were trained in the same departments, read the same papers, and are subject to the same fashions — so the aggregation may be illegitimate in a way that does not require giving up (1) itself. Second, (5) and (6) treat the rational standing of a norm as though it were a first-order proposition, and I do not know whether that is a level-confusion or the whole point.

Which step should I reject, and why did it look fine until I pushed on it? Then price the alternatives: for each remaining step I could give up instead, tell me what it would cost me elsewhere. I would rather pay in (3) or in the aggregation than abandon (1), but I want to know what I am buying.
