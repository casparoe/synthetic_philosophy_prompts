I am drafting a short paper on Carroll's tortoise and the justification of deduction, and I would like substantive comments before I commit to the argument. To fix the setting: the tortoise has Achilles enter into his notebook the two premises of the first step of Euclid's first proposition, labelled A and B, and their conclusion, labelled Z, and then declines to draw the conclusion; when Achilles adds the conditional "if A and B are true, Z must be true" as C, and then further conditionals as D and E, the tortoise accepts each one and still does not accept Z. The tortoise asks to be forced, logically, to accept Z as true. My notes follow.

1. What the regress is about. The tortoise never asserts anything false. He accepts A and B, and accepts each conditional as it is written down; he is simply not compelled. What he demands cannot be satisfied by any further entry in the notebook, because inference is the transition itself rather than another item written down. The regress therefore registers a category mistake in the demand, not a gap in our logical knowledge.

2. Where the tortoise stands. As I read him, the tortoise is the reader who grants the conditional and doubts the premises: he allows that if A and B are true then Z must be true, but will not grant A and B. The interest of the dialogue is that even this maximally cooperative reader is not thereby brought to Z, which shows how little a conditional does on its own.

3. The repair. Allow the rule itself into the book. Enter as R: from any A and any "if A then B", infer B. The tortoise now has A, B, C, and the rule that licenses the step, so nothing is left tacit and the regress terminates.

4. The wider moral. This is the same regress that threatens justification generally — an infinite chain, a circle, or an arbitrary stopping point. What the dialogue shows is that the authority of logic rests on nothing but our willingness to go on in a certain way; the rules of inference are conventions, and there is no further question about their authority to be answered.

Please comment. I want (a) any factual or interpretive errors, with the offending sentence identified and corrected; (b) a verdict on whether the repair in §3 works, and if not, exactly what the tortoise would do with R; (c) whether the moral in §4 follows from §§1–3 or slides between different senses of a term; and (d) the strongest objection to the paper's central claim that I have not addressed.
