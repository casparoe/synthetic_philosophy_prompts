I do interpretability work on anomaly detection in language models: we look for internal structure that implements a behaviour we would want to flag — a backdoor, say, a response that appears only in the presence of a particular kind of prompt — and then try to build detectors that fire on that structure elsewhere. A disagreement inside the team has turned into an argument about an analogy, and I would like the analogy audited rather than settled by seniority.

Here is the case. We characterise, in a small open-weights model, a circuit that implements one such trigger-conditioned behaviour, and we train a probe on it. We then examine a larger model built by a different group, from a different data mixture, with an architecture that differs in several details, and we find structure that occupies a similar causal role, responds to the same sorts of inputs, and lights up under the same probes. The draft report says the two models contain "the same mechanism." Our lead defends the phrase with an analogy: this is convergent evolution. Independent lineages arrive at the same solutions — wings, camera eyes, echolocation — because the problems and the physical constraints are the same; so it is no surprise that independent training runs arrive at the same circuit, and having characterised the mechanism in one model we have, to that extent, characterised it in the other. My own unease is sharpened by a remark from a biologist friend, who says that if we are going to use the biology we should use the whole of it, and that the interesting half is the half about what makes two structures the same structure rather than merely the same trick.

I would like an audit with the following parts.

First, the correspondence, set out explicitly — a table is fine. What in our case plays the role of lineage, of common descent, of the conserved developmental toolkit, of selection pressure, of the trait, of the environment, of the level at which a trait is individuated? Where more than one thing in our case could occupy a slot, name the candidates and say what turns on the choice.

Second, where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks matter for the use we are putting it to. I suspect that "the same mechanism" is being asked to do two jobs at once, and I want them separated: (a) predicting that a detector built on one model will fire on the other; (b) asserting that the two models contain the same safety-relevant thing, so that a finding about one is a finding about the other. Say which job the analogy can carry and which it cannot.

Third, what the analogy imports without our noticing. The homology/convergence distinction is, I gather, contested and level-relative: the same two structures can count as homologous considered as organs and convergent considered as wiring, and there is a further notion on which very different organs turn out to be built from the same conserved toolkit. If we take that framework over, do we commit ourselves to there being a fact of the matter about how many mechanisms a model contains, independent of our level of description? Relatedly, we sometimes find two structures within a single model that both appear to implement the behaviour, and I do not know whether that is one mechanism occurring twice or two mechanisms.

Fourth, whether the conclusion the team actually needs — that the finding transfers — survives once the analogy is weakened to something like: similar training pressures produce functionally similar solutions. If it survives, say precisely what it survives as, and what it no longer entitles us to assert.

Fifth, whether there is a better analogy for what we are doing, and what it does better than the evolutionary one.

Keep this concrete about our case rather than a general essay on analogy; I care about which disanalogies do work and which are decoration.
