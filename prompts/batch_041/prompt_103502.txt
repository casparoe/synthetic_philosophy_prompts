ok so I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I've been watching two of my ML friends argue about this for months and I want to actually see the whole thing laid out in one place. Friend one says: a model is only safe if you can state a worst-case guarantee — something that holds for every input, including ones an adversary picks specifically to break it, and if you can't rule out the bad case you have to act like it's live. Friend two says that's the same mistake as refusing to ship quicksort because its worst case is O(n²): you don't get to condition on the adversary you can't even name, what you actually care about is the harm you expect across the inputs you'll really see, and worst-case thinking just paralyzes everyone into shipping nothing.

Write a dialogue between them. Ground rules: both are sharp and neither is a strawman. The worst-case person gets real ammo — adversarial inputs, distribution shift, and the point that your "typical case" estimate is itself a guess from a limited and probably unrepresentative sample. The average-case person gets real ammo too — unbounded input sets you can't defend against, the gap between "no bad case exists" and "we didn't find one," and the fact that a guarantee that only holds for the model you happened to train is doing alot less work than it sounds like. The thing I most want them to dig into is that last bit: whether a guarantee that's conditioned on the actual training run even counts as a worst-case guarantee, or is just average-case wearing a costume. Have them really go back and forth, respond to each other's actual points instead of talking past each other, and don't cop out with a "both are right" draw — let one of them end up with the better case if they have it.
