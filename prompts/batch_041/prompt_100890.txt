I want you to treat a specific position in decision theory as the object of scrutiny and give me a map of its exposure: what would have to be true, or what would we have to observe, for the position to be false.

The position is this. When an agent chooses under risk, with known probabilities over outcomes, the standard expected-utility prescription misdescribes what rationality permits. Instead of weighting each outcome by its probability alone, a rational agent weights outcomes by a transformed function of their cumulative probability. Concretely, she ranks the outcomes of a lottery from worst to best and evaluates it by taking the worst outcome's utility and then adding, for each step up to a better outcome, the utility increment contributed by that outcome multiplied by a risk function applied to the probability of doing at least that well. The risk function is a fixed, agent-specific, increasing map from the unit interval to itself, sending 0 to 0 and 1 to 1, and its curvature encodes the agent's attitude toward risk, with a convex function yielding risk aversion over gains and a concave one risk seeking. On this view the classic violations of the independence axiom are not mistakes but the signatures of a coherent, rank-dependent preference structure, and ordinary expected utility is just the special case in which the risk function is the identity.

Assume the position in its permissive form: rationality does not dictate any single risk function, but it does require that an agent's risky choices be representable by one and the same fixed risk function, applied uniformly across all of them, with the risk function and the utility function separately identifiable from her preferences.

Now tell me what would count against it. I want the defeaters sorted from decisive to merely awkward: the choice patterns or experiments that would falsify it outright, the formal results that would show it collapses into a rival or into vacuity, the intuitions it is committed to that turn out unacceptable, and the structural facts about its own machinery that its friends should worry about. In particular, I want you to address the identification of the risk function against the utility function, whether a single risk function can be calibrated to fit behavior across stakes of very different sizes, what the position owes on dynamic or sequential choice and whether it can survive the diachronic arguments that sink other non-expected-utility theories, how it relates to the more general rank-dependent and capacity-based frameworks it may be a special case of, and whether confining the view to known probabilities leaves the ambiguity cases as a defect or a legitimate boundary. Then tell me which of these a committed defender would themselves concede and which they would dismiss as question-begging, which single consideration does the most work, what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a real problem for a view of this kind or simply the price of a permissive theory.
