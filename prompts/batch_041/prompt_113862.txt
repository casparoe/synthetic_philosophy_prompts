I sit on the scientific advisory committee of a national conservation agency — the body that recommends whether a given taxon is added to the statutory protected list, and under which of the statute's four protection categories. I teach philosophy of biology, which is how I ended up on the committee, and I have been asked to write the decision memo for our next agenda item because I am the only member with no institutional stake in the outcome. The memo should read as my single-authored recommendation rather than a summary of our discussion, and it does not need citations or a literature review: the committee has the file, and what it lacks is a defensible argument.

Here is the file. The taxon is a small spring-dwelling fish known from a single basin in the eastern highlands. It was described in the 1970s as a subspecies of a widespread congener on the basis of fin-ray counts and colour, but a genetic survey in the 2010s found that it is not that congener's sister lineage at all: it appears to be a stabilised hybrid lineage descended from two other species in the genus, one of which is already listed. It has been isolated in the spring since roughly the end of the last glaciation, is fixed for a chromosomal rearrangement that leaves its hybrids with either parent only partially fertile, and differs from both parents in spawning time and diet. It is not, however, reproductively isolated in the strict sense: where the spring outflow meets the main river it interbreeds with a downstream population of one of the parent species. The committee's handbook instructs us to treat a taxon as a species if it qualifies under at least two of the four concepts the agency recognises — morphological, biological, phylogenetic, ecological — and separately permits listing of distinct population segments that are not species at all. Three members have already staked out positions. One argues that the hybrid lineage fails the biological concept outright and is at best borderline morphologically, so that listing it requires leaning on whichever two concepts happen to pass, and that any sufficiently isolated population will pass the phylogenetic and ecological tests, which makes the two-concept rule vacuous. Another argues the opposite: that hybrid lineages are a normal product of speciation, that refusing to list one amounts to protecting an imagined purity, and that the ecological test is the only one that tracks what we actually care about. A third, representing the angling association, says the population is a hybrid swarm and that listing would close a recreational fishery. There is also an awkward item in the file: the spring's cold, stable flow depends on an irrigation dam built in 1958, and the agency's own restoration plan proposes removing that dam in 2031, which would probably extirpate the population.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. I need one clear recommendation among the options actually open to us — list under a named category, list only as a distinct population segment, decline to list and refer the taxon to a management plan, or defer for further study — with a time frame attached (when we revisit it, and what would trigger an earlier review), the reasoning that gets us there, and a fair statement of the strongest case for the recommendation I am rejecting, strong enough that its author would recognise it as their own. The memo also has to leave the committee with a criterion it can apply to the next case, so please do not resolve this by announcing that species are conventional and leaving it there. Finally, say plainly what the recommendation commits us to about the dam.
