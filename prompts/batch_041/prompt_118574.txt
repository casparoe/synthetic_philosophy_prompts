I'm a PhD student in ethics finishing a chapter on fitting-attitude accounts of value, and before I commit to a formulation I'd like an audit of the commitments I've accumulated. Please act as a philosopher of normativity with expertise in the theory of reasons and the metaphysics of value, think carefully, and begin by identifying which pairs of commitments look like candidates for tension before you assess them. Here is where I stand. (1) For something to be good is for it to be a fitting object of a valuing attitude — more fully, for it to have properties that give reasons to value it. (2) Reasons are the fundamental normative unit, and anything else that is normative — oughts, value, fittingness — is to be explained in terms of them. (3) Fittingness is a primitive relation that admits of no further analysis, and the right-kind reasons for an attitude are exactly those that bear on its fittingness. (4) Whether something is a reason for me does not depend on what I happen to desire. (5) The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is genuine, and the correct response is that considerations about the consequences of an attitude are never reasons for that attitude: the demon who threatens the world unless I admire him gives me a reason to want to admire him, or to bring my admiration about, but no reason at all to admire him. (6) All the same, I take the fact that my trusting someone would make them trustworthy to be a reason to trust them, and not merely a reason to want to trust them.

Which of these pull against one another, which only appear to, and how badly? Order the genuine tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere, and finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. Be explicit about any apparent conflicts that you think dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely.
