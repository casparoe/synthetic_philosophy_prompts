For some years I have taught a final-year seminar on Tolstoy's What Is Art?, and I have gradually become convinced that the book makes available — and perhaps encourages — a pattern of reasoning about art that I can recognise at once and cannot name.

The shape of it is this. Someone wishes to account for the power a work has over them, and locates the cause in the maker: the poem is raw and honest; the film is plainly the work of a director who cared; the quartet is played by people who mean it. Asked how they know that the maker felt it, they offer the only evidence they have — the power the work has over them. The two claims then stand as one: the maker's sincerity explains the effect, and the effect evidences the sincerity. I meet it in my students' essays, and I meet it in the review pages. I also meet it, in a subtler form, in Tolstoy himself, for whom infectiousness is "the one indubitable indication distinguishing real art from its counterfeit" and sincerity is the condition that most increases infectiousness — sincerity being established, on his account, precisely by the receiver's feeling that the artist was himself moved. When the feeling is absent he does not conclude that the receiver is at fault; he concludes that the artist was not sincere, and there follows immediately a verdict about the artist's motives and about the class of art he belongs to.

Two features of the pattern hold my attention. First, there is no route to the middle term that does not pass through the effect it is invoked to explain; it can therefore never be disconfirmed, since the absence of the effect is simply re-described as the absence of the middle term. Second, the middle term is evaluative as well as explanatory: to say that the maker did not really feel it is to explain the failure and to condemn it — and to condemn the maker — in a single breath. I have tried to file the pattern under the two warnings I half-remember from criticism, one against appealing to the maker's intention and one against appealing to the audience's response, and it will not go into either drawer, because it is the joint of the two.

Is there an established concept for this — for an explanatory term whose sole evidence is the effect it explains and which is at the same time a term of appraisal? If there is, tell me how it differs from the nearest concepts and which of them fits best. If there is not, coin one, and say where its edges lie: whether the phenomenon requires the middle term to be evaluative, or whether the humbler case of explaining an effect by naming whatever produces it is the same thing; what becomes of it when independent evidence of the maker's state is available; and how it is to be distinguished from the entirely respectable business of inferring a cause from its effect. I am not after a label to file the reasoning under — I want to know what the thing is, what it is not, and where it stops. Please answer in British English.
