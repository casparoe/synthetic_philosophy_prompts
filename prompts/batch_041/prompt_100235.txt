I am a novelist, and my manuscript has reached the argument I cannot settle. It concerns habitat destruction, and it is put in the mouth of a biologist who has spent thirty years in tropical forests. A hectare of intact forest, she observes, holds an enormous number of sentient animals — mostly invertebrates, but also a great many small vertebrates — and the lives of nearly all of them are bad: brief, spent in hunger and fear, and ending in predation, parasitism, or slow starvation. The same hectare turned to pasture, plantation, or pavement holds far fewer animals. If a life can be bad for the one living it, and if most lives in the wild are bad on balance, then clearing a forest reduces the total quantity of suffering in the world, and the conservationist's horror at the burning hectare is a mistake: what is being destroyed is not a good but an engine for producing misery. Her former teacher, a philosopher who has spent his career defending the value of the nonhuman world, replies only that the argument is monstrous and that the worth of a forest cannot be summed over its inhabitants. That reply is not good enough for me; I need to know whether the argument is sound, not whether it is agreeable.

Write an essay that decides the matter. It should take seriously, rather than gesture at, at least these difficulties: whether the welfare of a wild animal is the sort of thing we can estimate at all, and whether our ignorance here cuts symmetrically or asymmetrically; whether lives as different as a beetle's and a chimpanzee's can be placed on a single scale, so that the sums the biologist wants to perform are even well-formed; whether bringing about a world in which some number of bad lives are never lived is a benefit, and to whom, given that the animals in question are precisely the ones who do not exist in the world that is supposed to be better; whether clearing land reduces suffering or merely substitutes one population for another — cattle, rats, sparrows, pets — and whether such a substitution should count as neutral; how the acute suffering caused by the clearing itself, the displaced and the burned and the starved, should be weighed against a diffuse reduction in suffering over the decades that follow; and whether the biologist's conclusion, if sound, would license the deliberate destruction of habitat or merely deprive us of one reason to oppose it.

The essay should reach a verdict and defend it. Do not end on a non-committal note, and do not take refuge in the observation that the question is hard. If the honest verdict is that the argument succeeds only under assumptions we cannot now justify, then say so, name those assumptions precisely, and state what would have to change for the verdict to reverse.
