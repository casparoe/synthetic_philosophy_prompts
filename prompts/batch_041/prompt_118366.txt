You are a philosopher working at the intersection of classical Indian epistemology and film. I am preparing a lecture for a comparative philosophy course and want a reading of Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) through Jain anekāntavāda. Think carefully before you write, and set out the technical apparatus first: anekāntavāda, the manifoldness of real things; nayavāda, the doctrine that every judgment articulates only one standpoint among the seven nayas; and syādvāda, the sevenfold predication in which each predication is qualified by syāt, meaning roughly in some respect.

Then give me the reading, and do not smooth over the hard case. Anekāntavāda is not relativism or skepticism: the Jains hold that there is a single complex truth, fully grasped only by the kevalin. So does Rashomon dramatize many-sidedness, or contradict it? The bandit, the wife, the samurai speaking through the medium, and the woodcutter disagree about the same events in the same respect, and the Jain scheme does not permit that. I also want you to test a thesis I am tempted by but not confident in: that the film is less a dramatization of anekāntavāda than of the classical Vedāntic objection to syādvāda, namely that it equivocates and flouts non-contradiction, and that the frame at the gate, with the monk, the baby, and the woodcutter's admission about the dagger, quietly reinstates the single authoritative standpoint the film has spent an hour dissolving. Where does the film resist that reading? Close by saying what the film contributes to the debate that the texts and their critics cannot. Write in American English.
