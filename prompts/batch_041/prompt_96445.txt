Two friends of mine, Dana and Milo, spent most of a long dinner arguing over whether special relativity really forces us to regard the future as being as real as the present, and eventually asked me to referee. I stayed out of it and took notes rather than take a side, and I would rather have you assess what they actually said, point by point, than tell me which conclusion is currently in favor. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Dana began with the argument usually called the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. Take an event occurring right here, right now — my raising my hand. In one inertial frame that event is simultaneous with some event in a distant galaxy; in a second frame it is simultaneous with an event in that galaxy's past; in a third, with an event in that galaxy's future. Since simultaneity is relative, there is no frame-independent answer to the question of what is happening "now" over there. Yet we all grant that whatever is happening now is real. It follows, she said, that events lying in our own future — an event in that galaxy that we would place after the present — are already real, because they are "now" from the standpoint of some admissible frame. She added a vivid variant: if I take a short walk down the street, my plane of simultaneity tilts, and an event in that galaxy which a moment ago lay in its future becomes simultaneous with me. My stroll cannot alter what exists. So the future events must have been real all along.

(2) Milo replied that this shows only that simultaneity is frame-relative, and that the argument quietly substitutes "simultaneous with me" for "real." As he put it, the argument needs the premise that any event simultaneous with a real event is itself real, and that premise is simply eternalism in miniature — the very conclusion under dispute. A presentist, he said, may deny that "now" tracks any frame's simultaneity slice, and then nothing about the future follows.

(3) Dana conceded that the simultaneity premise was dispensable and reformulated. What the argument really relies on, she said, is that for two spacelike-separated events there is no frame-independent fact as to which is earlier. So if the future were genuinely open, there would have to be a frame-independent fact about which events have already become — a privileged "now." But Minkowski spacetime singles out nothing of the kind: no preferred foliation, no distinguished family of simultaneous slices. The presentist must therefore add structure that the physics does not contain.

(4) Milo answered that a privileged foliation is not incoherent, merely empirically inaccessible. A theory, he said, can be Lorentz-invariant in all its observable predictions while still containing a preferred frame in its fundamental description, and such hidden structure is not unheard of in physics. Special relativity rules out a detectable privileged frame, not a hidden one, and the presentist is entitled to posit the hidden one.

(5) Dana countered that a foliation with no observable consequences is a posit doing no explanatory work, and that even the "moving spotlight" picture, on which the present slides along an otherwise static block, requires such a foliation and so adds structure as well. Other things equal, she said, we should prefer the reading that adds least, and that is the block universe.

(6) Milo said that Occam's razor cuts the other way: the block treats every future event as already real, which is itself a substantial posit, whereas the presentist posits only a foliation. He then pressed a different point. "Real," he said, is not a predicate of physics; special relativity is a claim about geometry and causal structure, and the step from "no frame-independent order among spacelike events" to "everything exists" requires a bridge principle that no experiment or theorem supplies. He added that simultaneity is convention-laden, so a tilted "now" is a poor foundation for a metaphysical conclusion.

(7) Dana's parting shot was that the conventionality point proves too little: the standard simultaneity relation is, uniquely, the one the causal structure sanctions for a given observer, so it is not the free choice Milo makes it out to be. And in any case, she said, the causal structure by itself already denies us a universal present, whatever we say about distant synchrony.

They asked me to settle it and I could not. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of (1) through (7), say whether it is sound, partly sound with a specific flaw, or mistaken, and state exactly what is right and what is wrong in it. Then identify where the crux of their disagreement actually lies, and say whether the Rietdijk–Putnam argument as Dana states it establishes eternalism or merely puts pressure on one version of presentism. Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them are overlooking. I do not want a verdict on who won; I want the specific points of agreement and error.
