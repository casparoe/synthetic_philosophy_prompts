I'm about 180 pages into a novel -- near-future, nothing wild, the only speculative element is a machine a character builds to double-check her own reasoning -- and I've hit a wall that's less about plot than about what's actually true. The machine reasons in a formal system and can talk about what that system proves (it has a provability predicate, so it can form claims like "my system proves A"). My character, a crank but a rigorous one, wants to hand it the most natural self-trust rule in the world: for any claim A, if I can prove A, then A. A friend who does this stuff for a living told me this is exactly the move that kills it -- Löb's theorem: in any system strong enough to talk about its own proofs, if the system proves "if I prove A then A" then it already proves A, so adopting that as a blanket axiom makes the thing prove everything and it goes up in smoke. Great, that's chapter 18, it's a beautiful scene. The problem is I don't know what the space of *fixes* looks like, and I refuse to let the machine be saved by hand-waving that any reader who knows the theorem will laugh at. So before I write chapter 19 I want the actual spec: what does any adequate account of a self-trusting reasoner in this setting have to deliver?

Give me the desiderata -- the cases it has to get right (the machine that trusts itself flat out and dies; the one that trusts only proofs it already wrote down before some cuttoff; the one that trusts a slightly weaker subsystem than the one it's actually running on; the one that wants to hand off to a modified copy of itself and can't see why the copy should be trusted); the puzzles it has to dissolve rather than dodge; the distinctions it can't collapse. I have a hunch one of those distinctions is between having a proof of A in hand and being able to prove that I would prove A -- they feel different to me but maybe they aren't, and if the account says they're the same I want to know what breaks. For each requirement, say what case is behind it and whether it's non-negotiable or just the taste of one camp.

And here's the part I most want: tell me which of the requirements can't all hold at once, and which single one is doing the most work in ruling out the pretty solutions. The machine has to be able to actually *do* something -- an account that saves it by making it trust nothing, or by making it too weak to reason about itself at all, is a nonstarter for me dramatically and I suspect philosophically too, so if that's the only escape hatch, say so plainly. Same for the well-known families of fixes if they're dead ends: bounded proof search, trusting only one level down, letting it be inconsistent in some controlled way -- one sentence each on why each does or doesn't fly, no citations, I don't need a reading list, I need to know what my machine can and can't be.
