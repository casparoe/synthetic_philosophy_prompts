Write a short story, roughly 1,500–2,500 words, about a junior clerk in the records office of a fictional country that is adjudicating land claims after a long dispossession. The office's founding charter obliges it to keep a counter-ledger: a register of everything the official records omit. But the charter also fixes the counter-ledger's grammar — entries may be made only by negating a category the official records already possess, so a family absent from the land survey can appear only as "not surveyed," a village burned off the map only as "not gazetted." The story should turn on the clerk's discovery of what that grammar does to the people the counter-ledger was meant to preserve, and on a name that the official records leave no category to negate. Let the conceptual stakes surface through the plot rather than through exposition: that an archive's power lies in its categories and not merely in its contents, and that a record of a silence may reproduce the silence it documents. Do not let the ending resolve that tension in the clerk's favor.
