I'm a novelist by trade, and for reasons that still escape me I'm the lay/community member on the research ethics board at the university here (they wanted a non-scientist, I wanted the library access, it seemed like a fair trade). There's a consciousness-lab protocol on Thursday's agenda and I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting, so I'd like you to draft it for me.

The protocol, roughly: they want to recruit around thirty people who are in the middle of long meditation retreats. A phone app pings them a few times a day and they rate things like how much it currently feels like there's a 'self' present; then there are long recorded interviews where they describe the moments when the sense of self seems to drop away entirely. The lab's stated aim is to publish this as evidence about the nature of consciousness itself — they want to claim the reports show that the sense of self is constructed and can be absent. The consent form says the participants' reports will help us discover what consciousness really is, and one of the forms has a seperate checkbox where they have to confirm that their descriptions accurately reflect their inner states. There's also a debrief step where people who report 'no self' moments get told that experience might be an illusion, which, honestly, as somebody who spends his life making things up, feels like a lot to spring on someone at the end of a nine-day sit.

The bit of theory I keep circling back to is heterophenomenology — Dennett's method. My understanding of it: you take everything a subject says about their experience seriously, but you treat the report the way I'd treat a character's narration in a novel. It establishes a fictional world — what the subject claims to experience — and you're not allowed to slide from "she says there was no self there" to "there was no self there". The subject is the authority on what they're saying, not on what's actually going on in them. Then you explain why they say it using everything else you've got: their brain, their body, their training, the room they're sitting in.

What I need is the memo. Short written opinion for the board, recommending a concrete course of action on this protocol — approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions plus a date by which the lab has to come back to us — with reasons the lab can actually argue with, and a fair statement of the strongest thing they've got going for them (they'll say first-person reports are the only data anyone has about experience, and that an ethics board has no business deciding what counts as evidence for consciousness). Also, one of my fellow board members is going to stand up and say the reports are just stories and therefore worthless, and I'm pretty sure thats a misunderstanding of the whole idea, so please head that off in the memo too.
