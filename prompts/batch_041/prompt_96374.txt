I am drafting a position statement on the ethics of the long term for a graduate seminar, and I would like it audited for consistency before I circulate it. The statement consists of the following eight claims.

1. Welfare is the only intrinsic good, and the value of a state of affairs is the sum of the welfare of all who exist in it. The date at which someone exists is not by itself a reason to count their welfare for less, so I reject pure time discounting.

2. The great majority of the welfare that will ever be realised lies in the future, and that future may be indefinitely long: civilisation could persist for an enormous span at a high level of welfare, or it could end within a century or two.

3. It follows that the most important thing we can do is to reduce the risk that civilisation ends prematurely or is permanently derailed. Work on existential risk therefore outranks near-term humanitarian work in expected-value terms.

4. Our grip on the long-run consequences of our actions is nevertheless very weak. For most interventions I do not think we can assign meaningful probabilities to the various ways in which they might shape the far future.

5. The correct decision rule is to maximise expected value. I accept the consequence that a sufficiently small probability of a sufficiently large good can outweigh any large but finite benefit that is certain, and I do not treat this 'astronomical stakes' reasoning as a reductio of the rule.

6. My attitude to risk is not symmetric, however. I would accept a modest certain cost for a very small probability of an enormously valuable outcome, but I would not accept the mirror-image trade: I would refuse to run a very small risk of ending civilisation in exchange for a certain moderate benefit, even where the two trades looked arithmetically equivalent.

7. Because of (4), the interventions worth pursuing are those that are valuable across a wide range of plausible empirical scenarios and a wide range of defensible moral views, rather than those that are optimal under a single forecast.

8. We should act now, on the best interventions presently available to us, rather than deferring the question to a later generation that will be better placed to judge.

Tell me which of these claims are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and, for each genuine tension, what the cheapest repair is and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the package. Order the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and finish by naming the single revision that would resolve the most while giving up the least.
