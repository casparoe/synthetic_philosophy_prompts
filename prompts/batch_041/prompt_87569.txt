I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about the ethics of belief, and I should like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts amount to. The two familiar poles are not much help on their own — the view that belief must in every case answer to the evidence, and the view that certain questions licence a pass beyond it — because I seem to sit somewhere between them, and I want the principle to be extracted from the cases rather than imposed on them. Treat what follows as data: these are my unforced reactions, not positions I have argued for, and I would rather you told me which of them I have to abandon than that you saved all of them by an ad hoc clause.

1. A climber at a crux move must commit to the belief that he can make the leap; if he holds the question open he will hesitate, and hesitation will kill him. He knows the evidence about his chances is finely balanced. I think it is not merely permissible but admirable for him to work himself into the belief.

2. A man has no evidence either way about whether his wife has been faithful, and no way of getting any; he could simply decline to form a view. I think it would be wrong of him to settle on her fidelity just because believing it would make him a more affectionate husband.

3. A scientist stakes her laboratory on a hypothesis before the evidence is in, treating it as true in order to design experiments. I take this to be entirely proper.

4. A juror, moved by a general faith in people, comes to believe the defendant innocent against the weight of the evidence. I think this is a dereliction even though the belief makes him gentler.

5. A man privately comes to believe, against the prognosis, that his dying friend will recover. I do not condemn the private belief, and I think it lets him be present and kind; but I would condemn him for telling the friend so.

6. Someone working for a political cause she has good reason to think will fail nonetheless believes it will succeed, because the belief sustains her and her comrades and is in part self-fulfilling through their morale. I think this is admirable.

7. A student who has not studied comes to believe he will pass, in order to calm his nerves. I think this is contemptible, even though the confidence may help a little.

8. Here is the one I cannot settle: a teacher who, against the evidence, comes to believe that a struggling pupil will succeed, where the belief tends to make itself true, since the pupil works harder because the teacher expects it. I keep changing my mind.

I have a hunch that the cases turn on a distinction between beliefs that fix a verdict about how things already stand and beliefs that enable something to be done, but I cannot make that hunch earn its keep against cases 5 and 6, so I would rather you tested it than adopted it.

What I want from you is a definite answer, not a survey. Say what principle, or small set of principles, best captures the seven verdicts I have given, and identify, for each clause you propose, the case that forces it. Say what your principle predicts for the eighth case and which way you would come down on it. Say which of my seven verdicts it forces me to give up, if any, and be honest about the cost. Finally, name the further case that would most sharply distinguish your principle from the nearest rival. I would much rather you committed to one account and defended it than laid out four and left me to choose. Please write in British English.
