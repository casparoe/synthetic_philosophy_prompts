Please describe the most important implications of the two-spheres case—two iron spheres, alike in every intrinsic respect, alone in a symmetric universe, each to the left of the other—for the principle that numerical distinctness requires a difference in properties. I am less interested in the familiar verdict that the case refutes the principle than in the implications that follow if we keep the principle but revise what counts as a property, a relation, or a mode of presentation. In particular, work through the implications for: whether the spheres are weakly discernible by the relation of being to the left of the other (that is, discernible only by a relation they bear to one another, not by any property either has alone); whether the case supports haecceitism, anti-haecceitism, or neither; what it does to structuralist and bundle-theoretic accounts of individuality; and how the lesson transfers, if it does, to identical particles in quantum mechanics, where permutation symmetry is often said to show that particles of the same kind are not individuals. I am especially interested in the least discussed implication: that the case may not be a counterexample to the identity of indiscernibles at all, but a demonstration that our ordinary notion of a property is too coarse to capture the distinctions that individuate. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the arguments and their consequences. Please state how confident you are in each claim and single out the point you are least sure about.
