I've been going back and forth with a friend alot about the lottery and preface paradoxes, and I want to nail down what I actually think by laying out my gut verdicts and asking what principle, if any, fits them. Here's the setup. In a fair 100-ticket lottery with one winner, I think you can know your ticket will lose after the draw only if you've seen the result; before the draw, you don't know it will lose, and you also don't know it won't win, even though you're 99% sure it won't. But I also think it's perfectly rational to believe of each ticket, one by one, that it will lose -- not just to have high credence, but to flat-out believe it -- as long as you don't put those beliefs together. Once you conjoin all 100 into a single claim that ticket 1 loses and ticket 2 loses and so on through ticket 100, I think you've got something you shouldn't believe, even though each conjunct is something you're allowed to believe. That's the lottery side.

On the preface side: suppose I write a 20-chapter book and I'm justified in believing each chapter's main claim, because I did the work on each one, but I know from experience that books like mine usually contain at least one error somewhere. I think I can rationally believe each chapter claim, and I think I can rationally believe the whole book is mostly right, but I don't think I can rationally believe the conjunction of all 20 chapter claims. Now the pair that's bugging me: I think a careful historian can rationally believe each of 30 independent claims in a biography, and also rationally believe that at least one of these 30 claims is false, but I don't think she can rationally believe the conjunction of the 30. Yet in a different case, where the 30 claims are not independent but all rest on the same source, and she knows the source is 90% reliable, I think she shouldn't believe each one individually either, or at least not with the same confidence. I keep feeling these two cases should be treated the same because the arithmetic looks similar, but my gut treats them differently, and I can't tell if that's a principled distinction or just a bias toward independent evidence.

I've been calling my tentative principle the reserve view: you're allowed to believe any claim whose credence clears some threshold, but you're not allowed to believe a conjunction unless the credence you'd need to reserve against error is actually available, and the reserve grows with the number of conjuncts and with how correlated they are. Does that capture all my verdicts, or do I have to give one up? Please state the principle or a small set of candidate principles precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me what each predicts for (a) a 10-ticket lottery, (b) a 1000-chapter book where each chapter is nearly certain, and (c) a case where you believe each of two claims at 0.9 and they're perfectly correlated -- can you believe the conjunction? Also tell me whether the principle should be about belief, credence, assertion, or practical betting, because I suspect I'm sliding between them. Answer in American English, and don't just tell me the standard solution -- I want the principle that actually fits the verdicts above, including the one I might have to abandon.
