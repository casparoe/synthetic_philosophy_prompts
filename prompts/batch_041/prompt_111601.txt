I am presenting a paper at a small departmental workshop and need to prepare for the discussion. The paper argues that the moral difference between lying and misleading is not a difference in kind. The room is specific, and I want the objections this room will actually raise, not a literature survey. In the audience: two Kantian normative ethicists whose own work concerns truthfulness and the right not to be deceived; one consequentialist who reasons in terms of the long-run health of the practice of testimony; and one philosopher of language who works on the semantics/pragmatics boundary and will be listening for category mistakes. There may also be a moral psychologist who studies how people react to being deceived.

My thesis, briefly. I claim that once we hold fixed everything except the mechanism of deception -- the hearer's reasonable expectations, the stakes, the speaker's aims, and what I call the hearer's epistemic exit -- lying and misleading are morally on a par. By epistemic exit I mean the standing opportunity an utterance leaves the hearer to reach the truth through the ordinary exercise of their faculties: whether the words, taken at face value, invite or survive checking, whether anything in the situation would prompt the hearer to check, and so on. My contention is that the felt moral gap between the two tracks the exit conditions rather than the mechanism: where the exits are equal, our verdicts converge, and where they diverge, they diverge because the exits do.

I propose a pairing test. Take any two cases that differ only in whether the speaker asserted a falsehood or merely led the hearer to infer one; if the exit conditions are identical, the moral verdicts should be identical as well. I claim that when we actually run the test, they are. One anchor pair: a doctor who lies -- telling a patient the scan was clean -- to a patient who could easily obtain the report and check (a lie with the exit left open), versus a doctor who uses only true statements, arranged so as to exploit the patient's assumptions and leave no cue to check (misleading with the exit closed). My view predicts that the second is at least as bad as the first.
