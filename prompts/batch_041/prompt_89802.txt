Okay, this is an odd one, and I'm not sure I've set it up right, so bear with me. I'm 71, retired a few years back from geology — forty years of mud and seismic lines — and since then I've been reading a lot of philosphy, which is my wife's fault and also the best thing that's happened to me in ages. Anyway, I've gotten roped into giving a little speech on Saturday and I could use help.

Here's the setup. Our town is putting in a grove down at the old quarry — a couple dozen oaks, the slow kind that take a century and a half to get big — and because I spent most of my career in that quarry, they want me to say a few words at the planting. The crowd is neighbors, the garden club, a bunch of little kids with shovels, a couple of my old field crew, and a reporter from the weekly paper. Not a philosopher in the bunch, which is fine, because I don't want to give a lecture. Five minutes, tops.

The reason I'm asking you instead of just winging it: I've been reading Samuel Scheffler, and one of his ideas has been rattling around my head for weeks. He points out that we seem to care a lot about whether humanity keeps going after we die — not our own survival, I mean the whole human thing carrying on without us. And he thinks it runs deeper than we notice: that a lot of what makes our projects feel worth doing, right now, quietly depends on our assuming there'll be people around long after we're gone. His test case is a thought experiment — you die of natural causes, and then you learn that humanity itself is going to be wiped out not long after. He claims that would devastate you even though nothing bad happens to you. He calls that continued human life our afterlife, and he means it with no religion in it at all, just the ongoing life of people after us.

So here's what I want. Write me the speech. Warm, plain, no jargon, nothing that would make the garden club squint at me. But I want Scheffler's idea doing the real work underneath it — shaping what I can and can't say — not trotted out as a name-drop. Two things I can't do. I can't just say we're planting for the next generation, that's the exact Hallmark line I'm trying to dodge. And I also can't stand up there and deliver the bleak version, that nothing we do matters because we're all eventually gone. Somewhere between those two is where the interesting thing lives.

And there's one more wrinkle I keep coming back to, which I'd like the speech to sit with honestly rather than tie up in a bow. Scheffler's whole thing is about human projects and a human future. But an oak doesn't need us. That tree will keep going — it'll outlive every person standing there, and probably the town, maybe the species — whether or not there's anybody left to care. So is my caring about that oak a counterexample to him? Is there a kind of care that doesn't depend on a human afterlife at all? Or does even that secretly need there to be someone, someday, who walks past it and notices? I don't need the speech to answer this. I want it to make the room feel the question without ever announcing that there's a question in philosophy.

Oh, and no religious consolation — I'm a scientist and an atheist and everybody there knows it, so nothing about the grove watching over our grandkids or the trees carrying our spirits. Honest and warm at the same time, that's the whole trick. Maybe go easy on the geology too, or I'll get ribbed for it.
