My friends and I got into it over drinks about Dennett's free-floating rationales — the idea that you can attribute a reason for a trait or a behavior ("the moth's coloring keeps it from getting eaten") even when nobody and nothing is actually doing any reasoning. I'm a fan of the move, but Priya insists it's just as-if talk in a lab coat: you re-describe the behavior in intentional vocabulary and call it an explanation. I want to see where it actually earns its keep, so rank the cases below by how much treating the rationale as real buys you — real explanitory and predictive mileage, supports counterfactuals, survives the system being messed with — down to where it's a redescription and nothing more. Give me a tier list (S/A/B/C/D, with S meaning the rationale is doing genuine work and D meaning pure as-if), one sentence per item on why it lands where it does. If any item is rigged, or if the ranking dimension itself is smuggling in an assumption, tell me.

The cases: (1) A mayfly that lives a single day as an adult — "so it can dump its genes before the predators find it." (2) A QWERTY keyboard, "arranged to keep fast typists from jamming the typebars" (or whatever the real story is — don't get hung up on the history). (3) A coral's branching shape, "solving the problem of capturing the most light per gram of calcium." (4) A crypto exchange's fee schedule that nobody designed as a filter but that in practice screens out low-volume traders, "so it can keep the order book clean." (5) A trained sentiment classifier that keys on the word "but," effectively "reasoning" that whatever follows the "but" is the real verdict. (6) An intersection where every driver's individually smart lane-switching cancels out into a jam nobody wants — the jam "rationally" allocating delay.

Priya also has a name for a rationale that's still there but does no work anymore — a vestigial rationale — so flag any case you think is one. Tier list first, then the one-liners, 350 words max.
