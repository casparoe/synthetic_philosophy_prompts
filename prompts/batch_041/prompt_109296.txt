I am writing a short paper for a second-year moral philosophy seminar, and I have been working from a chapter in a well-regarded popular book on ethics that sets out to explain Kant's universalizability test to readers who have never opened the Groundwork. The chapter puts the test more or less as follows (I am paraphrasing closely, but the substance and the tone are the book's):

'Kant's test for whether an action is morally permissible is simple. Take the rule you are following -- he calls it your maxim -- and ask what would happen if everyone followed it. If the rule could be followed by everybody without the practice collapsing, it passes; if universal adoption would destroy the very thing you are relying on, it fails. That is why promise-breaking is wrong: if everyone broke promises whenever it suited them, nobody would believe promises any more, the institution would wither away, and your own act of breaking one could not even do the work you wanted it to do. Kant calls this a contradiction in conception. Where universalizing produces no outright contradiction but you still could not bring yourself to want a world in which everyone acted that way -- say, a world in which nobody ever helped anyone -- the maxim fails a weaker test, a contradiction in the will, and what you get is a duty of imperfect obligation: you should help sometimes, but you get to choose when. The upshot is that Kantian ethics is rule-utilitarianism in disguise, since what makes a maxim wrong is that its universal adoption would be bad for everyone. And because the test is mechanical, it returns a verdict on any action you feed it, which is how Kant could claim that morality is a matter of pure reason rather than sentiment or custom. In the end, it is the Golden Rule with a good deal of German scaffolding.'

I have to write about this, and I genuinely do not know how much of it to trust. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a specialist in Kant's ethics would accept it as stated, would wince at it as a familiar simplification that distorts something important, or would say that Kant explicitly denied it? I would like the audit to be concrete rather than a general essay on the categorical imperative: in particular I want to know whether the book's account of why the false-promise maxim fails is faithful to what Kant is actually doing, whether the contradiction in conception and the contradiction in the will are as close to one another as the book makes them sound, and whether the book has quietly dropped something that Kant himself treated as central. Then tell me whether the book's closing verdict -- that the test just is a disguised form of rule-utilitarianism -- survives the corrections you make, or whether it collapses once the test is described properly.

For what it is worth, my own instinct is that something has gone wrong when the test is described as a forecast about what other people would do, since I do not see why a prediction about the behaviour of others should be what makes an act wrong; but I may simply be missing the point. Two loose ends I would like you to tie off as well. First, a classmate says the "mechanical" claim cannot be right, because you can always gerrymander the description of your maxim -- at whatever level of generality happens to suit you -- and get whichever verdict you want, and she insists this is an old objection rather than a new one; is she right, and does it damage the book's picture or only a caricature of it? Second, I have been told that Kant's own late reply to Benjamin Constant about lying to the murderer at the door, where he insists you must not lie even to save your friend, is the test operating exactly as designed rather than a lapse on Kant's part; does that verdict really fall out of the test as the book describes it, or out of something else in Kant's system that the book leaves out? Please be explicit about where the summary is doing real philosophical work and where it is just handing me a slogan.
