ok here's my position set on punishment and the atonement and I want to know if it actually hangs together before I put it in a seminar paper.

1. I'm a retributivist about criminal punishment: the guilty deserve to suffer in proportion to what they did, and that desert is the main thing that justifies the state imposing hard treatment. Substituting an innocent for the guilty is unjust, full stop.
2. I think it's completly fine for a third party to pay an offender's fine or a tortfeasor's damages. If my friend pays my speeding ticket, I owe nothing more; the state has no further complaint against me. Same for a debt.
3. The difference between the fine case and the prison case is that imprisonment is essentially personal — the hard treatment has to be undergone by the offender himself. A fine is just a transfer of money, and money is fungible. So substitution works for fines but not for prison, flogging, etc.
4. Because of (1) and (3), I think penal substitution — Christ taking the penalty for our sins — is morally confused. You can't punish an innocent, and you can't transfer the punishment.
5. I also hold that the state should treat like cases alike: if two sanctions have the same function (say, deterrence plus proportionality), then any difference in who can bear them has to be explained by something other than the function.

I've read Lewis on this and I think the two-minds observation is real — we let friends pay fines but not serve prison terms — and I take (3) to be the explanation of why. But something still feels off about the package. Which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and what's the cheapest way to fix the real ones? Order the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give.
