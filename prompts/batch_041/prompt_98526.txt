I want my jurisprudence students to feel the textualism/purposivism fight in their gut instead of memorizing it as two flashcards, and I think the way to do that is to make them actually rule on something and then live with the ruling. So: design me a runnable classroom activity — rules, roles, a decision with real (if small) stakes, and a debrief. Setting is a philosophy of law seminar, about 18 students, one 75-minute session. The idea has to show up in what happens to them, not in me explaining it afterwards.

The raw material is all invented, so don't worry about whether it's a real bylaw or a real case. Here's the fake statute:

Marsh End Promenade Bylaw, section 4: No person shall operate an amplified sound system on the Promenade between the hours of 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. Section 2 defines "amplified sound system" as any device that reproduces or increases the volume of sound by electrical means.

The only window into what the council was after is the minutes of the debate, which record that the bylaw was pushed through after residents in the flats backing onto the Promenade complained they couldn't sleep because of the late-night music from the two bars on the Promenade, and quote a councillor saying the whole point was to let people sleep.

Two disputes land in front of the students:

Case one: at 11 p.m. a woman whose flat overlooks the Promenade sets a small electric white-noise machine on her balcony rail, mainly to drown out the bar noise so she can actually sleep. Breach of section 4 or not?

Case two: at 1 a.m. a drum circle sets up on the Promenade — acoustic drums, nothing electrical, no amplification. Loud enough to keep the same residents awake. Breach or not?

I want the students to have to give a yes/no ruling on each and actually impose or waive the fine, so the call costs them something, and I want them to have to defend the ruling as a rule that will govern future cases, not just this one. Ideally each side gets to feel the pull of the method it's been told to use, then gets flipped onto the other method on the same facts, so nobody gets to be smug from one seat. Then, when the two chambers' rulings predictably collide, they have to merge them into one bylaw that won't immediately break — and I'd love it if they discover for themselves that the tension is structural rather than just sloppy drafting.

No need to cite real cases or scholars; keep the debrief at the level of the two methods and what each one costs. Please give it to me in these sections, in this order:

- Setup: how to split the room, what each side is told, the physical staging. ~150 words.
- Materials: the handout(s) I'll print, including the bylaw, the two cases, and whatever scoring/fine scheme you want. ~200 words.
- The rounds: step-by-step rules with timings for the whole 75 minutes. ~350 words.
- The swap: the side-flip mechanic and exactly what it's supposed to do to them. ~150 words.
- Debrief: map the moments in the activity onto textualism and purposivism — where each method earned its keep and where it pinched. ~300 words.
- What'll go wrong the first time: the predictable failure modes and how I should handle each. ~200 words.
- What the activity can't do: be honest about the feature of the real dispute this can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. ~120 words.

One thing to keep in mind: the payoff or fine should not need real money, and the whole thing should run with just paper.
