I would like you to build a Frankfurt-style case to order and then show that it does the work it is meant to do. The purpose of the exercise is to test whether the principle of alternative possibilities can be refuted by an example in which the agent's will is left entirely alone: nothing is done to her mind, and yet she could not have avoided doing what she did. The verdicts must fall out of the structure of the case; a case that merely announces that she is blameworthy, or merely declares her alternative unavailable, fails.

Requirements for the case. Keep it realistic — a hospital, a workplace, a family, a committee — with no brain surgery, implants, drugs or demons, and nobody who can read minds or know in advance what she will decide. In the actual sequence she must deliberate in the ordinary way, act on her own reasons, and know what she is doing: she must not be coerced, and she must not be mistaken about what the act is. She must nonetheless have had no way of avoiding it. You must secure that without making her ignorant of anything that matters, and without turning her action into the effect of a device or of an operation on her will. What closes the alternative is to be a person standing ready to step in; you must state exactly what that intervention is conditional on, and the condition must be something observable rather than a state of her mind or a glimpse of what she is about to do. That person must not act. And the case must be one in which her deliberation, her reasons and her act are precisely what they would have been had that person not existed at all.

There must also be one thing she genuinely could have done: a hesitation, the beginning of a refusal, a first step towards doing otherwise. Identify it, and call it hollow if, given the arrangement, nothing could have come of it; then explain why it is hollow. The case must not depend on any earlier choice of hers — no self-binding, no resolution she once made — and you should say whether any earlier moment in her history offers her a real alternative, and what follows for the case if it does.

Then do the work. Give the verdict of a theorist who holds that blameworthiness requires that the agent could have done otherwise, and say which premise carries the weight. Give the verdict of a theorist who will settle for her having been able to begin to do otherwise. Give the verdict of a theorist for whom only the actual sequence matters. Say which reading you think is right, and why the hollow alternative cannot ground her blame. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the real work; say what happens to the case when that feature is replaced by the more familiar one, a person who would have intervened by operating on her will directly; and say what happens when it is removed altogether. Finish by putting on the other hat: state the strongest case a defender of the principle of alternative possibilities can make against your example, and say whether it succeeds.

Write this as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables — the way one would write a long letter. Use British English throughout. The case itself should be no more than a paragraph; the rest may take the room it needs.
