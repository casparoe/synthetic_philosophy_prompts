Bit of a weird request, but here goes. I'm TAing a jurisprudence seminar and next week is the Hart–Devlin thing — the whole fight over whether the criminal law can ban conduct just because it's immoral, with no victim. Every year the same discussion happens: someone says harm principle, someone says society falls apart if you let the shared morality erode, someone says that's just majoritarianism dressed up, and then we're out of time. So I want to prep something that forces a bit more precision, and what I'd like from you is a tier list of positions on the question of whether the state may enforce morality as such. S down to F, a couple of sentences per item on why it lands where it does, and please don't hedge everything into A-minus/B-plus mush — I want real gaps, and I want to know what the bottom tier would need to become to move up.

Here are the five. (1) Disintegration: a society is partly constituted by its shared moral code, so conduct that flouts the code threatens the society itself and not merely the people who witness it, which is why criminalization can be justified even with no identifiable victim. (2) Harm plus toleration: the only good reason to criminalize is harm to others (with the narrow paternalist exceptions), and disgust at other people's conduct is never a reason; the idea that a society's morality is all of a piece, so that letting one bit go erodes the rest, is either false or at least unsupported in a plural society. (3) Moral independence: the state may not enforce a view of how to live, because doing so counts some citizens' preferences about how other people should live as though those were ordinary interests — the objection is to the counting, not directly to the claim that the conduct is wrong. (4) Disapproval-tracking: criminalize in proportion to how widely and how intensely the conduct is morally disapproved of, as measured by surveys, with no claim that the disapproval tracks anything true about the conduct; moralism minus the metaphysics. (5) Consistency: don't ask whether the conduct is wrong, ask whether permitting it would make the law's other moral pronouncements look arbitrary — a ban is justified when the alternative is a visible double standard, and not otherwise.

Two things I care about most, so spend some space on them. First, which two of the five are hardest to tell apart in practice, and is that because they're genuinely close or because one is doing the other's work quietly? Second, does anything in your top tier change places if the society in question is deeply pluralistic rather than homogeneous — I have a hunch that two of these positions are seperate answers to different questions and only one of them actually cares about pluralism, but I can't articualte why.
