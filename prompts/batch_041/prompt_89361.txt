I work in the legal office of a mid-sized environmental regulator. Inspectors send us three or four statutory interpretation questions a week — whether a permit condition covers a particular discharge, whether an e-scooter is a 'vehicle', whether 'within twenty-eight days' counts the day of service — and we must answer them the same day, usually with no lawyer to hand and no access to a case-law database. Our answers decide whether a business is fined, so they have to be defensible. Our Interpretation Act permits us to look only at the long title, any purpose clause and the other provisions of the Act, and forbids us from using Hansard or committee reports; one of its clauses also says the Act 'is to be read in accordance with its purpose', which may or may not apply to the whole Act.

Please write a decision procedure we can actually run on a question, grounded in the textualist/purposivist debate: the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, where a step stops with the plain meaning of the words, where it stops with the Act's purpose, and what to do when the two pull apart. It must keep clear the difference between the meaning of the words, the intention of the Parliament that enacted them and the purpose the Act serves, and it must mark the points where the answer is a judgement call rather than a deduction. Run it on three cases: an e-scooter under a statute written for motor vehicles; a pollution permit whose literal words exclude the seepage it was plainly meant to catch; and one case of your choosing in which the procedure returns an answer we will not want to hear. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do.
