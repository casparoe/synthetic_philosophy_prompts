I teach a third-year undergraduate module on the philosophy of medicine, and I am setting the final paper. I need one essay question on the naturalism–normativism dispute about health and disease, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar opposition between value-free and value-laden accounts of disease; it has to make candidates apply the accounts to concrete cases and then adjudicate between them. Please build the question on the three stipulated cases below, which I will reproduce in full on the paper (because the cases are stipulated, candidates need no outside knowledge, and no candidate should be penalised for failing to cite anything — the marking scheme must not reward citations or names, only arguments).

Case A (founder effect). In one small, historically isolated community, a single allele has reached high frequency through a founder effect: roughly seven adults in ten carry two copies. The allele causes progressive sensorineural hearing loss beginning in early adulthood. In the wider national population the same genotype occurs in fewer than one person in five hundred and is classed as a rare disorder. No other organ system is affected, and the community has no sign language.

Case B (age-related muscle loss). Between the ages of 40 and 80 the great majority of people lose a substantial fraction of skeletal muscle mass and strength; this loss is a leading contributor to falls, fractures, and loss of independent living. The loss is typical for the reference class of human beings in the seventh to ninth decade of life and is not attributable to any identifiable lesion.

Case C (isolated congenital anosmia). A person is born with a complete, isolated absence of the sense of smell. Every other sensory, motor, and cognitive function is typical for their age; they report no distress, and apart from minor practical inconveniences — they cannot smell smoke or spoiled food — they lead an unimpaired life.

The question should require candidates, for each case, to state whether it counts as a disease, and — where the account they are using distinguishes disease from illness — whether it also counts as an illness, under each of three accounts: (i) the statistical-normality or biostatistical account, on which health is statistically normal functional efficiency relative to a reference class; (ii) a pure normativist account, on which disease is whatever is judged harmful or bad, with no value-free factual core; and (iii) a hybrid harmful-dysfunction account, on which a disorder is a dysfunction in a naturally selected function that also causes harm. The question must then ask which single one of the three cases is the strongest evidence that the biostatistical account cannot settle its reference class without a covert appeal to values, and require candidates to state the naturalist's best reply to that pressure and to judge whether the reply succeeds.

The discriminating move I want the marking scheme to reward is this: recognising that Case A is the case that presses on the reference class, that the naturalist's principled reply is to fix the reference class by naturally selected species design rather than by local prevalence, and that this reply does not end the dispute but relocates it — to the theory of function, and to whether a selected-function notion of dysfunction can itself be specified without covert norms. A top answer therefore finishes on the terrain of whether dysfunction-talk presupposes a norm, rather than merely asserting that the statistical account is conventional. A weak answer will stop at the word conventional, or will treat disease and illness as one thing and so miss that a naturalist can concede that Case C is a disease while denying that it is an illness. Case B is the main tempting distractor for the reference-class sub-question; the marking scheme should explain why it is not the reference-class case (age is already a licensed reference class in the account, so Case B tests the gap between harm and dysfunction, not the gap between competing reference classes) while crediting a candidate who, having chosen Case A, nonetheless uses Case B to sharpen the point.
