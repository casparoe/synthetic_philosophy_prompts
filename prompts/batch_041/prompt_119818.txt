I spent thirty-five years in primatology, retired about six years ago, and have since drifted — via a long argument at a conference bar — into co-convening a master's seminar on the evolution of morality for the philosophy department. Next month I have to set the end-of-term essay paper, and I want one question on the WEIRD problem: the fact that most of what gets called moral psychology was run on Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic undergraduates, and what that does or doesn't do to the idea that some moral claims are true independently of what anyone thinks. My trouble is that every answer I get is the same two-step shuffle — people disagree, so it's all relative, so nothing's objectively true — or the mirror-image shuffle where the student insists there's an innate universal moral grammar and then sort of trails off. I want a question neither shuffle can answer.

So I've invented a study for them to reason about. It's made up, and I'd like the model answer to be upfront about that — please don't let it dress the thing up as a real finding, speculate about whether anyone has actually run something like it, or cite literature as though that literature were this study. The population is left deliberately generic for the same reason. Here's the study. Anthropologists spend two years with a small-scale society and use two translated vignettes with a forced choice plus an open follow-up. Vignette one: a man privately eats a fish his clan's rules forbid him to eat — nobody is harmed, nothing is lost, he isn't caught. Vignette two: a man takes most of a widow's herd to meet an obligation owed to another lineage; her household goes without meat for months and a child falls ill; taking the animals is exactly what the rules require of him. The responses invert both of the axes we usually measure at once. The fish-eater did something gravely wrong; it would have been just as wrong if nobody had ever found out; and it isn't wrong merely because it's the rule — the rule merely reports how things are, and the ancestors couldn't have made it otherwise. The man who took the herd did nothing wrong at all; he did what he had to do; and if an outsider said it was wrong, that would just be the outsider's custom. Second finding: respondents who had spent six years or more at the mission school gave almost exactly the standard Western undergraduate pattern — the fish is a mere custom, the herd-taking is wrong in itself — while their siblings who never went to school gave the other pattern. Nothing else they measured (age, sex, wealth, distance from the road) predicted the flip.

The question needs to force them through four moves: (1) say what "objective" is doing in the claim under test and prise it apart from "universal" and "widely agreed"; (2) say what argument, if any, the invented data give against objectivity, and name the extra premise that argument needs; (3) deal with the schooling result, since I expect nearly all of them to reach for it as a debunking tool — I want to see whether they notice that it debunks the schooled group just as efficiently, so that the finding is symmetric unless they can independently justify calling one side's formation the distorting one; and (4) say which meta-ethical positions are left standing and which, if any, the data actually kill. If they can also say what evidence would bear on the objectivity question at all, that's the top of the range. Treat it as a 45-minute essay question, candidates writing roughly 800 words, marking scheme out of 20.

Please act as a professional philosopher who teaches metaethics and knows the cross-cultural moral psychology literature well, and who is good at writing exam questions that can't be answered by reciting a summary. Before you draft anything, think through where the real crux sits and sketch a short plan — I'd rather you spend the effort there than on polish. Then give me: the question; a model answer of about 750 words; and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth adding up to 20 and one line per point on how candidates typically get that point wrong. Keep the model answer and the scheme honest about what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — I don't want a live dispute presented as the consensus, and I don't want my own hunch (I lean realist, for what it's worth) turning up as though it were the standard view. Everything in British English, please, and don't fuss about my spelling — I've been in the States too long.
