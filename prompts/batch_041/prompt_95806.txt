I sit on a small review panel at a state consumer-protection office. Our job is to triage complaints that a company or a campaign has manipulated people — a subscription service with a guilt-inducing cancellation flow, a political ad built on a misleading implication, a targeted feed, a public-health push — and to decide, in about fifteen minutes each, whether the conduct is manipulative enough to send to a full hearing or whether it is just aggressive persuasion that we have no business regulating. None of us is a philosopher, we have no research staff, and the complaint volume is high, so what I need from you is a procedure rather than a lecture: a checklist or decision tree we can actually run, in order, on the complaint and the evidence attached, with a clear stop rule at each stage, and a one-page writeup we can file to justify the call. I keep getting stuck on the same seam. Some influence works by handing people reasons and letting them weigh those reasons — an honest ad, a doctor's advice, a scare tactic that states a true risk — while other influence works around the reasoning, by exploiting a bias, manufacturing a feeling, hiding who is behind the message, or making the seller's preferred choice the path of least resistance. I have a rough three-way cut I'd like you to test and then revise or replace: manipulation by misinformation (false or misleading content), manipulation by influence (emotional or social pressure that bypasses deliberation), and manipulation by architecture (defaults, friction, timing, targeting), each crossed with whether the audience is general or someone singled out for a known vulnerability. Tell me where that cut is wrong, which distinctions are doing real work and which collapse into each other, and which steps in your procedure are genuine value judgments dressed up as findings about manipulation — mark those explicitly rather than burying them.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases and show your work for each. First, a utility that ships a pre-checked consent box and a cancellation flow that adds steps and asks the customer to confirm twice, with a sad mascot, before letting them leave. Second, a city health department that runs emotional testimonial videos and loss-framed messaging to raise flu-vaccination rates among people who have been ignoring it. Third, a dating app that, for users its model has flagged as lonely, surfaces profiles chosen to maximize time-on-app rather than match quality. I want at least one verdict that runs against my instinct — if the procedure clears the health department when my gut says manipulation, or condemns the dating app on grounds I hadn't considered, say so plainly rather than softening it. Where the procedure genuinely runs out and a human has to decide, name that point and tell me who should be in the room for that call.

Give me a committed answer: pick one procedure, defend the cuts you make, and don't hand me three options and leave the choice to me. Where you are unsure, say what evidence or argument would change your mind. Please write in American English.
