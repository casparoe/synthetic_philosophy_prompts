Two friends and I have been going around in circles since an argument about our city's school-funding formula — one of them thinks a more equal world is better just for being more equal, the other thinks only the total matters — and I have been writing down my own verdicts on imagined distributions, welfare scored from 0 to 100, in the hope that they add up to a principle I can actually state. Leveling down: between (80, 20) and (20, 20), where the second is reached by destroying the better-off person's surplus while the worse-off person gains nothing, I say the first is better — a state of affairs does not improve by making someone worse off when nobody is made better off. But between (50, 50, 50) and (50, 50, 500), where the first two people are in exactly the same position either way and the third is far better off, I say the second is better; and since the second is the more unequal of the two, I take this to rule out any view on which equality is good in itself. Priority: I would rather have (95, 95) than (100, 90) even though the total is lower, because the worst-off person does better; but I would rather have (100, 90) than (40, 40), because the worst-off person does worse in the second even though that world is perfectly equal. So far, so prioritarian: what matters is how the worst off are doing, with extra weight, and not how they compare with anyone.

Two items on the list will not fit that. First, my verdicts turn out to depend on how a distribution came about, not only on what it is. Take the leveling-down case again. I said (80, 20) beats (20, 20) when the 80 is destroyed from outside. But suppose the person at 80 is holding her advantage only through something I regard as an injustice — a monopoly granted by a bought official — and she renounces it herself, fully aware that the person at 20 gains nothing; I do not think the resulting (20, 20) is worse than (80, 20). I am inclined to say the loss of an unjustly held advantage is no loss at all, and that her renunciation leaves things better. Second, when the person at 20 is there because she staked everything on a long shot and lost, I feel much less pull to raise her than when she is at 20 through illness or because she was born into it: an identical pair of numbers strikes me as less bad when the shortfall is the upshot of a risk she freely ran than when it was imposed on her. In both cases the same numbers get different verdicts depending on their history, which nothing in the prioritarian sketch above can deliver.

What I want is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures as much of this list as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to distributions I have not thought of, together with an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would force me to give up — and why that verdict is the one that should go rather than the others. Please also say what your principle implies for a case I have not listed, and for the one I keep going back and forth on: the person at 20 whose choice was real but was made from a set of options so bad that every one of them was a loss, so that I cannot tell whether to call her shortfall chosen or imposed. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would push you to a different principle — and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
