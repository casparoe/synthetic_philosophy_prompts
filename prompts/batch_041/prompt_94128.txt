Not a philosopher — I'm a backend engineer — so go easy on me. My team shipped a ranked-choice poll for picking which projects we do next quarter (users rank five options, we run it through an instant-runoff-ish tally). Someone pointed out its gameable, and it turned into a whole argument at standup. A coworker of mine (ex-econ, now infra) wrote this up and I can't find the hole in it. He says the load-bearing part is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem:

(1) Any procedure that takes everyone's rankings over 3+ options and outputs one winner is either dictatorial — one person's ranking always decides — or manipulable, meaning there's some set of everyone's rankings where some user gets a better outcome for themselves by submitting a ranking that isn't their honest one. He says that's a theorem, not an opinion.
(2) Any outcome that can be changed by someone lying reflects who lied best, not what people actually want. That's worthless as a signal.
(3) Our poll has five options and it isn't dictatorial.
(4) So our poll is manipulable, and the numbers we get out of it are partly a product of strategizing rather than preference.
(5) Nothing above depended on it being our poll or on the options being features. Same argument for any multi-option decision we make: which design to ship, which of three architectures, which candidate to hire.
(6) So every multi-option decision procedure we have is worthless, and the only non-gameable option is to let one person decide.
(7) Letting one person decide is obviously worse, so we're stuck. The honest conclusion is there's no legitimate way to aggregate what people want when there's more than two choices, and anything we build here is theater.

Which numbered step do I give up? Why did it look airtight, and what do I pay by dropping it? Things I'd rather not lose: that the poll is worth running at all, and that a result which just reflects lying is bad. Also, if the real fix is "keep the options on a single spectrum," or "add randomness," or "use a prediction market or auction instead," I want to know what that actually buys me and what it costs, because I keep getting those as one-liners and I dont buy them yet.
