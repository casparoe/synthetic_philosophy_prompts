I am a high school junior and a member of my school's film and philosophy club. Next month we are screening Memento, and I have been asked to write a short piece for our club blog and then lead the discussion afterward. Most of my friends want to talk about the tattoos, the Polaroids, and the twist, but I have a different angle I would like to develop, and I am not confident I can do it without overreaching. I have been reading about a puzzle in the philosophy of mind that I think the film is secretly about, and I would like you to help me work it out.

The puzzle concerns two ways a brain might revise experience after the fact. On the Orwellian option, the brain rewrites the content of what you experienced: you seemed to see a red light, but the memory is replaced by a memory of a green one, so your own past experience is falsified. On the Stalinesque option, the brain delays consciousness itself and then presents you with a fabricated narrative, so you never actually lived through the revised version. The color phi phenomenon is often used to motivate this: when two dots are flashed in different positions with the right timing, subjects report seeing a single dot move and change color, even though the second color only appears later. The strange part is that both options seem to preserve the same report—the subject says she saw the dot move and change color—so the difference is invisible from the first-person point of view. That is what makes it so hard to tell which one is true, and it is why I think a film that is all about the first-person point of view might help.

Read Memento through this distinction. Leonard Shelby cannot form new memories, so he tattoos facts onto his body and takes Polaroids; the film’s color scenes run backwards, so we learn causes after effects. Does Leonard’s condition dramatize one of these options, or does it show that the distinction collapses when there is no continuous self to do the revising? Is the audience’s experience of the reverse narrative an Orwellian revision—we reinterpret earlier scenes in light of later ones—or a Stalinesque one—we are simply fed the story in an order that makes us feel we were there? Which scenes—the Sammy Jankis flashback, the final reveal with Teddy, the moment Leonard chooses to burn the photograph and write a warning to himself that Teddy is lying—best illuminate the idea, and where does the film resist the reading? I would like an essay that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously, and that ends by saying what Memento adds to the debate that the color phi phenomenon alone does not.
