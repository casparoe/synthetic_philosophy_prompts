Last autumn I was drawn by lot onto my city's citizens' assembly on housing, and it has turned me into an unexpected convert: I now want the assembly to recommend replacing our elected housing committee with a standing citizens' chamber — ninety members chosen by lot, staggered three-year terms with a third rotating each year, no one eligible to serve twice, and, on any given bill, a vote only for members who have passed a short competence check on that bill's subject area. I am one of three rapporteurs presenting this next month to a joint session of the city's career councillors and their senior officers, plus two invited academics: a deliberative-democracy scholar who is convinced sortition works only under heavy facilitation, and a public-choice economist who is convinced all of this is self-interest wearing a new hat.

Act as a political theorist with parliamentary experience. Before writing anything, brainstorm briefly about what each part of that room actually cares about, then give me the questions I will really be asked: grouped by who will ask them and phrased in their own vocabulary, ranked by how likely and how damaging each is, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft, and finally the one question I am hoping nobody asks. My core case is that elections select for campaigning skill and appetite for office rather than judgment; that a random sample aggregates better than a self-selected elite; that the per-bill competence check answers the ignorance objection; and that staggered rotation answers the capture objection. Tell me what to concede early, what to hold, and where my own position is weakest.
