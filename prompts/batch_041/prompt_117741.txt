Turing suggests that instead of trying to program a machine to simulate an adult mind, it would be more promising to produce a machine that simulates a child's mind and then subject it to an appropriate course of education. The child machine would start with something like a blank or nearly blank mind, and the education would consist of a mixture of reward and punishment, with the teacher shaping the machine's behavior over time. He then introduces a distinction between the mechanism of the machine and its writing: the mechanism being the fixed, hardware-like part, and the writing being the modifiable part, the instructions or notes that the machine can alter as it learns. He remarks that from our point of view mechanism and writing are almost synonymous, and that the character of the machine is a product of both. He also floats the idea that the machine might be allowed to modify its own program, and that random variation combined with selection might substitute for some of the educator's work.

That looks like a regress: the learning machine can only be educated by something that already knows what it is supposed to learn, so the learning-machine program presupposes the very thing it was meant to explain or produce. Turing seems aware of something like this, because he keeps insisting that the educator need not be intelligent in the full sense, that the process can be largely mechanical, and that the education might be something we could in principle carry out without understanding it. But I can't tell whether that is a genuine answer or just a postponement. I also can't tell how the mechanism/writing distinction bears on the regress: if mechanism and writing are nearly synonymous from our point of view, then the distinction between the fixed hardware and the modifiable program may not do the work Turing needs it to do.
