You are a philosopher of biology with a strong background in cognitive anthropology and cultural evolution. I'm preparing a seminar on whether culture can be treated as a genuinely Darwinian process, and I want a careful comparison rather than a survey. Please start by briefly laying out, in your own words and without attributing positions prematurely, what is at stake in treating cultural items as units of inheritance at all — what would have to be true of them, and what explanatory work the move is supposed to do. Then compare the two most influential answers: the replicator-centered picture associated with Dawkins's notion of the meme, in which cultural items are self-copying units that spread and are winnowed by selection, and Sperber's epidemiology of representations (also called cultural attraction theory), in which most cultural items are not copied faithfully but reconstructed each time by minds with shared cognitive biases, so that distributions are explained by attraction toward favored forms plus the ecology of the minds involved, with true replication reserved for special cases. I'd like the comparison organized around specific pressure points: what each treats as the unit and whether that unit has to be stable; how each accounts for fidelity and for the sheer amount of transformation that transmission involves; whether the stabilizing factor is selection among variants or attraction toward attractors; what each predicts about how an item changes as it passes along a chain; and whether the difference is a deep theoretical disagreement or largely a matter of how much copying versus reconstruction happens to occur.

To make this concrete and to keep it from turning into a recitation of the standard debate, work through a test case I've invented and ask how each framework handles it. Imagine a short funny anecdote, about ten sentences long, that circulates orally through a chain of eight friends, each retelling it once to the next. Over the chain the anecdote stays recognizably itself, but several details drift in a non-random way: a minor character gets younger, a number in the story rounds off to something tidier, an incidental detail is dropped, and the punchline acquires a sharper and more symmetric structure that every later teller preserves. A few other details wander randomly and no two tellings are identical. Using this case, tell me which framework gives the more illuminating account of the convergent drift, whether the meme theorist can accommodate it without abandoning the replicator idea, and whether the convergent details are better described as selection or as attraction. Finish with a verdict: are these two views genuine competitors, or is one a limiting case of the other, and which of the two you would defend if you had to explain the spread and stabilization of cultural items in a population. Be precise about where the frameworks actually diverge and flag any place where the apparent disagreement rests on a merely verbal difference.
