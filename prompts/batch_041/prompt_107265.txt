I'm a doctoral student in political theory, and next week I've agreed to run the discussion section for a friend's undergraduate course on human rights — eighty minutes, about twenty second-years, none of them law students, most of them having picked up the word genocide entirely from news coverage. The topic does something odd to a room: everyone arrives certain they know what it means and that they would recognize it on sight, and the conversation tends to die the moment the obvious cases are named. I don't want you to explain the concept to me. I want a plan for teaching it, and I want the plan built so that by the end the students see genocide as a constructed and contested category rather than a self-evident moral one.

The spine of the session should be the distance between Lemkin's original, quite broad conception — for him a coordinated plan aimed at destroying the essential foundations of a national group's life, one that could work through the disintegration of the group's political and social institutions, its culture, language, religion, and economic existence, and not only through killing — and the far narrower thing the 1948 Convention settled on, which binds the crime to a closed list of acts, to an intent to destroy, and to a restricted set of protected groups. I want that narrowing to land in the room as a shock, as a political bargain someone struck, rather than as a definition to be memorized. So tell me the order the components have to come in and why each depends on the one before it; the single carrying example the whole session should hang on, and what that example must contain and must conspicuously lack (I'm inclined toward a case with no massacre, no explicit order to kill, and a policy that officials describe as development and national integration — a coastal people whose fishing grounds are ruined by a dam and industrial runoff, whose language is banned in schools, whose children are sent away to boarding schools, and whose ceremonies are criminalized, carried out by a mix of state agencies and private firms); the misconceptions the students will bring or form — I'd guess genocide equals mass killing, that the legal definition is either the moral truth or a mere bureaucratic technicality, that intent has to be announced, that only states can commit it — and exactly where in the sequence to head each off; one question I can put to them at the end that would show whether the idea landed rather than whether they can recite the Convention; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level. I'd also like a second, smaller case for the check — one aimed at a political faction or a caste rather than an ethnic or religious group — because the Convention's list of protected groups is itself part of the compromise, and I want them to feel that exclusion rather than merely hear it.

Throughout, be explicit about how confident you are in the pedagogical claims you're making, and single out the one point in the plan you're least sure would actually work with a room like this.
