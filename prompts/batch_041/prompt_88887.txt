I'm a third-year PhD student in metaphysics, writing a dissertation on dispositions and their relation to laws, and I run a fortnightly reading group that has spent most of this term on the conditional analysis and the counterexample literature that has grown up around it. For the group's end-of-term social — we invite the philosophy of science people, a couple of sympathetic historians, and whoever else can be bribed with wine — I've been asked to contribute a comic piece, and I want something that is genuinely funny to people who have read the fink-and-mask material and merely baffling rather than embarrassing to everyone else. Could you write a deadpan FAQ page, of roughly 700 words, for a fictional public agency — call it the Bureau of Dispositional Certification — whose business is inspecting objects and issuing certificates confirming that they have the dispositions their owners claim for them? It should read like a real government guidance document: a short "About this page," then numbered questions and answers, and a footnote or a form number or two. The comedy has to come from the metaphysics, not from jokes about bureaucrats: the answers should keep colliding with the fact that the Bureau's tests can never be adequate, because a thing can fail to manifest a disposition it really has (the vase is certified fragile and survives the drop test because it was packed in foam; the cube is certified soluble and sits in the water because it has been coated), because a thing can manifest the behaviour without having the disposition at all (the mimic), and because in the finkish cases — where the very stimulus that would trigger the manifestation is what destroys the disposition — the test removes precisely what it is supposed to be testing for. Let each answer absorb the problem it has just conceded by introducing a new form, a new fee, or a redefinition, so that by the end a reader who knows the literature can see that the conditional analysis has been quietly replaced by an indefinitely long schedule of exceptions, and that the Bureau's certification procedure has become either circular or empty — but never say any of that out loud, and never let the document wink. Include one item in which the Bureau's own staff philosopher dissents in a footnote, and one item whose answer is internally inconsistent in a way the reader can catch. Invent every name, form, case number and date rather than gesturing at real ones; no real philosophers, papers or institutions should appear.

Then, outside the document, add a short note in your own voice, no more than 200 words: tell me where the piece takes liberties with the technical material, and take a definite position — not a survey of options — on the point the piece leans hardest on, namely whether the fink, mask and mimic cases show the conditional analysis of dispositions to be false outright or only to need a more careful statement. Give your reasons, and say how confident you are.
