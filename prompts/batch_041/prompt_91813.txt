I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind and comparative cognition, and I'm drafting the final. I want a single essay question built on the four-grade taxonomy of minds that Dennett lays out in Kinds of Minds — Darwinian creatures (fixed by natural selection), Skinnerian creatures (which can learn by reinforcement), Popperian creatures (which can model the world internally and let a failed hypothesis die in place of a failed body), and Gregorian creatures (Popperian creatures that also wield mind-tools: words, notations, procedures inherited from a culture). The question must not be answerable by listing the four grades with their one-line definitions.

To force that, build it around a candidate fifth grade I'm introducing: the mimetic creature — a creature that acquires and applies culturally transmitted mind-tools by imitation and reinforcement alone, with no capacity to run a tool on a merely imagined case; it can apply what it has only to what is in front of it. The question should make students decide whether this is a genuine fifth grade, a mere subtype of the Gregorian grade, or an incoherent one, and — more importantly — should force them to say whether the four grades are meant as a strict hierarchy of containment (each defined by adding one capacity to the one below) or as a taxonomy of where variation and selection occur, and whether the ordering is a ladder of capacity, of developmental sequence, or of freedom from the environment. A good answer will notice that the hypothetical has bite only if Gregorian is defined by tool use rather than by simulation, and will say what would have to be true for the ordering to be a hierarchy of freedom rather than of mere complexity.

Give me the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks — the standard should separate students who understand the taxonomy from students who can merely recite it. In the model answer, state how confident you are in the interpretive reading you are defending and single out the point you are least sure of. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: genuine fifth grade, Gregorian subtype, incoherent, taxonomy does not decide.
