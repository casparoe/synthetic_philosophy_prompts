I host a podcast on moral philosophy, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a view I'll call threshold lexical negative utilitarianism. As she formulates it, suffering and enjoyment are not commensurable across the whole scale: there is a lexical threshold such that any suffering that keeps a life below the threshold takes strict priority over any amount of enjoyment that would not raise it above the threshold, while above the threshold suffering and enjoyment can be traded off at a finite rate. She also holds that the threshold is not a sharp line but a region of indeterminacy, and that this indeterminacy is metaphysical rather than merely epistemic. I want the interview to press her on the view rather than let her restate it.

Draft ten to twelve questions, arranged in a sequence that builds. Include at least one question each on: what exactly is being denied when she says pleasure and pain are not commensurable below the threshold, and whether that denial is a claim about value, about measurement, or about rational choice; how the threshold is located and whether it can be specified without circularity; whether the view is really a form of negative utilitarianism or a hybrid that merely borrows its name; how it handles aggregation across persons, especially cases where a large number of mild pains are weighed against one intense pain; and what would count as evidence or argument against the view, given that its central discontinuity may be insulated from empirical test. For each question, add a follow-up that responds to the answer she is most likely to give, and where relevant a second follow-up that presses the point if she deflects. End with a question that asks her to distinguish the parts of her position she takes to be settled from the parts she regards as genuinely contested among specialists, and to say which contested part she would most like to see resolved.

For every question, include a brief note to me, the interviewer, separating what is settled from what is contested; do not let a contested view be presented as the consensus. No citations or reading lists. The questions should stand on their own for a listener who has not read the literature.
