My aunt finished chemo for lymphoma in the spring and got the all-clear in July, and ever since she's been telling anyone who'll listen that the drugs didn't do it. The chemo was just the moment God picked to heal her, she says — the pill doesn't cure anything, it's only the knock at the door, and the door gets opened from the other side. She's also a district nurse of twenty years' standing, which is what makes it interesting: she'll chase a patient's antibiotic dose to the minute, and last month she was properly angry with a pharmacy for dispensing the wrong strength of something. When I said that looks like having it both ways she said there's no contradiction at all — the pills have no power of their own, but God has bound himself to give the cure only when they're there, so skipping them is testing him rather than using them. Same with the knife that doesn't really cut, apparently, which didn't stop her sharpening hers before the roast on Sunday.

So: is that a real position or is it just doing the work of a real position without paying for it? And what do I actually say at lunch? Half the family thinks she's gone soft in the head, the other half thinks she's being admirably humble about her own luck, and I don't think either of them is right. I want a straight answer here, not a both-sides survey — take a position and defend it. Tell me whether her view genuinely holds together, and specifically whether it's really any different from just saying the chemo cured her, given she acts on it in exactly the same way. If the honest answer is that the difference is purely verbal, say that; if there's something she's got hold of that I'm missing, say that instead. And give me the strongest thing I can put to her that doesn't just make me the smug one at the table — including where my own objection is weakest, because I'd rather find that out now than at the table.
