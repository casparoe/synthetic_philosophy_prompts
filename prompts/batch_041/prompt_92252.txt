I'm finishing a seminar on decision theory and I've come away convinced by Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory — convinced enough that I've started using it as my default framework for thinking about risky choice. What persuades me is not just that it accommodates the Allais preferences that embarrass expected utility, but that it does so in a principled way: the theory is rank-dependent, so the weight an outcome receives depends on where it sits in the ordering of a gamble, and it keeps beliefs, desires, and risk-attitudes as three separately recoverable components, each derivable from preferences. I also take it as a real selling point that it gets risk aversion at small stakes without licensing the absurd large-stakes behaviour that calibration arguments press against expected utility, and that it treats risk aversion as a rational attitude rather than a mere error or a failure of consistency.

What I want from you is a map of the theory's exposure. If I hold that risk-weighted expected utility maximisation is the correct standard of instrumental rationality — not merely a good descriptive model of how people behave — what would count against that? I'd like the defeaters sorted by how decisive they actually are, and I'd like you to be honest about which ones the theory's own defenders would concede rather than dismiss, since I'd rather hear the concession from Buchak's side than the objection from her critics. Specifically, I want the formal and structural worries pressed hard: what exactly does rank-dependence cost — which axioms it gives up, what happens in two-stage or compound-lottery settings where the reduction of compound lotteries is unavailable, and whether the resulting dynamic-choice and money-pump troubles are fatal or are simply the unavoidable price of departing from expected utility. I also want you to say whether the consequence that a rational agent may prefer not to receive costless information counts as evidence against the theory or is something a committed defender will happily accept, and whether the risk function is genuinely a rational attitude or in the end a taste — because if it's a taste, I worry the theory's normative content thins out into a redescription of whatever preferences an agent happens to have, and I want your verdict on whether it is thereby unfalsifiable and whether that is a defect in a theory of this kind. Finally, two extensions I care about: how the theory behaves when risk-attitudes are heterogeneous, as in collective or policy choice, where different agents have different risk functions and something has to be said about aggregation and about the veil-of-ignorance argument Buchak uses to reach her distributive conclusions; and whether the framework extends without damage to imprecise probabilities and to astronomically large or infinite stakes. Tell me which defeaters would move a reasonable person and which are merely awkward, and end with your own assessment of how well insulated the position is. Keep the whole response under 900 words.
