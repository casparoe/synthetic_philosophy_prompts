I sit on the clinical AI assurance committee at the hospital network where I work (I'm the token philosopher, which is why they keep handing me things like this). Next Thursday we vote on whether to let the ED start using a vendor's triage-and-sepsis-alert model, and I have to circulate the written opinion beforehand. Heres the wrinkle. The vendor never had a clean outcome signal to train on -- sepsis-onset labels are noisy and land days late, and the "good outcome" you'd want to reward (patient survives, gets the right bed, doesn't bounce back in a week) is contaminated by everything else the ward does to them. So instead they supervised the thing on the process: clinicians annotated the reasoning steps they'd want to see (check the lactate, weigh the history, don't anchor on the first set of vitals), and the model got scored step by step rather than on whether the patient actually did well. Their deck calls this "getting the values in before the outcomes arrive." Two of my colleagues think this is obviously the responsible thing and want to certify it as-is. One thinks it's just outcome supervision with extra steps and a nicer conscience -- if the outcome is what we actually care about, grading the reasoning is grading a means to an end we could have graded directly, so we should wait for the outcome labels to catch up. I'm less sure than either of them.

The thing I can't shake is that nobody has shown the annotated chain of reasoning is causally upstream of the recommendation. There's a known phenomenon where a model produces a tidy rationale that reads like the reason but isn't the reason the output came out the way it did -- the rationale is post-hoc, generated after the answer and dressed up to look like a derivation. If that's what's happening here, then step-level supervision isn't supervising a process at all; it's rewarding the model for emitting text that *resembles* good clinical reasoning, which is a far easier target to hit and a far easier one to game. So the vendor's whole proposal now seems to rest on an empirical claim they haven't tested -- that the steps matter. Please draft the short opinion I'll circulate before the vote: it should land on a concrete recommendation (certify, reject, or certify with specific conditions and a review date), and if you go the conditional route, make the conditions things the committee could actually check rather than "monitor for safety." Engage the strongest version of both of my colleagues' positions -- don't strawman the outcome-only view, it's a serious position -- and say plainly what would have to be true for the process-based route to be the right call. One more thing: the vendor has offered to run what they're calling a "step-ablation audit," where they delete one annotated step at a time, re-run the model, and flag any step whose removal doesn't change the recommendation as "decorative." I honestly can't tell whether that's real evidence or circular, so if it belongs in the recommendation at all, say what it can and can't show.
