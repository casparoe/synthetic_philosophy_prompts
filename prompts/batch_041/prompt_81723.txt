I have spent the past two years on a comparative project about what states know about their citizens, and I keep hitting the same wall in seminars. The German participants treat data protection as an entailment of human dignity: a person's control over information about themselves is constitutive of who they are, so the state's knowledge is a standing offence and the burden of justification always lies with whoever wants to collect. The American material runs in two directions at once, a deep suspicion of official snooping alongside a fierce demand for disclosure, with officials' emails, salaries and calendars expected to be public and some version of the thought that if you have done nothing wrong you have nothing to fear surfacing whenever ordinary citizens are asked what they have to hide. The Chinese material I have been reading treats information as a governance resource: the point of knowing who is trustworthy is to make cooperation and credit possible, so visibility can be a form of civic standing rather than a threat to it. Each camp hears the other two as either naive or sinister, and after a year of this I no longer believe they are simply quarrelling about how much privacy is worth.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a survey. Is this a verbal dispute, with privacy, transparency and trust picking out different objects in the three vocabularies? Is it a substantive disagreement about values? Or are the three traditions answering different questions under a single heading — how to shield the person from the state, how to hold the state accountable, how to make the state's information socially productive — so that the appearance of one dispute is an artefact of shared vocabulary? My own hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that the disagreement about privacy is downstream of a disagreement about what the state is for, and in particular about whether the state is primarily the threat to the person or the guarantor of the person, and that consequently no amount of conceptual clarification will move it. In my notes I have been calling the three stances the shield, the audit and the ledger readings of state knowledge, though I cannot tell whether that carving is doing real work or merely decorating the obvious.

Tell me which parts of the apparent clash survive once the verbal and the question-substitution diagnoses have been subtracted, and what would count as progress: a distinction that makes the three positions commensurable, a case that forces one of them to give ground, or nothing at all, with the honest verdict being that the question does not admit of resolution and that the impasse is not a scandal. Where my characterisation of any of the three traditions is a caricature or simply mistaken, say so rather than building on it. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Write in British English, and do not send me reading lists or citations — I want the diagnosis, not the bibliography.
