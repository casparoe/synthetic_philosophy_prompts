I have been going back and forth with a friend about whether brain size could ever be morally relevant -- whether, if it turned out that some creatures have much larger or much smaller brains than others, that fact could by itself bear on how we ought to treat them. He thinks the question is confused, and he argues it with an analogy he is very fond of: a brain, he says, is like the hardware of a computer and the mind is like the software that runs on it. Brain size, on this picture, is just a hardware specification -- the neural equivalent of transistor count or clock speed -- and no one supposes that a computer's rights turn on how many transistors it has; what matters, if anything does, is the software. So, he concludes, brain size cannot be morally relevant, only the mind can be, and any argument that leans on size has committed a category error, mistaking a property of the substrate for a property of the thing that could matter. This surfaced in a seminar on animal minds and I keep wanting to object but I cannot tell whether I am resisting the analogy or the conclusion.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply take a side. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly -- a table is welcome -- saying what plays the role of the hardware, the software, the clock speed or transistor count, the program's "rights," and so on, and being candid about which items on the target side have no clean counterpart on the source side. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and in particular which features the computer picture quietly imports that the brain/mind case may not have: the assumption that the mind is fully separable from, and portable across, substrates; the assumption that more of a hardware quantity must translate into more of whatever the morally relevant capacity is; the assumption that the only way size could matter is by being that capacity itself rather than a fallible sign of it. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten his conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. Finally, say whether the conclusion -- that brain size is morally irrelevant -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it survives only in a weakened form (say, as the claim that size is at most evidentiary rather than constitutive), and whether a better analogy is available for what he is trying to express.

End with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of SURVIVES, SURVIVES-WEAKENED, or COLLAPSES.
