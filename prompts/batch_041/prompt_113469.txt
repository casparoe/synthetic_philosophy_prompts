I serve on the interpretive planning committee of a large municipal museum, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's board vote on the interpretive policy for a permanent gallery opening this autumn. The gallery presents three foundation narratives side by side — the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus — as the stories three communities have told about how they began. The board is not being asked whether any of them happened. It is being asked to approve a labeling scheme that will be printed on the wall text and reproduced in the school materials, and that scheme is where the trouble lies.

Our curators' draft assigns each narrative one of four labels: attested, where the central events are corroborated by independent evidence; anchored, where they are not, but the narrative presupposes a documented world of places, institutions, and social forms; traditional, where what the gallery exhibits is the community's transmission rather than any event behind it; and contested, where competent scholars divide. Two objections have been filed. Our community advisory panel argues that the labels smuggle in a verdict no matter how they are worded — that calling the Exodus anchored rather than attested, or declining to say either, will be read as taking a side, and that there is no neutral formulation available. The curator replies that a story can make history without having happened: the Arthurian material shaped real institutions of kingship and real claims of descent, and the Exodus has functioned for centuries as a live political text quite apart from what occurred in the delta, so that the gallery's business is the narrative's career and not its truth value.

I want your memo, addressed to the board, recommending a concrete course of action — adopt the scheme as drafted, amend it in a specified way, or replace it — with a timetable, since the labels go to the fabricator in four weeks. Two things I would like you to take seriously rather than gesture at. First, the word "legend" does at least two jobs: it can mark a story's provenance as unverifiable, or it can mark that the story's truth is not what it is for, as in a civic legend. Decide whether the gallery needs two terms where the draft has one, and whether that distinction survives contact with these three cases. Second, the scheme as drafted is not exhaustive or mutually exclusive: "contested" seems to be a remark about the other three labels rather than a label of the same kind, and a narrative can be both anchored and traditional. If the scheme is incoherent, say so and give me the repair; if it is salvageable, give me the amendment. Treat the advisory panel's objection at its strongest, not as a public-relations risk to be managed. State how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
