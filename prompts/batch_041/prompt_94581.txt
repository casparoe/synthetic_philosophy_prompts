I'm a second-year PhD student in a lab that runs a great deal of self-report work — participants tell us what they noticed, what they intended, how vivid the image was — and somewhere along the way I started keeping a private ledger of the moments when my own introspective reports were worth something and the moments when they were worth nothing. I'd like you to read the ledger and tell me what principle, if any, generates my verdicts, what that principle implies about cases I haven't entered yet, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I would rather have a principle that keeps most of the ledger and says plainly what it cannot keep than a tidy one that quietly ignores the entries that embarrass it.

The entries I feel sure about: when the dentist asks which tooth and I say the upper left rather than the one beside it, I treat my report as authoritative — I would stop her hand. When I say that the shape which flashed was a face rather than a house, or that the ambiguous figure just resolved into a duck, I treat that as authoritative too, even though I cannot say what about the shape did it. I trust my report that I have been rehearsing an argument in inner speech for the last ten minutes. But in the demonstration where I picked the more attractive of two faces, was handed the other one, and explained my choice fluently, I gave a confident explanation of a choice I never made, and I now think explanations of that kind carry no weight at all; I do not trust my report that I took the second apartment because of the light rather than because the rent happened to be a round number; I think my reports that I was reading carefully rather than skimming, or trying hard rather than going through the motions, are close to worthless; and when a friend with the same training insists she has no visual imagery whatever while mine is as vivid as a photograph, I think we are each reporting something and neither of us is reporting evidence — though I would sooner trust her report than her account of why she lacks imagery. My tentative principle, in a friend's vocabulary that I have half-adopted, was that introspection is trustworthy when it reports what is currently before the mind and untrustworthy when it reports what put it there. Two clusters of entries spoil it. First, I trust "I took the job because it was in the city I wanted" — plainly a report about what put something there — and I cannot see what distinguishes it from the apartment case except that I happen to have independent corroboration for that reason; if corroboration is doing the work, then my principle is not a principle about introspection at all, and something like "treat causal and explanatory self-reports as testimony about oneself that requires outside support" would be the rule, at the price of the job verdict. Second, I treat "it's a face" as authoritative but not "my imagery is more vivid than hers" or "the pain is worse than yesterday's," which are equally presentational; and I do trust some reports about states that are not present at all, such as my belief that the deadline is Friday or my memory of having decided to call my mother.

So: give me the principle, or the two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is earned by which entry; say what each candidate predicts about (a) my report that I just understood the sentence I read, (b) my report that the joke did not amuse me but that I laughed because the person beside me did, and (c) my report that I am remembering last Tuesday rather than reconstructing it; and tell me honestly which entries it cannot accommodate. If the real answer is that I have been running together two different questions — the authority of a report and its reliability — then say so, and tell me which of my verdicts that split leaves standing.
