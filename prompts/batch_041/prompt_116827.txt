The background: the usual "heterogeneity problem" for hedonism is that pleasures look like they've got nothing in common. I've been playing with an escape hatch I'm calling etiological hedonism: what unifies pleasures isn't how they feel or what they do but where they come from — they're all states produced by the same reward-learning machinery (roughly, the thing that computes positive prediction errors and drives reinforcement). On this picture "pleasure" is more like "poison": a heterogeneous kind picked out by causal origin, not intrinsic character.

Assume for the sake of argument that this is at least coherent, maybe even true. I definately want the most important implications — not whether it's true, but what follows from it. What does it do to the hedonic calculus, and to summing pleasure across people for welfare comparisons? Does it quietly collapse hedonism into desire-satisfaction, or does it leave hedonism a genuinly distinct axiology? What does it mean for how happiness researchers and economists should be measuring well-being? And what am I missing — implications for addiction, for moral status, for the alleged value of "higher" pleasures, for the experience machine? Prioritize the non-obvious ones, and flag anything that hinges on empirical assumptions I could be wrong about.
