I've been defending a view for a few years now and I'd like someone to try to take it apart properly. The view: phenomenal consciousness is present in any animal whose nervous system does two things at once -- it pools signals from different modalities and motivational systems into a single control state that then drives behavior, and it lets that state be reshaped by valenced feedback, so the animal learns that this is good and that is bad and changes what it does accordingly, not just its reflexes. Call it the integration-plus-valence criterion if you want a handle on it. On this view the usual suspects are in (mammals, birds, cephalopods, fish), most of the arthropods are in (I've argued this at length for insects and I'm on record saying decapods probably count), and the cases that make me squirm are the ones where the criterion seems to give an answer I don't like -- it looks like it might hand consciousness to C. elegans and to some pretty dumb machines, and it seems to withhold it from at least one case I'd have a hard time giving up. I was arguing about this with a colleague after a seminar on invertebrate neurobiology and she said my criterion was basically anthropomorphism with extra steps, which I don't think is fair, but it's the kind of thing I want you to actually test rather than wave at. I'm not asking you to rule on whether the view is true. I'm asking what would count against it.

So give me the defeaters, ranked. Which are fatal and which are merely awkward. Push on the obvious soft spots and the non-obvious ones -- the behavioral criterion is doing all the work here and I know behavior underdetermines mechanism; I know about the unfolding-style argument against any view that ties consciousness to a particular causal structure; I know the no-report literature makes life hard for any criterion that leans on what an animal does; I know the whole thing is downstream of a bet about which theory of consciousness is right. Dont just recite those back to me. I want to know which specific observations or experiments would actually make me give the view up, what it predicts that its rivals don't, and whether you can construct a case where my criterion says conscious about something no reasonable person would count -- and then tell me whether that's a reductio or just a bullet I have to bite. I'd also like to know what a defender of this view would themselves concede counts against it, not only what critics shout from the cheap seats. And be honest about whether I've rigged the thing so it can't lose: if the criterion has been qualified into unfalsifiability, say so, and say whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just the normal condition of the field. Sort the whole thing from this should make me abandon it down to annoying but survivable. American English please, and feel free to be blunt -- I'd rather be embarrassed here than in print.
