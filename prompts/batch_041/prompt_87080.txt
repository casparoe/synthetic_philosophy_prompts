Alright so I'm a fourth-year PhD student working on constitutional theory, my dissertation is basically about entrenchment and democratic legitimacy, and I've ended up holding a package that a friend in the department keeps telling me is incoherent. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. Here's where I've landed:

1. No generation of the living can legitimately bind its successors. Democratic legitimacy requires that binding collective decisions stay answerable to the ongoing judgment of the people who are actually here. Anything else is a dead hand.
2. Entrenched constitutional rights, the ones ordinary majorities can't touch, are legitimate and do real work. I'm not a pure majoritarian. Some things should be off the table for ordinary politics.
3. In the personal case I'm firmly pro-precommitment. Someone who signs an irrevocable advance directive while lucid, or a Ulysses contract, or a binding sobriety contract, can genuinely bind their later self, and it isn't a failure of autonomy when it holds.
4. What makes a personal precommitment binding, I think, is that the earlier self is the author and the later self is the same agent. The authority comes from the autonomous act, not from the state's say-so.
5. I also think a precommitment is only defensible if the bound self, on reflection, would still own it. If the later self would repudiate it wholesale, something has gone wrong and the binding starts to look like coercion.
6. The amendment route is what rescues constitutional entrenchment: the people are never truly bound, because they can always change the constitution themselves, so entrenchment is self-imposed rather than imposed. Though I'll admit I mean formal availability of the procedure, and I'm honestly not sure how much work it does once you notice how hard amendment is in practice.

Where does this hang togehter and where doesn't it? Be specific about which pairs are genuinly in tension and rank them by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and then tell me which single commitment I'd have to drop or revise to clean up the most. I suspect at least one of these tensions is only apparent and dissolves on inspection, so please don't just flag everything. And say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your verdict about which commitment is the one to give up, and where is your reasoning most likely to be wrong.
