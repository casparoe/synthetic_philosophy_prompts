Ok so I'm running a session for our department's philosophy of law reading group — about a dozen people, mix of grad students and a couple of faculty, 90 minutes — and I want to do something hands-on instead of the usual everyone-summarizes-a-chapter thing. What I want them to actually feel is the problem of a legal system that gives itself the power to rewrite its own rules. Not hear about it. Hit it.

Here's my plan, and I need you to flesh it into something runnable. I hand out a little ruleset at the start, printed on cards, and we play a stripped-down game for about 40 minutes. This is the ruleset I've sketched:

Rule 1. Everyone starts with 10 points. First to 30 points wins and the game ends.
Rule 2. On your turn you either (a) move 2 points from yourself to another player, or (b) propose a change to the ruleset.
Rule 3. A proposed rule change passes if a majority of players vote for it.
Rule 4. Rules 1 through 3 are locked and can only be changed by a two-thirds vote.
Rule 5. Anything the rules don't cover is decided by whoever's turn it is.
Rule 6. Rules may only be changed by the procedure in Rules 2 through 4.

I think there's a hook in there. At some point somebody proposes lowering the two-thirds threshold in Rule 4 by a simple majority, or repeals Rule 6, or writes a rule saying this rule can't be repealed, or uses Rule 5 to declare that Rule 4 just doesn't apply to this particular vote. When that happens I want the group to keep playing and have to decide, using only the ruleset, whether it worked. And there's no referee — I refuse to be the referee, that's the whole point.

What I need from you:

1. The activity design. Setup, materials (rule cards, role cards?), turn order, how the 90 minutes breaks down, and how I nudge them toward the interesting spot without tipping my hand. Should some players get secret goals that push toward the paradox? If so, what goals, and why those ones specifically.

2. A handful of crisis scenarios to keep in my back pocket in case they never find the interesting move themselves — but they have to be presented in-game, as something a player does, not as me stepping in to lecture.

3. A debrief, 15-20 minutes, that maps what happened onto the real issue: why a system that amends itself seems to need a rule standing outside the thing it amends (or why it doesn't), what's actually going on when the amendment rule is the very thing being amended, whether there's some bootstrap rule that just has to be taken as given, and the difference between changing a rule and changing the rule that lets you change rules. Give me questions to ask that get them to see it themselves rather than me explaining it.

4. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I've run games before and I know they dont go the way you plan.

5. Be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce — the part where I just have to tell them.

One constraint: dont make it depend on anyone having read Nomic or any particular book or article. Half of them won't have, and I want the ruleset on the cards to be the whole world. If it helps to tweak my ruleset so the paradox bites harder, definately do that, but keep it short enough to fit on a card and simple enough that people get it in two minutes.
