A colleague in my department writes accessible essays on epistemology for a general audience, and he has sent me a draft in which he tries to summarize, in a single paragraph, the literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. I know the area well enough to be suspicious of the summary but not well enough to say exactly where it goes wrong, and I would like an audit before I reply to him. His paragraph runs roughly as follows.

The debate, he writes, begins from an obvious point: you can be mistaken not only about the facts but about what your own evidence supports, so there is a class of evidence — higher-order evidence — that concerns the reliability of your reasoning rather than the subject matter itself. The question everyone is fighting about, he says, is whether it can ever be rational to believe something while believing that believing it is irrational. The conciliationists answer no: if you learn that an epistemic peer disagrees with you, rationality requires you to move toward their position, and by the same reasoning, if you learn that your faculties are impaired, you must discount your first-order judgment. The steadfast camp answers yes: on their view the first-order evidence is simply what it is, and the higher-order evidence about your reliability is just one more item to be weighed in the total evidence, so that if the first-order evidence still favours your belief, you may keep it. He then flags two further claims. First, the Equal Weight View tells you to split the difference with anyone who disagrees with you. Second, the so-called independence principle — the claim that higher-order evidence must not be allowed to do first-order work — turns out to be nothing more than evidentialism restated, since evidentialism already says that only evidence may govern belief. The upshot, he concludes, is that because we are all fallible, the steadfast position cannot be held coherently, so some version of conciliationism must be correct, and the only live question is how much one ought to conciliate.

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me what a specialist working on higher-order evidence would accept, what they would merely wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I want the audit organized around the distinctions the summary blurs rather than around a list of names: which of the positions he attributes to the two camps are genuinely held and which are his own conflations; where he has overstated a modest thesis or turned a contested premise into a truism; what a defender of each camp would say in reply; and which of his technical terms are being used in their everyday sense rather than their technical one. Then tell me whether his concluding inference — from universal fallibility, to the incoherence of steadfastness, to the truth of some conciliationism — follows from anything accurate in the summary, and if not, exactly which step fails. Finally, rank the errors by how much damage each does to the paragraph as a whole: which one or two, if corrected, would change the shape of the summary, and which are mere loose talk. I am inclined to think he is roughly right, so please tell me plainly if I should not be.
