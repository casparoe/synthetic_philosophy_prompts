I'm a program officer at a small foundation that can fund one full-time researcher for three years on the ethics and moral mathematics of food — desk work only, no lab, no fieldwork, no new data collection. I've been arguing about this with friends for weeks and keep landing in the same tangle. The familiar farmed-animal arithmetic says chicken is the worst offender per calorie, since a kilogram of chicken represents vastly more individual lives than a kilogram of beef; but the arithmetic blurs once land is taken seriously, because beef consumes far more land and feed per calorie, pasture substitutes for or displaces wild habitat, and crop cultivation itself kills large numbers of small wild animals through ploughing, harvest, and storage. Some people push this to the conclusion that a grass-fed ruminant diet might kill fewer animals than a plant-based one; others reply that this only looks plausible if you count deaths rather than suffering, or if you attribute crop deaths to plants rather than to the animals that eat the crops. Underneath all of it sits the wild-animal question: the conservationist reason to farm intensively on less land and spare the rest reverses if wild animal lives are on balance bad rather than good, and nobody seems to agree whether those lives are net positive, net negative, or simply unknowable at our current resolution.

I'd like you to act as an expert in applied ethics and cause prioritization — someone who knows the farmed-animal and wild-animal literatures and also has a good eye for telling a tractable research question from an intractable one.
