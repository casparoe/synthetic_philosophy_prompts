My mate Priya runs the style desk at a magazine and I'm trying to reverse-engineer her actual rule before she does the next issue, because we've got money on how she'll go. Her calls so far: she strikes 'chairman' even when the chairman is a man, and puts 'chair'; strikes 'mankind' for 'humankind'; strikes 'man-made' for 'artificial'; strikes the generic 'he' and uses singular they; strikes 'actress' in news copy (wants 'actor') but leaves it alone in the awards listings, because that's the ceremony's own name for the category; strikes the modifier in 'female engineer' and 'woman doctor'; and she vetoed a colleague's plan to respell 'history' as 'herstory', on the grounds that 'history' has got nothing to do with 'his'. But she leaves 'manhole' alone, and she leaves 'mansplain' alone, saying it names something real that men do. She's the one who put me onto Hofstadter's Person Paper, and she reckons the swap test is the whole game.

Next issue, four things land on her desk: (1) a profile where the subject is a woman and the writer has used 'chairwoman' all the way through; (2) 'the girls in the marketing team', the writer's own words for three colleagues in their thirties; (3) 'Hi guys' as the greeting in the staff newsletter; (4) 'the man on the Clapham omnibus' in a legal explainer, i.e. the phrase judges use for the ordinary reasonable person.

Predict what she does with each one. Then tell me the rule she's actually running — and be honest that her past calls are definately compatible with more than one rule. Give me the rivals, say what each predicts for those four, and tell me which one or two of the four would actually decide between them. If two of the rivals survive everything, say so and name the further case that would kill one of them off. Last thing, quick: if the swap test is really the point, does her rule commit her on 'blacklist'/'whitelist' and 'master/slave', or is it a rule about sex only? I've got a bet riding on wich way she goes.

British English please. And I don't want the style-guide line on any of this — I want her rule, not the Guardian's.
