Write a literary short story that stages, rather than explains, a puzzle about the badness of pain. The governing premise is a fictional neurosurgical intervention that can abolish the affective dimension of pain while leaving its sensory and discriminative dimensions intact. A patient who undergoes it can still locate a noxious stimulus, rate its intensity, and distinguish it from warmth or pressure, but reports that it no longer hurts in the sense of bothering or distressing them; they may say, calmly and without irony, that the pain is present but not bad. This is a stipulated analogue of the clinical phenomenon sometimes called pain asymbolia, in which patients appear to feel pain without the usual aversion and suffering, and you should treat the premise as given rather than as something to be researched. Center the story on one such character and on the people around them — a clinician, a family member, perhaps an ethicist, perhaps another patient whose pain is ordinary and who finds the first patient's reports unintelligible or obscene — who must decide what to make of those reports.

The story must force, without resolving by fiat, the question of where the badness of pain actually resides: in the raw sensation itself; in a separable affective or motivational component that the procedure has switched off; or in something relational — the subject's attitude toward the sensation, the fact that it is unwanted, or the way it interferes with what the subject cares about. Let the characters articulate genuinely opposed positions, none of them a strawman. One should hold that a pain one does not mind is not, prudentially or morally, a harm at all, so the procedure removes the harm rather than concealing it. One should hold that the sensation is bad in itself whether or not it is disliked, so that the procedure merely masks a harm and leaves it in place. One should suspect that the dispute is partly verbal, that pain and badness have been pulled apart in ways that make the question hard even to state. Let the stakes become concrete through the plot: whether to continue or withdraw analgesia, how much weight to give the patient's self-report about their own condition, whether the character counts as suffering or as a special case that exposes an assumption in everyone else's moral reasoning, whether they can be wrong about whether they are being harmed.

Requirements:
- Carry the philosophical content through scene, dialogue, and concrete detail; the narration must not lecture or gloss the debate.
- Give no single character the author's voice, and do not end with a tidy thesis. A sharpened but unresolved ending is preferable to a moral.
- Keep the prose literary rather than expository, and let the conceptual pressure arise from what happens and what people say to one another.
- Do not name real researchers, cite real papers, or invoke actual clinical cases; the scientific premise is yours to stipulate.
- Aim for roughly 1,800 to 2,600 words.
