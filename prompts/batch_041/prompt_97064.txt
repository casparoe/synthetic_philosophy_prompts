So me and my mate got into it last night about tranquilism — the view that the only thing that's intrinsically good for you is the absence of craving and suffering, and the only intrinsic bad is craving and suffering themselves; pleasure and getting what you want only matter insofar as they cut down on craving. I kind of like it, he thinks it's obviously broken, and after two hours neither of us could say clearly who was winning.

Here's the shape of his objection. He says the view has to pick one of two readings of what "tranquility" is, and both are bad. On the first reading, tranquility is a positive felt state — a kind of calm or peace you actually experience. But then tranquilism is just hedonism with one particular pleasant feeling singled out, and it owes us a story about why calm is the only pleasure that counts, which looks arbitrary. On the second reading, tranquility is a bare absence — no craving, no suffering, nothing added. But then the best possible life is a dreamless sleep or a medically induced coma, since a comatose person has zero craving and zero suffering and also zero of the pointless extra activity that a monk has to keep up. So either the view collapses into ordinary hedonism, or it recommends that we all go unconscious, which he says is a reductio. And he thinks any move like "well the coma patient isn't really aware, so it doesn't count" is cheating, because awareness isn't the absence of craving — it's an extra value smuggled in.

Can you lay out what's actually going on here — is this a genuine dilemma for the view or is he stacking the deck? I want the precise structure: which reading of tranquility the view should take, whether the "bare absence" horn really does favour the coma, and what the actual escape routes are. I suspect it hinges on what "craving" is supposed to be — a desire, a felt urge, a kind of tension — because if craving can only exist in a creature that's awake and engaged, the coma might not be tranquil in the relevant sense at all, it might just be nothing. But I don't know if that saves it or just moves the problem. Also tell me whether a tranquilist is allowed to care about anything besides the absence of craving and suffering (being conscious at all, being a certain kind of agent) without contradicting the core claim, and where the line is. I'd rather have the dilemma spelled out with its escape routes than a "well, it depends" — I've had enough of that from him.
