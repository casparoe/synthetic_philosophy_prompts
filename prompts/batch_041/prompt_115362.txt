I recently read the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov on my own, and ever since I keep hearing the same summary of it used as if it were an established fact about human beings: that people do not really want freedom, that they will hand it over with little regret in exchange for being fed, for being given something to believe, and for someone to obey. The version I keep meeting goes roughly, 'People don't want freedom; they want to be fed and told what to believe.' Most recently a relative used it to argue that a government which manages people's lives for them is not condescending but kind, and that any politics built on self-government is asking more of people than they can give.

I have no training in philosophy and no library at hand — I am not looking for citations or a reading list — but I would like to know what that one sentence could actually be asserting, because I suspect it is doing several different jobs at once and that some of those jobs are far stronger than others.

Please give me the following.

The readings (roughly 600–900 words). Lay out the distinct, precise claims the slogan could be making, ordered from the almost trivially true to the most radical, and state each as a definite thesis rather than a mood. I expect the list to include, among others: a claim about what most people in fact prefer; a claim about what freedom costs the person who holds it; a claim that freedom and happiness are in tension by their very natures rather than by circumstance; a claim about which freedom is at issue, for instance freedom of conscience as opposed to political liberty; a claim that the many are incapable of freedom while a few are capable of it; and a claim about the means by which the incapable are to be governed — that they must be managed through the three things the chapter associates with miracle, mystery, and authority. For each reading, say in a sentence or two what else you would have to accept if it were true.

The verdicts (roughly 400–600 words). For each reading from the first section, say whether it is a platitude, a live empirical claim, a conceptual or definitional claim, a normative claim, or simply false; and say which readings are incompatible with which others, since I suspect the slogan's persuasiveness comes from sliding between ones that cannot all be held at once.

What the chapter itself needs (roughly 200–300 words). Which of these readings does the Inquisitor's argument actually require in order to go through? Is he committed to the strong claim about everyone, or only to something weaker about most people — and does his speech survive being read as the work of a self-deceived man rather than a reliable narrator of human nature?

What the slogan is doing when people use it (roughly 200–300 words). In the uses I described — my relative's, and the general run of them — which single reading is being invoked, and does that reading actually support the conclusion being drawn from it? Flag any place where the conclusion needs a normative premise that the descriptive reading cannot supply.
