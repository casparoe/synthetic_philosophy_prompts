A colleague in my reading group has been arguing that supervaluationism about vagueness refutes itself, and I cannot locate the mistake. His argument runs as follows. (1) A term is vague only if its extension has no sharp boundary; that is the datum any theory of vagueness must preserve. (2) Supervaluationism says that a sentence is true just in case it is true on every admissible precisification of the vague terms it contains, where a precisification is a way of making those terms perfectly precise. (3) Every admissible precisification of 'heap' assigns it a sharp boundary -- some largest number of grains that is not a heap. (4) So the sentence 'there is a sharp boundary to the extension of "heap"' is true on every admissible precisification. (5) By (2), supervaluationism therefore counts that sentence as true, full stop. (6) But by (1), if there is a sharp boundary then 'heap' is not vague after all. (7) So supervaluationism denies the very datum it was built to preserve, and is incoherent. I would rather keep both supervaluationism and the datum than accept (7), but I cannot see how to do it. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, once the datum is repaired, does the repaired version leave the theory facing some further problem of its own? And how does the epistemicist -- who simply accepts that vague terms have sharp boundaries, only unknowable ones -- fare by comparison?
