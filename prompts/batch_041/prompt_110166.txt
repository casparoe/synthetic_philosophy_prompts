For some years I have kept a private ledger of my own reactions to morally charged moments in video games, and I can no longer make the entries agree with one another. I would like you to tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, generates the pattern; where any such principle forces me to abandon a verdict I actually hold; and what it predicts for a case I have not listed.

The ledger runs roughly as follows.

Mowing down anonymous enemies in a shooter carries no moral weight for me whatever; I do not hesitate and feel nothing afterwards. Killing a dog that serves as a companion character is genuinely upsetting, and I will reload an earlier save to undo it. Killing a named non-player character who, in a scripted scene, begs me to spare her gives me pause and stays with me afterwards. Killing a character rendered with motion-captured acting and a detailed face weighs more heavily than killing a low-polygon character occupying the identical role. A character I have been assured, outside the game, is merely a few hundred lines of code still makes me feel bad when I betray him. Killing another human player's avatar in a competitive match matters to me, but only because a real person sits at the other end. Killing a nameless, endlessly respawning enemy that exists only as a grinding target is, again, nothing at all. A sequence in which I must personally press a button to torture a character feels distinctly worse than a cutscene in which a character is tortured while I merely watch, even though I know the character is equally fictional in both. Choosing not to save a character when I easily could feels worse than actively killing her, even though I did less. And harming a character modeled on a real, recently deceased person feels like desecration in a way that ordinary fictional violence does not.

Here is what I cannot reconcile. Realism plainly seems to matter: the detailed face weighs more than the low-polygon one. But a crude multiplayer avatar belonging to a stranger weighs more than the most carefully rendered non-player character, which suggests that what matters is not the character's realism or apparent feeling but the presence of a real mind behind it. Yet the interactive torture weighs more than the passive one, and in both there is no real mind at all; the sole difference is what I did. So sometimes the character's apparent status seems to be doing the work, and sometimes my own agency seems to be.

I keep drifting toward a principle of this shape: an act inside a game has moral weight exactly to the extent that performing it would cultivate or express a vice in me, so the object of concern is the disposition I am rehearsing and never the fictional victim. Test that principle against the ledger. If it survives, say what it does with the entries that appear to turn on the victim's apparent suffering; if it does not, tell me which verdicts I must give up and why. State the surviving principle or principles precisely, identify the entry that motivates each clause, and give their verdicts on a case I have not listed: a game whose non-player characters are driven by a large language model and, within the fiction, plead, remember, and hold grudges, so that no two encounters are ever scripted.
