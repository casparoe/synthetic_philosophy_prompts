I'm in a four-person political theory reading group that meets in the back of a bar and argues till closing, and lately we've been stuck on a fight between the two sortition people in the group. Both of them are already sold on randomly selecting citizens, so this isn't the usual elections-versus-lotteries debate, it's an argument inside the tent. J. is a full lottocrat: she wants to scrap the elected chamber entirely and replace it with a randomly selected, constantly rotating body, no elections, full stop. M. is pro-sortition too but only as a citizens' assembly or two bolted onto an elected parliament, advisory or agenda-setting, with elections kept. M.'s first objection is that a lottery is only random among the people who say yes, so if only a small slice of invitees accept, you've basically got a lottery over the kind of person who says yes to a citizens' assembly — the retired, the civically keen, anyone who can take a month off work — which means the descriptive-sample business is doing far less work than lottocrats advertise. Her second objection, and she definately thinks it's the worse one, is that even a perfectly representative assembly needs somebody to pick the question, the witnesses, the briefing pack, the facilitators, and none of those people are randomly selected or electorally accountable either, so full lottocracy doesn't abolish unelected power, it just relocates it from politicians to experts, chairs and facilitators. J. comes back with: elections are riddled with the same unelected power — donors, party machines, whips, media owners — and at least a lottery makes the question of who decides auditable, plus you can lotterise the agenda-setting too and rotate fast enough that no facilitator clique forms. M. also worries that a lottery gives you nobody to throw out, and J. says that's romanticising elections, since voters can't assign credit or blame anyway.

Could you write the dialogue between J. and M. for me? Aim for 900-1100 words, both of them fully competent, no strawman on either side, and actual back-and-forth — each one has to answer the other's specific objection before moving on, not just restate their own pitch. I'd like them anchored to one concrete decision rather than floating above it, so have them argue over something like whether to approve a big infrastructure project or raise the pension age, whatever keeps it grounded, and please keep them arguing on the merits rather than dropping famous names or literature, since half the group hasn't read any of it. It's fine if they don't resolve it; I'd actually rather they end on the point where they're genuinely stuck than on a tidy handshake. Also don't feel obliged to have one of them suddenly see the light — they've been at this for three weeks and nobody has budged yet.
