I have no academic training in philosophy — I run a small bookkeeping practice — but I have spent the better part of a year reading Bentham on my own, and I have drafted a short essay that I would like you to comment on. My interest is in his views on sexual morality, though the essay ends up being about the machinery underneath them. Here is the draft:

The Guest List Problem in Bentham's Calculus

Bentham's method for judging a law, as I understand it from An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), is to count up the pleasures and pains an act produces, weighting each by how intense it is, how long it lasts, how certain and how near at hand it is, and whether it breeds more of its own kind; then to repeat the sum for every person affected and strike a balance. He calls the last of these dimensions "extent," which he glosses as "the number of persons to whom it extends; or (in other words) who are affected by it."

My puzzle is about who gets counted. Bentham is emphatic that a legislator must not treat his own feelings of approval or disapproval as a reason for anything — he names the habit of doing so the "principle of sympathy and antipathy," and he treats it as a rival to the principle of utility rather than a part of it. Yet in the very instructions for running the sum, he tells us to "take an account of the number of persons whose interests appear to be concerned." Appear to whom? The method is supposed to settle whose interests count, but the only instruction we get is to count those whose interests seem to be involved, which is the very question we brought to the method in the first place.

This matters most, I think, for the cases Bentham himself cared about. I have read that he argued, in an essay left unpublished in his lifetime, that sexual acts between men ought not to be criminal at all. I find that conclusion attractive. But I cannot see how he reaches it from the calculus alone, because a great many people are made genuinely unhappy by knowing that such acts occur, and their unhappiness is a real pain rather than a pretended one. If they are on the guest list, the sum can easily come out in favour of punishment, and the fact that their displeasure is mere disgust is not something the calculus itself can register, since as I read him the value of a pleasure or pain depends only on the dimensions he lists and not on what kind of pleasure or pain it is. If they are not on the guest list, then something has to keep them off, and that something is a judgment about which pains are legitimate to count — which looks like exactly the kind of moral judgment the method was meant to replace.

So my claim is that the calculus has a guest list problem: it is an excellent machine for adding up pains once you already know whose pains to add, and it has nothing to say about the prior question. I suspect this objection exists in the literature under some other name, but I have not been able to find it stated in this form.

What I would like from you is not reassurance but criticism. Specifically: (1) is the objection sound, or have I conflated two different questions — say, the question of who is affected with the question of whose interests ought to weigh? (2) is there a reading of Bentham on which he has a principled answer here that I have simply missed? (3) is the problem specific to Bentham, or does it also afflict any view that treats the distress of onlookers as a genuine cost? And (4) tell me which parts of the essay are doing no work and could be cut.

Please be explicit about how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure about.
