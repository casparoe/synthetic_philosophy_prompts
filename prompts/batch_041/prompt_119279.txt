I host a small podcast on philosophy of mind, and next week's guest is a cognitive scientist whose whole career has been about one question: where meaning lives. Her answer develops a three-way distinction drawn in Gödel, Escher, Bach — the frame message, which tells a receiver that this is a message at all; the outer message, which tells it how to decode the thing; and the inner message, the content that emerges once decoding has succeeded. Her own contribution is deflationary. She holds that the inner message does almost no explanatory work, that meaning properly so called resides in the outer message, and that because any frame can be spoofed — a text can present itself as a diary and be fiction, a photograph can present itself as documentary and be staged — the frame is at most a pragmatic nudge whose authority is borrowed from the outer message. She also denies that any message means anything intrinsically, apart from a decoder.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the position rather than inviting her to restate it. I want at least one question on each of the following, and for each of those, the follow-up that would press hardest given the answer she is most likely to give:

- whether a frame message can itself be framed, or whether her account of how a message gets recognised as a message either regresses without end or has to terminate in something non-symbolic;
- the spoof case and the coincidence case — a frame that lies, and a pattern that was never sent but that a receiver successfully decodes — and what each shows about whether outer-message meaning requires a sender at all;
- how the outer message is itself to be understood: as a shared structure between message and world, as a matter of what a receiver can infer about the sender, or as a third thing her account still owes us.

The remaining questions should be equally specific. Assume the listener has not read the book and that no citations are available, so every question has to carry its own setup and be intelligible on its own. Nothing about her career, her influences, or how she came to the topic. Close with a question about what would have to be true for her to give up the three-way distinction in favour of plain talk about context and content.
