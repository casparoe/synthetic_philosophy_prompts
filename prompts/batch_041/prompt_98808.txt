For some years I have been trying to reconstruct, from her verdicts alone, the implicit theory of higher-order knowledge held by a colleague, Dr. Alden. She is a careful and consistent epistemologist, and my aim is to state the rule she is in fact running rather than to catch her in an inconsistency. Her track record, in cases I have tried to describe neutrally, is as follows.

1. Nadia, a healthy adult, has a throbbing headache and is attending to it. Dr. Alden holds that Nadia knows she has a headache, and also knows that she knows.

2. Tomas has been warmed so gradually that he can no longer tell whether he is cold or merely cool. His belief that he is cold is true, but it could easily have been false: a degree warmer or cooler and he would have had no opinion. Dr. Alden holds that Tomas does not know he is cold, and so does not know that he knows.

3. Priya has been told by a wholly reliable friend that the library closes at six; she believes it and it is true, but she has never once turned her mind to her own epistemic standing. Dr. Alden holds that Priya knows the library closes at six, but does not know that she knows.

4. Sam knows a simple arithmetic fact with a comfortable margin — he has checked it twice and could not easily have erred. He also believes, on the strength of a passing feeling of confidence for which he can give no reason, that he knows the fact. Dr. Alden holds that Sam knows the fact but does not know that he knows it.

5. Lena is in unmistakable pain and has reflected on the fact that she is. Dr. Alden holds that she knows she is in pain, and knows that she knows.

6. Yuki believes the clock reads three because it reads three; the clock has in fact stopped, though by chance it shows the right time. Dr. Alden holds that Yuki does not know it is three, and does not know that she knows.

The case she will be asked to judge next is this. Rosa knows, with a comfortable margin, that a certain bridge is out — she has seen the barriers and the notice, in good light, with no luck in play. Rosa also believes that she knows the bridge is out, but this second-order belief is a brute hunch: she can give no reason for it, and if pressed would have nothing to say. It is nevertheless so constituted that in every nearby case in which she would form the belief that she knows, she does in fact know; there is no close world in which she holds the higher-order belief while failing to know.

I would like you to do the following. First, set out the candidate rules that fit Dr. Alden's six verdicts equally well. My own hypothesis is that at least two are in play, and that one turns on whether the higher-order belief is robust, in the sense of not easily having been false, while the other turns on whether it is backed by reasons the subject actually possesses; but correct me if I have misdescribed the field, and tell me if a third rule fits at least as well. Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about Rosa. Third, give your own best guess at Dr. Alden's verdict and the reason for preferring one rule over the others. Fourth, name the further case that would most cleanly separate the rules, and say what each rule predicts about that case. Throughout, mark off what is settled among epistemologists from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
