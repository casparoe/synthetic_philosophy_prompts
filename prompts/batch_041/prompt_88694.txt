This is a bit of an odd one but it's eaten my evenings. I'm in a dispute with my letting agent over a clause that reads one way in the plain present — if you sublet without permission you're in breach — and another way in the second bit, where subletting means the deposit would be forfeit. My flatmate, a linguistics postgrad, says I've wandered into the long-running row about whether indicative and counterfactual conditionals get one semantics or two, and having read around it, it really does look stalled. One camp says both are just modals, the if-clause is a restrictor, and the difference falls out of the ordering source or the background — one machine. The other says counterfactuals need a selection function or a causal model, while indicatives are epistemic through and through — two machines. What neither handles cleanly, as far as I can tell, is the future pair: 'if it rains tomorrow the match will be cancelled' versus 'if it rained tomorrow the match would be cancelled'. Both are equally unsettled, so settledness cant be doing the work, yet they're plainly not the same sentence. There's also the older thread I only half-grasp, about an indicative's probability being the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, and that not cashing out truth-conditionally. My hunch is the row isn't really about the data at all but about what a semantic theory owes us. So what kind of disagrement is it actually — a factual one about which sentences each side gets right, a split over whether semantics has to explain the felt difference between the two, or two groups answering different questions, truth conditions versus assertability? And be honest about what would count as progress: a construction one side can't handle, a proof that the two-machine story is just the one machine in disguise, or nothing, because the question is under-specified.
