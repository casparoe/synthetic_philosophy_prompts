I am a high school student preparing a piece for my school's philosophy club, and I would like you to write me an original short story of roughly 1,500 words. Please write the story itself in British English, with British spellings and idiom throughout, though I am writing this request in American English. I do not want a story about whether machines can think, or about a robot that announces it is conscious; I also do not want any citations, reading suggestions, or references to real researchers, laboratories, or books. Everything the story needs should be explained from within it, and nothing in it should depend on the reader having encountered the ideas before.

The story must be built around one specific difficulty in the philosophy of mind. Panpsychism, in the version I am interested in, holds that the smallest constituents of reality each possess some extremely simple form of experience, and that a rich human experience somehow arises out of an enormous number of these tiny subjects. The difficulty is the combination. Even granting that every grain of the world has its own faint flicker of experience, it is not clear how any quantity of such subjects could add up to one subject having one experience, rather than a crowd of separate experiences that merely happen to occupy the same head. I want that problem treated as a live, unsolved engineering difficulty, not as a mystery to be waved away with a shrug or dissolved by declaring the question meaningless.

Here is the premise I would like you to use, though you may alter incidental details as long as the mechanism stays intact. A research institute manufactures what it calls composite minds: engineered assemblies of cultured tissue, or of some invented micro-scale units, each of which is stipulated to have its own rudimentary experience. The assembly procedure is routine and commercially valuable, and the institute certifies each finished assembly as a single mind on the basis of its behaviour, its internal integration, and its ability to report on itself. Your protagonist should be the person whose job it is to sign off on that certification. The story turns on what happens when the constituent units are queried individually and give testimony that does not fit the certification: they insist nothing has happened to them, that each is still its own small subject, and that the composite voice speaking for the assembly is speaking over them rather than as them.

Please make sure the story does at least two of the following, and make them felt in scenes rather than explained in lectures: that no amount of behaviour or internal integration settles whether there is one subject or many; that the simplicity of the parts seems unable to yield the richness of the whole; and that combination may amount either to the survival of the parts, which would make every assembly a form of conscription, or to their extinction, which would make every assembly a mass grave. Let characters give both answers a fair hearing, and let the ending leave the difficulty genuinely open.
