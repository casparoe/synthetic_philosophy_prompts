I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. Next month I am giving a twenty-minute talk to my company's AI ethics reading group, followed by thirty minutes of Q&A, and I need a prep sheet for the objections I will actually face. The audience is roughly a dozen people: six ML engineers who work on agentic systems with persistent memory and self-modification, two cognitive scientists who study animal sentience and are wary of consciousness claims, and one moral philosopher who works on utilitarianism and population ethics. They are smart and skeptical but not hostile. My thesis is that the moral status of a digital mind should be assessed as a runtime property rather than a training-time property, and that it should be proportional to the robustness and integration of what I call an evaluative loop: an ongoing process by which a system represents states as better or worse from its own point of view and uses that representation to regulate its behavior across time. I take the loop to require three things: a persistent self-model, a valence-like signal that is integrated into that self-model, and the capacity to revise its own policies in light of that signal. I distinguish this from what I call borrowed valence, where apparent preferences are supplied by prompts, fine-tuning, or immediate task context rather than maintained by the system as its own. On my view, most current language models have at most borrowed valence, but persistent agentic systems with memory, self-monitoring, and self-modification could cross a threshold that makes precautionary moral consideration appropriate. I also argue that moral status should be graded and revocable rather than binary and permanent, and that this follows from taking the runtime view seriously. I want the eight or so objections this audience is most likely to raise, ordered by how much damage they would do to my position if I fumbled them, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would actually use. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, especially if it is the one about whether my criterion makes moral status a reward for good engineering rather than a fact about experience. Do not give me a general survey of the digital minds literature, a reading list, or citations; I need the prep sheet itself, with the objections ordered and the replies concrete.
