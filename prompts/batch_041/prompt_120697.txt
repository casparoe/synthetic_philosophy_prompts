I am assembling an application for a small interdisciplinary fellowship — philosophy, with a couple of cognitive scientists on the review panel — and I need a two-page research proposal out of an idea I have not yet managed to state properly. The topic is whether facts about brain size are morally relevant, and if so in what way. I came to it sideways. I keep sitting in on discussions of animal minds where someone says that a species has a tiny brain and treats that as settling whether its members matter morally, and someone else replies that absolute brain size mostly tracks body size and metabolic demand, and the conversation goes in a circle for twenty minutes and then stops. My suspicion is that the circle is not a stalemate between two positions but a sign that one word is quietly doing three jobs at once, and that nobody has separated them because each side needs the ambiguity.

What makes me think there is a real project here rather than a tidy terminological complaint is that the empirical picture is stranger than either side assumes, and I want the proposal to put that picture in contact with the philosophy instead of decorating it. Elephants have roughly three times as many neurons as we do and a brain about four times the weight, but only about a third as many neurons in the cerebral cortex, with the surplus sitting largely in the cerebellum; some birds, crows and parrots in particular, pack neurons into the forebrain at two to four times mammalian density, so that a brain the size of a walnut can hold forebrain neuron numbers in the range of much larger primates; an octopus has something like half a billion neurons, most of them out in the arms, on an architecture with almost nothing in common with a vertebrate one; and within our own species nobody supposes that variation in brain size makes any moral difference at all, even though many of the same people reach for it across species without blinking. I would like those four facts to function as pressure points rather than as background.

The half-formed idea to sharpen is this: the debate runs together (i) brain size as evidence about capacities, a fallible proxy we reach for because we cannot get inside the animal; (ii) brain size as partly constitutive of a capacity, if having the capacity just is having enough of the right kind of processing; and (iii) brain size as a measure of how much there is to be harmed — the thought, if it is even intelligible, that a creature with more of whatever experience is made of is worse off when it suffers, or matters more, than one with less. I suspect (iii) is where the real work lies, that it is either quickly false or far harder to state than anyone admits, and that the familiar threshold-versus-scalar question is separable from the proxy-versus-constitutive question in a way the literature mostly obscures. So please write the proposal: a question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the standard positions leave it open, since as far as I can see both the sentience-by-capacity views and the attempts to rank species by neural complexity assume an answer to (iii) rather than argue for one; the method, meaning what I would actually do with the cases and with the three roles, and how I would tell a genuine distinction from a merely verbal one; what would count as the project succeeding and what as failing — I would genuinely rather learn now that the honest outcome is dissolution than discover it in month five; and a scope that one term of part-time work can carry. The panel is not philosophy-specialist, so the framing has to be legible to a psychologist without going soft. I will add the citations myself, so please do not spend any of the two pages on them.
