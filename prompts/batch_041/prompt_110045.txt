I host a small podcast on everyday ethics, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a position I want to press hard. Her view, as I understand it, is this: visitors are morally required to follow a local convention when it is a live coordination equilibrium that locals rely on and when noncompliance would impose a real cost on them, but not when the convention mainly encodes or reproduces a local status hierarchy; residents are required to follow their own conventions only under the same test, so local practice does not generate an obligation merely by being local. She also holds that the line between coordination conventions and status conventions is not fixed in advance: the same act, such as tipping, queueing, or jaywalking, can belong to both categories depending on context and history, and a convention can migrate from one category to the other. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate this. I want at least one question each on (a) how she distinguishes thin coordination conventions from thick conventions that distribute burdens and benefits, using tipping and queueing as the test cases, and what would settle whether a convention has switched categories; (b) whether a visitor's ignorance, foreignness, or inability to learn the convention in time can excuse noncompliance, whether there is a positive duty to learn local conventions before or during a visit, and how she avoids the visitor's dilemma of either free-riding on local practices or being conscripted into local injustice; and (c) how the view handles conventions that are widely followed but unjust, and conventions that are illegal but widely ignored, with jaywalking and tipping as the examples. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and make the follow-ups progressively harder to deflect. Also include one question about whether the view collapses into moralizing mere etiquette, and one about whether it underrates the moral value of local attachment, respect, or solidarity. Finally, ask what evidence or argument would lead her to abandon the qualified local-convention principle. For the whole set, say which questions you think are genuine pressure points and which are likely cheap shots, what answer from her would make you think the view has more resources than the questions assume, and end by saying what consideration would most change your own assessment of whether the view is defensible.
