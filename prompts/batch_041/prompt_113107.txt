I'm assembling a small pamphlet — part anthology, part family thing — and the last piece is meant to be a poem about a village that was drowned to make a reservoir: the church tower still standing above the waterline, the graves moved or not moved, the old road now a dive site for teenagers from the city that drinks the water. I don't want the usual elegy. I've been rereading Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I keep thinking that the three ways he says we can relate to the past — monumental, antiquarian, critical — are really three ways of looking at that drowned village, and that a poem could be built so that a reader feels the shift between them rather than being told about it.

So write the poem. I want three numbered movements, each taking up the same subject and the same handful of concrete details — the tower, the water, the drowned road, the people who had to leave — but each rendering it in one of the three modes. The same formal skeleton should govern all three: I'll leave the exact form to you, but the movements should be metrically and structurally parallel, close enough in length and shape that a reader notices the second is a rerun of the first with the temperature changed. I want a refrain — a single line, or a line and a half — that returns in every movement but does different work each time: in the first it should sound like praise or an invitation to imitate, in the second like piety or the keeping of a ledger, in the third like an indictment. Let the form come apart in the third movement, so that whatever held the first two together visibly fails, and let the poem end there rather than resolving.

Don't gloss the three modes inside the poem or in a note before it; if someone who knows the essay can't tell which movement is which, I've failed. And please don't give me the moralized version in which the critical movement is plainly the correct one — as I read him, Nietzsche's point is that each mode has its own sickness when it dominates, so I want each movement to be seductive on its own terms, including the one I'm least comfortable with. After the poem, add a short note — a paragraph or so — on the choices you made: why that form, why that refrain, and where the poem takes liberties with Nietzsche's account or leans on a reading of it that is genuinely contested. Commit to definite choices rather than hedging; I would rather have a poem with a shape I can argue with than a tasteful blur.
