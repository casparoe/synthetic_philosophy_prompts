I am leading the session at next month's meeting of our departmental rational-choice reading group, which is half philosophy graduate students and half economics graduate students, seventy-five minutes, one whiteboard, no handouts. The topic is money pumps, but I want the whole session organized around the difference between the synchronic and the diachronic versions of the argument, because that is precisely where I keep losing the economists in the room: they concede without blinking that an agent with a cyclic preference ordering can be traded around a circle in a single sitting, and then they cannot see what is supposed to be irrational about an agent whose preferences merely shift as time passes, and I am fairly sure the two cases are not doing the same work. Do not explain the material to me; plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place before the diachronic case can even be posed as a problem, and in what order, so that it feels like the continuation of one argument rather than the introduction of a second one? I assume at least three pieces are needed — some account of what it is for preferences to be indexed to times, some account of what a trade is when its two ends occur at different moments, and some account of whether the person doing the pumping has to be able to make binding commitments across those moments — but tell me the real dependency structure, including anything I have left out, and where in the sequence the pieces have to fall.

I want to open with a distinction I have been trying to get straight myself, between a money pump and a Dutch book, and to frame the session around the claim that a pump is really an assertion about the exploitability of a disposition rather than about the inconsistency of a preference set. Tell me whether that framing will carry seventy-five minutes or whether it will quietly mislead, and if it misleads, what the honest framing is. For the carrying example I had wanted something concrete and contemporary rather than the stock three-way swap: a subscriber who holds Plan C and whose preferences run A over B, B over C, and C over A, and who has a "change your plan at any time for a small fee" option, so that she moves C to B in January, B to A in April, and A back to C in July, ending exactly where she began and out three fees. Does that example do the work, or what does it lack? In particular, is the spacing across months doing anything essential or is it decoration, and what would the example need in order to make her loss read as a consequence of her preferences rather than of her gullibility? I am also unsure whether the same example can be stretched to cover the harder case in which the preferences themselves reverse with time rather than staying fixed in a cycle that is merely exercised at different dates, so tell me whether one case can carry both or whether I need a second.

Then give me the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface in that room, with the precise point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off. I already expect "so she is irrational because she loses money," "any intransitive preference is enough," "she could simply have resolved to stick with one plan, so nothing has been shown," and "a pump demonstrates something only if someone actually pumps her," but I do not trust my ordering and I expect at least one I have not anticipated. Finish with a single question I can put to the group at the end that would reveal whether they have understood why the diachronic case is a problem at all rather than merely a story about weakness of will, and tell me what I should openly refuse to settle in seventy-five minutes and say so out loud.
