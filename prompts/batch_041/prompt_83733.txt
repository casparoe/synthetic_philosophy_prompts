Its been bugging me for a while. Take the ulcer story. Two doctors in Perth in the early eighties, Robin Warren and Barry Marshall, find these spiral bacteria living in the stomach lining of people with ulcers and argue that ulcers are basically an infectious disease, not the product of stress and spicy food and too much acid. They get laughed at for about a decade — Marshall drank a brew of his own bacteria to give himself gastritis because nobody would listen — and then it flips, antibiotics become the standard treatment, Nobel in 2005. (That's the version in my head; correct me if I've mangled a detail, it doesn't matter much for what I'm after.)

Every telling of it has the same shape: brave outsiders against a blinkered establishment in the grip of dogma. What I want is a hindsight-bias audit of the telling rather than of the science. So: (1) What did a competent gastroenterologist actually have in front of them around 1983 — what evidence was live, which rival explanations hadn't been ruled out, what would you have needed to see to jump? (2) Then pull apart three things I suspect keep getting blended: creeping determinism (the outcome quietly reshaping what people remember having thought at the time), retrospective determinism (the claim that the outcome was inevitable given the causes), and straight presentism, i.e. judging the skeptics by what we know now. Which of those is doing the work in the standard story, and are they really three different things or one thing wearing three hats? (3) And are Warren and Marshall's own later accounts exempt from any of this, or is the hero's recollection just as outcome-colored as the villain's?

Then flip it. Hindsight bias is usually invoked in the direction of "we couldn't have known" — it functions as an excuse. Here the skeptics were in fact wrong and the evidence was in fact there, so does the episode support the hindsight-bias framework or embarrass it? Is there a difference between the skeptics having erred and their having been unreasonable, and what would you have to show to license the stronger claim? Last thing: the narrative fallacy. Is that an explanation of why the story takes this shape, or just a name for the shape? Give me a case where the narrative-fallacy diagnosis is wrong — where the tidy story about an episode is the true one, not a retrofit.
