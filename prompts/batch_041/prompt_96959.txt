Last night a friend and I spent three hours on Berkeley's master argument — his case that nothing can exist unperceived, which turns on the claim that you cannot so much as conceive of an object existing unconceived — and we got no closer to agreement, largely because we were answering different questions while using the same words. Please build the instrument that would have sorted us: a decision tree that carries a reader from answers about concrete cases to a definite position on whether anything can exist unperceived, and that classifies people by what they say about cases rather than by the label they favor.

Write it for two readers like us. We have each read Berkeley once, we are not specialists, and a first question of the form 'do you accept the inconceivability premise?' would be useless to us. Aim for eight to twelve branching questions and a comparable number of leaves. Each branching question should be a case or a claim that a reader can answer without seeing where it leads, and each branch should be labeled with the distinction the question turns on, not with a position name. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: settle the questions and the combinations of answers they allow first, then locate the positions in that space.

I want these cases on the tree, in whatever order the logic dictates: the tree in a park with no one and nothing around to perceive it; a rock on a planet no mind will ever visit; the state of the universe before any mind existed; the furniture in a room while its only occupant sleeps; an object that is in principle undetectable; a camera or sensor recording a scene with no one watching the feed; the objects in a dream; and the general question whether your inability to conceive of something shows that it cannot be. Include, too, the question of what you are actually doing when you imagine a tree no one perceives — whether that imagining is itself a perception of the tree — and the question of whether 'I cannot conceive of X' licenses 'X cannot exist'.

These positions need to appear as leaves, under whatever names you think fit, with 'unnamed' where you think no one holds the view: Berkeley's own, in which unperceived objects are kept in being by a permanent perceiver; the same idealism with no permanent perceiver, so that objects simply lapse when unobserved; the reduction of facts about unperceived objects to facts about what sensations you would have under certain conditions, again without God; the view that unperceived objects exist but only as appearances, never as they are in themselves; the flat denial of the premise, on the ground that we conceive of unperceived objects all the time; the reading on which the argument establishes only something about our concepts and nothing about the world; the reading on which it is a verbal illusion resting on an equivocation between imagining and conceiving; the view that there is no fact of the matter about whether an object exists while unperceived; and the view that 'an object no one could perceive' is not false but nonsensical, so that the question should be dissolved rather than answered. Keep the last two apart if you can, and if you cannot, say why.

Give each leaf one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Where the tree leaves a combination of answers with no position on it, say so and say why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you have asked, flag them as inseparable rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them. No reading lists or citations, please; I want the structure, not the scholarship.

Begin with the tree itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of what I have asked; the notes on branches, unoccupied combinations, and inseparable leaves can follow once the tree is laid out.
