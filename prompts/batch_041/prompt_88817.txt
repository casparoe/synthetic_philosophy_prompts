I've ended up pretty convinced by the Dennett line on qualia -- that the whole package (intrinsic, private, ineffable, directly apprehended properties of experience) is an incoherent posit, and that once you've said everything about what a creature can do, say, and discriminate, there's nothing left over to explain. The problem is I can't tell whether I've actually been argued into this or just talked myself into it. So: what would count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones the view's own defenders would concede really do count against them, as opposed to the stuff critics like to throw at it (zombies, Mary, the hard problem).

Three things I'd want you to deal with, because they're the ones that look most threatening to me. First, the partial-report result: flash a 3x4 grid of letters for a fraction of a second and people can only report four or so of them, but if you cue one row immediately afterwards with a tone they can report most of that row -- which some people read as showing that far more was consciously seen than could ever be reported, i.e. experience overflowing access. Second, so-called no-report setups, where you infer what someone is experiencing from eye movements or pupil dilation rather than from anything they say. Third, the Chase-and-Sanborn style case where a person says their coffee tastes different now and you can't tell whether the coffee changed or their standards did -- I've never been sure wether that cuts for or against the view.

Give it to me as exactly six numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion. One of the six should be your honest read on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- if every introspective report that looks like it counts against it just gets redescribed as another judgment, does anything count, and is that a problem for a view of this kind? Tag each item with how confident you are in it, and flag the one you're least sure of.
