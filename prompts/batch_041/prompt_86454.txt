So this is a weird one, but I think you'll enjoy it. I was at a two-week residency upstate and got stuck at the same dinner table every night with two people I now know far too much about: Marcus, an evolutionary psychologist who studies cooperation, and Delia, who teaches philosophy of biology and has spent alot of time around that field and clearly come away bruised. Somehow the conversation landed on whether fiction is an adaptation or a byproduct, and by night three it had turned into a whole running argument. I mostly just sat there eating bread, because both of them definately had a point and I honestly could not tell who was winning.

It went roughly like this. (1) Marcus: storytelling is universal, ancient, and expensive — people burn absurd amounts of time and emotional energy on events that never happened and can't feed them, and it turns up in every culture we know of — so the burden falls on anyone who says it's just a free rider on other capacities. His bet is that fiction is social simulation: practice for navigating coalitions, norms, status, other minds. (2) Delia: universality and cost don't get you adaptation. Language is universal too, and nobody thinks every use of it is a separate adaptation; and reading a novel is not that costly compared to, say, having a baby. She figures fiction is a byproduct of theory of mind plus language plus the fact that simulating is intrinsically rewarding, and she brought out the cheesecake line — our taste for sweet and fat is an adaptation, cheesecake is not. She also said his story was a just-so story: you can always invent the selection pressure after the fact. (3) Marcus: fine, but the byproduct view has to actually predict something and it doesn't. Fiction isn't a thin random sliver of culture — it's overwhelmingly about agents, intentions, betrayals, kinship, status — and it reliably triggers real grief and real arousal, which is what you'd expect if it were recruiting machinery built to model social life, and not what you'd expect from a spillover. He also cited those experiments where reading literary fiction supposedly boosts empathy scores. (4) Delia: those empathy studies got knocked down hard in replication, so they're not a load-bearing beam. And the content point cuts the other way — stories are about agents because agents are what our cognition is built to process, which is a claim about our cognition, not about fiction being designed. Cheesecake is also reliably about sweet and fat; that doesn't make cheesecake an adaptation. Then she pressed him on what finding would ever make him give up the hypothesis. (5) Marcus: he admitted half his colleagues think it's a courtship display instead — a costly signal of intelligence and creativity — and said that's fine, rival hypotheses are how the science works, they make different predictions and you go test them. Delia said the fact that the same evidence seems to underwrite two incompatible adaptations is kind of the whole tell.

Then they both turned to me, because I write novels for a living and was therefore apparently the expert. Can you actually adjudicate this move by move — rate each of the five from clearly wrong to clearly right and say why — and then tell me who's more right overall, and what each of them is missing? I'd especially like to know whether there's a version of Marcus's view that's genuinely testable rather than just plausible, whether Delia's byproduct line is smuggling in an assumption she won't own up to, and whether it should change anyone's mind that I personally make my living off the thing we were arguing about. Please don't be gentle with me on that last part.
