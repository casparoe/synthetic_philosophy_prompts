A reading group I'm in has been circling the question of how moral revolutions actually happen — why practices like chattel slavery, the aristocratic duel, and footbinding, each of which had able and well-known critics for a very long time, nonetheless ended when they ended and not generations earlier. I walked in assuming the answer was basically "the arguments finally won." I've walked out holding an argument, built mostly by a friend in the group, that I can neither refute nor accept, and that seems to prove considerably more than he wants it to. Here it is. (1) A moral revolution, as we're using the term, is a large and durable change in what people actually do and in what they will tolerate being done — not merely a change in what they will concede when cornered in an argument. (2) For slavery, the core abolitionist argument — that the practice treats beings who can reason and suffer as mere tools, and that no one is born owning another — was available, in something close to its final form, long before the practice was actually abolished; much the same holds for the case against the duel and the case against footbinding. (3) When a consideration is fully available and unchanged across the entire period in which the outcome also remains unchanged, that consideration is not what produced the change. (4) Therefore the arguments did not produce these revolutions. (5) What produced them were shifts in what the powerful stood to gain and in what their honor and status required of them: the dueling class abandoned the duel roughly when it stopped reading as courage and started reading as thuggishness, and slaveholding elites abandoned slavery when the economics and the politics of it turned. (6) Generalizing: in moral revolutions, argument is epiphenomenal — it arrives after the fact to ratify a change that interest and honor have already made, and its job is to make the new arrangement articulable rather than to bring it about. (7) If argument is epiphenomenal in that way, then arguing well rather than badly makes no difference to what actually happens, and the reformer's real work is theater — or at best a species of bookkeeping. A fourth member of the group keeps proposing a repair: that we should distinguish a cause from an enabling condition, and that arguments are enabling conditions of revolutions whose trigger is interest or honor. Nobody in the room agrees on whether that rescues anything or just relabels the concession. (I have a vague sense the whole line of thought is downstream of something Bernard Williams wrote, but I can't recall where, so please don't lean on the attribution — I care about the argument, not its lineage.)

I can't accept (7); it makes the petitions and the testimony and the pamphlets into decoration, and I don't believe that, and I'd rather keep (2) and (5) than give (8) up. But I also can't see where the chain breaks, and every repair I've tried on my own either reinstates the naive "arguments are decisive" view I started with or quietly keeps the epiphenomenality and just calls it something nicer. So: tell me which step to reject, and be honest about whether the argument is valid and the real question is which plausible premise is cheapest to surrender, or whether there's a subtle slide I'm missing. If it is a slide, explain why the step looked fine, then price the three packages for me — argument-primary, honor-primary, and interest-primary — including what each has to say about the fact that the same arguments sat there unused for centuries, and what each owes us in the way of a mechanism that would make the timing come out right. Finally, since I'd like this to be more than a verbal dispute: say what evidence would actually discriminate between the packages, and whether the fourth member's cause-versus-enabling-condition distinction is a genuine repair or only a way of having (6) and denying (7) at the same time.
