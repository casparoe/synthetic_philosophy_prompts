A friend of mine and I have been argueing about the Kripke/Wittgenstein rule-following stuff for like half a year now and we can't even agree on what we're disagreeing about. He's a straight-up dispositionalist: to mean plus is just to be disposed to give the answers the plus-rule gives, and if your dispositions don't reach to some future case then you don't mean anything determinate about that case yet, tough luck. I think Kripke's skeptic kills that, and he thinks the skeptic only kills the naive version. Fine, the finiteness objection is real (my dispositions are finite, the rule isn't), but I say the normativity one is definately the one that matters: I can be disposed to add wrong, and my being disposed to say '5' after 57+68 wouldn't make '5' right. Once you take that seriously the only thing left is the community — correctness is whatever the community would accept as going on the same way. My problem with that is it seems to help itself to the notion of the community responding the same way, which is the thing we were supposed to be explaining.

Write the joint statement we should have produced, as an adversarial collaboration. Claims we can both sign, about what the skeptical argument does and doesn't show, about what an account of meaning owes us, about what all the 'same rule' talk is doing. Then our actual disagreements, each phrased in wording the other side would accept as fair. Then the cruxes. Two cases I want in there: (a) a small net trained on arithmetic that answers the sums it never saw the quus way — does either of us have to say it means plus, and does the dispositionalist story even apply to a system whose dispositions you read off its weights? (b) two sub-communities that split on how to extend some term, where I claim the community view can't say whether they mean different things or whether one of them is just wrong, and he claims it can. For each case, both our predictions, written down before we start arguing about it again.

Numbered agreed claims, then a disagreements table.
