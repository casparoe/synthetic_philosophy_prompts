I am presenting a paper at a decision-theory workshop next month and need a Q&A preparation sheet. The audience consists mostly of formal choice theorists working in the revealed-preference and axiomatic tradition; there will also be two or three behavioral economists, a couple of people from the Simon-inspired bounded-rationality tradition, and one or two philosophers of economics. They care about representation theorems, about whether a proposed distinction carries any behavioral content, and about whether a model is doing genuine explanatory work or merely relabeling.

My thesis is that the way bounded rationality is usually modeled today — as optimization subject to a budget on information processing, in the style of rational-inattention models — is not a version of satisficing at all, and that Simon's original notion contains a feature these models cannot capture. That feature is what I call the fixity of the aspiration level. I distinguish two things the literature tends to run together: aspiration as threshold, in which the aspiration level functions as a stopping rule that could in principle be derived from a preference ordering over search paths, and aspiration as standing commitment, in which the agent holds the level fixed across the whole search episode and treats it as not revisable by the search itself. My claim is that only the second is genuinely non-maximizing, that the first collapses into constrained optimization — which is why rational-inattention models can represent threshold satisficing without remainder but cannot represent standing-commitment satisficing at all — and that the fixity, not the mere presence of a threshold, is what makes the stopping decision rational without making it maximizing.

Prepare me for the question period: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper, each phrased the way one of these people would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Where a concession is cheap, say so; where I should hold the line, say why, and identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.

Refer to positions and arguments rather than citing works — I will supply the references myself, so no bibliography, reading list, or citations. Keep each reply within two minutes of speech. Write in American English. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in a short phrase, the single objection most needing to be defused for the paper to survive.
