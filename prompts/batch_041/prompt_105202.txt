Alright, I've been arguing for a view about repugnant markets and I want you to try to break it rather than just agree with it. The view: the reason kidney sales, vote selling, and price gouging after a disaster strike us as wrong isn't that money corrupts some fragile value the thing has. It's that the transaction broadcasts a message about what kind of society we're in -- it tells everyone that this is the kind of thing that's up for sale, and that in turn reshapes what people think the thing is even for. Call it the billboard view. If thats right, two things should follow. First, the wrongness is essentially public: a kidney sale that literally no one ever learns about, and that has no knock-on effect on anyone's expectations, isn't wrong at all. Second, the real fight in these debates is almost never about the character of the buyer or the seller but about the audience watching. So what would count against this? I want the cases and arguments that should genuinely move me, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, including the ones a defender of the view would themselves have to concede. And give me an honest call on whether I've just made the whole thing unfalsifiable by tacking on "and no knock-on effects."
