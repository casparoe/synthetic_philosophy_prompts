For the past several weeks a reading group I'm in has been working through rational choice and decision theory, and I've come away with the uncomfortable discovery that my own verdicts about what it is rational to do refuse to line up behind any single story. I'd like you to help me find the tightest principle that fits them — or to show me that none can. Here are my gut verdicts. (1) In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against an anonymous stranger — no repetition, no reputation, no chance he ever learns what I did — I defect, and I feel no pull toward anything else. (2) In the same Prisoner's Dilemma, except that the other player is a duplicate of me made moments before we choose, who will run the same deliberation and reach the same choice, I cooperate; defecting there strikes me as a kind of incoherence, as if I were betting against myself. (3) In Newcomb's problem — two boxes, one transparent and holding a thousand dollars, one opaque, and a predictor who has called it correctly a hundred times running and has already placed the million in the opaque box or not according to what he foresaw — I take only the opaque box, and no causal story I tell myself dislodges that verdict. (4) In a contest whose entire prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries, where each entrant may submit any number of entries by writing a single number on a postcard and the prize goes to the holder of a randomly drawn entry, my instinct is to write '1', even though I can see the arithmetic that says to write an astronomically large number. (5) In a variant where twenty people are assembled, forbidden to communicate, and told that if exactly one of them sends a collect telegram by noon tomorrow, that person wins a billion dollars, while if none or several send one nobody wins anything, I feel I should send one with some modest probability, and I would not treat the telegram as a sure thing.

What troubles me is that (2), (3), (4), and (5) all seem to be driven by a principle that forbids me to treat my own choice as an isolated event — something like: choose as you would have everyone symmetrically placed with you choose, or: let your choice be guided by what it is evidence about the choices of others — while (1) is the plain case of simply taking the option that pays me best whatever the other man does. My tentative attempt at a principle is that I should defect precisely when the other party's choice is causally independent of mine, and cooperate or randomize when it is not; but I cannot tell whether that survives (3), where the predictor's act is, on any ordinary causal reading, already over and done with before I choose. The case I keep going back and forth on is a Prisoner's Dilemma against a stranger whom I know to have been paired with me only because we were both screened as people who tend to reason alike — nothing about his choice is caused by mine, and yet the correlation makes me hesitate.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of clearly separated ones, that yields all five verdicts? Please state it as precisely as you can, name which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon if it cannot have them all, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I have not described: a Prisoner's Dilemma against a stranger whom I have good evidence to be a committed cooperator of the kind who would himself cooperate in case (2); and the twenty-person telegram setup with the twist that I have good reason to believe the other nineteen have all resolved to abstain. If the only principle that saves every verdict is a disjunctive or gerrymandered one, I would rather know that plainly than have it dressed up; I am less interested in being told the questions are ill-posed than in seeing the tightest principle that fits, with an honest account of where it breaks.
