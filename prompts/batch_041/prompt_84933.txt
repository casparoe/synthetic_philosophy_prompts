I am preparing a seminar on temporal consciousness, and I want to use Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) as a test case for Daniel Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of experience. The distinction, as I understand it, is meant to dissolve a puzzle about the color phi phenomenon: subjects report seeing a dot change color midway along its apparent path, before the second dot has even been presented, which is impossible if experience is assembled in real time. Either the experience is edited after the fact, in the manner of Orwell's Ministry of Truth, or the content is only published to consciousness after the relevant processing is complete, in the manner of a show trial with a predetermined verdict. Dennett's claim is that once we see how both stories can accommodate the data, we should stop asking which one is true, because there is no fact of the matter about the timing of the experience itself. I want my students to ask whether Rashomon dramatizes that same dissolution or whether it quietly assumes the opposite.

Please give me a reading of the film that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously. In particular: does the film's structure of four incompatible testimonies — the bandit's, the wife's, the samurai's (delivered through a medium), and the woodcutter's — illustrate the Orwellian and Stalinesque options, or does it trade on a different phenomenon altogether, namely the difference between what was experienced and what is reported? I suspect the film is mostly about lies, shame, and self-serving narrative, not about the timing of sensory content, and I would like you to say so if that is right, rather than forcing the analogy. But I also want you to consider a case the film raises that Dennett's dichotomy does not obviously cover: the samurai speaks through a medium, so there is no continuous subject whose experience could have been revised either before or after the fact. What does that do to the distinction? Does it show that the distinction presupposes a unified subject in a way that is not innocent, or can it be extended to cover such cases?

I would also like you to construct, as a thought experiment, a variant of the color phi experiment that I have been calling the Rashomon phi. In this variant, two observers watch the same brief display and give incompatible reports about when the color change occurred — one reports it before the second flash, the other after. The experimenter must decide whether to treat the disagreement as a difference in the timing of their experiences, as a difference in their post-hoc reconstructions, or as evidence that the question of timing has no determinate answer. What would Dennett say about this case, and is that answer satisfying? Where does the film anticipate or resist that answer?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of where Rashomon resists the philosophical reading. The frame narrative at the Rashomon gate, with the commoner's cynicism and the priest's distress, and the final scene with the abandoned baby, seem to me to pull against a purely deflationary reading of the testimonies. If the film's ending suggests that some moral fact survives the collapse of factual agreement, does that commit it to a fact of the matter that Dennett's account would deny? Or can the two be reconciled? I am less interested in a verdict on the film than in a reading that shows what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the color phi literature does not.
