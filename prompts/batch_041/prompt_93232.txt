I'm a PhD student in normative economics, and I'm writing a piece for the satire issue of our departmental magazine. I'd like you to draft the internal adjustment manual of a mutual insurance cooperative that has adopted Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as its official doctrine of distributive fairness. In this world, every contested payout is settled by asking what arrangement a member would endorse if she thought she were equally likely to turn out to be any of the cooperative's members, and then maximizing expected social welfare; the target of the satire is our habit of treating that result as a costless derivation of utilitarianism from thin assumptions about rationality. Write it as a sequence of numbered clauses in the flat, self-satisfied register of a compliance department, roughly 600 words, and let the comedy come from the theorem's actual joints rather than from generic jokes at utilitarianism's expense. The fine print should quietly take positions on: whether the parties' preferences are assumed to satisfy the expected-utility axioms and what the manual does with a member whose preferences are incoherent under risk; whether the weighted-sum conclusion follows from the Pareto principle or is simply assumed; what the equal-probability assumption is doing, and whether it is what licenses equal weights rather than some other weighting; whether the derivation needs interpersonal comparisons of utility or is designed to avoid them; and the member whose preferences rank another member's misfortune above her own gain, whom the manual must accommodate as well, since the aggregation is a weighted sum over whatever preferences people happen to have. Include one clause on members whose preferences depend on other members' outcomes, and a closing clause that converts the veil of ignorance into an actuarial procedure with a premium schedule. A reader who knows the theorem should find it fair and funny; a reader who doesn't should still be able to follow the plot.
