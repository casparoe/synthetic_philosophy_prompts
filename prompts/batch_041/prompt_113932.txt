I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, and I've been assigned the presentation on Wigner's old puzzle about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics — why structures developed with no physical application in view keep turning out to be exactly the language some later physics turns out to need. I don't want to just summarize the usual positions; I want to make a defensible claim about which of the proposed explanations actually do any explanatory work, so I've drawn up the list below and I'd like you to tier it for me. Treat the tier list as the main deliverable: put each item in one of four tiers — does the heavy lifting, genuinely contributes, gestures at something true but explains little, fails — and give one or two sentences per item justifying the placement. Please resist the temptation to put everything in the middle tier; I'd rather you commit and be wrong than hedge. A friend in the seminar insists that the first and third items below are the whole story and that everything else is mystification, which I don't believe, but I'd like the tiers to make clear where I'm parting company with him.

Here are the candidates as I've written them up for my slides. (1) The selection effect: we count the hits and forget the misses, and the regions of the world we bother to mathematize are precisely the ones that admit it. (2) The evolutionary-psychological line: our cognitive machinery was shaped by a world containing regularities, and mathematics is the residue of that shaping, so the fit is unsurprising. (3) The deflationary line: mathematics is a toolkit we build and rebuild until it fits, so effectiveness measures our willingness to revise rather than any pre-existing harmony between mind and world. (4) The Platonist or structuralist line: the world just is a mathematical structure, so there is nothing here to be explained. (5) The anthropocentric line: the world is somehow built to be tractable to creatures like us, so the fit is a fact about us as much as about the world. (6) The line that mathematics is downstream of physics: the applications that impress us are artefacts of how mathematics was historically shaped by physical problems. (7) The line that every consistent mathematical structure is physically instantiated somewhere, so finding one in nature is a selection effect of a different kind.

Along with the tiers, I'd like three further things. First, rate each candidate on a second axis: does it make any further claim that could turn out to be false, or does it merely restate the phenomenon in different words? I suspect several of these are unfalsifiable redescription, and I want to know which ones, even if that means demoting something I've placed high. Second, tell me which items are parasitic on which — for instance, whether the deflationary and selection-effect lines can be held independently of each other, or whether one collapses into the other once you press on it. Third, if I want the strongest single position on the puzzle, which two or three of these could I combine without contradiction, and which combination would I be foolish to try to hold together?
