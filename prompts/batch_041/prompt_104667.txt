I've been chewing on something for a while and I could use a second pair of eyes, so here goes. I'm a backend engineer by trade — no philosophy degree, so forgive me if I use the wrong words for things.

There's this Milton Friedman idea (I think people call it the F-twist? not sure) where he says a theory's assumptions don't have to be realistic, it only matters whether the predictions come out right. That always bugged me a bit but I never had a reason to care, until now, because I want to write a blog post riffing on it and I'd like you to tell me whether the arguement actually holds water or falls apart.

My rough idea goes like this. In software we model things using assumptions we know are false, all the time. Last year I sized a cluster using a queueing model that assumed requests arrive as a Poisson process and that the queue has infinite memory. Neither is true of our system — bursts are not Poisson and memory is very much finite — but the model predicted our p99 latency well enough to stop us over-provisioning, which saved a fortune. So far, so Friedman, fine.

But here's the bit I actually want to push on. I want to claim there are two different kinds of false assumption and Friedman's defence only rescues one of them. The first kind, which I'm calling an idealisation, is false but it's pointing at something real in the target — like treating customers as infinitely patient, or a frictionless plane, or prices as continuous. The falsehood is doing honest work. The second kind, which I'm calling scaffolding, is just there to make the maths go through and doesn't correspond to anything in the world at all — assuming infinite memory, assuming every latency is exponential, assuming the traffic is stationary. And my claim is: for scaffolding assumptions the model can be beautifully predictive and still tell you nothing, because you've smuggled the answer in through the back door. The prediction is right for reasons that have nothing to do with why the thing works.

So what I'd love from you:

(1) Does that idealisation/scaffolding distinction survive contact with an actual philosopher, or have I just invented a distinction that dissolves the moment you look at it hard? I have a nagging feeling it collapses into something more boring.
(2) Am I mangling Friedman? If I'm going to name-drop him in a blog post I'd rather not be wrong about what he actually claimed.
(3) Can you think of a case where a purely scaffolding assumption — something with no counterpart in reality whatsoever — nonetheless led to genuine insight, not just a good number? If you can, that's probably a problem for me.
(4) Honestly, would this be interesting to anyone who isn't me, or is it one of those things that only feels deep at 1am?

Please don't just tell me it's a lovely idea and then move on — I'd much rather you poke holes in it. Tell me where it's weakest.
