A mate of mine did a history MA and is now definately of the view that he can do the whole objectivity debate in one paragraph. He's writen it out for me because I've got to teach a session on it next term and I've never properly read up on it. His version:

Ranke started it all by saying the historian should just show how it actually was and keep himself out of it completely — no interpretation, no theory, just the documents. Carr then showed that this can't be done, because facts don't come with labels on them and the historian is the one who turns a fact of the past into a historical fact, so really there are no facts, only interpretations. White finished the job: history is just another kind of fiction, a story with a beginning, middle and end, and since he boiled it all down to four ways of writing, you could emplot anything as tragedy or comedy and it wouldn't matter which, because it's form all the way down and truth is just a matter of the shape you pick. The moral is that every history is really a political intervention, and the only honest thing is to say you're making it up as you go.

I'm fairly sure this is too tidy, but I can't quite put my finger on where it goes wrong, and I'd rather not stand in front of a room and bluff. Go through it a claim at a time: what would a historian who actually works on this accept, what's a crude simplification with a kernel of truth in it, and what would Ranke, Carr or White themselves have rejected out of hand? What I'm most unsure about is the last bit — does anything the three of them held actually license the idea that all history is politics and so anything goes, or would each of them have denied it? And if I've got fifty minutes with sixth-formers, which bit of his paragraph do I have to unteach first? Don't send me a reading list, I won't get through it.
