ok so a slightly weird ask. My old supervisor died a few weeks ago — he was a physicist his whole life and an Einstein obsessive in the philosophical sense: Spinozist to the core, no personal god, no afterlife, and a hard determinist who really did think the sense that you "could have done otherwise" was an illusion that evaporates once you actually see all the causes. He was also, weirdly, the warmest and most forgiving person I've ever known, and he insisted the two things went together — that seeing everything as caused is what makes you gentle with people instead of blaming them. I never quite believed him but I never worked out why.

I'm giving the memorial remarks at the department thing in two weeks. Half the room are physicists and grad students who knew exactly what he thought; the other half is his family, some of whom are religious and are hoping to hear that he's somewhere now, or at peace, or that god took him home. I can't say any of that — he'd have called it false and it'd feel like a betrayal. But I also can't just deliver a seminar; it has to actually comfort people.

Can you write it, maybe six or seven minutes? And build it around the thing that always bugged me: how is warmth, and forgiveness, and moral seriousness supposed to fall out of a picture where nobody was ever free and the self is basically a useful illusion? He thought it followed; use that as the spine of the thing rather than something to smooth over. Don't sand him into a saint and don't pretend determinism is a consolation it isn't — but land it somewhere both halves of the room can actually be in.
