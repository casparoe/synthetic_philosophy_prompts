Since last spring I have served as the volunteer data lead for a regional nonprofit that runs a program matching care coordinators — nurses and social workers who make home visits and help untangle benefits, medications, and appointments — with patients likely to need a great deal of help over the coming year. We have roughly four hundred coordinator slots and about forty thousand patients in our network, so we built a model that scores patients and ranks them for outreach. By the usual measures the model performs well: its scores line up closely with how often patients actually end up in crisis, and among the patients it flags, the share who genuinely turn out to need intensive help is about the same in every patient group we can identify. That last property is what has split our board. A board member has noticed that because one group in our network carries a substantially higher burden of illness overall, a model with that property will flag that group at a higher rate; she calls this targeting, and she wants us to adjust the model until each group is flagged at the same rate. Our chief medical officer counters that the model is simply reporting the truth and that the truth is unequal — and that equal flag rates would mean turning away, from the higher-burden group, patients who are sicker than some of the people we would enroll from the other group, simply because that group's baseline is higher. There is a further wrinkle I cannot get out of my head: the thing we are predicting is, in effect, how much care a person consumed last year — claims, visits, admissions — and we all know that tracks access to care at least as much as it tracks illness. The group with the higher burden is the group with the worse access, so I am no longer confident that the truth the model reports is the one we think we are measuring.

Two other proposals are on the table. One colleague wants to strip out group membership entirely and simply rank everyone by score, on the grounds that if we do not look at the categories we cannot be discriminating against anyone. Another suggests abandoning the ranking altogether and holding a lottery among everyone above a fixed threshold, since a lottery is the one thing no one can call biased. My instinct is that both proposals are dodging something, but I cannot say precisely what, and I am wary of choosing whichever option lets me feel cleanest.

I have a vote on the committee and need to bring a recommendation in two weeks, so what I want from you is not a survey but an argument I can defend in the room. Tell me whether there is a principled answer here or only a choice among harms; if there is an answer, which considerations should settle it and which are red herrings. If this is a known form of conflict rather than a local mess, tell me what its general shape is and where its hard edges lie. I would like to know whether the board member's demand is even internally coherent, whether anything can satisfy both what she wants and what the medical officer wants, and how much the label problem changes the analysis — does the fact that we are predicting care consumption rather than need mean this whole dispute sits downstream of a measurement error we should fix first? Then give me a concrete recommendation, and be honest about who bears the cost of it and who bears the cost of the alternatives. Tell me what I should say to the board member and to the medical officer, and what I should concede to each. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need to understand the shape of the problem well enough to argue for one option without pretending the others are stupid.
