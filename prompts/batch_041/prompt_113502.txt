I teach a 90-minute session of an advanced undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of economics and rational choice, with twelve to sixteen students. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel, from the inside, the force of the idea that a person's preferences are constituted by what they choose rather than merely measured by it — and then makes them feel what that identification costs. The students should not hear the name of the idea, or any of the standard terminology surrounding it, until the debrief. Nothing should be lectured up front.

The mechanic I want, which you should develop in detail and may improve on but should preserve in outline, has two roles. The choosers may only choose: they may never rank items, state preferences, or explain themselves, and the facilitator should enforce this with a visible rule. The analysts watch the choosers, may not put questions to them, and after each block must write down a complete ordering of the items together with a numerical index, using any numbers they like, consistent with everything they have seen. The analysts then stake something on their index by predicting unseen choices.

The activity should escalate through five stages, and I want each stage to be a change in the rules rather than a new speech from me. First, ordinary pairwise choices among a small pool of items that are cheap to distribute and trivial in value — a snack, a few minutes of break, a token that buys a small privilege. Second, a probe in which the composition of the choice set changes while the same pair of items remains available, so that some choosers flip their choice over that pair once a third, unattractive option joins the menu; the analysts must revise their index and should not be told in advance that this will happen. Third, a separate numeric probe: each chooser names a price, in tokens, at which they would part with each item, and the analysts construct a second ordering from the prices. For some choosers this ordering will conflict with the ordering built from choices, and I want the analysts to have to say which one is the person's utility. Fourth, an invitation to a trade cycle: the chooser is offered the items two at a time around a loop and pays one token per accepted trade. Any chooser who accepts every step walks all the way around and is worse off in tokens while holding what they started with. Fifth, a brief in which the analysts, now cast as advisers to a benevolent planner, must recommend whether the planner should block the chooser's access to one item on the ground that it lowers the chooser's utility. The index they have built cannot license that recommendation, and I want them to find out why by trying to write it.

After the choice stages, and before any discussion, each chooser privately rates their satisfaction with each item on a simple dial. For some participants the dial will disagree with what they chose and with what they said the items were worth. I want the debrief to open on that disagreement and to resist resolving it too quickly, since the whole point is that the students should feel the pull of two incompatible readings of utility rather than be handed the correct one.

The debrief must do four things. It should map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the idea: the difference between treating choice as evidence of a preference and treating it as the definition of one; the difference between an ordering and a measurement, and what the analysts could never answer with an index that only orders; why the pure reading makes the planner's criticism incoherent rather than merely hard to justify; and what the price probe does to the story, since it smuggles in a cardinal quantity the choice data were supposed to supply. It should tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect at least that some choosers will refuse to walk the cycle, that some analysts will break the rules by asking the choosers what they like, and that the group will be too polite to make each other worse off — and how to handle each failure without wrecking the effect. It should be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. And it should end by naming the idea and its terminology.

Constraints: no real money, and tokens must convert to something trivial or to nothing at all; no technology beyond a whiteboard, index cards, and printed tally sheets; no reading assigned beforehand and no citations anywhere in the plan. Give me a runnable design — materials and how to prepare them, role assignment, the full rules, the token scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the ninety minutes including the debrief, and the facilitator's moves at each transition. Take care that no participant is put in the position of having their tastes publicly diagnosed; the discomfort should be conceptual, not personal.
