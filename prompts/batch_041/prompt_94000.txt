Two research camps have argued for years about what is really happening when someone — or something — induces a concept from a handful of examples, and they have agreed to write the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration. The shared testbed is the Bongard-style problem: a pair of sets of items, typically six per side, distinguished by some property, where the solver must find the distinction and then classify new items. In this document the problems are given as text descriptions and small relational encodings rather than as pictures, so that nothing turns on visual perception.

The first camp (call them the Rule-First camp) holds that to induce a concept is to recover a discrete, recombinable rule — a predicate over features and relations — and that generalization is the application of that rule; among rules that fit the examples, the one with the shorter description is the one to keep; and a solver that cannot state its rule has not solved the problem. The second camp (the Similarity-First camp) holds that concepts are graded similarity structures, that solving a problem consists in aligning a learned similarity metric with the partition, that any rule a solver states is a post-hoc description rather than the mechanism, and that the metric is what generalizes.

Assume the collaboration has settled on a battery of problem families that both sides have agreed to use:

1. Negative-existential problems, in which the distinguishing property is the absence of a relation — for example, no part of a figure contains another part — so that there is nothing positive for a similarity measure to lock onto.
2. Second-order problems, in which the distinguishing property is a relation between the two sets rather than a property of any item: every item on one side is the image of some item on the other under a transformation, and the transformation itself varies from example to example.
3. Deceptive-similarity problems, in which surface similarity is high within each side but the correct distinction is an abstract property shared by items that look dissimilar, so the obvious clusters cut across the partition.
4. Re-encoding pairs, in which the same abstract distinction is instantiated twice, once geometrically and once in an invented non-visual notation — a table of labeled parts and relations — with the two instances presented together.
5. One-shot problems, with a single item on each side.
6. Set-level problems, in which the distinguishing property belongs to the whole set rather than to any item — for example, the items on one side jointly form a closed chain under some relation.
7. Ambiguity problems, in which two different rules fit every given example and diverge on the probe items.

Write the joint statement. It must be a document both camps could put their names to, and it should have four parts.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what both camps accept about what a Bongard-style problem is, what counts as solving one, how the battery should be scored, what the battery can and cannot establish, and what a fair test would look like. Include only claims that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and say explicitly which of the families above both camps agree are fair tests.

Second, a table of the remaining disagreements. For each, give the question in dispute; the Rule-First camp's formulation of its own position, in wording that camp would accept; the Similarity-First camp's formulation of its own position, in wording that camp would accept; and what each side takes to be the crux of that row. At a minimum, cover the format of a concept; whether a rule a solver states must be the mechanism that generalizes; what success or failure on the re-encoding pairs shows; whether the ambiguity problems have a determinate correct answer; and whether solving is a binary or a graded matter.

Third, the cruxes: for each, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp, together with the probe, case, or argument that both camps agree in advance bears on it, and each camp's prediction recorded before the fact. The predictions must be specific enough that the two camps would disagree about what to expect, and the opposing camp must be able to say in advance what it would concede if the prediction failed.

Fourth, a short section identifying which of the disputes look verbal or terminological and which are genuine, with reasons.

Write in American English, use no names or citations, and keep the whole statement to roughly 1,200 words.
