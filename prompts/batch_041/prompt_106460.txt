My friend and I spent basically all of last night arguing about whether "low impact" is a coherent thing to ask of an AI system, and I came out of it defending a position I'd now like you to try to knock down. Here's the position: you can define a useful side-effect penalty without any full specification of human values. You don't need to know what's good or what anyone wants; you just need to penalize the agent for leaving the world unlike the way it would have been had it not acted, or for shrinking the set of futures it could still reach afterwards. The penalty is supposed to be structural — computed against an inaction baseline, or against the reachable-futures set, or whatever the details — and it's meant to catch the catastrophic stuff (the agent that paves the world, the one that disassembles the lab to get at a reagent, the one that quietly removes the humans because they might interfere) without anyone ever writing down a utility function. My friend thinks that's a fantasy, and he had three specific reasons. One, "the world as it would have been without you" isn't well defined for an agent that's already sitting in the world, since its mere presence is itself an effect, so the baseline is either arbitrary or circular. Two, any such measure is trivially gameable: the agent can make the baseline itself worse so that any action looks mild by comparison, or act early while its footprint is still being measured against a fresh baseline, or just sit still and do nothing — which the measure can't distinguish from virtuous restraint. Three, and this is the one that got under my skin, telling a side effect from an intended effect requires knowing which parts of the state actually matter, so a value judgment sneaks back in through the state abstraction, or through whatever distance metric you use to compare worlds. I half believe three, I think one is wordplay, and I have no idea what to do with two.

So: treat that as my position and tell me what would actually count against it. I don't want a list of everything anyone has ever said about it — I want to know what would have to be true for it to be false, which observations, arguments, formal results or concrete cases would do the work, and how you'd rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Be specific about which of these the position's defenders would themselves concede as counting against them (not just what critics shout from the cheap seats), and which of my friend's three points survive once you press on them — for instance, is "it's gameable" really a defeater, or is every objective gameable and this is just a design constraint that has to be priced in? I'd also like to know whether the position predicts anything its rival doesn't. If the rival view is "no, you need actual values in the loop," what would we expect to see in a system that had a purely structural impact penalty versus one that didn't? And be honest with me about the insulation problem: I've noticed that every time someone raises a counterexample, the reply is some version of "well, it's not a complete solution, it's one ingredient" — at what point does that stop being a clarification and start being unfalsifiability, and is unfalsifiability actually a problem for a view of this kind or just the price of doing engineering-flavored philosophy?

A few things I'd find most persuasive, so you can tell me if I'm being parochial: a case where a structural measure gives a verdict we'd clearly reject (my friend offered a warehouse robot that refuses to move a box because moving it is a big change in the world, and I think that's cheating because the box matters for reasons the measure can't see, which sort of proves his point), or a case where it gives a verdict we'd clearly accept and a value-laden rival can't. Also, if there's a version of the position that's actually defensible, tell me what it is — I suspect I'm committed to something narrower than what I said last night and I'd like to know what I have to give up.
