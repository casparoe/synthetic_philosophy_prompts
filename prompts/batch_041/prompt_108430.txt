I have no background in philosophy or economics, but I've been chewing on a line I keep running into under the heading of Goodhart's law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." I've now heard it do very different kinds of work in different arguments, and I suspect it is several claims wearing a single sentence.

I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher of science with a specialist's grasp of measurement, incentives, and the philosophy of the social sciences, and to reason slowly and carefully rather than reaching for the standard commentary. Please begin by brainstorming on the page — all the genuinely distinct things that sentence could be asserting — before you commit to any verdict. I would much rather see the full menu of readings than a single preferred one explained at length.

Here is where I've actually met the line, since I think the reading that matters is usually the one the surrounding argument needed:

1. A colleague in a teaching-review meeting used it to argue that any attempt to measure teaching quality is doomed, and that we should therefore abandon measurement altogether.
2. Someone in a reading group used it to claim that if a metric gets gamed, then the underlying goal the metric was supposed to capture was never real to begin with.
3. A friend used it about a fitness app's step counter to mean something much weaker — that once you chase a number, the number stops tracking what you cared about.

I've also tried to sort the readings myself, and I've come up with some rough labels. Please treat these as placeholders to be sharpened, renamed, corrected, or discarded, and tell me which are really the same claim in different clothes:

- a tautology reading, on which "good measure" just means "a measure nobody has an incentive to distort," so the sentence is true by definition;
- a regression reading, on which the point is statistical and has nothing to do with incentives — that selecting on the top of a noisy measure drags you back toward the mean;
- a broken-ruler reading, on which the measure is only ever an effect of the goal rather than a cause of it, so pushing on the measure leaves the goal where it was;
- a moved-goalposts reading, on which the meaning of the metric, or of the goal itself, shifts once people start arguing about it;
- a price-of-signal reading, on which the measure was informative only because nobody was optimizing it, and optimization makes it cheap to fake;
- and a not-a-law-at-all reading, on which the sentence names a loose family of mechanisms, some inevitable and some dependent on particular institutions.

For each reading, please tell me: what precise claim it makes; whether it is trivial, analytic, substantive, or actually false; whether it is supposed to be necessary or merely a contingent tendency; and whether the "ceases to be a good measure" part concerns the measure losing its epistemic value (you can no longer learn from it) or its instrumental value (you can no longer use it to get what you want). I suspect those two come apart, and I want to know how.

Then, and this is what I most need: say for each of the three uses above which reading or readings would actually support it, and whether any reading makes the stronger versions (uses 1 and 2) defensible. I'm fairly sure the sentence is doing illicit work in at least one of those arguments, but I can't yet say which reading would have to be true for the argument to go through, or whether any plausible reading delivers it.

Finally, tell me what the sentence would have to be hedged into in order to be true — the conditions under which measuring and targeting actually do work fine — and give me a short list of test questions I could put to the next person who deploys the line, so that I can tell which reading they mean.
