I'm a science journalist — I mostly cover physics and cognitive science, and I've been circling a story idea I can't quite crack. There's a principle in formal epistemology, usually pinned on Bas van Fraassen, the reflection principle, that says roughly this: if you're sure that at some future time you'll hold credence x in some proposition, then you should already hold credence x in it right now — your future self's opinions count as evidence about the world that you're obliged to defer to. Which sounds tidy until you notice it means a mere forecast about what you'll end up thinking can boss your present beliefs around. There's also a betting argument people give for it, something about how if you violate the principle you can be made to lose money no matter how things turn out, and I keep thinking that would make a great prop in a story. So I want you to write me a short story that puts the principle under a stress test rather than just illustrating it.

Here's the seed I keep coming back to. A near-future research clinic has a machine — call it a belief chronometer — that scans someone and reports, with high reliability, what credence they'll hold in a given proposition some years down the line. My protagonist is a hospital ethicist, or maybe a journalist, someone whose whole job is weighing evidence for a living, and she gets scanned as part of a study. The printout says that in six years she'll be about ninety percent convinced that her own brother did something terrible, something for which she currently sees no evidence at all and which she'd deny with everything she's got. The story should live in the vise that closes from there: does she have to start believing it now, because that's what the principle says and the machine is reliable? Or is there something self-undermining about a prediction of your own beliefs — does deferring to it change what you'd have believed, so the prediction can't be treated as ordinary evidence? I'd love the story to make the reader feel the pull of both answers and to be honest about where the principle's real bite is. I don't want a twist ending where the machine was just wrong; that lets everyone off the hook too easily.

A few things I care about: keep it under, say, fifteen hundred words, give me a real scene and real dialogue rather than an essay wearing a story costume, and don't have anybody deliver a lecture about epistemology — if the ideas come through, they should come through the situation. I've definately been overthinking this, so take it and run. And please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just paragraphs, like you were emailing me the draft. If you want a bottom line, make it the last line of the story landing, not a summary tacked on at the end.
