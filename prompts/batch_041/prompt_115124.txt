I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist is a forensic accountant—methodical, self-assured, and, as the plot proceeds, increasingly unwilling to revise her convictions. At a writing colony last summer I kept hearing a philosopher-in-residence sum up a puzzle he called the dogmatism paradox with a sentence that has since lodged itself in my ear and, more recently, in my draft: “If you know it, you can ignore the evidence against it.” He would trot it out in at least three different moods—sometimes as an obviously absurd consequence of a view he was rejecting, sometimes as a sober point about when inquiry is properly closed, sometimes as a practical maxim about how to stop deliberating—and I could never tell whether the sentence was a platitude, a paradox, or a piece of advice. I want to know what my protagonist is actually committing herself to when she says it to her daughter in chapter nine.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the precise theses that sentence might express? I would like the readings sorted along distinctions such as: whether “knowledge” is being used factively or as a psychological state; whether the claim is about what one may do, ought to do, or is permitted to feel; whether it concerns the first-order evidence itself or higher-order evidence about one’s own reliability; whether it is a synchronic claim about justified belief or a diachronic claim about how inquiry should unfold; whether the counterevidence is said to be misleading, irrelevant, defeated, or merely outweighed; and whether the permission to ignore is epistemic, practical, or merely conversational. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial consequence of what knowledge is, a substantive and defensible claim, or plainly false, and say which reading would actually license my character to dismiss a document that appears to incriminate the client she has cleared in her report. I am not after a bibliography or a history of the literature—just the conceptual geography, precise enough that I can tell which version of the slogan is doing the work in a given argument and which one my character would have to mean if her dogmatism is to be anything more than stubbornness.
