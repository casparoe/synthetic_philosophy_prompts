I'm teaching a graduate seminar on Marx this term, and the unit on exploitation and alienation has left me in the embarrassing position of being unable to state the principle behind my own judgments. I've written the cases out and worked through them as I would with a student, and my verdicts are these. A garment worker on a piece rate, who owns nothing but her labor power and has no say over the pace or design of the work, is both exploited and alienated. The members of the worker-owned cooperative she might join, who own the shop, vote on the division of the surplus, and rotate the disagreeable jobs, are neither. A surgeon employed by a for-profit hospital chain, paid extremely well, in control of the details of her operations, and evidently absorbed by the work, is exploited — the chain's owners appropriate a surplus her labor produces — but not alienated. A tenured professor at a public university, comparably paid and comparably in control of her work, is neither exploited nor alienated. A gig driver who owns her own car, sets her own hours, and keeps her fares minus a commission the platform calls a fee for matching and payment, is not exploited, since no one appropriates a surplus from labor performed with her own means of production, but she is alienated, since an algorithm assigns and prices the work and she has no say in any of it. And a self-employed potter who sells her pots at a market she does not control is neither exploited nor alienated. I know what Marx's own formulations are; what I want is the principle, if there is one, that these verdicts add up to.

Two of them sit badly with the rest. The surgeon and the professor are both selling their labor power to an enterprise they do not own and are both paid well for it, yet I want to say that only the surgeon is exploited, and the only difference I can point to is that the hospital is for-profit and the university is not — which, taken seriously, seems to exempt any state or non-profit employer, and I don't believe that. The driver and the potter are both at the mercy of a market they do not control, yet I call only the driver alienated, and the only difference I can name is that the potter's market feels like weather while the driver's algorithm feels like a boss. My own attempt at a principle is this: exploitation is a distributive relation, obtaining when the product of a person's labor is appropriated by a party that does not produce it and that stands in a relation of ownership over the means of production; alienation is a relation between producers and their own activity, obtaining when the purposes governing that activity are set by another agent rather than by the producer. This gets the cooperative, the potter, and — I think — the driver right, but it makes the professor exploited after all, and it leaves the surgeon's exemption resting on the character of her employer rather than on anything about her relation to her work.

Could you tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually captures the verdicts I've listed, stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is doing and which case motivates it; what the principle predicts for cases I have not put to myself — in particular a worker cooperative that hires a few non-member employees at the going wage, and a craftsperson whose only possible buyer is a single firm that sets the price it will pay; and which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to give up. I would rather have a spare principle and one overturned verdict than an elaborate one that saves everything by fiat.
