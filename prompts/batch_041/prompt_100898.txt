I'm trying to write a short piece on what Dennett calls the strange inversion of reasoning — the thing he says Darwin and Turing both pulled off — and I want to read it to a small grad reading group (half phil of mind, half phil of bio), so it has to actually hold up. Below is the draft skeleton plus the bits I'm nervous about. Can you tell me where I'm wrong or hand-waving, which part is weakest, and honestly whether the main claim is a real claim or just a redescription? Be blunt, I'd rather find out now than in front of the group.

The setup: the traditional picture has comprehension coming first — to get competence (a well-designed organ, an intelligent answer) you need someone who understands what they're doing. Darwin inverts it: natural selection produces competence, i.e. adaptation, design-like structure, with no comprehending designer anywhere in the loop. Turing inverts it again: you can get competence, behaviour that passes for intelligent, with no comprehension, no understanding, no inner light. So both replace "mind first, behaviour later" with "behaviour first, mind maybe never."

Main claim, the one I most want comments on: these two inversions get run together as if they were the same move, but they aren't. Darwin's inversion is productive — it hands you a mechanism (variation plus selection) that actually generates the competence, so comprehension is genuinely displaced, explained away by a blind process. Turing's inversion is permissive — it removes a demand (the demand that the machine understand) rather than supplying a mechanism that generates the competence. Turing tells you your allowed not to require understanding; he doesn't tell you how the understandingless thing does the trick. That second half — whatever makes the behaviour come out right — is exactly what he leaves open, and it's arguably what the last seventy years of AI has been filling in. So the two inversions are structurally different even though they look identical from the outside.

Two things I'm unsure about:

1. Dennett's free-floating rationales. I want to say the Darwinian case still has reasons in it, just not reasons anyone reasons with — the why of an adaptation is real but unowned. Does that actually do work, or does it just relocate the comprehension into the observer, i.e. us, reading the rationale off the structure? If it's the latter, Darwin's inversion isn't displacing comprehension so much as renting it from the theorist, and my productive/permissive line starts to wobble.

2. Is comprehension even the same word in both halves? In Darwin it means something like intentional design or foresight. In Turing it means understanding, consciousness, the inner light. If those are two different senses, maybe the parallel is a pun, and my productive/permissive distinction is quietly doing the work that the word comprehension was pretending to do. I think that's fine, but I want to know whether it collapses into "two different things called the same name, so what."

Also a half-baked third thought I can't decide whether to keep: Turing's move might not be an inversion at all but a redefinition — he doesn't argue that competence arrives without comprehension, he changes the question so comprehension drops out of the criterion. If that's right, the inversion is in what we're permitted to ask, not in the order of nature. Worth a paragraph, or a distraction?

So: where's the substance wrong, which of the three bits is the weak link, and is the central claim actually doing anything?
