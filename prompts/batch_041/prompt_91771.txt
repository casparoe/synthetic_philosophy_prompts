I'm a postdoc in epistemology, and next month I give a work-in-progress paper to the department's epistemology reading group. I know this room well enough to be nervous about it. The regulars are mostly analytic epistemologists with broadly externalist instincts; two of them are card-carrying Williamsonians who can reconstruct the margin-for-error argument for anti-luminosity from scratch and will want to know exactly where my premises diverge from his; there is one formal epistemologist who works on safety and sensitivity conditions and will recast anything I say in those terms whether I like it or not; there are two people from the epistemic logic side who cut their teeth on the KK literature and its introspection axioms and will immediately ask what my restriction does to positive and negative introspection and to the usual closure-driven collapse results; and there is one Kantian-leaning skeptic about externalist knowledge who thinks the whole luminosity game is a distraction from the real question of justification. Nobody in the room works on vagueness, but I expect at least one of them to reach for a sorites objection anyway, because it is the cheapest move available against me.

The paper argues roughly this. The standard anti-luminosity argument is usually presented as if it were perfectly general, but it depends on a structural feature of the cases it uses: the fact in question has to lie on a smooth continuum, so that neighbouring cases in a series can be made arbitrarily close while still differing in what one is in a position to know, and it is that arrangement that makes the margin requirement bite. My claim is that this is not a feature of knowledge as such but of a particular class of facts. Where the fact known is discrete and fixed by convention or by a bright-line rule rather than by a continuously varying quantity — whether a switch is on or off, whether one has crossed a threshold that the rules themselves define, whether a move is legal in a game — the cases cannot be arranged into the required series, no margin is needed, and luminosity is restored. So I want to defend a domain-restricted KK principle: knowing implies knowing that one knows for knowledge of discrete, convention-fixed facts, while the principle fails, as the anti-luminosity argument says it fails, for the continuum cases. I am aware this is a strange shape for a thesis and that its interest depends entirely on whether the restriction is principled or ad hoc, which is precisely what I am worried about.
