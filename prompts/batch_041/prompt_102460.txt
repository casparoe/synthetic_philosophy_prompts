I have no background in philosophy — I repair marine electronics for a living and read this sort of thing at night — but I've gotten stuck on Daniel Dennett's idea that there are three stances you can take toward any system: the physical stance, where you predict it by treating it as matter obeying the laws of physics; the design stance, where you predict it by assuming it was assembled to serve some function; and the intentional stance, where you predict it by assuming it has beliefs and desires and asking what it would be rational for it to do. What I keep chewing on is his further claim that some patterns are real even though they aren't part of the world's fundamental furniture — that a pattern earns its reality by letting you compress and predict, so that the question of whether it's really there gives way to the question of whether treating it as there pays off. I may well be mangling this, and what I want is a story that puts the idea under pressure rather than illustrating it.

Here's the seed I have in mind, though change anything that gets in the way. A researcher is on a small vessel studying a vast nightly migration of deep-sea animals; the sonar traces read like a single thing that changes course around obstacles and seems to hesitate, and the only way she can predict it is to ask what it wants. Her two colleagues pull in opposite directions. One insists the whole business is currents, chemistry, and temperature, and that her intentions are a story she's telling herself; the other is convinced the migration is an exquisitely designed system that no one designed, refined over ages by nothing but selection, and thinks the intentional talk is a lazy shortcut for a design description she hasn't finished yet. Then something forces a practical decision — whether to warn the fishing fleet, say, or move the ship — where the three stances yield three different predictions and the disagreement carries real stakes.

I'd like the story to stay close to the characters' work and their arguments rather than having anyone lecture, and to be honest about where the concept strains: if treating the swarm as an agent is indispensable for predicting it, is that enough to make the agency real, or is indispensability just another word for convenience? I'd especially like an ending that resists the tidy resolution without tipping into nihilism — I want to feel why Dennett thinks this is a liberating answer and why it still leaves a lump in the throat.
