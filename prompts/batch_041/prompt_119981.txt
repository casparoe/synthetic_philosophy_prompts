I spent thirty-five years as an analytical chemist — method development, nothing glamorous — and I retired two years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy with the same obsessive streak I used to bring to a bad chromatography problem. Right now it's Kierkegaard, and specifically the stuff he wrote about the press and about what he calls the public.

What keeps me up is that it isnt the usual complaint. He isn't saying the papers lie, or that they're biased, or that the editors are venal. He had just been dragged through a satirical paper himself, and instead of writing a grievance he comes up with a genuinely strange bit of ontology: he thinks the press doesn't merely spread opinions, it manufactures a new kind of entity — the public — which has no members. Set against that is the crowd: an actual bunch of people in a square, capable of deciding something and therefore capable of being answerable for it. The public can do neither. It can't be addressed, it can't be held to anything, and yet it can be everywhere at once and it can end a person's life. Then there's what he calls leveling, which he treats as an abstract process rather than an act — nobody does it, everybody does it, and the person being leveled down is himself a participant in his own leveling, which is the part I find hardest to get my head around. The upshot, in his telling, is an age that reflects endlessly, talks endlessly (he has a particular contempt for what gets translated as chatter), and decides nothing; envy is the mood underneath it all and it dresses itself up as enthusiasm; nobody is answerable because nobody is anybody in particular. His remedy, as far as I can tell, is not better journalism but the single individual, and I honestly can't decide whether that's profound or a dodge.

Here's what I want. Write me a short story, maybe 1,200 to 1,800 words, that actually turns on these ideas rather than name-checking them. Please don't set it in 1840s Copenhagen — put it somewhere I'd recognize, an institute or a small town or a professional society — and give me people who are recognizable types rather than villains. I'd like the difference between a crowd and a public to be something the reader feels rather than gets told: maybe a real crowd shows up at some point, or conspicuously doesn't, and it should matter that your protagonist has nobody to blame and no forum in which to answer. Let the leveling be visible as a process that the leveled-down people are complicit in. Don't let anyone lecture, and dont put any quotations from Kierkegaard into it.

Then, kept clearly separate from the story, a short note for me about the philosophy itself: which parts of what you just dramatized are actually his and which are your extrapolation, and where the people who know the texts disagree with each other. I'm especially curious whether the public-and-leveling material is best read as social criticism or as a religious point about the single individual before God, and whether he takes the press to be the disease or only a symptom of something older. Please separate what's genuinely settled from what's contested, and if you're giving me one camp's reading, say that's what you're doing instead of letting it sound like the consensus. I'm new enough at this that I can't yet tell the difference, and I'd rather see where the seams are than get a smooth answer.
