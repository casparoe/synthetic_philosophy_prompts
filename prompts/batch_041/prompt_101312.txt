I have been reading around in forecasting and collective judgment, and I keep encountering a pattern for which I cannot find a name that fits exactly.

The first instance: a large group of forecasters, each of whom is only slightly more reliable than a coin on the questions at hand. If their mistakes are unrelated — each erring for their own idiosyncratic reasons — the group's majority verdict is far more accurate than any individual member, and it grows more accurate still as the group is enlarged. Size is a virtue; the mediocre many reliably outperform the excellent few.

The second instance: the same group, but now the members share a common source of error. They read the same few outlets, were trained on the same material, or reason from the same background assumption, so their mistakes point the same way. The result inverts. The group is not merely wrong; it is confidently and extremely wrong, and enlarging the group makes the collective verdict more extreme in the wrong direction. Size has become a vice; the many are worse than the few, and worse in proportion to their number.

What interests me is that the only thing that changed between the two cases is whether the individual errors are aligned, and yet the effect of adding members reverses sign. I have heard the first case celebrated under the general heading of the wisdom of crowds, and I gather there is a classical result about majority voting and individual competence that underwrites it. But I am after the second case specifically — the amplification of a shared error by aggregation, where more participants make the group more confidently wrong — and I want to know whether that has an established name and analysis of its own.

If it does, tell me precisely what the extra condition is that flips the classical result, and how the second case is distinguished from the first. If it does not have a name, give me one and define it, and then say how it relates to, and differs from, the nearest things I do know of — groupthink, information cascades, and group polarization. I care much less about the label than about the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, whether the crucial feature is the alignment of the errors or merely the presence of a shared bias, whether the shared error must point in one direction or can be a shared tendency to scatter, and where the edges of the phenomenon lie.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Within that list, also tell me what would change your verdict — which consideration or argument would lead you to a different name or a different carving — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
