and heres what the student wrote:

"Naturalism says that whether someone is healthy or diseased is a purely biological or medical fact, and that value judgements play no essential role. Normativism says the opposite: calling something a disease always involves a value judgement, usually that the state is bad or unwanted for the person. Boorse is the main naturalist. On his biostatistical theory, health is statistically normal species-typical functioning, and a disease is a statistically subnormal functioning of some part relative to a reference class. So for Boorse, illness is the value-free theoretical notion, while 'disease' is the value-laden term for what the patient actually feels and reports. Nordenfelt, following Boorse, defines health as the ability to reach species-typical vital goals, which makes him a naturalist too, since species-typical functioning is a biological fact.

On the island case, since 95% of the population is deaf by 50, deafness is statistically normal there, so Boorse is forced to classify it as healthy — not a disease at all. That shows the naturalist can't capture the intuition that the islanders really are impaired; normativism does better because it can point out that deafness is bad for them. Nordenfelt would say the islanders are unhealthy because they can't achieve the vital goal of hearing.

Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis is naturalistic through and through. He says a condition is a disorder when there's a dysfunction, i.e. a failure of some mechanism to do what it was naturally selected to do, and the harm clause is really just a restatement of that: if a mechanism fails to do its selected job, that's already a harm. So he's really a naturalist like Boorse, just with evolutionary language.

The practical upshot is that the choice between the two matters for things like insurance and medicalisation."
