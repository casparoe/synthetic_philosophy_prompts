I was in the common room when two visiting speakers—one a value pluralist, the other a decision theorist—got into an argument over a case in a colleague's draft. The case: a surgeon must choose between performing a risky operation that may save a child's life but will likely leave her in chronic pain, and declining the operation so that she dies peacefully. The surgeon is choosing between a life with suffering and a peaceful death. The value pluralist said these two outcomes are incommensurable, so the surgeon cannot be criticized for either choice; the decision theorist said that does not follow, because the surgeon can still make a rational choice by weighing the values even if no common measure exists. They went back and forth several times, and I lost track of who had the better of it.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) The value pluralist began by distinguishing what she called thin incommensurability from thick incommensurability. Thin incommensurability is when two values have no common measure but can still be compared ordinally in some contexts. Thick incommensurability is when they cannot be compared at all. She said the surgeon's case is thick: there is no fact of the matter about whether a painful life is better or worse than a peaceful death, so both choices are permissible.

(2) The decision theorist replied that this distinction is doing all the work and has not been justified. He said every case of apparent thick incommensurability can be redescribed as thin once you specify the context. In this case, the context includes the child's own values, the surgeon's professional obligations, and the likely consequences. Once those are specified, he said, there is a determinate answer, or at least a range of answers that can be ranked.

(3) She answered that appealing to the child's values just changes the question. The child may value both life and the absence of suffering, and may be unable to rank them. Appealing to professional obligations may also fail, because the obligations to preserve life and to prevent suffering can conflict. If the values are genuinely incommensurable, no amount of contextual specification will produce a ranking.

(4) He then proposed a covering principle: when in doubt, minimize suffering. That, he said, gives a determinate answer in this case and in many others. It is a rational policy for decision-making under value conflict.

(5) She objected that the covering principle is itself a value commitment that ranks suffering below life, or above it, depending on how it is applied. It does not resolve incommensurability; it simply picks a side. And if the patient or the surgeon rejects that principle, the decision theorist is back to square one.

(6) He said that in the absence of a common measure, one can still use second-order comparisons. For example, one can ask which choice is more fitting to the relationship between the surgeon and the child, or which choice the surgeon would regret less. Regret, he said, is a rational criterion: if you would regret one choice more than the other, that is evidence that the choice is worse for you, and that is a reason to avoid it.

(7) She replied that regret is not a measure of value; it is a psychological fact. It may be irrational, and it may be shaped by factors that have nothing to do with the values at stake. The fact that you would regret something does not mean it was worse. At most, regret is a heuristic, not a justification.

(8) He conceded that regret is imperfect but said it is the best we have when no common measure exists. He added that if she insists on thick incommensurability, she must explain how a surgeon can deliberate at all. If there is no fact of the matter, deliberation is pointless, and yet surgeons do deliberate and often reach justified conclusions.

(9) She said deliberation can be rational even without a fact of the matter, because it is a way of discovering what one cares about and of committing to a course of action. The rationality is practical, not theoretical. The surgeon can make a choice that is authentic or wholehearted, even if it is not uniquely correct.

(10) He said that "authentic" and "wholehearted" are just redescriptions of the decision, not criteria for its correctness. If both choices can be authentic, then the theory cannot distinguish between them, and it collapses into the claim that whatever the surgeon chooses is fine. That may be true, but it is not a theory of rational choice; it is a refusal to theorize.

(11) She said that refusing to theorize may be the right response when the values are genuinely incommensurable. The demand for a single ranking is a demand for a false precision. The best we can do is to make the choice with as much sensitivity as possible to the values at stake, and to accept that there is no algorithm.

(12) He replied that this is a counsel of despair. If there is no algorithm, there is still reasoning: we can give arguments, weigh considerations, and criticize choices as insensitive, hasty, or self-deceived. That is not a refusal to theorize; it is a different kind of theorizing.

They left it there. I would like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the numbered moves, say who has the better point and why, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say what both parties are missing, and whether there is a way to resolve the dispute that neither of them has considered. I am not asking which speaker won overall; I am asking for a point-by-point assessment of the arguments as they were actually made.
