I have no academic training in economics and have been teaching myself decision theory on my own, so I hope you will correct my vocabulary along with my reasoning.

My understanding is this. Expected utility theory is supposed to be the theory of how a rational person chooses among risky options, and its central axiom is independence: if you prefer one gamble to another, you must still prefer it after both gambles are combined with the same further chance of some common outcome. The Allais example is supposed to embarrass this axiom. Offered a sure $1 million, most people take it over a gamble with a 10 percent chance of $5 million, an 89 percent chance of $1 million, and a 1 percent chance of nothing. But offered an 11 percent chance of $1 million against a 10 percent chance of $5 million, most people take the long shot. Since the second pair is just the first pair with the same 89 percent chance of $1 million swapped for an 89 percent chance of nothing, the two answers cannot both satisfy independence.

What I cannot see is why this is called a paradox rather than a refutation. If independence is a claim about how rational preference must work, and nearly everyone violates it, then the claim looks simply false, and expected utility theory looks less like a theory of rationality than a description of an unusual temperament. So my first question is whether I am conflating two different things: a rule that a rational chooser is bound by, and a prediction about what people will in fact do.

My second question is about a repair I thought of, which I suspect is naive. Since the trouble seems to be that people treat a certain gain as special, why not leave the rest of expected utility theory alone and add an axiom that a sure gain always outranks a gamble that merely might pay more? Does the theory survive that patch, or does the paradox live somewhere the patch does not reach?
