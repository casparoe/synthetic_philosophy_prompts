I am a novelist, and my working vocabulary is narrators and implied readers, unreliable narration, accommodation to what an audience can bear, subtext, and the gap between what a text says and what its author is doing in saying it. Restate Galileo's case for how Scripture ought to be read when it appears to conflict with a demonstrated result in natural philosophy, as faithfully as you can, using only the terms I actually work in — and don't explain the case to me at the level of history, translate it. Then tell me what the translation costs: which of his moves come across intact, which I can make fit only by importing assumptions a novelist has no reason to grant, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I am most curious about his premise that the same author is responsible for both the book of nature and the book of Scripture — is that a claim about authorship my craft can even state? — and about whether what fails to carry over is a poverty of my terms or a sign that his position is doing work fiction's categories were never built to do. Argue it in my idiom; no citations, no reading lists.
