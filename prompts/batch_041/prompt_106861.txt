I teach a media-ethics unit and there's a move I keep seeing that I don't have a good name for. The pattern: somebody wants you to believe something false, but they never say it, and they never even say anything that quite means it — they get you to say it, or they answer in a way that only holds together if the false thing were already true. Two examples from real arguments. A company spokesperson is asked "when did you learn about the defect?" and replies "we take safety extremely seriously and our customers have alwyas come first" — nothing there admits they knew, but the answer only makes sense as a defense if there's something to defend. Or someone suspected of taking the money says "are you really accusing me of that?" and by making you justify the accusation gets you to drop it, without ever denying it. Press either of them and they say "I never said that." And they're sort of right: they didn't assert anything false, and they didn't quite imply anything false either — the false beleif comes from what you filled in yourself.

Is there a name for this? I know lying, and I know "misleading" is broad, and I've heard "paltering" but that seems to be about true statements that imply false ones, which isn't quite what's happening — here the speaker doesn't imply the false thing, they get the listener to supply it. So: is this a recognized thing with its own name, and if not, can you name and define it so it stays distinct from paltering, from ordinary misleading, and from lying by omission? I care less about the label than about where the edges fall — in particular whether it still counts when the speaker didn't intend you to fill it in, and whether a false belief has to result at all. Tell me where your answer is most likely to be wrong, or what would change your mind about whether this is one phenomenon rather than a few. Keep it under 300 words.
