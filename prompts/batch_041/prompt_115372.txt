I'm in a grad seminar on consequentialism and I've got to present on cluelessness next week. My problem is that everyone in the room -- me included -- thinks cluelessness is just a fancy word for risk: you don't know how it'll turn out, so you maximize expected value, done. I want a case where that reply fails, but built to my specs rather than lifted off the shelf; I'm sick of the "should I buy this coffee" and "should I vote" examples. So construct me a thought experiment. One paragraph of scenario, ordinary contemporary setting -- a job, a lease, a favour for a neighbour, a hiring decision -- no trolleys, no science fiction, no demons, no time travel, no beings with impossible powers. Assume the world is deterministic, so nothing in the case is a matter of chance and we can't rescue the point by saying "there was a 40% chance it would go badly." The agent has exactly two options, her evidence is perfectly symmetric between them (same foreseeable local effects, same information, no expected-value tiebreaker available to her even in principle), and yet the options differ substantially in how things actually go for particular people -- not a rounding error, not something you can wave off as negligible.

The catch, and this is the bit I actually care about: the difference has to run through a long, branching chain of interactions, so that the effect of the choice on any one downstream event is unpredictable in sign and size while the aggregate is determinate and large; and the case must not collapse if you give the agent more time and research. No amount of investigation on her part could settle it -- please don't cheat by making it merely hard to find out. The twist I want is that the cluelessness makes the expected-value (subjective) consequentialist say "either option's fine, go ahead" while the actual-consequence (objective) consequentialist says one of the two is a serious wrong. So cluelessness comes out as an excuse rather than a paralysis, which is the opposite of how it's usually sold.

After the scenario, walk me through what each theory says and why; tell me which single feature is doing the work of separating cluelessness from ordinary uncertainty; say what happens if I remove that feature, and whether the case then collapses back into a standard risk case (if it does, show me how); and tell me the cheapest way a defender of whichever view comes out worst would attack the case -- i.e. deny that my scenario is possible or relevant. Also keep the settled apart from the genuinely contested: I don't want a tidy consensus on this that doesn't actually exist among the people who work on it. Under 700 words, please.
