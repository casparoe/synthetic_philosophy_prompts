A friend and I argued about liberty for most of a long evening, and I came away more committed than ever to the republican view: what matters is not whether you are actually interfered with, but whether you live under anyone's arbitrary power. On that view, a contented slave who is never interfered with is unfree, a citizen bound by a law she has no share in making may still be free, and a benevolent master is still a master. Take that as my position, and tell me what would count against it.

Which cases would the view have to get wrong to be untenable, and does it in fact get any of them wrong? Does the notion of arbitrariness do real work, or does it either thin out into whatever the law happens to permit or fatten into a substantive theory of the common good, making the view a version of positive liberty after all? Can non-domination ever come apart from negative liberty in a way that is not merely a difference in how secure the same freedom is? And what am I supposed to say about domination without a dominator — markets, norms, structural dependence — where I can only seem to speak by analogy? I suspect that if the view can't say anything independent of my antecedent disapproval of the relationship, that is a problem rather than a selling point.

Rank the objections for me: which are fatal, which the view's own defenders should concede as real costs, and which are merely awkward. Finally, say honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is tolerable for an ideal of this kind or a sign that it has stopped risking anything.
