I'm a third-year PhD student working on nineteenth-century political thought, and in a fortnight I'm giving a paper to our political theory reading group -- the one room where the historians of political economy and the philosophers both actually turn up, plus two economists from next door who come for the biscuits.

My claim is this. The late Mill on socialism -- the posthumous Chapters on Socialism, the review of Thornton where he quietly gives up the wages-fund doctrine, and the revisions he kept making to the Principles -- usually gets read as a half-hearted conversion to egalitarianism, or as an old man hedging. I think it's neither. What he objects to in the existing order isn't that the labourer is poor; it's that he's dependent, that his livelihood sits in another man's hand. That's why cooperation and profit-sharing appeal to him (they turn the wage relation into a partnership), and it's also why he won't condemn communism outright while being obviously uneasy about the power it hands to the community over the individual. On my reading Mill never becomes a socialist in the levelling sense at all. He's a liberal who's been persuaded that the wage relation is a form of subjection a free society shouldn't tolerate indefinitely, and his stationary-state discussion is the tell -- he plainly doesn't think a society of people scrambling to get on is the summit of human life.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet for this specific room, not for Mill scholars in general. The audience:

- Two Cambridge-school types who'll want to know where "domination" comes from in Mill's own vocabulary, and who'll be suspicious that I'm smuggling in a modern republican framework and calling it history.
- A Marxist in the group who thinks Mill is a bourgeois hedge and that my reading is a rescue operation dressed up as interpretation.
- A classical liberal who knows On Liberty backwards and will say I'm inflating a few late essays into a whole position.
- A colleague who works on Mill and empire. She'll say you can't give an account of what Mill thought "dependence" was while ignoring that the same man spent his working life at the East India Company.
- An economist from next door who'll want to know whether the Thornton episode leaves my Mill with a coherent economics at all, or just a heap of concessions.

For each objection: how likely it is, how much damage it does to the paper, how that person would actually phrase it, what I concede immediately, what I hold, and a two-minute reply. Mark where the reply is soft -- I'd definately rather know in advance than find out live. And give me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. I've got a candidate of my own, but I want yours first, and you can be blunt about it.

Don't send me a reading list, I know the literature. And don't pad it out with the objections any Mill specialist would raise regardless of who's in the room -- I need the ones this lot will actually fire, in their voice, seperated out and ranked.
