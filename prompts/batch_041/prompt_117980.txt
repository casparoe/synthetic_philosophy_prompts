I'm a science writer, and at a conference dinner last month a neuroscientist and a philosopher of mind got into it about a syndrome I'd never heard of, and I've been chewing on it since.

The syndrome, which the neuroscientist called asymbolia for pain: after certain kinds of brain damage -- insular or parietal, sometimes following cingulotomy -- patients say they feel pain. They can tell you where it is and how sharp it is. But it doesn't bother them. They don't pull the hand back, they don't guard the limb, they don't show the usual autonomic signs, and some of them smile or crack jokes while you're jabbing them.

Her claim was that this is the cleanest evidence we've got that the awfulness of pain is a separate ingredient from the sensation: sensation in tact, awfulness gone. So the badness of pain isn't built into feeling pain; it's a second layer. Which means there's nothing incoherent about a creature having pains that aren't bad for it.

He said fine, they come apart, but look at what's actually missing. These patients don't just fail to mind the pain, they fail to protect the body. That isn't someone who notices a bad feeling and decides to tough it out. It's someone whose pain has stopped issuing its demand. And a demand is what pain essentially is. The badness is still intrinsic to the pain; what they lost is the machinery that carries out pain's command, not the badness.

She said a command is just badness in a lab coat -- what is it for a state to demand something, other than to be a state you're averse to? And if the demand can outlive the aversiveness, then they're not the same thing, and the aversiveness is the real badness.

He said if only the disliking were gone, why do they also stop avoiding the thing that hurts, even later? A state that had lost its sting but kept its content -- tissue damage, here -- should still be doing its job. What's gone is pain's grip on action, and that's the part that belongs to the pain itself.

I couldn't referee any of this. Can you go through the exchange and say who's right at each step -- what asymbolia actually shows, whether the badness of pain can come apart from the pain, and whether his demand move is a real alternative or the same claim restated. And what's the thing both of them are missing?
