My friend Marcus has been building his own theory of karma for years. He's read a pile of Buddhist and Hindu stuff but keeps insisting its his own system, so don't answer me with what Buddhism says, answer me with what Marcus says. He's got three words he uses for it: a ledger, when he thinks someone is paying off a debt; a stream, when he thinks an action is just shaping what someone becomes; and a field, when he thinks some larger thing is carrying it.

His verdicts so far, as best I can reconstruct them:

- A guy who defrauded a widow and twenty years later dies broke and alone, having completely forgotten her. Marcus: karma, definitely. You don't have to remember a debt for it to be owed.
- A surgeon who operated drunk and botched it, whose own son then died of an unrelated illness. Marcus: not karma. The son didn't do anything, and you can't pay someone else's debt with your kid. Just a tragedy.
- A soldier who took part in a massacre because he was ordered to, who was sick about it for the rest of his life and was never punished by anyone. Marcus: karma, and it's still running.
- A village where every household dumps a little waste and the river downstream turns undrinkable. Marcus: not karma. Nobody decided anything, it just happened. His line is that there's no karma without a chooser.
- A mob that drags a man out of his house and kills him. Marcus: karma, a lot of it, and he gets annoyed when people say they only did their bit — he says it doesn't get discharged by everyone shrugging.

The one I actually want to ask him about is a drug company. Board, written policies, an explicit decision to keep selling a painkiller after they knew it was killing people, thirty years of it. No single employee ever intended a death — each one did a small lawful job, and plenty of them privately thought it was wrong. Then the company folds and its people scatter. Does Marcus say there's karma there?

So: what's his verdict, what rule is he actually running, what other rules fit everything above just as well, and which rule do I have to adopt to get each verdict? Which of his earlier cases is the closest analog, the mob or the village? And what's the one further case that would tell the rules apart — I'm much less interested in the verdict than in the machinery, and if his record genuinely underdetermines this, tell me what principle he'd be signing up for with each answer.
