For an ethics unit in my high school (roughly twenty students, a single fifty-minute period), I would like you to design a runnable classroom activity — a structured deliberation or role-play, with rules, materials, roles, a timeline, and real though small stakes — that makes participants feel the force of a particular idea before anyone names it: that the informal social sanctions we impose on one another (shunning, public criticism, expulsion from a group, the quiet loss of standing) are a kind of punishment, and that this has consequences for when they can be legitimate. The idea should live in what happens, not in what I say afterward; I do not want the students to hear the words punishment, cancel culture, retribution, or any of the usual vocabulary until the debrief.

Please arrange the activity so that the group must actually decide, and then carry out, a sanction against one of their own for something that person has said or done. The stakes should be real enough that the decision costs them something, yet small enough to be safe — a share of a later reward, a turn, a place, or a mark of standing within the activity. No lecture up front.

Two things I especially want you to build in, because they turn on distinctions the students will not have ready-made. First, a twist: partway through, after the group has committed to a sanction, introduce a new fact that bears differently on two ways of understanding what they are doing — responding to what was already done, as a backward-looking answer to the offense, versus protecting the group from what might happen next, as a forward-looking measure of defense. If their decision shifts when the new fact arrives, they were doing the first; if it holds firm, the second. Second, force the group to settle its own boundaries — who counts as a member with standing to be sanctioned, and who has standing to sanction — and let the offender refuse to recognize the group's authority to judge at all. I suspect this "so what?" moment is where the felt core of the problem lies.

In the debrief, map specific moments of the activity onto its parts: where condemnation entered; where the group acted as accuser, judge, and beneficiary all at once; where the new fact forced the backward/forward distinction; where the boundary decision quietly determined whether the sanction had any authority; and where someone wanted to appeal and could not. Draw out what the group was doing without the safeguards we normally demand before punishment is justified — a neutral decision-maker, a burden of proof, a settled rule, a proportion between offense and penalty, and a chance to answer. Then be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce, so I know what I must supply myself — for instance, the scale, anonymity, and permanence of real deplatforming, or the way real sanctions accumulate over time rather than being settled in a single meeting. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; I expect at least that students will refuse to impose any real sanction out of politeness, and that the debrief will collapse back into re-arguing the original case instead of examining the concept.
