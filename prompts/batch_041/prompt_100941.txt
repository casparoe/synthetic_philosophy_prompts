Adopt the role of a philosopher of the social sciences who works on the metaphysics of social kinds — generations, cohorts, and other demographic categories — and who is also at home in the methodology of cohort analysis. Think carefully and at length before answering, and open your response by brainstorming the candidate concepts the phenomenon below might correspond to before committing to any of them.

I am a researcher who spends a great deal of time with survey and polling data and with the commentary built on top of it, and I keep encountering a pattern for which I have no name. I would like to know whether there is an established concept for it. I am not after a label by itself: even if you supply a term, I need you to say exactly what the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, and how it differs from the things it is most easily confused with. If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it, and give boundary cases.

Here is the pattern, described as plainly as I can manage.

First instance. A commentator reports that a particular generation is more anxious, lonelier, or less religious than earlier ones, and the evidence offered is a difference between today's twenty-somethings and today's sixty-somethings. But that same gap would appear if the trait were simply a function of age, or if something had shifted for everyone recently; nothing in the claim requires that anything be distinctive about the birth years in question. The generational vocabulary appears to relabel a cross-sectional age gap without adding anything to it.

Second instance. The label is then used as an explanation rather than a description: members of the generation are said to act in a certain way because they belong to that generation, as though the cohort were a cause, and often as though it were a single agent with preferences and a will — so that one can sensibly ask what they want, or what ought to be done about them.

Third instance. As the cohort ages, the traits attributed to it are quietly revised, and the phrase the younger generation always names whoever happens to be young at the time, so the same complaint recurs across decades. This makes me suspect that what I am noticing is really about age and about the present moment, not about birth years at all.

I have looked at several nearby ideas and none quite captures it. The age-period-cohort identification problem seems to be a statistical point — that age, period, and cohort effects cannot be separately read off the data without strong assumptions — whereas the thing I am describing occurs even when the statistics are impeccable, because it is a slide in interpretation. The claim that generations are socially constructed seems true but too coarse; it does not isolate the specific move I keep seeing. The ecological fallacy concerns inferring individuals from aggregates, which is not this. And I have come across the verdict that generations are not natural kinds, but that is a conclusion about their metaphysics rather than a name for the interpretive pattern itself.

So: is there an established term for this composite phenomenon — the conflation of age, period, and cohort, together with the reification of the cohort into a cause or an agent? If there is one, tell me what it is and how well it genuinely fits each of my three instances. If there is not, name it and define it, and distinguish it from the age-period-cohort problem, from social-construction claims about generations, from the ecological fallacy, and from the ordinary and defensible claim that a cohort can be a meaningful unit of analysis. I would especially value the boundary cases: what would have to be true for the move I am describing to be legitimate, and at what point does a cohort effect become a real, causally efficacious social kind rather than a relabelled age or period effect?
