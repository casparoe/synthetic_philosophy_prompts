I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and next week I have to open a workshop on the normativity of attitudes by setting out my own commitments and letting everyone else take aim at them. I would rather be audited now than ambushed then, so I want to know whether the package I am about to present hangs together. I have been told that it cannot all be held at once, but nobody has been able to show me where it breaks, and I have not managed to see it myself.

Here is the package. (1) Value is fittingness: for something to be good just is for it to be a fitting object of a favouring attitude (desire, admiration, approval and the like), and for something to be bad is for it to be a fitting object of a disfavouring attitude. (2) Buck-passing: the value of a thing is never itself a reason for anything; where there is reason to favour something, the reason is supplied by the lower-level features that make it valuable — its being pleasant, useful or loyal, say — and never by its value. (3) The object-given/state-given distinction is the whole of the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction, and it is exhaustive: only object-given considerations, that is, considerations bearing on the thing the attitude is directed at, are reasons for attitudes. A consideration that bears only on the benefits of having the attitude — a demon's threat to torment me unless I admire him, the fact that admiring my rival would make me happier, a reward offered for holding an intention — is at most a reason to bring the attitude about, and never a reason for the attitude. (4) Reasons are prior to fittingness: fittingness is not a primitive normative relation, and an attitude is fitting exactly when the balance of reasons for it supports it, so (1) is not a quiet appeal to a sui generis fit between attitude and object. (5) In the toxin case — an eccentric billionaire will pay me a fortune if at midnight tonight I sincerely intend to drink tomorrow afternoon a toxin that will make me painfully ill for a day but do no lasting harm, the money being mine as soon as the intention is in place and my mind free to change afterwards — I think one can rationally form the intention tonight and rationally decline to drink tomorrow. Intentions, then, answer to reasons that are not simply the reasons for the action intended; there are genuine practical reasons to intend as such. (6) A consideration counts as a reason for an attitude only if one could in principle reason one's way from it to the attitude; I am suspicious of reasons for attitudes that are necessarily inaccessible to reasoning. (7) It can be entirely sensible to work at an attitude — to cultivate gratitude, to rehearse a slight until indignation comes, to take steps to fall in love.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture on the literature. Which of these pull against each other, which pairs only look as though they do, and how badly does each real tension bite? Rank them by severity rather than reporting that everything bears on everything. For each genuine tension, tell me what has to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere; where the repair is to weaken a claim, say exactly how it should be weakened and what it could then no longer do, and where the tension is an artefact of how I have stated something, give me the better statement. At the end, tell me which single revision would clear up the most, and what I would be giving up by making it. Please write in British English.
