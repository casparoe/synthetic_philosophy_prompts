I'm starting a three-year postdoc in the fall with almost no teaching, and I get to work on whatever I want. I've pretty much decided its going to be the peer disagreement literature — conciliationism, steadfastness, higher-order evidence, the whole pile. The problem is I can't tell which of the open questions in there is actually worth three years of my life, as opposed to a good seminar paper. Half the stuff I read feels like the same paper: two idealized agents, identical evidence, a restaurant check, and then a fight about whether you have to split the difference. I don't want to write a seventh version of that.

There are three things I've been chewing on that might be where the real open questions are, but I honestly can't tell if they're genuine openings or just changes of subject. Use them if they're useful, tell me if they're not:

1. Almost everyone frames the question as what you should believe when a peer disagrees. But maybe the questions that actually matter are about what to do next — whether to keep digging, whether to defer in practice while privately thinking the other person is wrong, when it's worth spending a decade trying to change their mind. Does treating this as a question about inquiry rather than belief open anything up that the belief-first framing closes off, or is it the same questions in a new coat?

2. The literature is basically always about two people. But real disagreement is many-sided — a field, a committee, a whole literature — and then you need some story about how the many-sided version aggregates into anything at all. Is the interesting work there, or is that just social choice theory wearing a philosopher's hat?

3. The view seems to eat itself: if you and I disagree about conciliationism, then by conciliationist lights you should partly defer to me about conciliationism, which looks like it undercuts the whole thing. Is the self-undermining problem still a live question, or is it settled and I just haven't found where?

What I want from you is a ranked agenda: which open questions in this area deserve the years, and why. I care less about the list than the reasoning. For each one, tell me why it's upstream of the others — what other questions change their answer if this one gets settled — how tractable it is for one philosopher with three years and no lab, and what kind of result over the next year or two would move it up or down the ranking. The end I care about is work that's still being argued about in twenty years, not just a publication; rank accordingly, and if your top pick is something I haven't listed, fine, but say why it beats the three above.

Also, tell me where you're guessing. I'd rather have a ranking with its weak joints marked than a confident one I can't trust. And say what would change your mind — which argument or development would make you reorder it, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
