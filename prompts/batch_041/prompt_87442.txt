I have been reading a popular account of algorithmic information theory, and I would like a careful audit of one paragraph from it. The paragraph introduces Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference as follows:

"Solomonoff's great insight was that the problem of induction has a solution. A rational predictor should consider every possible computer program that might be generating the data, weight each program by two raised to the power of minus its length in bits -- so that shorter programs receive exponentially more weight -- and then predict by averaging over all of them. He proved that this procedure converges on the truth: whichever computable process is in fact generating the data, the mixture's predictions converge on the correct ones, and the predictor makes only finitely many errors along the way. What this amounts to is a mathematical vindication of Occam's razor. Simplicity is not a methodological preference or an aesthetic one; it is a theorem, because shorter hypotheses are provably more likely to be true. The scheme also explains why Bayesianism is the right framework for reasoning under uncertainty, since the weighting over programs supplies the prior that Bayesians would otherwise have to stipulate by hand; and it follows that the shortest program consistent with the data is the hypothesis to accept. The one real limitation is that Kolmogorov complexity cannot be computed, so the method is of purely theoretical interest. As a foundation for induction, however, it disposes of Hume's problem."

I am not in a position to adjudicate this myself, and I suspect the paragraph is doing something familiar: taking a genuine and beautiful theorem and inflating it. Could you go through it claim by claim? For each claim, I would like to know whether a researcher working on algorithmic information theory or on Bayesian foundations would accept it as stated, would accept it only with a qualification, or would reject it outright -- and in the last case, what the underlying theorem actually establishes. I am especially interested in whether the two headline conclusions, that Occam's razor is vindicated and that Hume's problem is disposed of, survive the correction of whatever else in the paragraph is wrong, or whether they rest on precisely the steps that need repair.
