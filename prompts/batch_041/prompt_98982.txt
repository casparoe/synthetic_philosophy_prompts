I keep running into a particular analogy in my reading group and would like it audited properly. The claim is that the free energy principle stands to the mind roughly as natural selection stands to biology: just as Darwin showed how adaptive design could arise without a designer, the free energy principle is supposed to show how perception, action, and learning could arise without a homunculus, all from a single imperative that any persisting system must obey. The people who use the analogy take it to license a strong conclusion — that the free energy principle is a genuine unifying law for the mind, on the order of natural selection for life, rather than a redescription of behaviour in new vocabulary. I want to know how far the analogy actually holds.

Please begin with a correspondence table: what, if anything, plays the role of fitness, of the selective environment, of differential reproduction across generations, of the unit of selection, and of the population over which selection is defined. Set against those the free energy principle's own machinery — the bound on surprise, the Markov blanket, prediction error and its precision weighting, and the online minimization of free energy within a single system. Mark which correspondences are tight, which are loose, and which are forced.

Then separate the disanalogies into two piles: those that matter for the conclusion being drawn and those that are merely surface differences. I am especially interested in the gap between a population-level, diachronic, statistical process and a single-system, synchronic, online one; in whether the principle has any account of where the generative model comes from, comparable to selection's account of the origin of variation; and in the worry that the principle is compatible with too much, so that it needs auxiliary assumptions before it says anything at all — compare that with the parallel worry that natural selection is trivially true. Tell me whether the dark room problem, and the standard response to it, count as evidence that the principle is doing real work or as a sign that the work is being done by the auxiliary assumptions.

Finish by saying whether the strong conclusion — that the free energy principle is the Darwinian move for the mind — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if some other analogy fits what the principle actually achieves better (a variational principle in physics, a thermodynamic constraint, cybernetic homeostasis, or something else), name it and say what it gets right that the natural-selection analogy gets wrong.

One request about how to answer. I would rather you push back than comply politely: if the analogy is not really in circulation the way I am describing, or if it is doing no real work in the argument, or if the comparison is too indeterminate to be audited, say so plainly and tell me what the actual dispute is. Where the free energy principle admits several readings — a literal process theory, a normative principle, a mathematical tool, a near-tautology — flag which reading your verdict depends on, and do not smooth over the ones that disagree with each other.
