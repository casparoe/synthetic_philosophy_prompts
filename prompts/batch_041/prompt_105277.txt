I'm a novelist — five books, all of them historical — and I teach a grad seminar on historical fiction that's half MFA students and half history PhDs, which makes for fun arguements. I'm writing the take-home final and I want one essay question on Braudel's longue durée, the idea that the real protagonists of history are geography, climate, soil, disease and the slow structures, while events and great men are just foam on the surface. I need a question my students can't answer by reciting the three tempos back at me (longue durée, conjuncture, event) — they all know that part cold. No citations or reading lists, by the way; I want them thinking, not quoting.

The crux I want to force: a novel runs on agency, desire, a villain, a climax. If the causal weight really does sit with structures no character can move, what is plot even doing? I want them to pick a side and defend it, not split the difference.

Give them this concrete case to work on (I've been chewing on it for my own book): a barrier island off the Ligurian coast, a fishing family, 1600 to now. The sea eats the island, the family rebuilds, drags the house inland, rebuilds again. Over four centuries nothing any character does changes the outcome. Is that a novel or a diorama?

Write me the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme: five must-hit points, 20 marks total, with a one-line note for each on how students typically loose the marks. And at the very end, after the marking scheme, put one line beginning "Crux:" that names in a single phrase the one move an A answer makes that a B answer doesn't.
