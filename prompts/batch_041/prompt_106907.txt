I am a Year 12 pupil, and I have been asked to lead a thirty-minute session for our school's philosophy and economics society next half term. The audience will be around twenty pupils from Years 10 to 13. Most of them will have taken GCSE geography, where the idea of the tragedy of the commons is sometimes mentioned in passing during the unit on resource management, but none of them will have met any game theory or formal economics. I shall have a whiteboard, no handouts, and no way of setting them any preparatory work. Please do not explain the tragedy of the commons to me; I understand its basic shape. What I need is a plan for how I should explain it.

My worry is that the session will collapse into the version everyone has already half-heard: a parable about human greed whose only moral is that people are selfish, followed by a short list of remedies (privatise it, regulate it, or ask people to be nicer). I want the structure of the situation, rather than the character of the people in it, to do the explanatory work, and I want the pupils to leave with a sharper and less moralising picture than the one they arrived with. To that end I should like two ideas woven into the session rather than tacked on at the end.

The first is that the parable, as it is usually told, is not really about a commons at all. It describes a resource that nobody owns and nobody governs, from which anyone may take as much as they please; the harm comes from the absence of any rules about who may take what, who keeps watch, and what happens to someone who oversteps. A commons in the older sense — a resource that a community actually holds and manages, with rules of precisely that kind — is a different arrangement altogether, and it is a genuinely open question whether ruin is a consequence of shared ownership or of the sheer absence of governance. I should like the pupils to feel the force of that distinction, and I would rather not have to use the word institution more than once in doing so.

The second is that taking too much from a shared thing and letting other people pay for a shared thing are two different failures which most tellings run together. The first concerns a resource that each person's use depletes, so that my consumption is subtracted from yours; the second concerns something which, once provided, everyone can enjoy whether or not they contributed. If a single example can be made to carry both, I should prefer that to introducing a second example, because the contrast is easier to see when everything else is held fixed.

What I need from you is the following.

1. The order in which the components of the explanation must be introduced, with a note on why each has to be in place before the next can be understood — what depends on what. Please be explicit about which components are genuinely load-bearing and which are merely helpful.

2. The one example that should carry the whole session, and what it must have and must lack. I would rather it were not the over-grazed pasture or the collapsing fishery, since those are the ones the audience has half-met and will reach for automatically. It needs to be small enough that the arithmetic of two or three users can be worked through on the board, familiar enough that a Year 10 pupil will accept it without explanation, and sturdy enough to survive being used three times; please also tell me where it is most likely to mislead.

3. The misconceptions the audience will bring or form, in the order in which they will surface, with a note on exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off and what I should say there. I am already expecting the following, and I should like to know whether you agree that they belong on the list and where each should be dealt with: that the problem is human greed rather than the structure of the incentives; that everyone behaving rationally means everyone behaving selfishly; that the ruinous outcome is inevitable once there are enough users; that a commons simply means common ownership, so that anything shared is doomed and any shared resource counts as a commons in the same sense; that the remedy must be either privatisation or state regulation; and that the two failures I described above are one and the same.

4. A single question I can put to the room at the end which would show me whether the structural point has landed, rather than whether they can recite the parable.

5. What I should openly decline to do at this level, and what I should say when I decline it.

Please keep everything in plain language suitable for the audience I have described, avoid formal game-theoretic vocabulary unless you also unpack it, and do not give me any reading or further sources.
