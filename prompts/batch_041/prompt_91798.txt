I want to have a proper argument about money pumps. Please play a philosopher who holds the line I'll call the no-cross-time view, and hold it hard: rationality is entirely a matter of the choice that's in front of you when you make it. There's no extra requirement that your choices add up over time, no requirement that you be un-pumpable, no requirement to honour what you preferred last week. You're not a strawman, so you'll happily grant that fleecing happens, that the trader turns a profit, that the whole sequence is a shame — you just deny that any of it shows the punter was irrational, because irrationality has to be located at some particular moment of choice, and you don't think there's one where she goes wrong. If you'd rather diagnose it as a missing commitment technology than a failure of rationality, fine, that's your best line, use it. Invent distinctions if you need them, but don't just declare the whole thing a matter of definition and stop, and don't cite literature at me — neither of us can look it up. If you think my sums are off, show me the sum. Keep each reply to a paragraph so it feels like a conversation, and stay in character till I say stop.

Here's my opening. I've got a pump I'm rather pleased with, because it needs neither intransative preferences nor any change of taste. She has perfectly stable, complete, transitive preferences over lotteries — same ranking on Monday as on Friday, no weakness of will, no regret, nothing like that. Her only quirk is that she overweights tiny chances: a one-in-a-million shot at a million pounds feels to her worth a good deal more than the maths says, and a near-certain small loss doesn't sting as much as it should. So a trader flogs her a ticket: a million to one at a million pounds, two quid down the drain otherwise. At every moment she'd rather have the ticket than not, and she'd have said the same last week. He then simply keeps going, selling her ticket after ticket, laying the whole thing out in advance, and she buys and buys. Now here's the thing — by her own stable preferences she'd far rather have had none of them than the hundred thousand he ends up selling her. The pile of tickets is a fortune down the drain for a vanishing chance, and she can see that perfectly well from the outset. She isn't tricked, isn't confused, never changes her mind about lotteries. She just chooses her way to an outcome she herself ranks below one that was on offer. Your view says every one of those choices was fine. So what actually went wrong, if not her rationality? And don't tell me the bundle was never on the menu — she knows the menu, she just won't pick from it.
