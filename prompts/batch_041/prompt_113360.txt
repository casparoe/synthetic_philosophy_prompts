Okay, novelist here — book three, and I've hit something I cant untangle alone.

My protagonist signs up for a service I'm calling a 'preference audit.' The company watches you for six months — what you buy, where you actually go, what you actually eat, which emails you answer at 11pm — and hands you a report on what you want, as opposed to what you say you want. The way they pitch it, every choice you make is evidence about what you were trying to get, and they just add it all up. Her husband has spent years saying he wants more time with her; the report suggests otherwise. Her best friend keeps waving the whole thing off with 'actions speak louder than words,' and that line is doing alot of load-bearing work in a scene that needs to be an argument, not a bumper sticker.

So here's what I want from you: the full menu of things 'actions speak louder than words' could actually be claiming. I can see a couple — behavior is better evidence than testimony; testimony doesn't count at all; there's no such thing as a want apart from what you do — but I don't trust my list, and I don't know which ones are true or which ones would actually get the friend her conclusion. Lay them out from the obvious to the ones that would make a philosopher's hair catch fire, and for each one say whether it holds up and whether it supports what she's using it for.

And a related thing I need for the plot: could the audit itself be wrong? If the machine is inferring what someone wants from what they did, is there any way two completely different people — different wants, different lives — could generate the same six months of behavior, so the report can't tell them apart? If that's possible, what should the friend be saying instead? I need one line of dialogue that's actually defensible. Right now I've got a fridge magnet.
