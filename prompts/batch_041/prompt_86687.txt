I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a metaphysics thing that actually matters to the plot, so I need you to compare two positions for me — properly, not a Wikipedia summary.

Setup: a museum has a bronze horse, very old. It gets knocked over, and the conservator repairs it, swapping out a cracked chunk for new bronze. Years later the museum melts the whole thing down and recasts it from that same metal back into the same shape. I've got two characters who disagree about what happened, and I want to understand both.

Character A thinks there's only ever one object here. "The statue" and "the bronze" are just two ways of talking about the same thing, so asking "did the statue survive the recast?" is a bit of a category error — the statue just is the bronze, and the bronze obviously survives being melted.

Character B thinks there are two objects all along, sitting in the same place at the same time — the statue and the lump of bronze — with different histories. The statue can't survive melting and recasting; the lump can. So the recast is the same lump but a brand new statue. (B also insists that melting doesn't even preserve the lump, because a lump is partly defined by how its bits are stuck together, so melt it and you've killed the lump too — I don't know if that helps him or hurts him.)

Compare these two. Which handles the repair case, the recast case, and the museum's catalogue better? The catalogue is where it bites, because someone has to decide whether the recast horse is the same acquisition or a seperate one, and under B's view the museum has quietly been acquiring lumps this whole time and its records are a mess.

I want the real tradeoffs — where each view gets awkward, what each one has to say about the other's best case — not just a verdict. And please don't name philosophers or hand me a reading list, I need the argument itself, grounded in the story. I'm going to put bits of this in dialogue so I need to be able to defend both sides.
