I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on technology and human survival. The room is half historians of science who know von Neumann's defense work and his game theory in detail, and half engineers and policy people who mostly know him as the stored-program computer guy and the bomb guy. I want the talk to be about his actual view of whether we can survive our own technology: not the standard "he predicted accelerating change and warned us" cliche, but the harder thing underneath it, that he treated the whole situation as a problem in unstable equilibrium, where the danger isn't technology by itself but the mismatch between what we can build and our institutions' capacity to keep it under control, and where he was willing to seriously entertain options most of us would call monstrous rather than assume the equilibrium props itself up. He wasn't a retreat-from-technology guy and he wasn't a comfort guy either, and the speech should not smooth that over. Structure the remarks around that tension so it does real work: it has to be honest about what he seems to have thought without sliding into either hagiography or a defense of it, and it has to land for the non-historians without dumbing down the part the historians in the room will be listening for. Five minutes or so, in the voice of someone who admires him and is a bit appalled by him. Paraphrase rather than quote him directly, I don't want to get a line wrong in front of this crowd.
