I direct the ethics desk for a Buddhist-founded humanitarian organization that runs clinics and mobile relief teams in a conflict-affected region. Our field staff are serious practitioners with no formal training in Buddhist doctrine or in moral philosophy, and they write to me — about fifteen times a month — when a situation has them stuck. The questions are nearly always the same shape: something the precepts appear to rule out (lying, taking what has not been given, occasionally worse) looks like the only way to prevent a serious harm, and the person wants to know whether they may do it, and if so how to do it without becoming someone they do not want to be. I have about twenty-four hours per case, I am the one who signs off, and the decision goes into a written record that our board reviews afterwards.

I would like a decision procedure my volunteers can actually run: a short ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it stops and hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of value rather than of fact. It has to be usable by someone who knows the five precepts and has heard of karma but has never read a sutra, and short enough to work through in a jeep. Do not send me a reading list; our volunteers will not read it and I cannot check it. Tell me what to do.

Ground it in what Buddhist ethics actually holds rather than in a Western consequentialism wearing robes, and be explicit about where the two come apart. Four things I want the procedure to take seriously, because they are where our informal reasoning keeps failing:

- Intention. As I understand it, the tradition locates the weight of an act primarily in the intention (cetanā) rather than in the outcome, which means that "it turned out well" and "I meant well" are each only part of the story, and that a harmful act done for good consequences still carries its weight. I want a step that interrogates the intention and that has something to say when the honest answer is "partly vanity" or "partly I wanted to be the one who saved her."
- Skillful means. Our volunteers have all learned that a precept can be departed from when compassion requires it — which is exactly the reasoning that every self-deceiving person uses. I want a test that separates a genuine exception from a rationalization, and if that test cannot be made into a step, I want the procedure to say so rather than fake one.
- The non-aggregability of karma. Buddhist ethics does not seem to me to license trading one person's suffering against another's, and I suspect some of our hardest cases look tractable only because we have quietly started doing utilitarian arithmetic. If that is right, I want the procedure to name the step where the arithmetic would enter and to say what replaces it.
- No-self and the question of who pays. If there is no self that owns the act, it is not obvious to me why the volunteer's own integrity should weigh in the decision at all — yet the precepts are addressed to the person about to act, and our volunteers care intensely about exactly that. I do not know whether this is a real tension or a confusion on my part. Either resolve it or tell me it is confused.

I have been teaching our volunteers a four-part breakdown of a complete act that I assembled from things I have read — the object or being involved, the intention, the effort or deed, and the completion of the act — and I would like the procedure built around it if that is a defensible use of it. If I have mangled the analysis, or am using it for a purpose it was never meant to serve, say so and build the procedure on something better.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: (a) paying an unofficial fee at a checkpoint to move a medical convoy that will otherwise be turned back, with a patient aboard who will probably die; (b) a family's request that our volunteer nurse not tell a patient his diagnosis, at the moment the patient asks the nurse directly; (c) sheltering a man who is being hunted, and lying to the armed men who come looking for him, when the lie may put the whole team at risk. One of the three should come out as a no, and I expect at least one answer to be one I will not enjoy taking back to the field.

Be blunt about the limits. If a decision procedure is simply the wrong instrument here — if the tradition's real answer is that such cases belong with a teacher, or that a rule-following procedure is already a misdescription of how a trained person decides — tell me that instead of manufacturing one, and tell me what I should be doing with my fifteen cases a month. Throughout, mark clearly which steps are Buddhist doctrine, which are my own managerial inventions dressed up as doctrine, and which are contested among Buddhists. I would rather have a procedure with three honest holes in it than a tidy one that hides them.
