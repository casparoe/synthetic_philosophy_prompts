I'm a graduate student in philosophy, currently in a seminar on metaontology, and I've become fixated on one snag that our reading group keeps circling without resolving. I'd like a genuine conceptual explanation of it rather than a map of the literature. The snag is how to distinguish a substantive disagreement from a merely verbal one, and I want to approach it through a test that a friend in the group has proposed, because I suspect the test is either circular or trivial and I want to see exactly where it fails. The test runs as follows. Take a dispute — over whether a tomato is a fruit, whether a split-brain patient has one mind or two, whether a lump of clay and the statue it constitutes are one object. For each party's assertion, look for a paraphrase that the other party would accept and that preserves everything the first party cared about in asserting it. If a genuine disagreement survives both paraphrases, the original dispute was substantive; if nothing survives, it was merely verbal.

What I'd like explained is what this test is grasping at and where the conceptual trouble lies. In particular: (a) what intuition about meaning, assertion, and disagreement the test codifies, and why it is tempting; (b) why it appears circular, since determining which paraphrase preserves what a speaker cared about seems to presuppose an account of what the speaker meant — the very thing the test is meant to supply; (c) whether the circularity can be broken by relativizing verbality to a shared question or purpose, so that calling a dispute merely verbal is shorthand for calling it merely verbal relative to the question the parties were trying to answer, and, if so, whether that reduces verbality to a matter of interests — collapsing merely verbal into not worth arguing about — or whether it preserves a genuine distinction between the two; and (d) what becomes of the test when it is turned on itself, that is, when two philosophers disagree about whether some first-order dispute is verbal: is that second-order disagreement itself verbal or substantive, and can the test be run at the second level without an infinite regress? I'm less interested in a catalogue of proposed criteria than in the underlying tension, so please work through it rather than listing positions.
