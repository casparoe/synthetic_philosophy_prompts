I'm a novelist — two years into a book that keeps threatening to become a different book — and I've been sitting in on a friend's seminar this term. She's a philosopher who's taught Pascal's wager for something like fifteen years, and I started writing down her rulings on various versions of it because they're so much more definite than I expected from a philosopher. Roughly: (1) the wager as Pascal sets it up, finite cost, infinite payoff, minuscule but nonzero probability — bet; she says the argument works, and oddly that she'd say so even though she isn't religious, because the reasoning is about the shape of the gamble and not about whether the religion is true. (2) Same wager but the cost is huge, though still finite — you give up your career, your country, your friends, most of what makes your life yours — still bet; she says no finite number is anything next to an infinite one, so the size of the sacrifice is a distraction. (3) An infinite payoff with an infinite cost on the other side — eternal bliss conditional on your child's eternal torment, say — and here she refuses to rule at all; two infinities don't outrank each other, she says, so the calculation never gets off the ground. (4) An infinite payoff at a probability of one in a googol, offered by a guy in a parking lot for five dollars — no bet. (5) The same one-in-a-googol probability, but offered by a church that's been around two thousand years — bet. (6) An infinite payoff riding on a proposition you know to be false — no bet, obviously. What I think I'm seeing: she bets whenever the tiny probability is "live," attached to something that's actually a going concern in human life, and refuses when the probability is a thought-experiment artifact; and she won't play at all when both sides are infinite. She also mentioned, in passing, that Pascal himself doesn't leave you there — he tells you to go through the motions, take the water, have the masses said, and says the belief will come along after. I might be garbling one of those, I've been taking notes in the dark.

Here's the case I actually need, because my protagonist has to make the call on the page and I refuse to write her a wager I don't understand. Same structure as (5): the payoff is infinite, the probability is live in the sense above (a long-standing tradition, not a parking lot), the cost is finite and she can afford it — forty years of Sundays. But the payoff is explicitly conditional on her *believing* it at the moment of death, sincerely, and not merely on her having shown up. And she's a woman who left the church precisely because she couldn't stand pretending, whose whole adult life is built on not saying things she thinks are false, and who knows that the only route to the required state is to spend four decades cultivating a conviction she takes to be false. Does my friend say bet? I want the rule she's actually running, not a menu of options. Is it just the live-probability filter plus finite cost, in which case nothing changes from (5) and my character bets? Or is there a side-constraint in there that I've missed — no bets whose required act she'd condemn on independent grounds — in which case the answer depends on whether she counts deliberate self-deception as one of those acts? Or is the real rule something about which acts can be options at all: you can't decide to believe, so the wager as stated isn't a decision problem, and the only wager actually on offer is Pascal's practice-version, whose payoff condition is the doing rather than the believing? Give me a definite prediction, tell me which of those three rules fits her track record best and why, and then tell me what single further case would sort it out — I keep thinking it's a wager with the same live probability whose required act is wrong in some non-epistemic way (you have to talk one particular stranger out of their faith, say), but if you've got a better discriminator, use yours. Commit to something. If I'm going to put a number in this character's mouth I want to know what you'd stake on it, and how confident you actually are.
