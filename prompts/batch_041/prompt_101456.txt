A freind in my history reading group has an argument that "multiple causes" is a myth, and I can't find the exit. (1) X caused E only if E wouldn't have happened without X. (2) A proper multi-causal explanation of E lists several causes — the drought, the blockade, the collapsed harvest, the panic on the grain exchange — each of them meant to be doing real work. (3) Now take any one, say the drought. The others are still sitting there. (4) If the others were already enough for E on their own, then without the drought E happens anyway, so by (1) the drought didn't cause it; same for every item, so the list names no causes at all. (5) If the others weren't enough on their own, then what we've got is only a jointly sufficient set, and calling each member a cause is just loose talk for a condition. (6) Either way the neat picture of an event having several causes collapses: you get one thing that really made the difference, or you get a set of conditions — not a list of causes. He reckons "overdetermination" is just the word we use when we haven't yet worked out which single thing really did it. I'd rather keep (2) than (6), but I can't see which step to give up or what giving it up would cost me. If the whole thing is a muddle and there's no clean line to draw, please just say so — I'd rather be told that than handed a tidy answer that papers over it.
