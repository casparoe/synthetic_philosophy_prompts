I direct the standards office of a national product-safety and environmental-health authority. Very little of what we regulate is injury; most of it is annoyance, irritation, disturbed sleep and low-grade stress. We issue binding conditions on products, workplaces and installations most weeks, each within a six-week statutory window, each signed off by me personally, each appealable by the party we regulate. My analysts are good at exposure assessment and have no philosophical training and no time to acquire any. What they need from you is a procedure they can run on a file without me in the room, and that I can defend to a tribunal. It must give the same answer whether a file describes a situation person by person or as a single population-level condition.

The board has already committed us to two principles, and they are why I am asking for a procedure rather than a cost-benefit manual. The first is a severity floor: a harm that the person affected would not notice, would not report and would not change their behaviour over is not counted one by one. The same harm, repeated on the same person, does count — and the board wants the procedure to say why those two cases come out differently, given that in neither of them does any single person ever experience the sum. The second is a no-amount clause: some harms are severe enough that no quantity of sub-floor harms can justify them. The board's consultant has proposed four triggers for promoting a sub-floor harm to a countable one — recurrence on the same person above a stated frequency; falling on people already below a baseline of functioning; being inseparable from something the person cannot avoid or opt out of; and belonging to a set of similar sub-floor exposures that jointly cross the floor for that person. I need to know which of the four the procedure should keep, which it should rewrite, and which it should throw out, and on what ground. My analysts have noticed that the floor and the no-amount clause point in opposite directions on our own case history, and they need to know which governs when they conflict.

Two files are on my desk, and the procedure has to settle both. Case one: a water authority proposes a treatment that leaves a faint odour in the tap water of eight million households, permanently. Most residents cannot detect it even when told to look for it; about one in ten notices something perhaps once a month; there is no health effect any doctor would recognise; nobody can opt out without buying water, and the authority is the only supplier. Case two: a household device has a failure mode that causes second- and third-degree burns to about forty people a year, some needing grafts, and the redesign that eliminates it would add roughly the price of a cup of coffee to a device bought by twelve million people a year. Our economists insist that price increase is a cost to be counted, and I do not know whether the floor applies to money, or whether the forty burns can be weighed against twelve million small costs at all.

My own instinct, which I would rather have tested than confirmed, is that case one is a nuisance we should not regulate and case two is an obvious violation. If your tests put either case the other way, say so in plain terms rather than bending the tests toward my instinct.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. Items one to three are the ordered tests, each stating the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where a yes ends the inquiry, and where the answer is a matter of values rather than facts. Item four is the verdicts on the two cases, including whichever one goes against my instinct. Item five is what the procedure cannot decide, the question it must hand back to me, and the earliest warning sign that it is about to misfire on files like these.
