I've talked myself into a view about the centipede game and I want to know how exposed it is. Heres the view. The backward induction 'paradox' isn't a discovery about rationality, it's a theorem about how strong the standard assumptions are. The whole collapse needs each player to be certain -- not just pretty confident, certain -- that the other will be rational at every later node, and needs that certainty to be common. Drop it to something like 'at each node I'm 95% sure you'll be rational there' and I dont think you get defection at node one; I think you get a few rounds of cooperation and then an unraveling, which is closer to what happens when people actually play the thing (they pass for a while, and how long depends on how much is at stake). So the game doesn't show that rationality eats itself; it shows the standard solution concept assumes more about another person than anyone could ever be entitled to. What would count against this? I want the arguments and results that should genuinely move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be, plus which bits a defender of the standard line would themselves concede are awkward for them. And be straight with me about whether I've just made the view unfalsifiable -- whatever data you show me, I can absorb it by adjusting how confident I say each player is. Finally, am I conflating two different fixes, making the horizon uncertain vs making the other player's rationality uncertain? I suspect I am. Also, does anything change if the last move is made by a machine that will certainly take? A friend reckons that's the whole paradox in miniature and I can't tell if he's right.
