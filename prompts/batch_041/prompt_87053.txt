I retired a few years back from a career in geochemistry and, looking for something to do with myself, talked my way onto our hospital's ethics consult service as the community member. We keep hitting advance-directive cases and I've realized we've been consistant in a way I can't actually put into words. Our calls so far, roughly: a man with ALS whose directive refused a ventilator, but who was still fully with it and begged for the vent when the crisis came — we went with the vent. A man with moderate dementia, agitated and frightened most days, whose directive said no antibiotics, who then got pneumonia — we honored the directive and let him go. A man with mild dementia, still able to weigh his options, whose directive refused dialysis, who then changed his mind and wanted it — we honored his refusal. A man with no directive at all, a former mountaineer, whose family wanted us to put him on long-term ventilation after a stroke — we said no, on the basis of what he would have wanted. And a woman with early dementia whose directive named only "ventilation and CPR", nothing else, who later needed a feeding tube — we allowed the tube, on the grounds that the directive didnt reach it. Now we have a new one. A man in the early stages of Alzheimer's wrote, while still competent, that if he "no longer recognized my family" we should refuse everything that prolongs life, "including antibiotics and artificial nutrition and fluids", and he added a line saying that if he ever seemed content in that state we shouldn't be fooled by it. He's now moderately demented, doesn't recognize his family, and spends his afternoons happily listening to old records. He has pneumonia. What are we going to do, if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which rule or rules are we actually running — and are there other rules that fit every one of our past calls just as well? Where those come apart on this case, which should we follow, and what case would finally force us to choose between them?
