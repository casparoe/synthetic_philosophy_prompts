Stanisław Lem's Solaris (1961) and Andrei Tarkovsky's 1972 adaptation are usually discussed as stories about the failure of contact with an alien mind, or, in Tarkovsky's case, about grief and conscience; Lem himself complained that the films traded away the novel's interest in the limits of human rationality. I want a reading of the novel, using the films as foils where the divergence is instructive, through a narrower and more technical problem: how one could ever identify the ontology of a system whose internal states are inaccessible. The ocean is the limiting case. Solaristics has produced an enormous catalogue of observed phenomena and no theory; the crew cannot agree on what kind of thing they are orbiting — a single vast mind with intentions, a swarm or colony of sub-agents, a purely physical process onto which we project mentality, or a mirror that returns only what we bring to it. Distinguish three positions the novel might be dramatizing and say which, if any, it actually takes: (a) the ontology is determinate but epistemically inaccessible, so the failure is ours; (b) the evidence underdetermines the ontology, so every identification is one translation manual among many; (c) the ontology is determinate but not expressible in our concepts at all. The visitors are the test case — Harey (Rheya in the Kilmartin–Cox translation) for Kelvin, with the analogous figures for Snaut, Sartorius, and the dead Gibarian only alluded to — since the ocean appears to answer the researchers with their own repressed material: are the visitors data about the ocean, or a mirror that tells us only about the humans, and does that ambiguity favor (a), (b), or (c)?

Two further things. First, the methodology: solaristics cannot intervene on its object and works from an n of one, so the usual experimental route to fixing an ontology is closed. Does the novel present this as a practical obstacle or a principled one, and does the scientists' proliferation of mutually incompatible theories read as Quinean indeterminacy or as ordinary bad science under an impossible brief? Second, where the novel resists the philosophical framing: is it really about ontology identification at all, or does it subordinate that question to something ethical or existential — the demand to be answered, the refusal to leave the question open, the wish for the ocean to be a person? End by saying what the fiction adds that the philosophy of other minds and the underdetermination literature do not: what it shows about the cost of the demand for an ontology, or about why we cannot simply suspend judgment. Keep the reading faithful in both directions — do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a thesis, and do not bend the philosophical problem to fit the plot.
