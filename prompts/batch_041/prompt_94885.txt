I'd like you to act as a philosopher of history who is also a real literary critic -- someone who knows Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler and Toynbee cold, and who won't let me get away with hand-waving. Before you write the reading, spend a few sentences brainstorming out loud: what are the actual positions on whether history moves in cycles or in a line, which of them are live options, and which of them have any teeth when you point them at a novel instead of the real world. Then do the reading.

Here's what I can't stop thinking about. Psychohistory. Hari Seldon's whole deal is that you can't predict individuals but you can predict huge populations, so the fall of the Galactic Empire is basically a law of nature and the only open question is how long the dark age lasts. That smells like a cyclical view of history wearing a lab coat -- empires rise, rot, fall, on a schedule, nobody in particular to blame. But then the Foundation keeps getting saved by individual people being clever (Hardin, Mallow), and then the Mule just torches the prediction entirely, which is the opposite claim: history turns on persons and contingencies. And the Seldon Plan itself is a linear, progressive thing smuggled in the back door -- the collapse is inevitable but the recovery is *engineered*, which is not what Spengler would say about a declining culture at all. Ibn Khalduns asabiyya / group solidarity wearing out over a few generations, and Polybius' cycle of constitutions (kingship sliding into tyranny, aristocracy into oligarchy, democracy into mob rule, then back to a strongman) both feel like they're in the book's DNA, but I can't tell if Asimov is actually using them or just reproducing the vibe of grand historical fiction.

So: read the Foundation trilogy through the cyclical vs linear debate, and be honest in both directions. Where does the cyclical idea genuinely illuminate a plot turn or a choice -- Seldon's trial, the Encyclopedists deciding to keep building vs the Board of Trustees, Hardin's brand of cunning where force is what you reach for when you've run out of brains, the Mule as the great-man counterexample that breaks the whole scheme? Where does the book resist the reading, i.e. where is it just doing space-opera stuff and the philosophy is decoration? And does the book actually *add* something the theorists don't have -- does psychohistory as fiction capture a version of the cycle/linearity problem that Polybius or Toynbee couldn't, or does it just beg the question by assuming determinism and then cheating with heroes whenever it wants a good scene? Don't reduce the novels to illustrations of the theories, and don't bend the theories to fit the novels. If the honest answer is "Asimov is genuinely confused about which view he holds and the confusion is the interesting part," say so and tell me why.
