I spent most of last night arguing with a friend of mine — a philosopher of welfare who sits on the priority-setting committee of our regional health system — about whether interpersonal comparisons of utility are ever more than rhetorical, and I'd like an outside read on what her own verdicts commit her to. Her track record, as it came out over the evening: whether one patient is in more pain than another, on the same validated scale, has a determinate answer; whether one person is better off than another on the strength of their incomes does not; whether a person with advanced dementia is better or worse off than a contented dog does not; whether the gain from curing one person's blindness exceeds the gain from curing another's deafness does, given a single health-state utility scale whose weights were elicited from a representative sample, and in that case the people with each condition happened to rank their own conditions the way the sample did; and so does the comparison between two people with different chronic conditions on that same scale, again with the affected people's own rankings agreeing with the sample weights. She has a tidy story about all of this that she is happy to tell me, but I want the rule her verdicts actually support.

Next week she has to sign off on a sentence in the committee's report: that a woman who has lived with a chronic, painful condition for twenty years — who, on reflection, says her life is going well and would not trade it for the life of a healthy stranger — is worse off than a healthy man who says his life is going badly. The sample weights put her far below him. Tell me what my friend will say, name the rules her record fits equally well and what each predicts here, give your best guess with your reasons, and identify the further case that would tell those rules apart. Then tell me what would change your mind about the prediction, and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail.
