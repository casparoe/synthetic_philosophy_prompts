You are a philosopher of decision theory with a good ear for comic timing. I am putting together material on counterfactual mugging and the blackmail problems in the same family, and I want a joke that a decision theorist would genuinely laugh at rather than merely recognise. Think carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming as instructed below.

Work from the following, and treat it as your whole brief: add no claims about the literature, cite no one, and attribute nothing to any real person, living or dead. Call the predictor Omega. In the standard counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin; if it comes up tails, it asks you for £100; if it comes up heads, it gives you £10,000 if and only if it predicted beforehand that you would have paid in the tails branch. The causalist's line is that your payment cannot affect a coin already flipped or a prediction already made, so you refuse. The policy or 'updateless' line is that what is being chosen is the rule you follow, and the rule 'pay' collects the £10,000 in the heads branch while losing only £100 in the tails branch, so it is worth adopting. Note the shape of that case: it is mixed-upside, because the paying rule still loses in one branch. The blackmail variants in the family work differently: someone who knows the coin arranges for the harm to befall you depending on the exclusive-or of the coin and your response, so that no branch of the world rewards you for paying, and the £100 is simply gone.

Now the wrinkle I want the joke to exploit, which I have not seen written up and which you should treat as the interesting part. Take a variant with no coin in it at all, because the branch-determining fact is Omega's computation of your own decision. Omega's rule is this: if its computation says you would pay, you are handed £10,000 and never asked for anything; if the computation says you would refuse, you are asked for £100 and the money is taken. Nothing is random. The prediction and your decision must land on the same value, so Omega is solving for the fixed point of the very procedure it is predicting, and the prediction is correct only if the agent's rule and Omega's output agree. Observe that this variant is all upside for the agent who adopts the paying rule and all downside for the agent who does not, which is not true of the coin version, where the paying rule still loses somewhere.

Write the joke. It should run to no more than 250 words, in the form of a short exchange or a compact anecdote, whichever gives you the better ending. It must be self-contained: a reader who knows counterfactual mugging but has never encountered the fixed-point variant should get it from the text alone. The humour must turn on the structure — the fixed point, the contrast between mixed-upside and all-upside cases, the fact that Omega is modelling a procedure whose output is what makes the prediction true — and not merely on the observation that decision theorists have peculiar problems. Avoid the lazy move of having a character declare the situation paradoxical or announce a label; if a character's reasoning is faulty, that should be the joke, and the reader should be able to see exactly where it goes wrong.
