I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, though I came to it from law — I read for a degree in it and practiced for two years before switching — so the common-law habit of reasoning from cases is more native to me than anything in the ethics literature I now read. My dissertation touches on the old question of whether one can learn to be good from exemplary lives: Plutarch's paired biographies, the saints' lives and the hagiographic idealization they underwent, and the contemporary revival of the idea that exemplars, rather than rules, are the primary objects of moral theory. I keep catching myself redescribing the whole debate in the vocabulary of precedent, and I want to know how much of it actually survives that redescription.

Please restate, as faithfully as you can, the principal positions here — that moral knowledge is carried by the study of particular exemplary persons rather than by the derivation of general rules, that biography is consequently a mode of moral instruction, and the standard objections that hagiography flatters its subjects and that an inimitable saint teaches nothing — using the vocabulary of common-law adjudication: precedent and stare decisis, ratio decidendi as against obiter dictum, distinguishing and overruling a case, the case method, and the contrast between case law and codification. Treat each position as a claim about what binds and what merely persuades, and let the comparison across Plutarch's pairs do the work that comparing precedents from different jurisdictions does.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the ethical debate pass through the translation intact, which pass only if I distort them, and which have no counterpart in common-law reasoning at all? I am especially interested in whether the residue marks a real gap — for instance, the thought that an exemplar may be supererogatory, going beyond what any rule could require, so that it can inspire without binding — or whether it merely shows that the talk of learning from lives was looser than the legal frame can tolerate. As a side question, say whether the case method is a better model of moral learning than it is of legal learning.

Tell me how confident you are in each part of this, and single out the point you are least sure of.
