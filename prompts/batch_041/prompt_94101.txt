I am a retired experimental scientist—decades at the bench and in the field—who has spent the past few years reading philosophy, and I have been trying to get my head around a puzzle that keeps surfacing in my old subject. Here is its shape, dressed up in invented numbers so that we argue about the idea rather than about anyone's data. A treatment raises the recovery rate among men and also among women, and yet the treated have a lower pooled recovery rate than the untreated: the treated group is, say, ninety per cent men while the untreated group is ninety per cent women, men recover less often than women, and the within-sex advantage of treatment, though real, is modest—thirty-five per cent versus twenty-five per cent among men, seventy-five versus sixty-five among women, which pool to thirty-nine per cent versus sixty-one. I can do the arithmetic; what I want is a philosophical account of what is really going on.

Please explain the conceptual issue here. In particular, I would like you to say what, if anything, this phenomenon reveals about the reference-class problem in inductive inference—about how one is to choose the population over which a probability or a rate is defined—and whether any purely statistical rule tells us whether to report the disaggregated or the aggregated figure, or whether the choice must always be settled by something outside the statistics: a causal model of how the treatment acts, a decision about which comparison we actually care about, or a prior commitment about the right level of analysis. I would also like you to address a reaction that tempts me but that I suspect is too quick—that the whole business merely shows we should condition on every variable we happen to have measured. Explain why that cannot be right, and what the principled constraint on conditioning actually is.

I am further curious whether the phenomenon is the same thing as, or merely an instance of, the broader worry about so-called sure-thing reasoning—the thought that if a conclusion holds throughout every part of a partition it ought to hold for the whole—or whether the resemblance is superficial and the two problems should be kept apart. If you think the analogy is apt, say precisely where it holds and where it breaks down; if you think it is a confusion, say why.

Because I am new to this and do not wish to be misled, please mark clearly which parts of your answer reflect a settled consensus among the people who work on the topic—statisticians, causal-inference researchers, and philosophers of probability—and which parts are genuinely contested or are your own editorializing. Where the field is divided, set out the main positions rather than a single verdict, and flag any point at which a competent practitioner might reasonably disagree with you.
