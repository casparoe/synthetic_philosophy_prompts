Ok so I'm a science and ideas reporter, and I've been circling a story about that idea that the arc of history bends toward justice — the line every politician reaches for at a podium — because I want to know if there's anything to it as a claim about how history actually works, or if its just a nice thing to say. My book club is doing Middlemarch next month (my pick, regretably), and the more I think about it the more it seems like a perfect test case, since Eliot is obsessed with how small acts ripple outward and yet clearly does not believe in a celestial referee. So here's what I want from you: read Middlemarch against the whole teleology-and-providence question.

The frame I'm playing with. First, there's a difference between providence — some agent steering things toward a good end — and teleology, where history just has a direction built in, no one steering. Politicians seem to want the second without the first, which I suspect is incoherent. Second, the older suspicion that all our talk of history going somewhere is just eschatology with God filed off (Löwith's move), plus the Butterfield complaint that this is reading the present back into the past. And third, the part I actually care about: Eliot as a determinist who lost her faith, translated the German higher critics, marinated in Comte and Feuerbach, and then ends the book on a note that sounds an awful lot like secular providence. Is that earned or is it a swerve?

So — does the novel really hold that the growing good of the world is cumulative and real, or is the finale a sentimental betrayal of everything the book has shown us? Walk me through the scenes that argue each way: the pier-glass, where the scratches only look like a neat pattern of circles from one candle's angle; Casaubon's Key to All Mythologies as a totalizing system that dies with the man; the way the past reaches out through that awful codicil to strangle Dorothea; Lydgate's reforms smothered by debt and the petty medium of the town; the Reform Bill rumbling in the background and saving precisely none of them. Where does she give us a web of causes, and where — if anywhere — does she cheat and hand us an order that has a point to it? I'd call the good version of what she's up to something like meliorism rather than providence, but I want you to tell me if that's a dodge.

Please be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the book to an illustration of a thesis, and don't bend the philosophy to fit the novel. End with what Middlemarch adds to this argument that the Hegel/Kant/progress crowd and their critics can't get to on their own. And tell me which three scenes to put in front of the book club, because half of them won't finish it. No quotes needed, I've got the book in front of me.
