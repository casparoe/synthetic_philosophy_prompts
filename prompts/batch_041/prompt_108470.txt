I sit on the grantmaking committee of a mid-sized philanthropic foundation, and I have a question about the machinery we use. I am an economist by training, so I can follow the arithmetic but not the philosophy underneath it. Everything we do runs through a single index: every candidate intervention — deworming campaigns, unconditional cash transfers, a legal-aid clinic, tuberculosis screening, a program that rehomes rescue dogs — receives a score in dollars per unit of good, and the committee funds everything above a threshold and nothing below it, publishing a ranked list of grants from best to worst. The unit of good is not taken from any published theory. It is fixed by a spreadsheet tab the staff call the moral weights, assembled over years by putting small improvements to the world to the committee one pair at a time: a year of moderate pain against a small loss of income, a year of blindness against a year of severe depression, an extra year of life at eighty against two years at fifty, and so on, each answer recorded as an exchange rate and then chained together so that everything ends up in one currency. Every individual comparison, taken by itself, struck everyone in the room as obvious. Two other features of the practice matter here. The spreadsheet contains a tab that scans the recorded rates for cycles, and in the years I have been on the committee it has flagged a cycle twice: once we went back and revised the offending rate, and once we simply left it in place and went on. And the same committee that treats the list as a real ranking will, in the very next agenda item, refuse to fund an intervention that would save several lives if it requires a single identifiable person to be harmed, and has quietly exempted a handful of ultra-rare-disease treatments from the threshold, paying something like a hundred times more per unit than anything else on the list.

What I want to know is what this practice presupposes. Does the workflow actually need betterness to be transitive, or only locally well behaved — can a committee that compares things pairwise and then chains the results get away with a relation that fails transitivity in the abstract, so long as no cycle ever surfaces in the table? Does it need a cardinal scale, or would any ordering that respects the pairwise judgments serve? Does it need a single common currency, or can it survive value pluralism with several currencies, provided they are traded off at fixed rates? What does it commit us to that we have not noticed — in particular, does ranking grants against a threshold and ranking whole world-states in the same currency force one and the same conclusion, so that a long enough series of small, individually obvious steps would commit us to preferring a vast population of lives barely worth living to a small flourishing one, and if so, is that a reductio of the method or of the intuition? And how should I understand the two anomalies, the rare-disease exemption and the refusal to harm one person for several lives: are they extra commitments layered on top of the index, are they the index applied with weights nobody has written down, or are they evidence that we do not really believe our own currency? I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the argument, and where you are unsure, say so rather than reaching for a name.

Please act as a philosopher of value and of measurement, at home in axiology, decision theory, and measurement theory, writing for a smart non-economist who may genuinely have to change the spreadsheet if you are right. Think carefully before writing, and begin by brainstorming the candidate presuppositions — transitivity, completeness, cardinality, interpersonal comparability, separability of the dimensions, and whatever else belongs on the list — before deciding which ones the practice strictly needs. Then lead with two or three sentences stating your overall verdict, and give the analysis as a single JSON object with these fields: "verdict" (string, two or three sentences); "implicit_theory" (string, the view of value and of the betterness relation the workflow requires, stated plainly, as if writing the committee's unwritten constitution); "presupposition_ledger" (array of objects, each with "claim" as a string, "status" as one of "strictly required", "fits the practice but not required", or "underdetermined by the practice", and "argument" as a string); "ruled_out" (array of objects with "view" and "why_the_practice_rules_it_out_or_fails_to", each a string); "cycle_tab" (object with "what_a_cycle_means", "what_leaving_it_in_place_commits_you_to", and "what_revising_the_rate_commits_you_to", each a string); "spectrum" (object with "chain" as an array of the comparison steps you would use, "where_transitivity_does_the_work", "what_the_chain_shows", and "escape_routes" as an array of objects each with "route" and "cost"); "anomalies" (array of exactly two objects, one for the rare-disease exemption and one for the refusal to harm one person for several lives, each with "reading" set to one of "extra commitment layered on top", "the index with unwritten weights", or "evidence the currency is not believed", and "justification" as a string); "if_replaced" (array of objects with "replacement_view" and "what_would_have_to_change_in_the_workflow"); and "residual_uncertainty" (array of strings). Be rigorous rather than exhaustive, and flag clearly wherever the practice turns out to be licensed by several incompatible theories at once.
