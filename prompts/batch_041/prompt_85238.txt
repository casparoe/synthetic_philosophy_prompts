I am to give the commencement address at the college where I teach history, and I would like you to write the address for me.

The occasion: a small liberal arts college, roughly six hundred graduates, an outdoor ceremony, nine minutes of speaking time. In the audience are the graduates; their families, many of them first-generation, for some of whom this is the most significant public occasion of their lives; the trustees; and, in the fourth row, my colleagues from the history department, several of whom believe my last book was wrong. The president has asked me to be uplifting. I have agreed to be uplifting and truthful, which is the harder assignment, because the graduates have been promised, in one form or another since they arrived, that history bends toward justice — that their efforts will be gathered into a long story that ends well. I have spent a career arguing that no such arc can be demonstrated, and I will not spend nine minutes pretending otherwise. What I want from this speech is a reason to act that does not depend on a promise about how things turn out.

Here is the material I want the speech to work with, and I would rather it work than display it:

- Three readings of the arc. On the first, the arc is drawn: history is a story with an author and an ending already written. On the second, there is no author, but the arrangement of things — institutions, interdependence, the widening of who counts — makes some outcomes steadily likelier. On the third, nothing bends on its own; the curve we see is the sum of what people did, read backwards through the records that happened to survive. I hold the third and feel the pull of the second; the first is what I must decline without insulting anyone in the audience for whom it is a matter of faith.
- An arc is a shape; a ratchet is a mechanism. A ratchet permits motion in one direction and blocks the reverse, and it can be taken apart. Some of what we call progress is held in place by institutions, norms and enforcement; some of it is a trend that could run the other way. I would like the graduates to leave with the habit of asking which of their gains are ratchets.
- Hope, expectation, promise. Hoping that something will happen is compatible with believing it unlikely; expecting it is not. A promise is not a forecast. I want to move the arc out of the register of prediction and into the register of commitment, and I want the speech to be honest about what that move costs.
- The ledger. If history has a direction, then the people destroyed along the way are the price of arrival. I do not want this resolved, only acknowledged, and not allowed to become a reason for standing still.
- And one sentence, no more, on the fact that the shape of the curve is partly an artifact of whose papers were kept.

Constraints on how it is written:

- Do not lay the distinctions out as a numbered taxonomy. This is a speech, not a lecture; the ideas should surface through how the speech handles its examples and through the order of its parts.
- Quote no one. No dates, no statistics, no attributions to named thinkers, no specific documented episodes — if you need an example, keep it generic. I have no sources at the podium and no wish to be caught.
- The hardest question in the room is this one, and it comes from the fourth row: if hope is being asked to do motivational work, is that not providence smuggled back in through the side door? Face it; do not glide past it.
- Leave room for the families whose own faith supplies them with an arc. Do not flatter them and do not condescend to them.
- The close should give the graduates something to do rather than something to believe.
- Plain spoken prose, warm, no throat-clearing about difficult times, no listicle cadences.

About 1,200 words.
