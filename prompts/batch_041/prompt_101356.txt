I am convening a two-day, invitation-only workshop on the singleton hypothesis, and I am to give the opening remarks — roughly eight minutes, immediately before the first session. The audience is deliberately mixed, and I have no interest in pretending otherwise. About a third of the room works on long-term AI governance and regards a singleton as, at best, a fragile hope and, at worst, a trap; another third are institutionalists and career diplomats for whom any talk of a single global decision-maker sounds either utopian or menacing; the remainder are funders and policy staff who have encountered the phrase and are not certain what it names. There are no professional philosophers present.

I want remarks that open the question rather than answer it, and several constraints make that harder than it sounds. I hold a view, but the remarks should not reveal it; my task is to make the disagreement sharp enough that people who differ can differ well. I also want to avoid the two moves that are everywhere in this area and that I find cheap — treating a singleton as merely a synonym for world government, and treating it as either plainly inevitable or plainly impossible. And I want to be candid about something uncomfortable: a gathering whose stated aim is to produce one unified set of recommendations is itself a small singleton, and I would rather say so than pretend the workshop is neutral ground.

Build the remarks around the framing below rather than the standard one. I have come to think of the singleton as a threshold rather than a thing: what matters is not who or what holds the power but whether the number of independent decision-makers still changes the outcome. Below the threshold, adding or removing one decider alters the result; above it, it does not. On this view a singleton might be a state, a model, a market, a norm, or a coalition, and the live question is not "could a single actor win" but "does anyone remain whose defection would change the outcome." Let a second distinction do work as well: between a singleton that decides the substantive questions and one that decides only the rules by which everyone else decides — a constitution rather than a sovereign.

Write the opening remarks. Introduce this framing in plain language for the non-specialists; lay the real disagreement on the table as a short set of questions the sessions will take up; and close in a way that sends people into the first session with something to argue about rather than a mood of consensus. Keep it free of named theories, citations, and reading lists — the audience does not need them, and the room would treat them as a distraction. Where the framing obliges me to say something that will irritate one faction, let it irritate them; do not smooth it over.
