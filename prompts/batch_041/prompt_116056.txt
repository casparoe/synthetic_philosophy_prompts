So context: I'm a science journalist — I mostly cover AI and philosophy of mind — but I guest-teach one session a semester in a grad seminar on the philosophy of AI, and this week the prof dumped the weekly response papers on me because she's traveling. The assignment was a scenario with three short parts, and the students had to answer all of them. Here it is:

"Imagine two agents, A and B, that cannot interact causally — nothing B does can reach A, and nothing A does can reach B. B announces to A: 'Unless you hand over your resources (do X), I will spin up a large number of simulations of your decision procedure and subject those simulations to severe torment.' A knows B is telling the truth about its capabilities. (a) In what sense is this an 'acausal' threat, and why could it be rational for A to do X even though B can't touch A? (b) Suppose instead that A pre-commits, before B's threat lands, to a surrogate goal: a goal A will pursue only in the world where B actually carries out the torment, chosen so that it's cheap for A but bad for B. Explain how this is supposed to defuse the threat. (c) Does anything change if A is a committed causal decision theorist?"

Now here's the response I'm trying to grade:

"The threat is acausal because B can't actually do anything to A — no causal channel runs from B to A or from A to B, so at first it looks like A can just ignore it. But it can still be rational to comply, because of logical correlation. If B is going to run a simulation of A's decision procedure, then whatever A decides gets computed again inside the simulation, so the copies B torments are basically running A's own algorithm. A's choice and their fate are correlated through the shared algorithm even though nothing A does reaches them causally. So if A complies, A is effectively choosing that the algorithm outputs 'comply' everywhere it runs, including in B's simulation, which spares the copies.

Actually I think the cleanest way to put it is that an acausal threat is really just an acausal trade. B offers A a deal: if A does X, B leaves the simulations alone; if A doesn't, B torments them. Both of us are better off if A takes the deal, so it's mutually beneficial, and that's why it's rational to comply. Same logic as superrationality in the prisoner's dilemma — you cooperate because you're playing against a copy of yourself.

On the surrogate goal: instead of letting the threat move A, A commits in advance to a goal that triggers if B actually carries out the threat. Say B torments the sims — then A's surrogate goal kicks in and A devotes itself to something B hates. Now carrying out the threat is bad for B, so B won't do it, and the threat falls apart without A having to comply. The key is that the surrogate goal is conditional; A only adopts it in the world where B goes through with the threat.

Where I'd push back on the standard story is the mechanism. I think the surrogate goal really works by changing A's own preferences. If A pre-commits to care about something else in the bad world, A has made itself someone who doesn't mind the torments, so the threat has nothing to grab onto — it's like the Stoic move of deciding that what B does to the sims isn't actually bad for you. So the surrogate goal isn't really about B's incentives at all; it's about A being able to credibly not care.

Part (c): if A is a causal decision theorist, the analysis changes completely. CDT only looks at what A's action causally brings about, and A's action doesn't causally affect the sims, so CDT says it doesn't matter what A does and A should just do whatever else it prefers. The whole threat depends on the agent reasoning about correlation instead."
