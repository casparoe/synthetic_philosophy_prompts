I teach physics and film at a small independent school, and a recent diagnosis in my family has made Einstein's determinism feel like something I need to think through rather than just teach. I am preparing a session for our film club on the Coen brothers' A Serious Man, and I want to pair it with Einstein's views on religion and free will. What I need is a reading of the film through those views — one that takes both the film and Einstein seriously, and that is honest about where the film resists the reading.

Einstein's position, as I understand it, has three strands. He rejected a personal God who intervenes in human affairs, regarding that idea as anthropomorphic. He described a cosmic religious feeling — an awe before the lawful order of the universe — as the source of both genuine religion and scientific inquiry. And he was a determinist who denied free will in the philosophical sense, often invoking Schopenhauer's remark that a person can do as he wills but cannot will as he wills. He also famously resisted the indeterminacy of quantum mechanics, though I am less interested in that than in how the film stages the tension.

The film seems to stage all of this. Larry Gopnik is a physics professor whose life is falling apart: his wife is leaving him for a man the film calls a serious man, his brother Arthur fills a notebook with a deterministic scheme he calls the Mentaculus and describes as a probability map of the universe, and the film opens with a shtetl couple and a possible dybbuk and closes with a tornado and a phone call about a chest X-ray. Larry consults three rabbis. The first tells him to change his perspective, comparing it to looking at a parking lot from a different angle. The second tells him a story about a dentist who found Hebrew inscriptions on a non-Jewish patient's teeth and never learned what they meant, and concludes that it does not matter. The third, the senior rabbi, has been unavailable all film and finally offers Danny only a line from a Jefferson Airplane song. Meanwhile, Larry's classroom lecture on the uncertainty principle and Schrödinger's cat includes the remark that even if you cannot figure anything out, you will be responsible for it on the midterm.

I would like you to address the following, in an integrated reading rather than a list.

First, the Mentaculus. It is a deterministic scheme, but it is also a kind of scripture — a way of finding order in a universe that seems indifferent. How does it illuminate or complicate Einstein's determinism? Is it a parody of his search for a unified theory, or a sincere image of it? And how does it relate to the film's other images of order and disorder — the dybbuk, the tornado, the chest X-ray?

Second, the prologue. The wife stabs Groshkover because she believes he is a dybbuk. If he is a dybbuk, there is no murder; if he is a man, there is. The film never resolves this. What does that ambiguity do to the question of free will and moral responsibility? Does the film suggest that we can never know whether an action was free, or whether it was determined by forces beyond us?

Third, the classroom scene. Larry's remark about responsibility in the face of uncertainty seems to conflate epistemic uncertainty with quantum indeterminacy. Is the film making a philosophical point, or is it trading on a confusion? How does Einstein's determinism stand in relation to the uncertainty principle as the film presents it? I am wary of a reading that uses the film to say something about physics that the physics does not support.

Fourth, the rabbis. All three fail to give Larry the answer he wants. Does the film endorse Einstein's rejection of a personal God, or does it go further and suggest that the cosmic religious feeling Einstein prized is itself a projection, a way of coping with a universe that has no order? Where does the film's sympathy lie — with Larry's search, with the rabbis' evasions, or with the indifference of the tornado?

Fifth, the title. The film applies the phrase a serious man to Sy Ableman, the man who takes Larry's wife, and it is also the eulogy for him. What does the film mean by seriousness in a universe that may be deterministic, random, or providential? Does Einstein's determinism help us understand the film's tone, or does the film's tone — comic, bleak, unresolved — resist the philosophical position?

I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to a set of illustrations of Einstein's views, and it should not distort Einstein to fit the film. Where the film resists the reading, I want you to say so. And at the end, I would like you to say what the film adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not — what it shows about the lived experience of determinism, or about the difference between cosmic religious feeling and the search for a personal God.
