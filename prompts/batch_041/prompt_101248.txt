I'm trying to put the ethics of eating on a quantitative footing, and I want the specification before any verdicts. Write the desiderata an adequate theory of the moral cost of a diet must satisfy — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the empirical findings it cannot contradict — with the case behind each requirement. It has to handle at least these: the difference between an animal being killed and an animal living a life that is on balance bad; the fact that the beef-versus-chicken comparison reverses depending on whether you count suffering per calorie or deaths per calorie, since smaller animals mean more individuals killed for the same nutrition; that crop cultivation itself kills field animals and vast numbers of invertebrates, so a plant-based diet is not a zero-harm option; that a pasture system spares field animals but keeps large animals alive longer before slaughter; the possibility that farming animals creates lives worth living, so the ledger might credit existence rather than only debit death; deep uncertainty about whether invertebrates are sentient at all; and the counterfactual of what the land and its wild inhabitants would be doing if it were not farmed. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are assumptions of a particular camp, which requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement excludes the most candidate theories. I am not asking which diet is right. End by saying where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or evidence would change your mind about which requirements are non-negotiable.
