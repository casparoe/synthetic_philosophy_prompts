I was at this small meditation group thing last week and two of the regulars, Renee and Tom, ended up going at each other for like an hour while the rest of us sat there with our tea. They're both pretty sharp and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it, so I'm hoping you can sort it out for me.

Renee started it by saying Buddhist ethics is basically just consequentialism in disguise. She said everything in it bends toward reducing suffering — for you, for other people, for animals, whatever — and the precepts are really just rules of thumb that you drop when following them would cause more harm than good. She gave the usual kind of example, somebody lying to a murderer at the door about where his victim is hiding, and said any Buddhist with half a brain lies in that situation, which shows the rule was never absolute.

Tom said that's a tourist's version. He said the consequences Buddhism actually cares about are karmic, and karma doesn't work like a welfare calculator — it ripens for the person who acts. If you lie to save somebody, fine, you saved them, but you've also planted something that comes back on you. He said that makes it not consequentialism at all. Consequentialism says great, one life saved, net win. Karma says you did a thing, and the doing of it is on you regardless of how it turned out. He said that's closer to a deontological or even a prudential system than to Mill.

Renee came back that he's got a Sunday-school picture of karma. She said karma isn't a cosmic ledger that pays you back — it's more like a description of how mental states condition the next mental states. Act out of greed or hatred and you strengthen greed and hatred; act out of generosity and you strengthen that. So the consequence is the shaping of the mind, and since minds are part of the world, that's still consequences, just a different currency than pleasure and pain. She said Tom was just assuming consequentialism has to be about external states of affairs, which is a pretty narrow definition.

Tom said fine, but then we've moved the target. If the thing being maximized is the quality of the agent's own mind, that's virtue ethics, not consequentialism — the locus of evaluation is character and not the total amount of good in the universe. And then he dropped what might be his strongest point: the whole thing denies there's a self anyway. If there's no enduring self, whose mind is improving over time? There's no owner of the karma. So agent-relative karma can't be about a person's long-run benefit, because the person isn't there to collect. He said that either makes it a kind of impersonal process, which weirdly pushes it back toward consequentialism, or makes it not really an ethics of action at all, just a description of causation in the mind.

Renee said that's clever but it proves too much, because if no-self dissolves karmic prudentialism it dissolves any prudentialism, and nobody thinks the Buddha was denying that it's better to suffer less. And she brought up the bodhisattva thing — the vow to keep coming back, even to the worst places, until everyone's free — and said that's obviously not a prudential calculation. If you're willing to take on suffering for others, you're acting like a consequentialist who just happens to think your own welfare is worth less than everyone else's. Tom said that's exactly backwards: the bodhisattva isn't maximizing anything, she's expressing compassion, and the point of the vow is that the compassionate act is good whether or not it works out.

Then Tom threw out a hypothetical I'm still chewing on. He said imagine a being who can either stay serene and clear while a huge number of others suffer, or throw herself into the mess and get her own mind wrecked for a long time — real hatred, real confusion, the works — but in a way that actually saves them. He said the consequentialist answer is obvious: wreck yourself. He said the karmic-prudential answer is also obvious: don't. And he said the tradition seems to say wreck yourself, which means it sides with the consequentialist against its own karmic bookkeeping. Renee said no, the tradition says the wrecking is itself a delusion, and she said something about how on the ultimate level there's no being who gets wrecked, which I didn't fully follow. Tom said that's a dodge that would excuse literally anything.

So yeah — can you go through this point by point and tell me who's right at each stage? Is Buddhist ethics consequentialist? Is Tom's agent-relative karma point a good one? Does the no-self thing actually cut against karma as a prudential system, or does Renee have a real answer to it? And does the bodhisattva example settle anything either way? Also tell me what both of them are missing. Please don't do the thing where the answer is just it depends, both have a point — I want actual verdicts on the specific moves. Write it as continuous prose, like you're explaining it to me in an email. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I don't need sources or a reading list or citations, just your reasoning.
