I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on scepticism, and I want from you a worked probability estimate rather than an opinion piece or a literature survey. There is no need to cite anything; I am interested in the argument, not the secondary literature.

The proposition I want a credence for is this: Moore's proof of an external world — the argument that begins from the premise that here is one hand and here is another and concludes that an external world exists — is dialectically efficacious against the sceptic. By dialectically efficacious I mean that a competent enquirer who sincerely entertains the sceptical hypothesis, that there is no external world and that the hands are not there, can be brought by that argument alone to rational conviction that an external world exists, without first having to be independently argued out of the sceptical hypothesis.

Two things this is not. It is not the claim that the proof is valid; I take that to be a matter of elementary logic. Nor is it the claim that Moore knew, or justifiably believed, that here is a hand. A proof may be rigorous and its author's premise may be known, and it may still be worthless against the person who doubts, because the doubter cannot be brought to grant the premise. It is that last question, and only that, that I want a number on.

Please decompose your credence into the sub-questions it actually rests on, with a rough probability and a one-line justification for each, then show how they combine. I would expect the decomposition to include at least:

(a) whether the premise can be known non-inferentially — whether the mere fact that this looks to me to be a hand can make it knowledge that it is one;

(b) whether the inference transmits warrant to the conclusion or instead presupposes it, so that the proof begs the question against the doubter;

(c) whether knowing something requires being able to rule out the sceptical hypothesis, or only requires that the hypothesis be false and the route to the belief reliable;

(d) whether the doubter's doubt is a state that can be rationally sustained, or an unstable posture that dissolves as soon as the doubter acts;

(e) where the burden of proof lies in the exchange, and whether that is a substantive point or a rhetorical one;

(f) whether the proof's force is first-personal — whether it works only for someone who can perform the premise for themselves, looking at their own hand, in which case what is offered is a proof-schema rather than a proof, and its availability to a hearer who must take Moore's word for the premise is a separate and much weaker matter;

(g) whether the proof is damaged by the fact that the same form of argument can be run in reverse, the sceptic reasoning from the premise that I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat to the conclusion that I do not know that here is a hand; that is, whether the symmetry of the two arguments shows that neither establishes anything, or whether the symmetry is exactly what a proof of this kind should look like.

Then tell me which of these the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move it most.

For calibration: I put my own credence at about 0.15. A colleague of a dogmatist temperament puts his at about 0.7, on the ground that the proof is perfectly good and that the doubter's refusal to accept the premise is the doubter's defect rather than the proof's. Tell me which sub-question our disagreement most plausibly lives in, and whether it is really a disagreement about Moore's proof or a disagreement about the epistemology of knowledge more generally — in particular, about whether knowledge has to be defensible to a doubter in order to count as knowledge.

Finally, and importantly: separate for me what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who have thought about this carefully, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus. If some of the sub-questions above are not live disagreements at all, say so and drop them from the decomposition rather than manufacturing balance.
