I'm assembling materials for a seminar on relational personhood — the family of views on which a person is not a self-sufficient individual but something constituted or sustained by relations of recognition, care, and mutual obligation, with the southern African Ubuntu tradition as the paradigm case. My recurring frustration with how these views get taught is that they collapse into a slogan, a vague gesture at collectivism, and then a quick objection about whether relationality can accommodate individual rights, and the discussion never reaches the interesting part: what a society actually built on a thoroughgoing relational conception of personhood would look like from the inside — what it would demand of its members, where it would grind, and whom it would leave outside. I'd rather have fiction than another essay, so please write me a short story, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, that dramatizes this.

Set it in a society that takes relational personhood seriously in its institutions, not merely its rhetoric. Give me a concrete mechanism rather than an abstraction: let personhood be a standing that can rise and fall with the state of one's relations rather than a fixed status conferred at birth, and let that standing be publicly recorded through some ordinary civic procedure — a register, a periodic hearing, a ritual of mutual acknowledgment that people grumble about the way we grumble about renewing a license. Let the consequences of diminished standing reach into law, property, and the capacity to be wronged, so that someone who has become relationally isolated — through estrangement, migration, bereavement, or a crime — discovers that the loss is not only social but institutional. I'd like the story to follow one character through a single situation in which the system functions as intended for someone else and cuts against this character, or reveals a cost no one in the society has had to name. Earn the philosophical content through texture — the forms people fill out, the awkwardness of a recognition ceremony, the way children are taught to think about who counts — rather than through characters who deliver lectures to each other.

Then, in a short afterword of no more than 300 words, tell me plainly where the story takes liberties with the real ideas and which contested readings of relational personhood it leans on: in particular, whether the version you dramatized is closer to a recognition-theoretic account, to a more metaphysical claim that relations literally constitute the self, or to a purely normative claim about what we owe one another, and where you stacked the deck in the society's favor or against it. Don't give me a survey of the literature or a reading list — I want your own committed choices. Commit to a specific institution, a specific failure mode, and a specific resolution or deliberate refusal to resolve, and defend the ones you chose.
