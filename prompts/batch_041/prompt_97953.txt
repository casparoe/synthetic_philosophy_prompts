Some years ago I retired from a career in observational geophysics, and since then I have been working through philosophy on my own — most recently an online course on epistemology and rational choice — and I find that the one topic where my old scientific habits and my new reading pull in opposite directions is the business of following the crowd. At a dinner last month a philosopher friend pressed me on why I found some instances of deference obviously sensible and others obviously foolish, and I could not give her a clean answer, so I would like you to extract the principle from my own reactions. I have tried to record the cases as honestly as I can, including the ones that make me look inconsistent.

Here they are. Arriving in an unfamiliar town, I see one restaurant with a long queue and one empty, and I join the queue: perfectly sensible. A physician who adopts a treatment simply because his colleagues all use it, despite having read one small study that points the other way: I call that a failure of rationality, and a serious one. A layperson who accepts the consensus of climate scientists on a question she cannot evaluate herself: entirely rational. A juror who says nothing after the first juror has spoken and simply falls in with the emerging verdict: I think she is being irrational even when the verdict turns out to be right. An investor who buys because prices have been climbing and everyone else is buying: foolish. A graduate student who has read the primary literature, disagrees with her lab's settled view, and stays silent at lab meeting: I want to say she is doing something wrong, though less wrong than the physician. Choosing a coat because it is the current fashion: neither rational nor irrational, just a matter of taste. A scientist who surveys the literature carefully, forms a view, and then revises it toward the expert consensus on the strength of that survey: rational, and in fact admirable.

My tentative principle is this: deference to others is rational exactly to the degree that their choices or beliefs carry information I lack, and irrational to the degree that I possess information they lack or that their choices are driven by one another rather than by evidence gathered independently. I can already see that this will not rescue every verdict above, and I would rather have a correct principle than a flattering one. Please tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, best fits the pattern; what work each clause is doing and which cases force it; which of my verdicts I must abandon or revise; and what the surviving principle predicts for cases I have not listed — in particular, a commuter who takes the crowded road because everyone else has taken it, a historian who accepts a dating that rests on a single much-cited authority whom nobody has re-examined, and a case where a crowd's unanimity is itself the very thing each member is responding to. I would also like to know whether any of my disagreement with myself is merely verbal, resting on a distinction between acting on others' evidence and acting on others' actions that I have not stated sharply.
