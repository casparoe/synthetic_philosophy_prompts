I direct research for a small charitable foundation whose stated aim is to reduce severe suffering, and I need to convert our board's suffering-focused commitments into a screening procedure that my two program officers can run without me. We receive roughly thirty proposals a quarter, each asking for ten to sixty thousand dollars; each officer has about ninety minutes per proposal, neither has any philosophical training, and I have to sign off before the board meets, so the procedure must produce a written rationale a trustee can audit.

The board has adopted a deliberately austere accounting, and I want the procedure to honor it rather than quietly liberalize it. Benefits are entered only as reductions in suffering, never as gains in happiness or flourishing. Suffering is booked in two separate columns, one for severe and one for mild, and the board has forbidden us to convert one column into the other; the procedure must therefore say what to do when the two columns point in opposite directions instead of silently assuming a common currency. The board has also declined to settle, and has asked me not to settle on its behalf, the question of proposals whose principal effect is to reduce suffering by reducing the number of beings who will ever exist.

Please write the procedure. It should be an ordered set of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, explicit stop conditions where the procedure returns a no, and clearly marked points where the decision must be handed to a person because the question is one of values rather than of fact. Where the board's commitment is ambiguous between readings, the procedure should say which reading it encodes and flag the step at which that choice was smuggled in, rather than pretending the ambiguity is resolved. It should also state plainly which of the standard objections to suffering-only accounting it can answer, which it deflects by stipulation, and which it simply cannot handle.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, an insecticide that kills a major crop pest instantly, replacing a method thought to leave the insects in prolonged aversive states, where the number of individuals affected dwarfs that of every other grant in our portfolio. Second, a contraception and breeding-moratorium campaign for an animal currently bred in large numbers into chronic mild deprivation, so that the intervention works by preventing future lives rather than improving existing ones. Third, a crisis line that prevents deaths among people in chronic severe distress, where the board's division is sharpest. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like; say so rather than massaging the procedure. Close with the cases you expect the procedure to misfire on, and what it cannot do at all.
