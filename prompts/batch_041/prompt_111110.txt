A slogan I keep encountering in metaphysics discussions is that grounding stands to supervenience as causation stands to correlation: correlation is cheap and ubiquitous, causation is the real relation that explains why the correlations hold; so too supervenience claims are cheap and ubiquitous, and grounding is the real, non-modal relation that explains why they hold. It is used to license a specific conclusion — that no supervenience thesis, however carefully stated, tells us what is fundamental, so we need grounding claims, and those claims are substantive rather than trivial consequences of necessities. Audit the analogy. First give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the correlated quantities, the causal relata, the laws or mechanisms, the confounders, and the intervention. Then say where the mapping breaks. Some breaks I would expect to matter: correlation is symmetric while supervenience is not; correlations hold between quantities, whereas supervenience holds between properties, facts, or sets of propositions; correlation is contingent and known a posteriori, while supervenience of the relevant kind is necessary and often knowable a priori; and causation is traditionally supposed to hold between distinct existences, whereas grounds are frequently internal to the natures of what they ground. A colleague then pushes the analogy further: if it holds, there ought to be a grounding analogue of the exclusion argument — the grounded is necessitated by its grounds, hence never a difference-maker, hence idle — and an interventionist test for grounding claims, in which we manipulate grounds and observe what changes. Tell me whether those extensions come for free or are artefacts of the analogy, which of the disanalogies matter for the advertised conclusion and which are idle, and whether that conclusion survives. If it does not, sketch the better analogy.
