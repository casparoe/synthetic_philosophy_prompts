I'm in a seminar on moral change and I've been asked to write on the abolition of the British slave trade in 1807 and of slavery in the British colonies in 1833, using the historiography of moral revolution as my frame. Two accounts are on the table, and I want them worked through the episode rather than summarized. The first is the economic thesis associated with Eric Williams's Capitalism and Slavery (1944): that abolition is best explained by the decline of the Caribbean plantation economy and the rise of anti-mercantilist, free-trade interests in Britain, with abolitionist sentiment functioning as a legitimating ideology rather than a cause; I know this thesis has been contested by historians who point out that the slave economy was still expanding and profitable at the moment of abolition, and I'd like you to keep that objection in play rather than pretending the economic story is settled. The second is Kwame Anthony Appiah's account in The Honor Code (2010), where moral revolutions happen not because arguments win but because a practice becomes a source of dishonor, and where the British campaign against the slave trade is treated alongside dueling in England and footbinding in China as one of the central cases. What I find odd about the honor account is the reflexivity: honor sustained the practice, and then a reorganized honor killed it. I'd like you to take that seriously as a mechanism rather than a slogan.

I'll fix the facts so you don't have to guess at them. The trade was abolished in 1807 and slavery itself in 1833, with twenty million pounds paid in compensation to slave owners and a period of apprenticeship for the formerly enslaved; the Haitian Revolution ran from 1791 to 1804; slave revolts and the fear of them were a standing feature of the period; the abolitionist leadership was largely evangelical and outside the gentry whose honor Appiah treats as decisive; and slavery persisted in Brazil, Cuba, and the American South for decades afterward. What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions. For each theory: who the relevant agents are, what they want, what mechanism is supposed to do the work, what timeline the theory predicts, and what evidence would count against it. Then where each one strains — in particular on timing, on why the outcome came when it did rather than a generation earlier or later, on what role the enslaved themselves played, and on whether the persistence of slavery elsewhere is a problem for an economic story that is supposed to be about British capitalism. Then the reverse direction: does the case confirm either theory, embarrass it, or fall outside what it was built to explain? If the episode suggests a third mechanism that neither account names, say so, and if either theory is simply silent on something I've asked about, say that instead of forcing it to speak.

Please organize the response under these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths: "The episode, fixed" (about 150 words, just pinning down what we're explaining and what would count as explaining it); "The economic thesis, run through the case" (about 400 words); "The honor account, run through the case" (about 400 words); "Where each theory strains" (about 500 words); "What the case does to the theories" (about 350 words); and "Bottom line" (about 100 words, stating which account survives the episode better and what would change your mind). Keep the prose plain and analytic — this is a case analysis, not an essay on moral progress in general — and don't pad the sections with restatements of the theories themselves.
