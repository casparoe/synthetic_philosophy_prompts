I've been trying to reconstruct the argument from the old dispute over whether dreams are experiences — specifically the side that says a dream is nothing over and above the impression we have on waking — and I've made it cleaner than I found it, which worries me. Here it is.

(1) When I remember an experience of mine, the memory-report answers to something: the experience itself, its traces, or the testimony of others who were present. In principle it can be checked, and in principle it can turn out to be false.

(2) A dream report answers to nothing of the kind. No one else is present at the dream; once awake the dreamer has only the impression of having dreamed; and no scar, photograph, or second witness can confirm or disconfirm that I dreamed last night of being chased.

(3) So the only thing that settles whether someone dreamed, and what they dreamed, is their own sincere waking report.

(4) A report that is settled by nothing but the reporter's sincerity does not describe an occurrence standing behind the report; it describes the reporter's present state — the impression, or the disposition to give the report.

(5) Hence a dream is not an experience that occurs during sleep. It is a waking phenomenon: the memory-impression that greets us on waking.

(6) Hence the received view that dreams are experiences is false.

Two things make me suspect it proves too much. First, if (5) is right, a dream cannot be what causes the terror, the mood, or the reluctance to sleep that we routinely explain by it, since the waking impression cannot be caused by the very thing it constitutes; yet those look like paradigm cases of causal explanation. Second, by (3) and (4) only a creature that can avow a dream can dream; a dog or an infant, which whimpers and twitches in its sleep, would neither dream nor fail to — yet we say of the dog that it is dreaming.

A friend says the argument instead dies at (2): sometimes there is an independent check, since a sleeper narrates the dream aloud and a bedmate hears it, or a lab records the sleeper speaking or moving in ways that match the waking report. And she adds a case I can't fit into the tidy story in (4): a false awakening, in which I dream that I have woken and am telling someone about the dream I just had, so that the avowal sits inside the dream rather than behind it. If such cases are real, is (2) false, or can the argument absorb them?

If I have to give something up, I would sooner keep (2) and (3) than (6). Which step is the weak link? Say why it looked compelling, and price the alternatives: what do I give up by rejecting (2), by rejecting (4), by accepting the conclusion? If (4) is the culprit, can I drop it without surrendering (2) and (3) — and what would the argument's defender say I have thereby lost?
