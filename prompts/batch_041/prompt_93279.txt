Ok so context: I'm a third-year PhD student, my work sits between quantitative genetics and phil of bio (I know, I know), and I've been voluntold to write a short story for this weird interdiscplinary seminar we run. It's meant to be fiction that does actual conceptual work, not a lecture with characters stapled to it.

So: write me a short story, 1200-2000 words, that is really about heritability — the technical notion, not the vague nature vs nurture thing everyone already has opinions about. Here's what I want it to get right, and what I want the plot to actually turn on:

1. Heritability is a variance ratio, measured in a particular population living in a particular range of environments. It's not a property of a trait and it's not a number that travels. Somebody in the story should find that out the hard way — an estimate from one setting that just doesn't hold up in another.

2. Nobody should say anything like "the heritability is 0.6, so 60% of it is genes." If a character believes something like that, I want them to be wrong and for the story to make them pay for it.

3. The same genotype should show up in two different environments and behave differently — an actual norm of reaction in the plot, and ideally a crossover: the genotype that wins in environment A loses in environment B. Rank reversal, not just a shrinking advantage.

4. Somewhere a character should conflate a within-group heritability with a between-group difference, and that conflation should be the hinge of the drama.

5. High heritability should not work as an argument that an intervention is pointless. If someone uses it that way, the story should show why that inference is a non sequitur.

Setting: I'd love a fictional plant or animal breeding station — variety trials, plots that are all suspiciously uniform because everything gets the same irrigation and fertilizer, a grower whose fields look nothing like that, a breeder under pressure to pick a winner off one season of data. But if you've got a better frame (a human cohort study, a school district, a made-up country rolling out polygenic scores for something) go for it, just keep the numbers honest. Actually no, scratch that — the breeding station is a good fit, use it unless you really can't.

Don't have anyone cite real papers, studies or scientists, and don't invent fake ones either — the world can be fictional and generic, I don't need a literature review inside a story. And please don't let the characters give each other little lectures about variance vs causation; the concepts need to be load-bearing in the plot, so the reader has to notice it themselves.

End with like three lines from you, outside the story, about which of the above you were playing with and where. Not a bibliography, just so I can check you weren't secretly writing about nature vs nurture.

One more thing — no tidy resolution where a clever statistician explains everything and everyone nods. I want it to end with the ambiguity intact, because that's kind of the whole point.
