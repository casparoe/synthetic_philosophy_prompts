My colleague and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about inverse reinforcement learning, and we stopped only because we had each talked ourselves hoarse, not because we had converged. His position is that when we infer a reward function from a set of demonstrations, we are inferring a fact about the policy the demonstrator happened to run, and that calling the fitted function a preference is a courtesy extended to a curve. Mine is that something genuinely about the demonstrator's preferences is recoverable, and that his view mistakes the instrument for the object. What kept the argument alive was a distinction I proposed and then could not state cleanly, and which I would now like to turn into a proposal.

The distinction is this: a demonstration is evidence for a preference only relative to a description under which the demonstrator was acting. The person who takes the stairs rather than the lift may be revealing a taste for exertion, a fear of the lift, a belief that the lift is out of service, or a habit formed when it was. The same trajectory is evidence for different reward functions depending on which description we suppose the agent to have been acting under. Standard formulations fix the description in advance: they define the state and action spaces, the features, the horizon, and the discounting, and only then ask what reward best explains the behavior. If the description is itself something the inference should deliver, then reward and description are inferred jointly, and the identifiability results that are supposed to reassure us about reward alone no longer apply. My colleague thinks this dissolves the target rather than refining it; I think it locates the target more precisely, and that the interesting question is what additional structure, if any, could pin the description down without circularity.

I would like you to write the short research proposal that comes out of this, one page, for a small interdisciplinary workshop that puts philosophers of mind and machine-learning researchers in the same room, and I will be circulating it to people who will not accept a hand-wave. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, rather than as a lament that preferences are hard to read off behavior. Explain why the current formulations leave the question open, without pretending that anyone has posed it in exactly these terms before. Set out the approach I would actually take and why it beats the obvious alternatives, which for my money are either adding a hierarchy of reward functions or falling back on the demonstrator's own verbal reports; say what would count as a result, what would count as the reframing collapsing into something already standard, and what would count as the project failing outright. Be honest about the scope that fits the page limit and say plainly what part of this could not be settled on that scale. Do not include citations or a reading list; I will add those myself, and I would rather the proposal stand on the shape of the problem than on appeals to authority.
