You are a philosopher specializing in social epistemology and the ethics of memory, with a firm command of the literature on collective memory and an equally firm willingness to set it aside. I would like your help in reaching reflective equilibrium. I hold a set of verdicts about cases in which a community's memory and the archival record conflict, and I want to know what principle, if any, generates them, what that principle says about cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts I must abandon. Please think carefully before you write. Begin with a short working brainstorm in which you list the candidate principles you can see, before committing to any of them; then give me the finished response in exactly the sections I name at the end. Do not supply citations, attributions, or reading lists — I want the argument, not the bibliography. Where you need a technical term, coin one.

Two background ideas shape how I frame the problem, and you should treat them as part of what needs testing.

First, I distinguish a community's memory — the narrative a group tells about itself, carried in ritual, song, curriculum, and monument — from the record, the sum of surviving traces, documents, and material evidence. I do not think these are two rivals competing to answer a single question. My tentative proposal, which I call the jurisdictional principle, is this: the record has jurisdiction over claims, and the memory has jurisdiction over belonging. A community's memory must give way to the record when the memory is being used to press something against someone — land, labour, deference, honour, a place in the queue — but need not give way when it is doing only the work of holding the group together.

Second, and harder to fit to the first, is what I call mnemonic trusteeship: a community holds its memory in trust for those who will need it later, so that some falsifications are conservative, preserving something a bare record would destroy, and others are extractive, taking something from someone. I suspect trusteeship cuts across jurisdiction rather than nesting inside it.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A mill town's centennial plaque names its founder as a philanthropist who endowed the library. The record shows he endowed it with wages he had withheld for two years. My verdict: the plaque must be corrected, promptly and publicly.

2. My family's story is that my great-grandfather walked four hundred miles to escape conscription. The only surviving document is a railway ticket stub. My verdict: I would not correct this at a reunion, and I would resent the cousin who did.

3. My union local's founding story is that the 1934 strike was won by solidarity on the picket line. The record shows the employer settled because of a court ruling the union had nothing to do with. My verdict: keep the story in the ritual — the annual dinner, the banner — and correct it in the bargaining history we use in negotiations.

4. A displaced community's oral tradition places its origin at a spring where archaeology has found no pre-modern settlement. The community holds an annual ceremony there. My verdict: the ceremony stands, and no one owes them a correction.

5. A community's memory that its ancestors were enslaved on a particular estate grounds its claim in a reparations proceeding. The record shows they were free labourers defrauded of wages. My verdict: they should be told, even though it weakens the claim — and I notice that I would not tell them if the claim were not at stake.

6. A museum label describes a ceremonial object as a gift from a local chief in 1897. The acquisition file shows it was taken under threat. My verdict: the label must be rewritten; the fact that the object now anchors the community's identity does not license the description.

7. A village claims a customary right to a cove before a tribunal. The record shows the custom was invented sixty years ago by a parish clerk. My verdict: the village should get the cove anyway, because two generations have relied on the arrangement and the neighbouring estate never objected.

8. A diaspora's memory is that its people were expelled in a single night. The record shows a dispossession spread over a decade. My verdict: leave the songs and the sermons alone, but the diaspora's own schools should teach the slow version.

Please write your answer in these sections, in this order:

Summary — your own view of what my verdicts add up to, in about a hundred words.

The verdicts, restated — my cases in a numbered list with my verdicts beside them, flagging any that you think I have misdescribed, and any pair that looks to you inconsistent on its face.

Candidate principles — two or three, each named, each stated precisely in two or three sentences, with the specific case that motivates each clause.

Where they come apart — four hundred to six hundred words testing each candidate against every case, including the pairs I cannot reconcile, and saying plainly which candidate survives and which verdicts it costs me.

Verdicts on cases I have not put to you — apply the surviving principle to each of the following, then to one case of your own invention, and say where it strains: (a) a hospital named for a surgeon whose record shows he operated on patients without consent, in a town that also credits him with saving it from an epidemic; (b) a language revival movement built on a grammar that a nineteenth-century missionary partly invented, so that some of the forms now called ancestral are his; (c) my late mother's account of a family quarrel, which letters I have never shown anyone contradict.

What I have to give up — the verdict or verdicts the best principle cannot keep, and what the loss costs me.

Open questions — no more than five lines.
