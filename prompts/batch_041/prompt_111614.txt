In my junior-year seminar on literary translation, we have spent the past month on what our teacher calls translation under formal constraint — the case in which a text seems to carry part of its meaning in its form, whether that form is a rhyme scheme, a fixed meter, a lipogram, or an acrostic — and she has had us reading Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot* (1997), his book-length study of the many English versions that have been made of Clément Marot's short sixteenth-century French poem addressed to a sick young woman. Her central rule, which she states flatly and refuses to soften, is that a translator who drops a constraint has incurred a debt: she may pay that debt by substituting some other constraint that does comparable work, but she may not simply pocket the loss and call the resulting text a translation. She also told us, more cryptically, that constraints interact, so that two half-constraints are not worth one whole one. For our assignment she wrote an eight-line poem of her own in French that rhymes ABAB and is also a double acrostic — the first letters of the lines spell one word, the last letters spell another, and both hidden words comment on the poem's surface. My two study partners, Dana and Reuben, have now argued about it for a week, and I honestly cannot tell who has the better case. Dana's version keeps both acrostics and the ABAB rhyme, but in line five she renders 'bolted' as 'locked' because 'bolted' would not fit her syllable count, and she rewrites line seven's image to make her last letters come out right. Reuben's version keeps the literal sense of every line and keeps the rhyme, but drops the second acrostic entirely, on the grounds that English has almost no words ending in the letters the French requires. The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Dana says that a version without the second acrostic has not translated the poem at all, because the hidden words are not ornament: the poem exists in this shape only because every line was chosen under pressure from the constraint, and the two hidden words are part of what the poem is saying. She compares Reuben to a copyist who reproduces a painting's subject but not its perspective — the same objects, arranged falsely. (2) Reuben answers that constraints are not portable, because they exploit accidents of a language: which English words end in which letters is a fact about English, not a fact about the poem. Forcing the acrostic, he says, makes him write lines that misrepresent the original's tone and invent images the original never had, and he would rather produce a text that says what the original says. He adds that the acrostic is a property of French letters, so 'preserving' it in English preserves a shape while the substance drains out — Dana's version is a waxwork, and a reader of his version comes away with a truer sense of the poem than a reader of hers. (3) Dana replies that if the constraint genuinely cannot be carried across, the honest conclusion is that the poem is untranslatable in that respect, and that a translator who reaches that conclusion should say what she dropped rather than quietly abandon it; and she says Reuben's argument proves too much, since by his logic a translator could turn a sonnet into free verse and call it better because it says what the original says, which would end the discipline altogether. (4) Reuben insists he never claimed form is decoration — he claims constraints differ in kind and that the translator's job is to rank them. His test: if you can imagine the poem having been written without a given constraint and its point surviving intact, the constraint was packaging; if the poem could not have existed without it, the constraint was meaning. The second acrostic, he says, plainly fails this test. Dana calls the test circular, since the only way to know whether the poem's point survives without the acrostic is to know what the poem's point is, and the acrostic is itself part of the evidence for that; a reader who never notices it will cheerfully report that nothing was lost, which is ignorance rather than evidence. She also asks who is doing the ranking and by what authority, given that Reuben's ranking happens to put the constraints he finds hardest to satisfy at the bottom.

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange rather than take a side wholesale. Go through moves (1) through (4) in order and rate each one — clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right — with a brief justification for each rating, and within moves (1) and (3) please also give a verdict on Dana's particular choices, namely whether rendering 'bolted' as 'locked' is a legitimate payment of the debt she incurred and whether rewriting an image to satisfy an acrostic is ever acceptable. Then tell me what each of them is missing that neither has considered, and whether their disagreement ultimately rests on some prior question they have left unstated — for instance about what a translation is for, or who its reader is — such that the argument cannot be settled until that question is settled first. Finally, say whether our teacher's rule about incurring a debt is even usable as stated, or whether it needs a standard of 'comparable work' that no one in the room has supplied, and if so, what such a standard would have to look like. Please write your answer in American English, and be concrete about which specific claims survive and which do not.
