A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about vagueness, and I want to send him a poem this morning rather than another message. He is an epistemicist: for any vague predicate there is a sharp cutoff, though we can never know where it falls, so the sorites argument has a false premise but no one can know which. I am a supervaluationist: there are many admissible precisifications, a sentence is true only if true on all of them and false only if false on all, and in between there is no fact of the matter. Our test case was a will that leaves a caretaker a reasonable sum; the estate is large, her needs are modest, and the probate court's several admissible readings disagree about whether a proposed figure is reasonable. Please write a pantoum of four or five quatrains in which the two refrains are the two positions—one insisting on a single threshold that cannot be named, the other insisting that no single threshold is singled out as correct—and the form's interlocking repetitions do the work of showing why the dispute is not merely verbal. The poem should be from the point of view of the executor, or of the sum itself, and should end without resolving the case: the last stanza should bring the two refrains together in a way that makes clear they are not answering the same question. Avoid the technical vocabulary in the poem itself, and make it a good poem for a reader who has never heard of either view and a recognizable one for a philosopher of language.
