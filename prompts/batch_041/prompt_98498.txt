I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or statistics, and I am trying to settle a design dispute on our experimentation team. A colleague argues that Simpson's paradox proves something quite strong: because an aggregate result can be made to reverse by conditioning on a sufficiently detailed partition of the data, no aggregate metric is ever a valid basis for a causal claim, and the only safe practice is to condition on every variable we have measured and report the finest-grained stratum. She wants to encode this as a hard rule in our experimentation platform: whenever the aggregate direction differs from the direction in every pre-registered subgroup, the platform should suppress the aggregate result and surface the most granular stratum instead. I am inclined to think there is something wrong with treating a purely arithmetic possibility as a theorem about causation, but I cannot articulate the error precisely, and I do not know whether the proposed rule would ever be defensible. Could you explain whether Simpson's paradox actually licenses the claim that aggregate statistics are invalid, and if not, how I should redesign the guard so that it flags the cases where a reversal is evidence that the aggregate is confounded, rather than the cases where it is an artifact of conditioning on the wrong variables? I would find it helpful if the answer distinguished confounding from mediation and collider stratification, and said something about what the target estimand has to do with the choice between aggregate and subgroup results.
