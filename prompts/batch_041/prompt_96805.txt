At a party last weekend the host ran an auction for a one-dollar bill in which the two highest bidders both pay, though only the winner receives the bill. I won with a bid of seven dollars; the runner-up paid six-fifty and got nothing. My partner says I should have cut my losses and stopped at two, and a friend who studied economics says the reverse: that the money already bid is gone either way, that the underbidder is always better off raising than folding, and that the mistake was hers, not mine. Both of them cite, as though it settled the matter, the maxim 'don't throw good money after bad.' It has been nagging at me for days, which I gather is itself part of the phenomenon. I want to know what that maxim can actually be claiming. Give me exactly five numbered readings, each a sentence or two, ranging from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is a description of how people behave or a rule for what to do, whether it is true, and which of the two pieces of advice it actually supports. No introduction or conclusion.
