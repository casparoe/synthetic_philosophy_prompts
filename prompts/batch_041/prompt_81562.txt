I am preparing the opening episode of a small podcast on historiography, and my guest is a historian whose recent book defends an unusually strong reading of the longue durée. His thesis is that the decisive causal work in human history is done by slow-moving structures — ecological and climatic conditions, demographic regimes, infrastructural systems, and the durable routines of everyday life — and that the apparent causal efficacy of battles, decrees, and great individuals is an artifact of the short time-scales on which historians and their readers habitually think. He accepts the layered picture commonly credited to Braudel, with a near-immobile geographical stratum, a slower stratum of social and economic conjunctures, and the fast stratum of events, but he parts company with most readers of Braudel by insisting that the event stratum is explanatorily inert rather than merely superficial. His habitual move in argument is to redescribe any counterexample as an event-level phenomenon and therefore beside the point.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for a forty-minute conversation. The first two may be expository; everything after them should press him where the position is genuinely vulnerable, and every such question should be paired with a follow-up aimed at the reply he is most likely to give. I want the questions to oblige him to say how structures are transmitted and how they ever change if events do no causal work, and what becomes of structural transitions if nothing at the event level can redirect a trajectory; to confront the objection that a structure is a retrospective abstraction imposed on a tangle of local agency, and to say whether the middle stratum of conjunctures is anything more than events at a slower tempo; to address whether his thesis is falsifiable or only retrospectively confirmed; to explain why the thesis is not simply a determinism that dissolves individual responsibility; and to answer the charge that treating individuals as causally inert is quietism dressed up as method. Close with a question that requires him to specify what evidence or argument would lead him to abandon the strong thesis. Do not include questions that merely invite him to restate the thesis or summarize the book, and nothing about his intellectual biography or working habits.
