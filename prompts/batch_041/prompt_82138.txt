I have no academic training in philosophy; I have been reading it on my own, late at night, and I have run aground in the middle of an essay by Charles Peirce called "How to Make Our Ideas Clear," which I understand was published in 1878. The earlier parts I think I follow: that the meaning of an idea is exhausted by the practical differences its object would make, and that belief is a settled habit of action rather than a private feeling. Then, near the end, he turns to truth and reality and I lose my footing. The passage I keep re-reading is this:

"Different minds may set out with the most antagonistic views, but the progress of investigation carries them by a force outside of themselves to one and the same conclusion. This activity of thought by which we are carried, not where we wish, but to a foreordained goal, is like the operation of destiny. No modification of the point of view taken, no selection of other facts for study, no natural bent of mind even, can enable a man to escape the predestinate opinion. This great law is embodied in the conception of truth and reality. The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real. That is the way I would explain reality."

He then raises the objection I want to raise — that this makes what is real depend on what is ultimately thought about it — and answers that reality is independent "not necessarily of thought in general, but only of what you or I or any finite number of men may think about it," adding that even if our perversity conceivably caused an arbitrary proposition to be universally accepted as long as the human race should last, that would not change what the belief resulting from sufficiently prolonged investigation would have to be. There is a footnote on the word "fated" which, if I remember it rightly, says that fate means merely what is sure to come true and can nohow be avoided, and that it is a superstition to suppose that a certain sort of events are ever fated.

My difficulties are these. First, what sort of necessity is he claiming? "Fated," "destiny," "foreordained," "predestinate" all suggest that the world is arranged so that inquiry must converge; but if he means only that we call "true" whatever inquiry would finally settle on, then the clause about the object of that opinion being the real seems to say that reality is whatever the last inquirers say it is, which he explicitly denies. I cannot tell whether the convergence is a claim about the world, a claim about us, or a disguised definition. Second, I am bothered by "not necessarily of thought in general": it seems to leave open that reality might depend on thought in general even while he denies that it depends on any finite thinker. Is that carelessness, or is he closer to idealism than his reputation suggests? And does his account require that a final opinion actually be reached at some time, or only that there be a fact about what inquiry would reach if carried far enough? Third, if no finite inquirer can ever hold the final opinion, what does this definition of truth do for anyone? By his own rule about meaning, a conception with no practical consequences ought to be empty, so I want to know whether he has an answer here or whether this is precisely where the trouble starts.

Finally — and this may be the same question — I have also looked into William James's lectures on pragmatism, where truth is treated as something closer to what pays, what works, what lets us find our way in experience. I may be mangling him. But if I am roughly right, Peirce seems to tell me nothing I can use while James tells me too much. Are they answering the same question at all, or is Peirce defining what truth is while James is describing how we recognize it?

I would be grateful for a patient, clause-by-clause reading of the passage, saying which words are carrying argumentative weight and which are rhetoric, and ending with a verdict on whether my three worries are misreadings. Please do not give me a general account of pragmatism; the summaries I have found are exactly what left me stuck.
