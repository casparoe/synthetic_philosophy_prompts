I'm a reporter — science and ideas, which means I spend a lot of my life in lecture halls listening to philosophers argue and then trying to explain the argument to normal people in 900 words. This one's been bugging me for months and I've never gotten a strait answer out of anyone, so I'm outsourcing it.

It's about what they call aesthetic testimony. Roughly: can you come to genuinely believe that a painting is good — not just believe that everyone says it's good — because somebody told you so? The pessimists say no, not really: other people's verdicts can point you at what to look at, but the goodness itself has to come first-hand. The optimists say that's just fetishizing first-hand experience, testimony is testimony, and if the source is reliable then your belief is justified. And then there's a middle camp arguing about what a trusted critic's verdict can legitimately do for you before you've seen the thing.

What I want is for you to restate that whole debate in the vocabulary of my actual job. I'm fluent in newsroom terms — sourcing, attribution, corroboration, single-sourced claims, anonymous sources, on and off the record, the "according to X" construction, the "could not be independently verified" hedge, the difference between what I can assert in my own voice and what I can only report as somebody else's view, blurbs versus our own review, aggregation sites. Be honest about it: don't dress the philosophy up in press jargon and call that a translation. Where a claim has to change shape to fit, flag that it changed shape, right there in the sentence.

Then the part I actually care about — the residue report. What comes through cleanly, what comes through mangled, and what has no home in newsroom vocabulary at all. And for each thing that doesn't survive, tell me who to blame: is my trade missing a distinction that genuinely matters, or was the philosophical claim vaguer than it sounded?

Two things I want you to take seriously while you do this, because they're what make me suspicious of the pessimists:

1. We run second-hand aesthetic claims constantly and nobody blinks. Critics are calling it the best film of the year. A blurb on a book jacket. An obituary that says someone was a great novelist. A science writer relaying that the field now regards a theory as elegant. So either we're all just sloppy, or there's a real distinction between what we're doing and the thing they say you can't do.

2. A case I keep tripping on: a friend with excellent taste tells me I'll love a book I haven't opened, and she's right — I know I'll love it before I read a page. That looks like aesthetic knowledge from testimony. But is "I'll love it" even the same kind of claim as "it's good"? I can't tell whether that's a counterexample or a dodge.
