So I'm a novelist — not an academic, I just make things up for a living — but the narrator of my current book is a pretty committed utilitarian, and I've somehow agreed to lead a session at my neighborhood bookshop's reading group on consequentialist cluelessness. Twelve-ish people, mostly fiction readers, a couple retired teachers, nobody with actual philosophy training, forty minutes, a chalkboard, no slides, and I should probably expect at least one guy who's read one Peter Singer book and thinks that makes him an expert.

Don't explain the idea to me, please — I've read enough to have the shape of it and I definately don't need another summary. What I need is a plan for how I explain it to them. So: what has to come first, second, third, and why — what actually depends on what. I keep wanting to open with the "even a tiny effect on a huge number of people could swamp everything" bit, and I suspect that's the wrong door to come in through; tell me if it is and which door is right.

What's the one example that should carry the whole thing? I don't want the trolley, they'll all have heard it, and I'd rather not do the butterfly-effect coffee-cup thing either. I want the carrying example to be one where the trouble isn't just that the odds are unknown but that you genuinely can't say what the outcome even is — and tell me what that example has to have and what it must not have, or it'll teach the wrong lesson.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, and exactly where in my sequence I should head each one off. My guesses: (a) they'll hear it as just "risk and uncertainty," expected-value stuff; (b) they'll hear it as "so we can never know anything, so anything goes"; (c) they'll hear it as the butterfly effect / chaos — a physics point rather than a moral one. I'm sure there's a fourth I'm not seeing.

One thing I specifically want them to get, that I think even the smart popular write-ups skate over: the difference between the version where the far-flung consequences are really there and you're just too small and short-lived to trace them, and the version where there arguably isn't any fact of the matter about "the total consequences" at all, because the future is still open and is partly made by the very choice you're trying to evaluate. I honestly don't know if those are officially two different positions or if I'm inventing a distinction, and I don't care — I want the plan built so the second one lands as a real thought and doesn't immediately collapse into "well then nothing matters." If I'm drawing a distinction a philosopher would call confused, build that correction into the plan instead of letting me embarrass myself.

Also: what do I flat-out refuse to get into in forty minutes, and how do I say I'm refusing without looking like I'm dodging? And give me one question to ask at the very end that'll tell me whether the room actually got it, as opposed to just feeling pleasantly unsettled — something where the right answer and the "I caught the vibe but not the idea" answer are clearly different.

Write it in American English, I'm publishing this side of the Atlantic. And keep it concrete — an actual running order I can walk in with, not a list of themes.
