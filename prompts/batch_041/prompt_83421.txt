I want to stress-test a package of views about grounding that I have come to hold, and what I want from you is a map of its exposure rather than a defense of it. By grounding I mean the non-causal, in-virtue-of relation between facts on which one obtains because another does — the relation we invoke when we say that a thing's being crimson is what makes it red, or that facts about a whole obtain in virtue of facts about its parts. The package has five parts.

1. Grounding is one relation rather than several: it is irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, and necessitating, in the sense that if A grounds B then A's obtaining necessitates B's.
2. Grounding is explanatory: citing a ground is giving an explanation, and which grounding claims are true is a matter of fact rather than of what we find illuminating.
3. Grounding is well-founded: there are no infinite descending chains, so every grounded fact ultimately rests on facts that are not themselves grounded.
4. The ungrounded facts are contingent — they could have been otherwise — and nothing grounds the fact that they are fundamental; there is no meta-grounding of fundamentality and no self-grounding either.
5. Grounding claims are objective: whether A grounds B does not depend on our explanatory practices, our conceptual scheme, or our interests.

What would have to be true for this package to be false? Rank the defeaters by how much they should move me, and sort them into three kinds: objections showing the package is internally unstable, objections showing it is externally false or unmotivated, and objections showing it is empty or unfalsifiable. For each, say what would have to hold for the objection to land, and whether that condition is actually met or merely possible.

I expect the sharpest trouble to lie in the interactions rather than in the parts taken singly, so work those out explicitly. What is the status of the fact that a given fact is fundamental: if it is grounded, does that strain the asymmetry of part 1 or enlarge the base in a way part 4 forbids; if it is a further ungrounded fact, is there more ungrounded structure than part 4 admits? Does the necessitation clause in part 1 force grounding facts themselves to be necessary, and does that sit with a contingent base? Can well-foundedness be argued for rather than stipulated, and if the only argument is that something must explain anything at all, is that an argument or a preference? Does the explanatory character of grounding in part 2 sit with the objectivity in part 5, given that explanations are things we give? And does the package collapse into a rival — if grounding claims turn out to be a redescription of essence facts, or of metaphysical necessity, or a manner of speaking with no relation behind it, which rival is the real one, and what is lost in the collapse?

Then tell me which part is load-bearing: if I must surrender one to save the rest, which one, and what becomes of the package without it? Say what it commits me to that its rivals do not. Say which defeaters a committed defender of the package would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to which only critics press. Distinguish the fatal from the merely awkward, and include defeaters that come from cases the package cannot describe — cases of mutual dependence, say, or of grounding that fails to necessitate — as well as from consequences it cannot accept. Finally, be willing to conclude that the package is insulated: if parts 1 through 5 are simply what I mean by grounding, say whether anything could count against it, and whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or just the price of a stipulative notion. I would be most moved by a case in which the package forces me to say something I cannot bring myself to say about a particular object — a whole and its parts, a determinable and its determinates, a set and its members — so if such a case exists, put it first.
