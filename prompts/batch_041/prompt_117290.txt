I retired from the lab three years ago — molecular biology, protein folding, nothing that ever got me near a philosophy seminar — and since then I've been going to a weekly philosophy group at the village hall, which is how I've ended up at sixty-eight trying to get my head round Kant. There's a woman there, Margaret, who taught law for thirty years and now reads nothing but ethics, and she rules on cases in a way I find completly convincing on the Tuesday and quite baffling by Thursday. I've been keeping a list of her verdicts on the old examples we've worked through, partly out of lab-book habit. (1) Borrowing money on a promise you know you won't keep: wrong — her reason being that if everyone did it, promising would stop working. (2) Lying to the man at the door who wants to know where your friend is hiding so he can kill him: not wrong. (3) Living your whole life never helping anyone, while expecting help yourself when you're in trouble: wrong. (4) Letting your talents go to seed and spending your life on amusements: wrong. (5) Killing yourself to escape a painful illness: wrong. (6) Keeping a promise because it happens to profit you: allowed, though she says it earns you no credit. (7) Enjoying music, food and your friends: allowed. (8) Telling a friend her disastrous haircut suits her: allowed; she says she isn't a fanatic about the truth.

Now the case I can't settle, and I'd like your help before Tuesday. My oldest friend is dying and frightened, and last week he asked me to promise two things — that I'd take his dog, and that I'd fly out to Australia and tell his sister he was sorry — and I promised both, and I mean to do neither: the dog is going to a couple I know who'll look after him far better than I would, and I'm not going to Australia. I promised to stop him being frightened, and he'll never find out. My hunch is Margaret will call it wrong, but I've stopped trusting my hunches. What I actually want is this: your best guess at her verdict and the reasoning behind it; the rule, or rules, she's really running (I suspect there's more than one, and that they've simply never had to disagree yet — I spent forty years fitting models to data and I know what it looks like when two different curves fit the same points); which of those rules fit her list equally well, and what each of them would say about my promise; and then the case we should put to her next that would pull those rules apart, so I can find out which one she's actually got. I know Kant gives the test in several different forms and I've never been able to work out wheather they come to the same thing, so please be explicit about which form you think she's using and why. British English, please — I've been in the States for years and my spelling has gone to pot, but I still like to see it done properly.
