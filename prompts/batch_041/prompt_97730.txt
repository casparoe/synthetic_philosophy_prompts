I don't have any academic training in medicine or philosophy, but for the past several months I've been reading whatever I can find about how hospitals decide that a person has died, and I've gotten stuck on a question I'd like to work through properly. What I want from you is not a summary of the debate between "brain death" and "circulatory death," but a thought experiment built to my specifications and then analyzed carefully. The purpose of the exercise is to see whether these two ways of defining death genuinely disagree about when a person dies, or whether they only disagree about which test to run — so the case has to generate the disagreement on its own rather than have the disagreement stipulated into it.

Please construct a single scenario with the following features.

1. It should be realistic and contemporary. Use only technology and techniques that exist today; no cryonics, no imagined future medicine, no miraculous reversals.
2. It should concern one patient at one moment, and at that moment the neurological criterion for death and the circulatory criterion for death should point to opposite verdicts — one of them says the patient is dead, the other says the patient is alive.
3. Please avoid the two examples everyone reaches for: the body kept on a ventilator after the whole brain has been destroyed, and the patient in a vegetative state with a working brainstem. I want a case that turns on something else.
4. Keep organ donation and transplantation out of the story entirely, so that no one's interest in procuring organs can be doing any work in the verdict.
5. The scenario itself should be one paragraph.

Then analyze what you have built.

- State, for each of three views — the whole-brain view, the higher-brain view, and the circulatory view — whether the patient is dead or alive at the moment in question, and why.
- Identify the single feature of the case that makes the verdicts come apart, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed — does it collapse back into an ordinary, uncontroversial death or non-death?
- Say which view comes out worst, and make the strongest argument its defender could give that your case cheats, misapplies the criterion, or proves less than it appears to.
- Finally, address whether the answer depends on how one reads "irreversibility" in the circulatory criterion — as "cannot be restarted" versus "will not be restarted because we have decided to stop."

Please write in American English, and explain everything in your own words; I don't need citations or a reading list, since I have no way to check them.
