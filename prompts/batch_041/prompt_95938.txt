I'm teaching a one-off 90-minute session on Hacking's looping effects to second-year psychiatry residents. They know the DSM and ICD cold, they have zero philosophy of science, and I'd bet half of them think this is a fancy way of saying "diagnoses carry stigma." Don't explain looping effects to me, I've got that part. Plan the teaching. What order do the pieces have to go in so it lands as a claim about classifications and people reshaping each other rather than as a slogan — what depends on what, and what has to come before what — plus the one case that carries the whole thing, where the misconceptions surface, and how I'll know at the end whether it worked.

Two constraints on the plan.

First, the obvious carrying cases are the ones they've half-heard of already (fugue, multiple personality/DID, homosexuality, the Rosenhan study). Tell me which of those are actually bad pedagogically and why, then give me something better. I was thinking prolonged grief disorder, which is new-ish in both DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 and drew a lot of pushback about pathologizing grief, or else burnout, which sits in ICD-11 but deliberately not as a disorder. Say whether either of those works, which one carries more, and what the case has to have and lack for the loop to be visible rather than just asserted.

Second: what has to be in place before they can predict a loop instead of recognizing one in hindsight? I want to close by inventing a category live — something like "post-scroll dysphoria" or "algorithmic compulsion" — and have them map where the loops would go: who adopts it, who resists it, what happens to the people it lands on, what happens to the criteria themselves. For that to be more than a bullshit session, tell me what the earlier sequence must have established and give me the actual prompt I'd put on the board. And if that exercise is too easy to fake, give me a sharper second check.

Then the misconception map: the ones they'll walk in with and the ones the material will manufacture, in the order they'll show up, with the exact point in the sequence where I head each off. I'm expecting "this is labeling theory," "social construction means it isn't real," "it's just stigma," "patients are copying symptoms off TikTok," "so diagnosis is bad and we should stop diagnosing," and probably "doesn't this apply to everything, like left-handedness or obesity?" Finally, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and announce that I'm skipping — the realism/construction fight, Hacking's later machinery for how a kind gets made, the detailed history of any single category — and why skipping those is the right call rather than cowardice.

No reading list, they won't read it anyway.
