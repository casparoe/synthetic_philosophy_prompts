The taste-bud analogy that accompanies moral foundations theory has started doing a lot of work in a seminar I'm in: moral intuitions are like the basic taste receptors everyone is born with, and cultures are like cuisines built on top of them. One camp uses it to argue that we should stop treating either side's moral claims as true or false -- morality is a matter of taste, and the psychology of left and right tells us about palates, not about who is right. Another camp uses the very same picture to argue the reverse: because the receptors are shared, there is a universal moral palate against which any culture's cooking can be judged. I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a summary of the theory. Set out the correspondences explicitly -- what plays the role of the receptors, the underlying physiology, the cuisine, the cook, the diner, the food -- then say where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for these two uses and the ones that are merely picturesque. I am especially interested in what the taste side of the analogy quietly imports into morality: its passivity, its lack of truth-aptness, its lack of any claim to authority over others, and the fact that nutrition gives taste a standard it has to answer to. Tell me whether either conclusion survives once those features are on the table, and if a better analogy is available, give it to me. Be committed: I want your verdict on which use, if either, the analogy can actually support, and what would have to be true for it to hold, not a survey of the options.
