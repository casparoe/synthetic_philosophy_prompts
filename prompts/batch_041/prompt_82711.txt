I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar in political theory — eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, a room with movable chairs and nothing more technological than a whiteboard. The topic is epistocracy versus democracy, and the same thing happens every year: the epistemic case for majority rule strikes half the class as a harmless curiosity and the other half as a proof, and nobody can say what would have to be true for it to fail.

Design a session in which they find out for themselves. What I want them to run into, in sequence and without being told the point in advance: that aggregating many independently mistaken judgments can be startlingly reliable; that this reliability rests on an assumption real electorates violate, namely that errors are not shared; that the obvious repair — giving extra weight to the demonstrably better informed — founders because "demonstrably" is a sample, so selecting the competent also selects for luck, and the selected will regress; and that deliberating before voting, which genuinely improves the average participant's judgment, may at the same time manufacture the very correlation that ruins the aggregation. They should not hear the theorem's name, or the name of the position built on it, until the debrief, and they should not be told in advance that a shared bias has been planted.

Give me a scenario with roles, a materials list, a run-of-show with timings, a scoring scheme that needs no money, and a debrief that maps each stage onto the corresponding claim: the independence assumption, the competence threshold, the difficulty of measuring competence without presupposing it, the difference between defending democracy epistemically and defending it procedurally, and the objection that disenfranchisement destroys the competence it presupposes. Tell me which stage will predictably collapse the first time I run it and how to handle that when it does. Be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce — above all that it requires answers I can mark right or wrong, which is precisely what the real dispute is about.
