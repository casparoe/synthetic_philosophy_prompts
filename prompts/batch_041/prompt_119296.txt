For eleven years I kept the books at a rural savings cooperative, and I have come to believe that the way outsiders measure corruption misreads the very societies they claim to diagnose. Next month I am presenting this to the board of a donor-funded governance programme: two economists whose reputations rest on cross-country corruption indices, a procurement-reform consultant who has spent his career on bid rigging, and two local staff from the region who tell me privately that they agree with me. My case rests on a distinction I have worked out on my own. A toll is paid to a person whose office everyone knows, at a rate everyone knows, and it buys nothing except passage; a bribe is paid to change an outcome the payer had no standing to change. Only the second is corruption. Policing the first — the standing obligations of kinship and neighbourhood, what gets called clientelism, the gift that is expected and returned over years — does not touch the second, because concealment is precisely what a toll never required, and driving the network underground makes it less accountable rather than more. Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this board will actually raise, in their vocabulary and roughly in the order they will come, ranked by how much damage each would do, each with the two-minute reply I should give and an honest note on where that reply is thin. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Continuous prose, please: no headings, no numbered lists, no tables.
