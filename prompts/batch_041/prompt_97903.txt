For the past semester a small reading group I'm in — three of us, plus a visiting economist who joins when he can — has been working through the standard treatment of games with incomplete information, and we keep getting stuck at the same spot: the common prior assumption. The disagreement has hardened into two positions. One of us, whom I'll call the deflationist, insists that the assumption is a mere representation choice and carries no substantive content. Her argument is a construction. Take any situation in which two agents hold genuinely different priors over some payoff-relevant state, and enlarge the state space by adding a hidden variable that is realized before either agent learns anything — a variable that fixes which of the two priors the agent was born with. Condition each agent's belief on that variable, put some suitable distribution over it, and you now have a single prior that both agents share over the enlarged space, with the original disagreement recovered as ordinary conditioning on private information. Since we can always perform this re-description, she says, the common prior is not an empirical or a normative constraint at all, and the two camps are talking past each other. The other of us, the substantivist, says this is a sleight of hand that fails to preserve what we were arguing about. Either the agents, once the hidden variable is added, no longer disagree about the state they care about — in which case we have changed the subject — or the disagreement simply reappears one level up, as a disagreement about the distribution of the hidden variable, which is exactly the heterogeneity we were told had been eliminated. On his view the assumption does real work: it encodes the claim that agents cannot differ in belief except by differing in information, and it is what underwrites the no-speculation results and the result that agents who share a prior and know one another's posterior beliefs cannot agree to disagree. I find I can argue both sides depending on the day, which is why I am asking.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this impasse verbal — the two of us attaching different meanings to the word prior, or measuring the assumption against different questions, one about how to represent a fixed informational situation and the other about what rationality permits? Is it substantive, with one side simply wrong about whether the enlargement construction preserves the disagreement, or with the two sides disagreeing about what a model is for? Or are they, as my working hypothesis has it, answering two different questions under one name: the deflationist asking whether every heterogeneous-prior model can be embedded in a common-prior model, and the substantivist asking whether the resulting model says anything true about rational agents — so that the exchange can never settle because the questions, though both phrased as being about the common prior, have different objects? Please argue the diagnosis from what each side actually does with the assumption, rather than surveying the literature or listing who said what, and treat my hypothesis as a hypothesis that may be wrong. Then give me an honest answer to the further question: given that diagnosis, what — if anything — would count as progress here? Would it be a formal result specifying exactly which properties of a type space survive the enlargement and which do not, a sharper distinction between the assumption as a constraint on a model and as a claim about the world, a decision about which of the two questions we actually care about, or nothing at all — an impasse that is a sign the question was ill-posed from the start? Tell me which, and why.
