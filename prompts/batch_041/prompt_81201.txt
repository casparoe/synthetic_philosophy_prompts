I am convening a reading group in a community hall on Marx's critique of political economy, and the next session is on technology and automation. The participants are intelligent non-specialists—two software engineers, a trade union organiser, a retired teacher, and someone who has read only one short political pamphlet—and I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and no expectation that anyone will have done the reading. I want the session to turn on a puzzle that I have not seen handled well in introductory accounts. Marx treats machinery as a means of producing relative surplus value, yet he also holds that value is created only by living labour; so a fully automated capitalist economy would be a contradiction, even though capital is driven to automate. At the same time, much automation does not replace workers but measures, paces, and disciplines them—the algorithmic warehouse is the obvious case—which suggests that the labour-saving story and the labour-disciplining story are not alternatives but two sides of the same movement. How do I teach this without either reducing it to the Luddite fallacy or turning the session into a seminar on the labour theory of value?

Plan the explanatory sequence for me. What has to be established first—perhaps the difference between the physical amount of machinery per worker and the value that machinery represents relative to wages, or between machinery as a productive force and machinery as a social relation—what depends on what, and where the pivot from one idea to the next must occur. I want one carrying example that can do the work throughout, concrete enough to be pictured but not so familiar that it answers itself; tell me what it must include and what it must leave out, and whether the algorithmic warehouse can carry it or whether it will make the session about surveillance rather than value. Then map the misconceptions the group will bring or form: that Marx is simply anti-technology, that automation necessarily immiserates workers, that the creation of a surplus population is a moral claim rather than a mechanism, that if machines did all the work value would simply come from them, and that the tendency of profit rates to fall is a prediction of doom rather than a counteracted tendency. Tell me at which point in the sequence each misconception should be headed off and what I should say there. Finally, give me a check for understanding that would show whether they have grasped the paradox rather than merely memorised the vocabulary, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level—say, the finer points of how surplus value is redistributed between branches of production—and how to justify that refusal without making the session feel incomplete.
