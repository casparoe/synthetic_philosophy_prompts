I spent thirty years as an insurance assessor, which is a trade built almost entirely on deciding what one may reasonably believe when the evidence is incomplete, and for the past two years I have run a monthly discussion group for adults at our public library. Next month the session falls to me, on the two pieces I have been living with for the better part of a year: William James's argument that there are questions on which a person may rightly believe ahead of the evidence, and W. K. Clifford's contention that it is wrong in every case to believe anything on insufficient evidence. I have read both many times and I still cannot say whether the two men are disagreeing with one another or simply describing different situations. Please do not explain the debate to me. I want you to plan how I should present it.

The group is fifteen adults, none of them philosophers: a retired physician who trusts nothing he cannot measure, two or three who take their religion seriously, a young woman training as a nurse, and several who come because it is warm and there is tea. Most will not have read the essays; I will be summarising aloud. I have a whiteboard, fifty minutes, and no handouts, and I would like to know roughly how the time should be divided. I do not need a reading list; there is no budget for one and I would not use it.

What I need from you is this.

First, the order of the pieces and what depends on what: which point must already be in place before the next can be said without sounding absurd or wicked, and at which moment the two essays genuinely touch and where they pass each other by. I am tempted to hang the whole evening on whether the belief in question is doing any work in the world — Clifford's shipowner could have checked, whereas James's cases are ones in which there is nothing to check in advance, or in which demanding proof would destroy the very thing being proved. Tell me whether that is a sound spine or whether it breaks when pressed.

Second, the one case that carries the whole session. I have two candidates and trust neither. The first is Clifford's shipowner, who talks himself out of his doubts about an old ship and sends it to sea with passengers aboard. The second is the kind of case James seems to have in mind, where the belief is not a prediction but part of what brings the thing about — someone deciding to believe in a friend who has not yet earned it, or a patient deciding to believe in her own recovery. My worry about the first is that everyone I have tried it on hears a story about cutting corners on safety and about the people who drowned, so that the believing is lost behind the harm; the case seems to want a version in which nothing goes wrong, and I am not sure that version survives. My worry about the second is that it slides into the wishful thinking Clifford was warning against. Tell me which single case should carry the evening, what it must have and must lack in order to do that, and what I should keep the other one for.

Third, the misconceptions and exactly where in the sequence to head off each. Those I have already watched form: that James is licensing belief in whatever is comforting; that Clifford is a cold man with no room in his philosophy for trust; that the disagreement is simply science against religion; and that everything turns on whether one can choose one's beliefs, which as far as I can tell neither of them ever explains how to do. Add the ones I have not thought of.

Fourth, the knot I want them left holding. Both men write as though believing were something one does and can be praised or blamed for, and in all my years I have not once managed to believe something by deciding to. If that is right, then either James's permission is empty or Clifford's prohibition is. I would rather the last ten minutes leave the group wrestling with that than let them settle into a comfortable slogan on either side, and I want to know how to get them there.

Fifth, the question I should put to the room at the end that would tell me whether they have grasped the shape of the disagreement rather than merely collected the slogans; and, finally, what I should openly refuse to go into at this level, and how to decline it without leaving them feeling cheated.
