My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Avicenna's flying man — the man created in midair, limbs spread, touching nothing, eyes covered, who is supposed to affirm his own existence while being able to affirm nothing whatever about his body — and I would rather close the argument with a poem than with another round of prose. Write it in the flying man's voice, in five or six short stanzas, with a refrain that is nothing but the bare affirmation I am. Each stanza should try to complete that line — with a hand, a place, a warmth, a memory of having been born — and each attempt should fail, so the line keeps collapsing back to the uncompleted I am. Let the form do the argument's work: what the case is meant to establish is that the self's grasp of itself precedes and survives the loss of every bodily predicate, so the poem must never let the I settle into being a particular thing; and it must not drift into the claim the argument does not make, that the soul is immortal, since at most it shows the soul is not a body. Then give me one final stanza in my friend's voice, carrying the objection he would not drop: that the whole experiment only works because the man is already a speaker who knows the word for himself, so that the I am may be something the experiment smuggled in rather than something it discovered. I don't want the poem to settle that either — leave the flying man's bare I am and my friend's suspicion standing beside each other, so that a reader who knows the argument recognizes both its force and the place where it can be doubted.
