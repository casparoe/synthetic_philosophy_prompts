ok so i need a short story, 1200-1800 words, that actually does something with illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. You know the line: we're genuinely conscious in every functional sense -- awake, responsive, discriminating, reporting, acting on how things seem -- but there are no phenomenal properties, no inner glow, no what-it's-likeness over and above the functional facts. The "seeming" is itself a real representational state; it just misrepresents itself as more than that. Which is why the interesting question shifts from "how does the brain produce experience" to "why are we so sure there's anything left over to explain."

Do not have a character explain any of that. No lecture, no seminar, no philosopher in the cast, no epigraph, and dont lean on any names or texts -- i want this to stand on its own. Also no one should say the word "qualia."

The setup, which is the part i actually care about. There's an experimental treatment for chronic pain, call it shading or whatever you like, and what it does is leave the nociception and the reflexes completely untouched. Patients still flinch, still yank a hand back, still startle, still rate the sensation the same way they always did -- "sharp, hot, deep, about an eight" -- but they insist none of it is bad anymore, and they can't tell you whats different, they get frustrated trying, they say things like "it's the same, it just doesnt... you know." And they stop protecting themselves. Someone keeps a hand under the broiler a beat too long because theres no longer any reason not to. Their whole relationship to their own body gets weird and nobody can say in what way.

Tell it first person, past tense, from the partner of someone who got the treatment. Give me the consult room and the consent form, a follow-up appointment, and one night at home. The tension i want is that the partner cannot get behind the patients flat "its fine" -- not because they think hes lying, but because the report itself seems to be doing something, and they cant tell what. And the patient can clearly tell that something is different, can point right at the difference, which means he's still introspecting something, the absence has a shape. Dont have anyone notice that, just let it sit there in the scene.

End it unresolved. Dont let the story settle whether hes still suffering, and dont let it settle whether the treatment worked. No last paragraph that tells me what to think.

Last thing: it should be accurate enough that someone who works on consciousness would recognize the position being staged -- in particular that this view isnt denying experience, its denying a property, and that a person who's had the hurt stripped out isnt numb or indifferent, theyre something stranger. But someone who's never heard of the hard problem should still just get a good, unsettling story about a couple. Casual register in the prose, not clinical.
