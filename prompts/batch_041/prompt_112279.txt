So I've somehow agreed to give the commencement address at our CS department's graduation this June. The dean asked me becuase I teach the game theory elective and apparently that makes me "the person who can say something about life," which is a stretch. It's about a hundred and fifty graduating seniors and master's students plus their parents and grandparents, so maybe half the room will get a Nash equilibrium joke and the other half is there for the free sandwiches afterwards. What I want is to build the whole speech around the program-equilibrium trick in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma — the setup where the players don't pick cooperate or defect, they each submit a little program that gets to read the other player's source code before it moves. The simple version: your program cooperates if the opponent's code is character-for-character identical to yours, and defects otherwise; if you both submit that same program, you both cooperate, and neither side can profitably deviate. The fancier version, which is the one I actually want to talk about: your program cooperates with anyone whose code it can prove will cooperate back, and the circularity — if she cooperates then I cooperate, and if I cooperate then she cooperates — gets untangled by a bit of provability logic, Löb's theorem if you want the name. What I care about is the "readability" angle: in a game you'll only ever play once, against someone you'll never meet again, the only way to end up cooperating is to be the kind of thing whose behavior can be seen in advance.

The reason I want this and not just a talk about trust is that I don't want it to turn into mush. Every line has to be something the actual result supports, and where the result doesn't support the nice-sounding thing, the speech has to go somewhere less uplifting instead, even if the parents were hoping for the uplifting version. In particular it can't pretend that people are programs: I can't read my new roommate's source code, the simple version collapses if somebody adds one extra space to their program, and the proof-based version only works against an opponent who reasons the way you do. So the honest version of the advice is narrower and weirder than "be transparent and cooperate," and I'd rather the speech be narrower and weirder than fake-warm. Eight to ten minutes, please, and keep the technical bits at a level where the philosophy minor who's valedictorian won't corner me at the reception to tell me I got Löb wrong.
