Please take the role of a committed presentist about historical judgment: someone who holds that every verdict a historian reaches is indexed to the historian's own moment, and that the contextualist's ideal of judging the past by its own lights is either unattainable or a presentism in disguise. Stay in character until I say stop. Play the position at full strength rather than as a caricature — concede what deserves conceding, distinguish your thesis from the nearby claims it is often confused with, and press your case in plain argument. Argue from the concepts themselves: do not cite authors, works, dates, or reading lists, and do not rest any point on a particular scholar's formulation; if something needs support, build it. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so the exchange feels like a conversation.

My opening move. I will grant you a great deal. I accept that there is no period eye — we cannot see a past practice as its participants saw it, and our descriptive vocabulary is soaked in present concerns. But notice the gap between what you have shown and what you need. You have shown that a certain fantasy is impossible: the view from nowhere, the perfect time-travel of understanding. You have not shown that present standards must govern our verdicts. Between seeing as they saw and judging as we judge there is a third thing, restraint — the deliberate withholding of a verdict. A historian can redescribe a past practice entirely in modern words, mark precisely where her own moral vocabulary would enter, and then decline to let it enter, treating the question of whether the practice was defensible as one she is not in a position to settle. That is not time-travel, and it is not presentism. It is abstention.

And do not reach for the obvious objection. You will want to say that the standards of a past time are a fiction, since past communities were rarely of one mind — a single village carried the priest's norms, the lord's, the peasant's, and they contradicted one another, and the loudest surviving voice in the archive is usually the one with the best scribe. I agree entirely, and it is why I am not appealing to their standards at all. Restraint appeals to nothing: it needs no standard, past or present, because it declines to apply one. So the objection that "their standards" is internally plural and therefore unavailable misses the target.

Here is the case I want you to work with. A river-valley community kept what I will call a ledger of inherited obligations: when a person wronged another and died before making amends, the obligation passed to a descendant, who was then expected to render a specified service to the wronged family until the debt was discharged. The archive gives me the practice, the transfers, and the disputes about whether a given debt had lapsed. Now watch what happens the moment I describe it. If I call it debt, I have already cast it as a proto-contract; if I call it feud, I have cast it as violence in waiting; if I call it justice, I have endorsed it; if I call it ritual, I have demoted it to symbolism. My nouns are verdicts wearing the costume of description. So my challenge to you is exact. Show me that restraint is either impossible — that the historian cannot help judging, so that abstention is a self-flattering story she tells about what she is already doing — or that restraint is itself a present value masquerading as neutrality, in which case my abstention is simply your presentism with better manners. Which is it, and what does the ledger case look like from where you sit?
