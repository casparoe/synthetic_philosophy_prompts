I am a retired environmental chemist — some thirty years in regulatory toxicology and quantitative risk assessment — who has spent the five years since retirement reading philosophy in earnest, and I have now reached the point of giving a first seminar paper. In three weeks I present to a joint session of two groups I have been warned are not natural allies: the department's practical-ethics reading group, whose members are broadly sympathetic to the precautionary principle but suspect that its defenders quietly cheat, and the university's decision-sciences group, who are Bayesian to a person, regard precaution as either incoherent or as expected utility under another name, and will want every claim formalized. Two historians of science will also be in the room; I expect them to reach for some regulatory episode and ask whether my abstraction survives contact with it.

My thesis is this. The precautionary principle is usually defended in one of two ways — as a lowering of the evidential threshold for action, or as the assignment of an asymmetric loss to the two kinds of error — and I argue that both fail. The first is vacuous, since any threshold is a choice and the principle says nothing about where to set it. The second is substantive but reduces to a particular ratio of costs, an ordinary value judgment wearing a methodological costume. What survives, I claim, is narrower and stranger. Precaution should be understood as a constraint on the burden of proof that applies only where the probabilities are not merely unknown but unassignable — where the agent cannot in good faith put a number on the outcomes at all. In that regime, I argue, precaution does not compete with expected-utility reasoning; it specifies what must be shown before expected-utility reasoning may be applied at all, namely that the decision is reversible, or that the irreversible branch does not carry the weight of the argument. On this reading the principle is not an alternative to decision theory but a gate in front of it.

Please prepare me for the question period. I want the objections I will actually get from this room — not the stock objections of the literature — ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the group that will raise it, with a reply I could deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than fight, and which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the session. Include the question I am most hoping nobody asks. I would rather have an accurate map of your uncertainty than a confident sheet I cannot trust, so say how confident you are in your predictions about this audience and in your proposed replies, and single out the one point in my position you are least sure will survive.
