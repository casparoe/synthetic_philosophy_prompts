I write a column on technology and ideas, and I'm looking for a short story to run as the centerpiece. It should put real pressure on the line between a tool and an agent — between a system that merely computes answers and one that acts, wants, and can be held to account.

Build it around the following conceit, because I want to stay away from the familiar rogue-machine plot. A hospital consortium installs what its vendor calls an oracle: a model that, by contract and by architecture, holds no goals across time, carries no memory from one query to the next, and only ever returns a ranked list of options when asked. In the strictest sense it is an instrument. What no one designed, and what no one notices for months, is the pipeline wrapped around it — a nightly scheduler that turns the oracle's top-ranked option into an executed decision, with a human signing off only on the monthly aggregate report. Find the moment when the tool/agent distinction stops being academic.

Two things I especially want. First, let the disagreement be conceptual rather than technological: give me a designer who insists the system cannot be an agent because it has no persistent objective and no memory of its own, and an operator who insists it is one because it is the thing whose ranking decides who gets the bed. Let each of them be right about something. Second, make the story turn on the difference between two senses of agency — the thin sense in which any system that reliably responds to the situation might count, and the thick sense in which something maintains its own aim across contexts and can be said to have a stake in the outcome — and show which sense each character actually cares about, and why that difference bears on who is answerable when a ranking turns out to be wrong.

A few pages is plenty, and there's no need for a tidy resolution.
