I'm taking a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and we have spent the last three weeks on the units-of-selection debate — whether selection is best understood as acting on genes, on organisms, or on groups — and I have reached the point where I can follow each week's argument closely and still not say what any of them is supposed to establish. Before I read further, I would like the specification. What, exactly, must a theory of the unit of selection deliver in order to count as adequate? I do not want a survey of who holds what; I want the list of requirements, each one paired with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference of one camp. My hope is that a sufficiently good list will do the adjudicating for me, because I keep finding that I can only tell whether an argument succeeds once I already know what it was meant to explain.

So, please: the cases the theory has to get right, with its verdict owed on each — a sterile worker whose own fitness is nil while its colony thrives; a meiotic driver that spreads through a population while making its bearers worse off; a gene that proliferates by damaging its host, whether that is a transposable element or the conflict between maternally and paternally imprinted loci; a trait that looks like restraint in the use of a shared resource, where the benefit lands on neighbours; and the major transitions, in which what was a population of individuals becomes a single individual — I take it a theory that cannot say what changes when that happens is missing something important. Then the distinctions it must not collapse: being selected of versus being selected for; selection versus mere sorting or drift at the group level; the unit of selection versus the unit of adaptation versus the unit of evolutionary individuality; and altruism versus mutualism, including the behaviours whose classification flips depending on whether fitness is measured in absolute terms or relative to one's groupmates. Then the results it cannot contradict: that there is an exact bookkeeping identity — the Price equation — into which any within-group and between-group contributions can be decomposed no matter what is causally going on; that the standard relatedness-weighted condition for the evolution of altruism, the rb > c inequality, can be derived in both the individual-level and the group-level accounting; and that in a great many models the two accountings are formally equivalent, so that any claim that they predict differently needs to be stated with some care. For each requirement, mark whether it is a genuine constraint or a commitment of one camp, and flag any pair that I should not expect to be jointly satisfied — my own suspicion is that a theory cannot both identify the unit of selection in the singular and accommodate the transitions in individuality in which that unit changes, and likewise that it cannot insist kin selection and group selection are distinct mechanisms while also holding that they never disagree about anything. Tell me whether I am right about those, and whether there are other pairs of the same kind.

To fix the purpose: I want a specification strong enough to settle, for any given trait, what the unit of selection is and why, and to arbitrate the argument two of my classmates keep having — one says the whole debate is bookkeeping, since any group-selection claim can be rewritten gene by gene and therefore adds nothing, while the other says the gene's-eye view is empirically empty because it can redescribe any outcome after the fact. If one requirement does most of the work of excluding the popular theories, say which, but I do not need a full scorecard; the criteria are what I am after. A page and a half or two, in whatever order makes the dependencies clear — where one requirement only makes sense once another is in place, I would rather see them ordered that way than grouped by topic.
