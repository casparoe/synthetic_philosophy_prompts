Until three years ago I was a member of a small, high-control religious community, and since leaving it the thing I have found hardest to think clearly about is the structure of the reasons I was given while I was inside. I was not simply told what to believe. I was given reasons to hold attitudes: to revere the founder, to feel gratitude for the discipline I was under, to be ashamed of my doubts, to want forgiveness. Almost all of those reasons took the form of consequences — what would happen to me, or to people I loved, if I failed to feel the right way — and the community had a phrase for the desired result, which it called heart obedience. I have spent a long time assuming that those reasons were simply bad reasons, or threats dressed up as reasons. I am no longer sure that is the right diagnosis, and I would like you to write an essay that reaches a verdict rather than surveying the options.

The philosophical background, as I understand it, is a family of puzzles about reasons for attitudes that seem to be the wrong kind of reason for the attitude in question: considerations that give one reason to have the attitude without speaking in favour of what the attitude is about. The standard example is a threat that makes it prudent to admire someone; the admiration is then something one has reason to arrange, but the threat says nothing about whether the person is admirable. What I want from you is an essay that weighs the main ways philosophers have tried to handle this — the proposal that the distinction we need is between reasons that bear on the object of the attitude and reasons that bear on the state of having it; the proposal that the puzzle shows something is wrong with analysing value in terms of fitting attitudes in the first place; and the deflationary proposal that there is no interesting distinction here, only reasons of different weights — and then commits to one. I am less interested in which view has the most adherents than in which one survives the cases.

Please test the candidates against two cases. The first is mine, or something close to it: a meeting at which members were required to express remorse for a doubt they had voiced, on pain of being cut off from everyone they loved, and at which the remorse, once expressed, was treated as genuine and as evidence that the person had been restored. The second is a case I would like you to construct yourself, in which a reason to hold an attitude is neither a threat nor a bribe but arises from a role or an institution — a court that requires contrition, say, or a workplace that requires enthusiasm — so that the pressure is constitutive of the practice rather than imposed on it from outside.

I also want you to assess a proposal of my own, which may well be confused. It seems to me that the mark of the wrong kind of reason might not be whether it concerns the object or the state at all, but whether it could be offered to the person as a consideration in a shared deliberation, addressed to them as a participant rather than applied to them as a mechanism. If that is right, then a great many reasons that look state-given would come out as the right kind, and some object-given ones would not. Tell me whether this proposal can be made to work, and if it cannot, what it is getting wrong.

Close by saying what your verdict implies for how I should understand my own past attitudes: whether the reverence and gratitude I genuinely had were ever responsive to reasons, whether it matters for how I should regard them now, and whether anything follows for how I ought to treat people still inside such communities. Write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way one would write a long letter. Please use British English throughout.
