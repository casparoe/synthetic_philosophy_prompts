I'm teaching a session of a "philosophy and science fiction" seminar next month and I've paired Orson Scott Card's Ender's Game with the dark forest hypothesis -- the argument from Liu Cixin's novel about why nobody in the galaxy is talking to anybody. What I need is a proper reading of Ender's Game through that argument, not a plot summary of either one, and not a survey that lists the options and shrugs.

Take the role of someone who actually works on this material -- decision theory under deep uncertainty, astrobiology, the ethics of preemption -- and think carefully before you write. Sketch the argument's premises to yourself first, then do the reading. Don't give me a reading list or citations, I don't need them, and don't quote the novel at me, I've read it.

The reconstruction I want you to test the book against runs roughly: (1) resources are finite and any expanding civilization eventually collides with another; (2) detection is lopsided -- you can be seen long before you can see; (3) no credible commitment holds across light-years and deep time, so cooperation can't be secured by agreement; (4) therefore, whenever a civilization can be located, a first strike is the dominant move. Two things I think the usual statement of the argument buries, and I want them in play. First, (4) only goes through if the strike is cheap and near-certain to succeed, which means the argument is always being run from the strong party's chair; run it from the weak party's chair and the conclusion flips to concealment rather than preemption. Second, it quietly assumes every party can model every other party as an agent with roughly comparable cognition.

So: does the novel vindicate this, refute it, or complicate it? Ender is a child who believes the battles are simulations until they aren't, and by then he's destroyed a species. Is the humans' strike the thing that manufactures the hostile universe, so that the dark forest turns out to be a self-fulfilling prophecy rather than a fact about the cosmos? Does the mismatch between a hive species and an individual-mind species break premise (2) or (3) rather than (4)? And what does the ending -- the last queen in her cocoon, Ender agreeing to speak for her -- add to the argument that the argument alone doesn't give us? Tell me which scenes I should put on the board for the class, and where the reading strains and you have to say so.

Be committed. Take a position and give your reasons, rather than handing me the interpretive options and letting me pick. Then finish with a last line of the form:

Verdict: <vindicates | refutes | complicates> -- <one-sentence summary of your reading>
