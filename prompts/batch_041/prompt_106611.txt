I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether approval-directed agents are intrinsically safer than goal-directed agents, and we kept failing to construct a case that settled the question. Our examples either made the evaluator corrupt or foolish, or made the goal-directed agent a straw man; whenever we cleaned those up, the two agents seemed to collapse into each other. I would like you to construct the thought experiment we could not. Specifications: there are two agents with the same information, the same world model, the same action space, and the same final objective. One is goal-directed: it chooses the action that maximizes expected achievement of that objective. The other is approval-directed: it chooses the action it expects the designated human evaluator to approve of most. The evaluator is stipulated to be honest, competent, and to share the same final objective; there is no deception, no reward hacking, no corruption, no superintelligence, and no exotic technology. The evaluator is not omniscient or perfectly Bayesian, but neither is the agent, and the evaluator is not ignorant of the relevant facts; approval is a human attitude toward an action, so it need not coincide with expected objective achievement. The case must be realistic—something that could arise in a hospital, a research lab, a small company, or a public agency—and the scenario itself should be no longer than a paragraph. The point of the case is to test whether approval-directedness can be less safe than goal-directedness in the opposite direction from the usual story: I want the approval-directed agent to act more recklessly than the goal-directed agent. The divergence must be generated by the structure of approval rather than stipulated: it should not come from the evaluator having different final values, being misinformed about the facts, or being irrational in a way that no competent human would be. If you conclude that the specifications are jointly unsatisfiable, say so and explain exactly which pair of constraints cannot be met, but only after trying seriously to satisfy them.

After the scenario, do the following. State, for each agent, the action it takes and the reasoning that leads there. Identify the single feature of the case that makes the two decision rules come apart, and say whether that feature is essential or whether it can be varied without changing the verdicts. Run the removal test: if the evaluator's approval were perfectly calibrated to expected objective achievement, or if the evaluator were replaced by an oracle who approves exactly in proportion to expected value, does the divergence collapse? Explain why or why not. Then give the strongest reply available to a defender of approval-directed agency—someone who thinks the case shows not that approval-directedness is unsafe but that the case has smuggled in an unrealistic assumption about how approval works—and say whether that reply succeeds. Finally, tell me what the case is for: I want to use it to argue that the safety of approval-directed agents depends on contingent features of the evaluator and the approval signal, not on approval-directedness as such, so the case has to earn that conclusion rather than assume it. Make the analysis explicit enough that I can check each step, and do not rely on citations or references; I care about the structure of the argument.
