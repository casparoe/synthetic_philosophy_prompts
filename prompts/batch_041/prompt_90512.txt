I am a retired experimental geophysicist who has spent the last several years reading history and philosophy of science, and I now sit on the prize committee of a small foundation that awards a biennial prize for a book-length history of science written for non-specialists. In my own field I have seen how a result that now looks inevitable was, at the time, one option among several, supported by ambiguous data. We are down to one finalist, and I must circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

The manuscript is a narrative history of a nineteenth-century controversy over the cause of a disease. It is beautifully written and has a clear arc: a lone investigator assembles decisive evidence, the medical establishment resists for decades out of professional self-interest, and the truth eventually wins. My colleague on the committee calls it a model of accessible history and wants to award the prize. I am uneasy. The manuscript repeatedly says the evidence was there all along and that the opponents' objections were plainly unreasonable. But when I try to imagine the room in 1850, with the instruments, base rates, and background theories then available, I am not so sure. The author has added a methodological appendix arguing that his narrative is not hindsight-biased because he attributes error only where a reasonable contemporary would have seen through it. He calls this the reasonable contemporary test. I think the test may be doing exactly the work it is supposed to prevent: it lets the author smuggle in the conclusions we now accept and then distribute them backwards as obvious. My colleague's reply is that some retrospective judgment is unavoidable, and that without a narrative the book would be unreadable and would fail to explain anything.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate. Keep it under 1,000 words. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- award the prize, withhold it, or award it with a public statement about the book's limitations -- and give the reasoning the committee can act on. It should engage the strongest version of my colleague's position, not just dismiss it. It should also reach a verdict on the broader question: can a historical narrative avoid hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy without collapsing into an unreadable chronicle of contingencies? I want a decision, not a survey. I do not need citations or a reading list; I care about the argument. At the end, say how confident you are in your central claims and identify the point you are least sure of.
