I'm in a moral psychology reading group and we keep going in circles on the same fight, so I'd like to outsource a careful version of it to you. Pretend your a philosopher of history with one foot in social psychology — someone who knows the Holocaust historiography and the situationism literature both, and isn't impressed by either side's slogans. I want you to take one real episode and run the whole debate through it: the Józefów action of July 1942, when Reserve Police Battalion 101 — a few hundred middle-aged reservists from Hamburg, working-class, mostly not Nazi Party members — rounded up the Jews of a small Polish town and, on their commander's orders, shot something like fifteen hundred of them, women and children included, in the woods outside town. Beforehand the commander, Trapp, reportedly in tears, told the men that anyone who didn't feel able to carry it out could step forward and be excused; about a dozen did, out of the whole battalion, and they weren't punished. That's the crux for me. So map the competing explanations onto that scene: Browning's ordinary-men reading, where the killing is driven by authority, drill, peer pressure and the slow escalation of a killing career; Goldhagen's eliminationist-antisemitism reading, where the willingness was specifically German and ideological from the start; Arendt's banality-of-evil picture; and the Milgram-style obedience story Browning himself leans on. For each, say exactly who is supposed to be the player and what is supposed to be the mechanism, then be honest about what the Józefów facts can and can't discriminate between — because it looks to me like both the situationalists and the culturalists can swallow the dozen men who stepped out, and I want to know whether that's right.

Then push on the theory from the other side. There's a philosophical version of the situationist challenge — Harman, Doris and co. — that says the social-psych evidence shows there are no robust global character traits, only local situation-bound dispositions, and that virtue ethics is in trouble. Ask whether Józefów is a good test case for that thesis or a bad one. Getting out was cheap and yet the battalion kept at this for another year and a half, through deportations and the manhunts after Jews who'd fled; some men complied while insisting they were sickened by it; some who got out of one action were back in the ranks for the next. What does all that do to the no-character claim — and does the local-traits repair, where traits are real but narrow and keyed to specific eliciting conditions, actually rescue the virtue ethicist or just relabel the problem? While you're at it, flag how much of the situationist case is really riding on Milgram and Zimbardo, and whether the recent archival work showing Zimbardo's guards were coached should change how much weight this episode can carry. Start by brainstorming the possible mechanisms before you commit to a reading — I'd rather watch you rule options out than pick one and force the facts to fit it. End by telling me what the episode shows about the theory: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case neither side ever anticipated.
