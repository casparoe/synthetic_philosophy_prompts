You are a political philosopher with expertise in the normative theory of political legitimacy, and a working knowledge of comparative politics and development economics. I teach in a philosophy department, and I would like you to serve as a referee for an argument I witnessed at an interdisciplinary workshop and could not settle. Please think carefully and proceed in stages: begin by brainstorming the plausible readings of the dispute, then restate the strongest version of each party's position in your own words, and only then evaluate. Where a premise is doing hidden work, name it.

The setting. The workshop included a normative political theorist and a development economist, and they fell into a dispute over whether a certain kind of regime deserves recognition as legitimate. The case was stylized rather than any particular country, though it was plainly modeled on real small, prosperous city-states whose governments are neither elected in any competitive sense nor constrained by an independent judiciary. Call the case the Concord: a compact, wealthy polity that over several decades has delivered rising incomes, low violent crime, reliable infrastructure, clean water, and unusually competent administration, under a technocratic council that controls budgets, land use, and the machinery of statistics. A referendum has been proposed that would replace the council with a competitive elected assembly. The question was whether the existing arrangement already possessed what recognition is meant to track, or whether the referendum would be creating legitimacy rather than merely redistributing it. What follows is my best reconstruction of the exchange. I take no side.

(1) Dario: The council's authority is justified by what it delivers. Legitimacy is not a prize for the manner of selection; it is a warrant to govern well. Where a government reliably secures order, growth, and basic welfare, it has done the thing governments exist to do, and the burden shifts to anyone who wants to replace it to show that the replacement would do better. The referendum is a leap into the dark.

(2) Mira: You are measuring performance against a baseline, but the baseline is the regime's own construction. It decides which problems count, controls the statistics, and writes the history. Prosperity is not a raw fact about the world; it is a claim produced by an apparatus the regime owns. So the evidence you cite has passed through the hands of the party whose case it is supposed to support.

(3) Dario: Then no government could ever be assessed on its record, since every government produces its own statistics. We routinely judge that some govern well and others badly, and we do not require the numbers to arrive from outside. Your skepticism proves too much. And the alternative you favor has its own manufactured baselines: incumbents everywhere define the terms of debate and suppress inconvenient facts.

(4) Mira: I am not claiming the record is unknowable; I am claiming it cannot do the work you assign it, because the record answers "did they deliver?" while the question at issue is "by what right?" A custodian may manage your affairs impeccably; that does not make the custodian your equal in authority. What is missing is the standing to demand reasons — the recognition that the governed are the kind of beings who are owed an account. Deliveries are not accounts.

(5) Dario: But people vote with their feet, and they are voting for this arrangement. Net immigration is high, emigration is low, and nobody is being kept in. Revealed preference is a form of consent, and arguably a more honest one than a ballot, because it costs something. If the residents could leave and do not, they have endorsed the deal.

(6) Mira: Exit is not voice. The option to leave is priced by the regime — by who is allowed in, by how easily capital and family can move, by what happens to those who go. And a person can prefer the current ruler to chaos without thereby authorizing the ruler. Consent extracted under a threat, even one the regime did not create but merely exploits, is not the kind that grounds authority. Nor are current residents the only ones affected: those who would have lived here under a different arrangement have no feet to vote with.

(7) Dario: Now you are importing hypothetical people to outweigh actual ones. And you keep skipping a further point: the arrangement is load-bearing. The economy is a complex machine the council operates, and dismantling it would impose real transition costs on real people. Call it lock-in if you like, but the competence that makes the government undemocratic is the same competence that makes its removal dangerous. That is a reason, not a trick.

(8) Mira: That is the most troubling argument of all, and it is self-sealing. If a government can acquire legitimacy by making itself too useful to remove, then legitimacy rewards entrenchment, and the more indispensable a ruler becomes, the less accountable it need be. Your principle licenses exactly the outcome it is meant to prevent. A justification that cannot fail is not a justification; it is a shield.

Now I would like a point-by-point adjudication. For each numbered move, rate it as sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and justify the rating briefly; do not collapse the moves into a single verdict. Then identify what each party is missing that neither seems to see. Finally, say who has the better of the overall exchange. Be explicit throughout about how confident you are in each judgment, and at the end single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, along with what evidence or argument would most move you. Write in American English.
