I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am to give the opening address — twelve minutes, no slides, and no notes if I can manage it — at a small literary festival whose theme this year is "the useful book". The audience will be roughly a third academic critics, a third working poets and novelists, and a third local readers who come every year and will forgive almost anything except being condescended to.

What I want to speak about is the knot in Du Bois's writing on art and propaganda, and I want the speech to be shaped by that knot rather than to mention it politely and move on. As I understand him, he holds at least four things at once, and they do not sit easily together: that all art has always served a cause, and that pretending otherwise is a self-flattery available chiefly to those whose cause is already won; that the art which serves freedom must nevertheless be beautiful, because a truth that has not been made beautiful is not seen; that the artist must be free — not a conscript, not a spokesman, not an instrument of the group's respectability; and that a people denied a usable past may need spectacle, pageant, mass forms, the deliberate manufacture of memory, and that he himself tried his hand at exactly that.

I want to test that knot against a distinction of my own, which I would like you to take seriously for the length of the speech and then, if you can, strain. I have come to think that we confuse two things when we argue about propaganda in fiction. A work delivers a verdict when it tells the reader what to conclude about the people it depicts; it supplies a warrant when it gives the reader the grounds from which to conclude something themselves. My provisional thesis is that when Du Bois says all art is propaganda he is naming the direction of a work's pressure, not the explicitness of its judgement — that he is asking for warrants, not verdicts — and that this is why his position is not the philistinism it is taken for. But the thesis has an obvious hole in it: the pageant is all verdict, and he valued it. So I want the speech to press on that hole rather than paper over it.

Please write the address. Constraints:

- Do not quote Du Bois, or anyone else, verbatim; I cannot check quotations and neither can you, so paraphrase. No titles, dates, or citations — the speech should not depend on them, and I would rather it did not gesture at them at all.
- Do not resolve the tension at the end. It should close on the difficulty, not on a reconciliation. It may close on an image.
- The philosophy must do structural work: the speech should be a demonstration of the distinction, not a report on it. If the distinction is right, the speech itself should supply warrants and withhold verdicts — including about the audience.
- Include, early, one concrete memory of my own: my grandmother ran the Christmas pageant at her church, and I was once made to stand very straight in a sheet and a paper crown while she told me that I was not playing a king, I was representing one. I have never worked out whether that is a warning or a licence, and the speech should not pretend to.
- Keep it speakable: short sentences where it matters, no subordinate-clause pile-ups, no string of three adjectives. Roughly 1,600–1,800 words.
- Write in British English throughout — spelling, idiom, punctuation. Avoid anything that reads as American.
- End with a single line I could actually say aloud while looking at the room.
