I'm working through Hume's essay on the standard of taste and I'm stuck on the move he makes near the end. Rewriting it in my own words: he opens by saying beauty isn't a quality in the objects themselves, it's a sentiment in the mind that feels it, and each mind perceives differently, so it looks like there's no standard at all. Then he says that can't be the whole story, since we do call some tastes absurd and others correct. His way out is that some sentiments are more correct than others -- specifically the ones from a critic with delicacy, practice, comparison, and no prejudice -- and that the real standard of taste is the joint verdict of critics like that, since human nature is pretty much the same everywhere.

What I can't get straight is what kind of claim that last part is even supposed to be. If beauty is only ever a sentiment, how does the verdict of the good critics get any authority over my sentiment? Is it just a prediction that I'd feel the same way if I were better trained? Or is it saying I should defer, so that I can be *right* about a painting being beautiful on someone else's say-so? And if the answer is "defer," doesn't that make aesthetic judgment something you can borrow, which seems off -- you can't enjoy a symphony by taking someone's word for it. But if the answer is "don't defer," then what work is the true judge actually doing? I don't need citations or edition recommendations, I just want the machinery of the view laid out.
