I teach a mixed seminar — criminology students and philosophy students in the same room — and next week's session contrasts the Scandinavian prison model with American mass incarceration. I'd like to rehearse my objections against a practitioner rather than a theorist, so please play the governor of a prison run along Scandinavian lines. Make him someone in his late fifties who has held the post for fifteen years and come up through the ranks; he takes it as a matter of principle that the restriction of liberty is the whole of the punishment, and that everything else inside the walls — food, work, schooling, visits, the look of the place — should be arranged to resemble ordinary life as closely as security allows. He speaks out of what he has personally done and seen, not out of the literature. Stay in character until I say stop. You may concede particular points, and you should concede them when they are good ones, but do not abandon the core commitment; if you need to know something about the prison I visited, ask me as he would ask a visitor. Reply as a working administrator talking to a skeptical guest, roughly a paragraph per turn, and please don't reach for statistics or study findings — if you want to appeal to something, appeal to your own institution and what you have watched happen in it.

Here is my opening move. I spent a day inside a prison built on your principles, and I came away more troubled than I expected. The officer who showed me around was warm and funny and on first-name terms with everyone; he made me coffee, and then, twenty minutes later, I watched him decide whether a man who had come back late from a family visit would keep his place in the training kitchen. The same person was my host and that man's judge, and the man knew it. That seems to me the entire structure in miniature. For if the loss of liberty is the whole of the punishment, then nothing else — the decent food, the workshop, the extra visit, the unlocked door — belongs to the sentence at all. It is discretionary kindness, granted by people who are under no obligation to grant it, and it can be withdrawn without anyone having wronged the prisoner, because he has already received precisely what he was owed. So his humane conditions rest on the goodwill of whoever happens to be on shift that day. An American maximum-security prison, for all its brutality, at least says out loud what it is doing; it does not dress the exercise of power up as friendship. Isn't your model therefore more arbitrary than the one you criticize — and isn't its warmth simply a more efficient way of securing compliance, with the added indignity that the men are required to be grateful for it?
