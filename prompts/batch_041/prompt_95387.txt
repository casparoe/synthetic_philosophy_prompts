Ok, so I'm trying to decide where to put my next couple of years. Background is logic and phil of science, no formal econ. I want to work on decision theory, and the cluster I keep coming back to is the Death in Damascus family: you're in Damascus, you see Death, you can stay or flee to Aleppo, and a predictor (perfect or just very good) has already put Death in whichever city you'll choose. Evidential decision theory goes unstable here -- if you're leaning stay, fleeing looks better; if you're leaning flee, staying looks better -- while causal decision theory just shrugs and says Death's location is already fixed, so it makes no difference. I'm less interested in adjudicating the original puzzle than in the open questions it opens up. Which of these actually deserve work, and in what order? Candidates: (a) does the instability show EDT is incoherent, or does it just need a ratification step, i.e. a rule that you should only pick an option you'd still endorse after learning you picked it; (b) is the problem even well-posed unless you specify the predictor's mechanism, and what changes when you do -- say the predictor is a deterministic physical process rather than a person; (c) how do randomization and mixed strategies change things, especially if the predictor knows your procedure but not its output; (d) should we be choosing acts at all, or policies / decision procedures, and does that dissolve the instability; (e) how much of this is just correlated equilibrium in game-theoretic clothing; (f) does the puzzle have any real analogue -- precommitment, self-fulfilling prophecy, whatever -- or is it a philosophers artifact. Also here's a wrinkle I haven't seen discussed: what if the predictors accuracy depends on how predictable you are, and you can make yourself less predictable at some cost (coin-flipping, cultivating spontaneity, etc.)? Does that turn it into a different problem, one about choosing your own predictability rather than choosing a city? Rank the questions (add any I'm missing) by how upstream they are, how tractable for someone with my background, and what evidence or argument over the next year or two would move them up or down. I want a defended agenda, not a survey, and definately not a reading list.
