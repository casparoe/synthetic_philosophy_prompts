I've been chewing on the two-identical-spheres thing for about a year now and I've hit a wall with it, so I want a diagnosis rather than another argument. You know the case: a possible world that contains exactly two iron spheres, same size, same mass, same composition, same temperature, both alone in an otherwise empty space, some fixed distance apart. I always mix up which way round the principle goes — the identity of indiscernables, or the indiscernability of identicals, one of them is the boring one everybody accepts and the other is the one this case is supposed to kill. Anyway, the group I argue with splits three ways. One of them says the case proves the interesting principle false: here are two things and there is no difference between them at all, so being two things can't consist in any difference you could state. Another says no, they're perfectly discernible, because one of them is the sphere that's exactly two metres from the other sphere, and that's a property it has and its twin doesn't — he calls that an 'impure' property and says the principle was only ever meant to be about pure or qualitative properties, and he says that about every counterexample anyone brings, which is the part that makes me suspicious. The third one says the world isn't even possible in the first place, because if space is nothing over and above the relations between things, then two spheres with no differences between them aren't two arrangements of anything, and describing them as 'two' is smuggling in what you're supposed to be proving.

I tried to settle it with my own variations and it got worse, not better. First: keep the two spheres but stick a small cube off to one side, so now one sphere is nearer the cube and the other is farther. Does that suddenly make them discernible? And if so, is the principle just trivially true in any universe containing anything else at all, which can't be what Leibniz meant. Second: a universe with one perfectly uniform sphere and nothing else — is that one thing, and does the principle have anything to say about it, or is it only interesting once there's a second thing? Third: a universe where everything is arranged symmetrically around the midpoint, so there's no outside object that could break the tie either, but the two spheres are mirror images in some respect I can't quite get hold of. A friend of mine who did a physics degree says this is the same fight physicists have when two descriptions of a situation are related by a symmetry, like rotating the whole universe, and they just shrug and call it the same situation — she thinks the sphere case is a disagreement about what we mean by 'the same world' dressed up as a disagreement about spheres. And my cousin keeps saying it's all about whether a thing has any 'thisness' over and above its qualities, a word I think he got off a podcast, and that once you decide that the case decides itself.

My own guess, and I'd like it tested rather than agreed with, is that most of this is verbal — that the two camps mean different things by 'property', or one is talking about properties and the other about what you could say or think or point at, and that the residue that isn't verbal is small and is about whether there's any fact of the matter about a thing's identity beyond its qualities. So: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, is it a hidden disagreement about possibility and conceivability, is it two questions wearing one name, or is it just genuinely hard? Say what each side is committed to that they might not have noticed, including whether the 'impure properties' move is legitimate or a cheat, and whether my three variations make the situation better or worse for either side. Then tell me honestly what would count as progress here — a distinction, a sharper case, a formal result — or argue that there's none to be had and say why. Please don't send me to anything to read, I can't look stuff up and a list of names I can't check is no use to me; I want the reasoning itself.
