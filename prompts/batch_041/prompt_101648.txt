I run an every-other-week reading group out of a coffeeshop back room — mostly ML grad students plus a couple of philosophy people, usually 11-13 of us, 90 minutes, a whiteboard and not much else. Next session I want to do mechanistic anomaly detection, but really I want to poke at the thing underneath it that I can't get straight: when we say two weird behaviors come from "the same mechanism," what are we actually claiming? In the alignment world people throw that phrase around like it's obvious, and I've never seen a clean story about what makes two mechanisms the same rather than merely similar-looking from the outside. So don't explain the concept to me — design me a 90-minute activity where the group runs into this problem with their own hands, before anyone says the word "mechanism," and then a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual distinction.

Here's the rough shape I'm imagining, but change whatever you need to make it actually work. I want to run it as a lab: most people are "interpretability researchers," and 3 or 4 unlucky volunteers each secretly play a "system." Each system is just a rule that takes an integer from 1 to 10 and spits back an output, written on a card only its player sees — so I can rig two systems to share a literal subroutine, or make two systems produce identical outputs across the whole probe range they'll realistically explore while differing off the edge, and so on. Researchers spend a limited budget of probes (say six per system, tracked on a sheet) and then have to cluster the systems into groups that, in their judgment, share a mechanism, and commit in writing to why. The trap I want to spring: the two systems that look identical under every probe they can afford are secretly different, and two that look different share a hidden part — so the clustering they'll naturally do (group by what the outputs looked like) is wrong, and the only honest move left is to argue about what "same" should even mean. Give me the materials list, the exact roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, the rule-card templates, and the moment where I reveal enough of the internals to make the trap land without it feeling like a gotcha.

Then the debrief, which is the part I care most about. I want it to take the moves they made and show that they were quietly choosing an individuation criterion — same outputs on the probed region, same response to interventions, shared parts, same functional role — and that these come apart, and to say which of those is a real live fork in the interpretability literature versus which is just me being confused. Also tell me what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it (I bet the player-systems leak their rules, and I bet somebody tries to brute-force by burning the probe budget in a dumb order) and how to handle each. And be straight with me about what a card game can't reproduce here — I assume it can't give them the high-dimensional, approximate, underdetermined feel of a real network where the mechanism is a hypothesis rather than a fact, but tell me what else I'm papering over. At the end, separate what's actually settled about mechanism individuation from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — please don't hand me a tidy consensus that isn't there.
