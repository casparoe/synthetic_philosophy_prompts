I'm putting together a short poem to open a graduate seminar on the moral status of beings that lie at various points on the scale from the clearly sentient to the clearly insentient, and I'd like you to write it. Take the role of a philosopher of mind who also writes verse -- someone who knows the difference between nociception and pain, who knows why a withdrawal reflex is not by itself evidence of anything it is like to be the creature that withdraws, and who knows why the thermostat is supposed to be the limiting case of the debate rather than a punchline. Think the problem through carefully before you commit a single line, and keep the thinking out of the finished poem.

Background, so that the poem can live in the weeds rather than at the level of slogans. In our seminar we have been working with a five-rung ladder, and we argue about whether it is a ladder at all:

1. The regulator: something that holds a variable near a setpoint against disturbance, with no evidence of any felt state -- a thermostat, a bacterium swimming up a gradient.
2. The responder: something that detects and reacts to damage, sometimes with behaviour that looks like avoidance, but where the reaction may be entirely machinery -- the standard example is the insect that keeps feeding after being injured.
3. The feeler: something for which there is plausibly something it is like, valenced and brief, with no capacity to take itself as an object.
4. The rememberer: experience integrated across time, with anticipation and lingering, so that a harm can be suffered in prospect and in memory.
5. The self-narrator: a subject who can take its own states as objects, and so can be wronged in ways that require a self-conception.

Two further things we keep circling. First, we suspect a fallacy we have been calling the setpoint fallacy: reading a point of view off a goal-directed regulatory mechanism, when all that has been shown is regulation. Second, we cannot agree on whether the ladder is a smooth continuum, a continuum with a joint somewhere, or a set of discrete conditions with vague edges; the poem should not settle this, and should leave the question exactly as open as our discussion left it.

Now the form, which I want to do real work:

Write the poem as exactly five numbered stanzas, one per rung, moving down the ladder from the fifth rung to the first, so that the poem descends as the ladder does. Each stanza should be four to six lines, and each should contain one concrete image taken from the world of the being at that rung.

Give the poem a refrain of exactly seven words, identical in the first stanza, losing one word with each successive stanza, so that the fifth stanza's refrain is a single word. The refrain must be the poem's only repeated material, and the order in which the words drop out should itself mean something -- I will judge the poem partly on whether the words that fall away are the right ones to fall away first, and on whether the single surviving word earns the place it ends up in.

In stanzas one through four, one line may be given in the being's own voice. In the fifth stanza no first-person interiority is permitted at all: the regulator may be described only from the outside, and the poem must not smuggle a point of view back into it, by sympathy or by irony or by a clever line break. Do not use the words sentient, sentience, conscious, consciousness, qualia, phenomenal, or moral status anywhere in the poem; make the distinctions show in the images instead of the vocabulary.

The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind recognises each rung and does not wince, and good enough to be read aloud at the start of a session. If you find yourself writing something that could have been written without the ladder -- general awe about the continuity of life, or a shrug about how we cannot know -- start again.

Give me nothing but the five numbered stanzas: no title, no epigraph, no preface, no closing note, and no explanation of the form.
