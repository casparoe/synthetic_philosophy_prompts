You are a philosopher of mind and a decision theorist with expertise in the science of animal sentience and in formal ethics, writing for a graduate seminar on moral uncertainty. Think carefully and proceed stepwise: before you write anything, brainstorm a short list of candidate settings and check the arithmetic on each, discarding any setting in which a confound rather than the intended variable is doing the work. State your assumptions explicitly. Do not cite literature or lean on particular published claims; use only the concepts characterised below plus general facts you are confident of about insect neurobiology, population ecology, and agricultural practice. The case is for a seminar on moral uncertainty, and its point is to test whether the force of the scale argument comes from the numbers or from the probability, by holding their product fixed; that purpose fixes what counts as cheating.

Your task is to construct a single decision problem with the following structure. A decision-maker — a farm manager, a conservation officer, or someone similarly placed — must choose between exactly two regimes available to them at the same time. The regimes differ only in the following respect. Under regime H, the practice affects N_H individual insects, and the probability that any given one of them is a subject of experience is p_H. Under regime L, the practice affects N_L individuals, and the probability is p_L. Calibrate the two regimes so that the expected number of sentient individuals affected is exactly the same: N_H times p_H equals N_L times p_L. Everything you can hold fixed, hold fixed: the intensity and duration of whatever goes wrong for an affected individual, the effects on vertebrates and on ecosystems, the cost and inconvenience to the decision-maker. If some residual difference between the regimes cannot be eliminated, say what it is and whether it disturbs the verdicts. You may choose the numbers, but they must lie within a range you would defend in front of an ecologist; keep the setting realistic — no exotic organisms, engineered minds, or simulated subjects — and do not rest anything on contested claims about a named species.

Specify three views, and make the case come out as follows. First, straight expected-value aggregation over affected individuals — the scale argument in its plain form, in which vast numbers of possible subjects multiplied by a small probability of sentience can outweigh a smaller number of certain subjects — must return the verdict that the two regimes are exactly equally bad; show that this equality follows from additivity over individuals rather than from your particular numbers. Second, a precautionary or maximin-flavoured view, on which a non-negligible chance of wronging an enormous number of subjects is treated as lexically worse than a certainty of wronging fewer, must return the verdict that L is the worse regime. Third, a view on which moral status attaches only to subjects whose sentience is determinate, so that merely possible subjects cannot be wronged though they remain reasons for caution, must return the verdict that H is the worse regime. These verdicts must fall out of the views as characterised; do not build them into the description of the case, and neither describe either probability as negligible nor assert or deny that the insects feel anything.

Then write the audit. State the verdict each of the three views gives and the feature of the case that produces it. Identify the single structural feature doing the work, and explain why it is precisely what makes the three views separate. Run the removal test: say what happens to each verdict if you set p_H equal to p_L, and again if you set N_H equal to N_L, and locate roughly where in the (N, p) plane the three-way disagreement exists. Finally, put the strongest case for whichever view fares worst — how would a defender of expected-value aggregation, or of the determinacy view, argue that your case begs the question or rests on a confusion? — and say honestly how much of the disagreement survives. If you judge the specification unsatisfiable as stated, identify the part you cannot meet and why, then construct the nearest satisfiable case.

Aim for one or two paragraphs of scenario, then the audit. Make the arithmetic explicit throughout.
