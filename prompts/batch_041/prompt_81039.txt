In a departmental seminar on the replication crisis, a colleague dismissed a failed replication of a well-known effect with the remark that "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence", as though that settled the matter. I suspect the saying is doing some real work, but I cannot tell how much of it is platitude and how much is a substantive, and perhaps false, claim. Please set out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing, ranging from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is defensible, contentious, or simply false — noting in each case what the reading presupposes about what evidence is, and how it fares once one distinguishes a frequentist from a Bayesian treatment of a null result (including what it assumes about the study's power). Finally, tell me which of the readings, if any, would actually license my colleague's dismissal of the failed replication, and whether that same reading would equally undermine the original finding. Please write your answer in British English.
