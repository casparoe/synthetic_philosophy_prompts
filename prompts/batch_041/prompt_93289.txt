I am currently enrolled in our department's upper-level course on decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and our final assignment asks each of us to design an examination question for next year's cohort, supplying the question together with a model answer and a marking scheme. I have settled on the St. Petersburg paradox, but I do not want a question that can be answered by reciting the familiar claim that the diminishing marginal utility of money dissolves the difficulty. I would like the question to force candidates to reason about why that claim is only a partial fix, and I have sketched a variant that I want the question built around.

The setup I intend to give is this. In the ordinary St. Petersburg game a fair coin is tossed until it first lands tails; if that happens on toss n, the payout is 2^n pounds. The expected payout is the sum over n of (1/2)^n · 2^n, which diverges, so the game has infinite expected monetary value. I then want candidates to consider an accelerated version in which the payout on a first tails at toss n is not 2^n but 2^(2^n), and to work out what this does to the standard resolution.

Please write the examination question, a model answer of roughly 700–800 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be worth 25 marks and be answerable in about 45 minutes, and I would like it structured so that candidates must: explain what the divergence of expected value actually conflicts with, and what it does not by itself show; present the diminishing-marginal-utility resolution and then argue precisely why it relocates the problem rather than removing it unless a further assumption is supplied; analyse the accelerated game, including its expected value and the fact that a logarithmic utility function does not tame it, in contrast to the ordinary game, which the same logarithmic utility function does tame; and finally state the general condition on a utility function under which no game of this kind can be constructed to yield infinite expected utility, and assess how plausible that condition is.

The model answer should be self-contained and should not appeal to any named literature, since candidates will not have references to hand, and neither the question nor the model answer should depend on citations. The marking scheme should identify the specific moves a first-class answer must contain, allocate the 25 marks across them, and note, for each part, the particular error that should cost candidates marks — for instance, treating the diminishing-marginal-utility point as a mathematical fact rather than a substantive assumption, confusing infinite expected value with an inability to lose, or failing to see that any particular concave utility function can be outrun by accelerating the payoffs.
