I am the managing editor for a secondary-school and introductory-college science list, and I am the last person to read a manuscript before it goes to the author for approval and then to press. The unit I am working through now runs to two chapters on why bodies and behaviors are the way they are, and it contains roughly forty sentences of the form X evolved because... — the giraffe's neck, the peacock's tail, the human appendix, male nipples, and a dozen claims about human behavior. I have no graduate training in biology, I get about an hour per chapter, and these books stay in print for a decade and get recycled into teacher guides and exam banks, so any sentence that survives me is taught as fact for a long time. I have read the spandrels critique of adaptationism and I do not need its history retold; what I want is its operational form.

So: write me a decision procedure I can run on each claim in turn — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers stop the process (keep as written, keep with a specified hedge, cut, or escalate to the author with a specific request), and where the procedure has to hand the decision to a person because it turns on a judgment rather than a fact. Two constraints. It has to work from the manuscript's own text: I cannot go to the literature, I do not want a reading list, and where a step needs something the manuscript does not state, the step should tell me to escalate rather than to guess. And it has to be usable by someone who knows the vocabulary but not the research. Assume the author will contest anything I cut, so every verdict needs a one-line rationale I can paste into a memo.

Build in the distinctions I keep getting wrong, at minimum these: current usefulness versus the reason a trait was selected; adaptation versus exaptation versus byproduct of some other change, and whether a sentence is claiming any of these or merely reporting an effect; claims about a trait versus claims about a difference between groups; whether the trait is one thing or a composite of parts that vary independently, since an adaptive story about a composite is often an artifact of how we described it; and hypotheses about the distant past versus claims about the present, including the mismatch case of a trait that was useful in a past environment and is not now.

A colleague wants us to adopt a default: treat byproduct of a developmental, architectural, or historical constraint as the null hypothesis, and credit an adaptation claim only if it beats that null on at least two independent lines of evidence. Tell me whether that is the right default, what should count as independent, and where the rule misfires — I suspect it fails on traits whose byproduct story is itself unfalsifiable, and I want the failure stated rather than patched over.

Then run the procedure on three sentences from the current draft and show the output line by line: the passage asserting that the giraffe's long neck evolved so that giraffes can browse above other herbivores, presented as settled and beloved by the author; the sentence calling the human appendix a vestigial organ; and the aside that male nipples are a byproduct of shared developmental pathways. I expect at least one to come back in a form the author will fight me on, and I want to know what to say to them.

Include the sentence-level rewrite patterns, because I need wording I can put in the margin: a hedge that flattens into some scientists believe is worse than useless, since the reader learns nothing about what is actually at stake. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, which of its steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and whether there is a cheap whole-chapter check that would catch a bias the per-sentence procedure misses — the possibility that the real problem is not which claims are wrong but which traits were given an evolutionary explanation in the first place.
