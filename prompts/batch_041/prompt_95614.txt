I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty-five years on the same beamline — who has spent the last four years reading philosophy of science. Next month I am to give the opening remarks at a small two-day workshop on structural realism, attended in roughly equal measure by working physicists and by philosophers of science, and I should like you to write those remarks for me. I want to hold up two of my old lab notebooks: one from 1981, in which I recorded the properties of a particle I was certain existed and thought I understood, and one from 2009, in which the same symbol appears inside an equation I would still stake my reputation on. I want the address to turn on the difference between those two notebooks — on the thought that what survived the intervening revolution was the structure and not the thing, and that this is not a defeat but the only sort of knowledge a physicist can honestly claim. There is one further idea I should like you to press on the room: that the recurring appearance of the same mathematical structure in systems with nothing else in common is the strongest evidence we have for that view, and that I must make the point without overclaiming. The philosophers will be listening for whether I have earned the claim, and the physicists for whether I have betrayed the trade, so please do not let it settle into a slogan. Kindly write it in British English, at roughly ten minutes' length.
