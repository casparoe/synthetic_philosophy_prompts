I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of mathematics, and I want to put a position I have come to hold under pressure. The position is this: I want to retain a robust truth-value realism about mathematics — mathematical sentences have determinate, mind-independent truth values — while rejecting the semantic constraint that is normally read into Benacerraf's dilemma, namely that mathematical truth must be explained by a uniform, object-involving semantics of the same kind we give for empirical discourse. Read that way, I think the dilemma is not a refutation of realism but a proof that this constraint, conjoined with the demand that mathematical knowledge be explained as a relation between a knower and the things known, cannot be satisfied. Drop the constraint and the epistemological horn loses its bite.

I would like to know what would count against this. Please separate what a defender of my view would themselves concede from what only a critic would press, and distinguish the defeaters that would be fatal from those that would be merely awkward. Specifically: can the epistemological horn be restated without presupposing the semantic constraint — say, as a demand that we explain the reliability of our mathematical beliefs — and if so, does my dissolution survive it? Can the view preserve the objectivity and determinacy of mathematical truth without objects, or does it quietly become fictionalism, or a deflationism that cannot make sense of mathematical disagreement? Does the argument that numbers cannot be identified with any particular sets, since several incompatible reductions serve equally well, return through the back door and force me to say that the rival reductions are not rivals at all? And does my move overgeneralize — if the same dissolution works for moral or modal discourse, is that a feature or a reductio? I am also unsure whether the position has enough content left to be assessed at all, so I want an honest verdict on whether it is insulated from refutation and whether that insulation is a defect.

Finally, tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and what consideration would change your verdict.
