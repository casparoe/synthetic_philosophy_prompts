For my Extended Project Qualification I am writing an essay on whether what we believe, expect or have been taught can change what we actually see, rather than merely changing how we describe or judge what we see. I have assembled the list of findings below and would like you to rate each of them, on a scale from 0 to 1, for how strongly it supports the claim that perception itself is penetrated by cognition. Treat 0 as meaning that the finding is entirely compatible with perception being sealed off from what we think — for instance because the effect could arise wholly after the perceptual stage, or because it is really a matter of attention rather than of cognitive content — and 1 as meaning that, if it holds up, it is close to decisive on its own. Please give a number for every item, rank the items from strongest to weakest, and group them into tiers.

For each item, add two or three sentences explaining what would have to be true of the underlying mechanism for the rating to be deserved. I am anxious not to overclaim, so for each item please say whether the effect is better described as attentional, as a matter of memory or expectation leaking in, or as genuinely cognitive content reaching the perceptual stage, and flag any item whose empirical standing you regard as contested or as resting on a single line of work.

The list:

1. The Müller-Lyer illusion: knowing that the two horizontal lines are the same length does not stop them looking unequal.

2. Phoneme restoration: when a speech sound is replaced by a cough, listeners report having heard the missing sound.

3. The McGurk effect: a listener shown lip movements for one sound while hearing another reports a third sound that was never presented.

4. Memory colour: a grey banana or a grey tomato is matched, or judged, as very slightly shifted towards the colour that kind of object usually has.

5. Ambiguous figures: the duck–rabbit drawing flips from one reading to the other while the drawing itself stays fixed.

6. The dress: the same photograph looks blue-and-black to some viewers and white-and-gold to others.

7. Colour categories across languages: speakers of languages that place the boundary between two colour terms in different places show a corresponding shift when they are asked to place a boundary between two nearly indistinguishable shades, in a task that never mentions the terms.

8. Placebo analgesia: people who expect relief from a sham treatment report less pain, and there is evidence of a change in the descending pain pathway rather than only in how they talk about the pain.

9. Inattentional blindness: someone counting passes in a film fails to notice a figure walking across the scene.

10. Expertise: a radiologist glancing at a scan, or a strong chess player glancing at a position, reports seeing structure where a novice reports seeing an undifferentiated mass.

11. Wine prices: the same wine described as more expensive is rated as more pleasant to drink.

12. Stroop interference: naming the ink colour of a colour word that names a different colour takes longer than naming the ink colour of a neutral word.

13. A hypothetical case I have invented for the project, not something I have read about: participants are taught a new label for the boundary between two nearly indistinguishable shades within a single session; afterwards, on a task that never mentions the labels and that asks only which of two patches matches a patch shown earlier following adaptation, their boundary shifts in the direction of the label they were taught.

14. A second hypothetical case of my own invention: participants are told that a piece of fabric is unusually rough, while a separate group is told nothing; both groups then adjust a comparison surface until it feels as rough as the fabric, and the first group set it rougher, even though the task is presented as being about calibrating the equipment rather than about the fabric.

After the ratings, please also tell me:

(a) which single item I should lead with, and why;
(b) which items I should drop if my supervisor says I may keep only eight;
(c) whether either of the two hypothetical cases, if it turned out to be real, would be stronger evidence than anything on the original list, and what exactly about them would make them so;
(d) one number between 0 and 1 for how strong the list is taken as a whole as evidence for penetration.

I am not asking for sources or reading suggestions — I only need the ratings, the ranking, the tiers and the reasoning.
