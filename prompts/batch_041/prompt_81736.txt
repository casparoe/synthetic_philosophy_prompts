Here's roughly what she told me, from my notes: "His big idea is that psychiatric categories aren't natural kinds the way gold or tigers are — they're what he calls interactive kinds. When you put a name on a way of being, the people who get the name start to understand themselves through it and adjust, which changes what the condition looks like, which changes the criteria in the next edition of the manual, which changes the people again. That's the looping effect, and it's why a diagnosis like dissociative identity disorder wasn't sitting there in nature waiting to be discovered — the manual invented it, and the loop is what keeps it going. Really it's labelling theory with a fancier name: the label produces the symptoms it claims to be describing. And since the label is doing all the work, the honest conclusion is that these conditions are real only because we've agreed to treat them as real — if the DSM dropped the category tomorrow, the illness would go with it." She pointed me to his older work on fugue and hysteria as the model.
