I'm a novelist (three historical ones, the last set in the sixth century) and I've somehow ended up on the collections committee of our county museum trust, which is a long story. We open a new permanent gallery in six weeks and I have to circulate a written opinion before the board meets on the 14th.

Here's the mess. The gallery's framing is that our founder-king, Cenred, converted, and founded the abbey at Marshend around 580. What we've actually got: one narrative source written maybe three hundred years later, surviving only in a twelfth-century copy, and its Cenred story looks recycled from an earlier saint's life — same miracle, same turns of phrase, different name. The charter that used to anchor the 580 date is a forgery; its witness list includes a man who died before the date on it. Coins from a 2011 metal-detector hoard carry a name one letter off, which could be him or could be somebody's cousin. And the local tradition that he's buried under the market square was first written down in 1889 by a vicar with a theory.

The curator says the public needs a story and that this is all standard medievalist buisness, and the donor who paid for the bronze bust will be furious if we demote Cenred to a local figure about whom little is known. My gut says we're building a waxworks, but I'm also the guy who needs the novel to work, so I don't entirely trust myself. Draft the opinion: what should the labels actually say, does the bust stay, does 580 survive, and give me a date by which we decide. Argue it, don't just rule — and take the curator seriously. If there's an honest version where the headline claim survives with hedges, I'd rather have that than a gallery of question marks.
