My two best friends from grad school are getting married in three weeks and I'm the one giving the toast, and I've definately managed to turn it into a philosophy problem I can't stop chewing on. Here's the thing: Dan is a card-carrying utilitarian — he still has the sticker on his laptop — and at their engagement dinner, with Priya's parents sitting right there, he said cheerfully that he'd accept a policy that made his own life go worse as long as it raised the total. Priya went quiet for the rest of the night and told me later it made her feel like she was marrying a line item in somebody's spreadsheet. So I've been rereading the old objection that utilitarianism doesn't take the separateness of persons seriously (I think it's Rawls's originally, or at least that's where I ran into it), and I want to write a toast that takes her side. The room will be maybe a third people from the department, and the rest are her enormous Catholic family and Dan's parents, who are lovely and would not know what an aggregate is.

So what I want from you is a five-to-six minute toast where the philosophy does the actual structural work instead of being decoration, which means two things have to be true at once. First, her reaction has to come out right: a person isn't just a place where utility happens to be located, and you can't trade someone's whole life against a slightly bigger pile of good elsewhere and call it a wash — and I want that made vivid with a case the whole room can feel, like: one of them gets sick, the other could take on half the suffering, and by the numbers nothing at all is gained, but everyone at the table knows something enormous is gained. Second — and this is the part I keep getting stuck on — the objection can't be pushed so far that it eats the marriage, because if two people are separate in the strongest possible sense, then one of them taking the worse job, or the worse city, or the last decade of her parents' life starts to look like a mistake rather than a gift. So the toast has to make sacrifice meaningful without falling back on the usual merger stuff about two becoming one and being each other's missing half; if the only way to make a sacrifice make sense is to pretend the two of them are one person, then Dan isn't the only one in that relationship with a problem. A name or two is fine if it lands naturally, but nothing that would make her aunt feel like she's at a seminar, no closing quote from a poem (everyone does that), and end on something concrete about the two of them instead — they met because of a broken photocopier, if that's useful. Around 800 words.
