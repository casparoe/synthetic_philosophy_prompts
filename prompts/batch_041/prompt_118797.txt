For a seminar paper in my environmental politics course, I have been trying to pin down something that I suspect everyone in the field takes for granted, and I would like your help in reconstructing it. The object of my puzzlement is not the idea of a carbon tax in the abstract but the particular bundle of design choices that nearly every working scheme shares. I want to know what that bundle presupposes — not what its advocates say about it, but what it must already believe for it to make sense at all.

The bundle, as concretely as I can state it, is this. A regulator sets a price on a tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent. Gases are made commensurable with one another by conversion factors that depend on a chosen time horizon, so that a tonne of methane counts as a fixed multiple of a tonne of carbon dioxide, and a multiple that would be far larger if the horizon were set in decades rather than a century. The obligation attaches to whoever releases the gas — a firm, a household, an importer of goods with emissions embedded in them — and can be discharged in more than one currency: by paying the charge, or by paying someone else, anywhere, to reduce emissions instead, within a window of years and often under a different jurisdiction's rules. Where the gas crosses a border, the charge is applied to imports according to their embedded emissions and, in some versions of the scheme, rebated on exports, so that the burden follows the trade flow rather than the gas. The revenue is not earmarked for the damage but recycled: into cuts in other taxes, or as an equal per-capita payment to residents. And the same government that runs all of this also announces a cumulative carbon budget — a fixed total quantity of gas that may be released before a temperature limit is breached — while its nature policy allows some habitats to be destroyed provided the damage is offset elsewhere, and declares a short list of habitats irreplaceable, for which no offset may be accepted at all.

What I would like from you is an account of what this practice is committed to.

First, what view of value does the price mechanism presuppose? I want the assumption of commensurability — that unlike harms can be expressed in a single unit — kept separate from the assumption of substitutability — that a reduction achieved here is as good as one achieved there. The two are usually run together, and I suspect the practice strictly needs only one of them.

Second, what theory of responsibility does it presuppose? The charge falls on whoever releases the gas, and can be passed on, transferred, or bought out; does that presuppose that responsibility for a cumulative harm is aggregative rather than attributable to particular agents and their own acts? Could an offset market be justified on any other view?

Third, what does the equal per-capita payment presuppose about who owns the absorptive capacity of the atmosphere, and is that presupposition required by the scheme or merely convenient for it?

I would then like you to say which parts of this are strict presuppositions, which rival views it quietly rules out, and which parts are merely well served by one view among several, since I suspect the scheme is underdetermined in places. Please take seriously the apparent inconsistency at the end of my description: the same government treats one class of environmental harm as fully commensurable and tradeable and another as absolutely protected, and it holds at once a price that treats the harm as a cost to be optimised and a budget that treats it as a quantity that must not be exceeded. Is that a principled distinction, a political concession, or evidence that the practice's implicit theory is unstable?

Finally, if the commensurability assumption were rejected, say what would have to change in the practice itself rather than merely in its rhetoric, and whether a scheme built on the opposite assumption could still count as a carbon price.
