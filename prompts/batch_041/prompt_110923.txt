I dont have any philosophy background, but my brother-in-law is in grad school and he hit me with this argument over Thanksgiving. He says:

(1) The right rules for a society are the ones you'd pick if you didn't know who you'd be in it -- rich or poor, talented or not, sick or healthy, or what you'd want out of life.
(2) Since you might end up at the bottom, you'd only agree to rules that make the worst-off position as good as it can be.
(3) That's the difference principle: inequalities are okay only if they help the least advantaged.
(4) So if the worst off could be made better off by changing the rules, even at some cost to the better off, then the current rules are unjust.
(5) There's almost always some change that could help the worst off, so almost every real society is unjust.
(6) So we're required to keep redistributing until the worst off are as well off as possible, even if it costs liberty, efficiency, or people's personal projects.
(7) But that seems to prove too much. It would make it wrong to buy a nice coffee while someone else is worse off, and it would justify endless interference in everyone's life.
(8) So something has to give. Which step is wrong, and what do I lose by giving it up?

He says the flaw is subtle and that people who like Rawls would reject one of the steps. I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can. Can you walk me through it, tell me which premise to reject, and explain what rejecting it costs? Please answer in American English.
