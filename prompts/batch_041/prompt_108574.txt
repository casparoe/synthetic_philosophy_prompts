I am preparing a short statement of my views on probability to circulate to a colleague before we co-teach a foundations seminar, so that where we disagree can be located in advance rather than discovered in front of the students. Before I send it, I would like the package audited for internal consistency. This is what I hold.

1. Probability is at bottom degree of belief. To ask for the probability of an event is to ask about the credences of a rational agent, or an idealization of one; there is no further, agent-independent fact for a probability to be.

2. I am nonetheless a realist about chance. The probabilities that appear in statistical mechanics and in quantum theory are objective features of the physical world — propensities of physical setups to yield outcomes — and would be what they are even if no agent held any credence about anything.

3. I accept what is usually called the Principal Principle: an agent who knows the objective chance of an event to be p ought to have credence p in that event, absent information that is inadmissible with respect to its outcome.

4. I take the authority of chance over credence to be constitutive rather than merely prudential. An agent whose credences persistently ignore known chances is not merely imprudent; their credences are defective as credences.

5. I hold that the rationality of a probabilistic credence function is exhausted by immunity to a Dutch book. Degrees of belief are rational exactly to the extent that no combination of bets the agent finds acceptable can be guaranteed to lose them money.

6. I do not think rational agents must be logically omniscient. No real agent is certain of every logical truth, and I do not regard this as a defect in their rationality.

7. In day-to-day statistical work I am a frequentist: I report confidence intervals and p-values rather than posterior distributions, and I have found this the right thing to do in most applied settings.

8. I regard the reference class problem as a decisive objection to the classical frequentist account of the probability of a single event.

Tell me which of these stand in genuine tension with which others. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity, most serious first, and for each say what would have to give, in which direction, and what the concession would cost — including what else in the package becomes unavailable once it is made. I am as interested in the pairs that only appear to conflict as in the ones that are real, so where a tension dissolves, identify the distinction that does the work rather than merely asserting that the two commitments can coexist. Finally, tell me which single revision would repair the most, and describe the resulting position. I am not looking for anything to read; I want the audit itself.
