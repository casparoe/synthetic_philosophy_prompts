Construct a thought experiment in which an approval-directed agent and a goal-directed agent, sharing the same information and the same values, diverge — and the approval-directed agent, the one that acts so as to earn a human overseer's approval, does the worse thing. Define approval-directed as selecting the action a sincerely benevolent, fully informed overseer would most approve of, and goal-directed as optimizing the very ends that overseer cares about. The divergence must not rest on the overseer being selfish, ignorant, mistaken about any consequence, or of bad character, nor on the agent manipulating, deceiving, or reshaping the overseer; and the overseer must be an ordinary human judge rather than an idealized one, so that approval and the good cannot simply be assumed to coincide. The setting should be ordinary — a hospital, a workplace, a relief operation — with no superintelligence or exotic technology, and the scenario itself should run to no more than a paragraph. Above all, the verdicts must be generated rather than stipulated: they have to fall out of what each agent is optimizing, not from your announcing them.

Then work it through. State the act each agent performs and the reasoning that carries it there. Identify the single feature of the case that forces approval and the objective apart, and explain why an agent maximizing the one rather than the other cannot help but peel off. Say whether removing that feature collapses the two back into agreement, and whether anything interesting survives without it. Finally, give the strongest reply open to a defender of approval-directed agency, including whether redefining approval as a verdict on outcomes rather than on acts would dissolve your case — and whether that redefinition leaves approval-directed agency with anything distinct left to say.
