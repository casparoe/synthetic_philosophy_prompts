I am a final-year philosophy student currently taking a seminar on Marx and the philosophy of technology, and I have arrived at a reading of Marx that I would like you to stress-test rather than summarize. The reading is this. Marx's scattered remarks on machinery do not amount to technological determinism, nor are they chiefly predictions about unemployment; they are a single thesis about the value form. On this reading, the machinery chapters of Capital and the related passages in the Grundrisse make one argument: capital, by progressively displacing living labour from production, tends to erode the very source of its own wealth, since value has its source and measure in labour time, and machinery under capitalist relations converts the productivity of social knowledge into a force that at once presupposes and undermines that source. The Grundrisse's optimism about general intellect and the reduction of necessary labour, and Capital's account of machinery as a weapon turned against the worker, are on this view two faces of one argument rather than an early optimism corrected by a later pessimism. Put crudely, the claim is that automation under capitalism carries a tendency to dissolve the value form from within.

What I want is a map of this reading's exposure. Please distinguish, and then rank from fatal to merely awkward, three families of defeater. First, interpretive defeaters: textual or systematic reasons to think the Grundrisse–Capital tension is genuine and that the reading papers over it, or that it is a retrojection onto Marx by later readers — the autonomist tradition in particular — of a position Marx himself did not hold, being more sober about machinery's stabilizing effects. Second, empirical defeaters: trends in actually existing automation, such as productivity gains with no crisis of value, working time that has not fallen, the appearance of new value-generating sectors, or the enclosure rather than socialization of knowledge; for each, I want an honest account of whether a defender can absorb it by saying the tendency has not yet been realized or is only a tendency, and which of them would genuinely bite. Third, conceptual defeaters: whether the thesis is the kind of claim that evidence could refute at all, given that for Marx value is a social form rather than a measurable magnitude. On that last point I want a verdict rather than a hedge. If every outcome — crisis or no crisis, deskilling or upskilling, immiseration or rising living standards — can be redescribed as consistent with the tendency, has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that fatal, or merely a sign that I have mis-specified what is being claimed?

Finally, tell me what a committed defender of this reading would themselves concede as counting against it, as distinct from what critics would allege, and which single consideration you would take most seriously if you held the view. I do not need citations or a reading list; argue it out.
