I reconstructed an argument for a seminar on the extended mind, and I've come to suspect it proves far too much — but I can't identify the step that gives way, and I'd rather not surrender the premises I actually need. I'd like a diagnosis.

(1) A process is cognitive when it plays the right functional role in a subject's cognitive economy; where an external process plays the same role an internal one plays, parity of role yields parity of status.
(2) Otto's notebook plays, for him, the role biological memory plays for Inga: it stores standing beliefs he accesses automatically and relies on without deliberation, and it guides his action as those beliefs would.
(3) So the notebook's states are cognitive states, and Otto's mind extends into the notebook.
(4) Nothing in (1)–(3) turns on the notebook's being paper, or portable, or in his hand rather than in a pocket, a server, or a lab's shared wiki.
(5) A colleague who reaches for a search engine, or a lab that maintains a wiki no single member has read, is coupled to those resources just as Otto is coupled to his notebook.
(6) So those resources are parts of their minds too, and since nearly everyone is now coupled to the same public internet, there is in effect one cognitive system most of us share.
(7) By the same reasoning my pen and the colleague I phone are parts of my mind.
(8) At that point "my mind extends beyond my skull" says nothing more than "I use external things," which nobody denied.
(9) So the extended mind thesis proves too much and should be rejected.

I want to keep (1)–(3). I can't tell whether the chain is valid and one premise is simply too expensive, or whether some step only looks valid. Tell me which step to reject, and then price the alternatives.
