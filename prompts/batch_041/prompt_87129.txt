In my ethics reading group someone keeps deploying 'you cant get an ought from an is' -- most recently to shut down my argument that since the model was trained on biased data we ought to fix it, and earlier to say that no amount of empirical work on what people actually want could tell us what we should do. I half believe the line and half suspect its doing something sneaky. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making -- one about formal entailment, one about how moral terms get their meaning, one about moral epistemology, one about what can motivate action -- and for each say whether it's true, trivially true, or false, and which reading actually gets my colleague his conclusion. I'm especially after the apparent loopholes: descriptive premises that already seem to contain an ought ('she's a bad friend', 'this is a broken knife'), whether the gap runs both ways (can you get an is from an ought?), and whether it's really a gap in inference or just an artifact of how you lay the premises out on paper. And what happens when the 'is' is itself something like 'this policy makes people worse off' -- does the slogan still bite? Give me a menu I can bring back, with a verdict on each reading, not a history of who said it.
