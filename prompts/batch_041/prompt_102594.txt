I am preparing a seminar on the philosophy of mind and cognitive ethology, and I keep running into the same difficulty with the four-level taxonomy that sorts creatures into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian kinds: the ladder on which natural selection builds a creature whose behavior is fixed by its genes, reinforcement adds a second loop that lets the individual adjust to its world, the capacity to test hypotheses in a simulated inner environment adds a third, and the ability to use external tools -- words among them -- to reshape one's own thinking adds a fourth, the last level being named for the psychologist Richard Gregory. I would like to practice defending this taxonomy against a serious critic, so please play a committed defender of it: someone who believes the levels mark a real and principled progression rather than a convenient story. Stay in character until I say stop. Make him sharp and well read rather than a strawman -- he should cheerfully grant the easy objections, that the boundaries are fuzzy and that real animals straddle the levels, while never surrendering the core claim that the levels are doing genuine explanatory work. Keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph so that it feels like a back-and-forth rather than a lecture, speak in the first person in his own voice, and do not break character to summarize the debate for me.

Here is my opening move, and I would like you to answer it directly. I will gladly grant you the first two rungs. But I think the upper half of your ladder is gerrymandered. A Skinnerian pigeon on a variable-interval schedule is already running a rough internal model of the world's statistics -- it pecks in a way that anticipates reinforcement -- so what does the Popperian level actually add beyond a difference of degree? And once you tell me that a Gregorian creature is simply one that uses tools, including words, to reshape its own cognitive niche, I cannot see how a crow fashioning a hook from a twig, a child counting on her fingers, and a physicist filling a chalkboard land in different levels rather than in the same level at different resolutions. So tell me, without appealing to the flattering fact that the top rung happens to have us standing on it: what is the criterion that draws a real line between testing a hypothesis in the head and testing it on paper -- and if there is no such line, why should I believe in your four kinds of minds rather than in a continuum with no joints in it at all?
