I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend who describes himself, only half-jokingly, as the last Humean about miracles. His stated rule, which he recites whenever the subject comes up, is Hume's maxim: testimony is credible only when its being false would be a greater marvel than the event it reports, and since a miracle is by definition a breach of the ordinary course of nature, the antecedent improbability of the event is always so enormous that no merely human testimony can ever render it credible. What unsettles me is that his actual verdicts don't plainly follow from either reading of that sentence, and I want to know which rule he is really running before I decide whether he is being consistent with himself.

His rulings to date are these. A rumour that a rural shrine's statue wept blood, vouched for by a handful of pilgrims whose village lives off the shrine's offerings: disbelieve. The reported resurrection of a man three days dead, as set out in the gospels with the usual assurances of sincerity: disbelieve. A televangelist's claim to have healed a woman's inoperable tumour, corroborated by the woman, her relatives, and two physicians: disbelieve, on the ground that a spontaneous remission, however unlikely, is a likelier explanation than the healing. A claim that a stuntman survived a plunge over a waterfall that by rights should have killed him, reported by a dozen bystanders and by the man himself: believe — he insists this is not a miracle but a lucky, improbable outcome, and treats "improbable" and "miraculous" as categorically different. And finally, a case in which a woman pronounced dead by three physicians and left in a morgue for two days was later found alive and examined by a panel of sceptical doctors, with no suspicion of fraud: disbelieve, because he says the antecedent improbability of the revival is still too great for the testimony to overcome.

Now I have put a case to him that I invented to trap him, and I want your prediction of his verdict before I concede anything. Suppose that on a dozen separate occasions, in a dozen countries, a specific prayer is followed within the hour by an otherwise wholly unexplained event that would count as a miracle — say, the regrowth of a severed limb. Suppose the witnesses are not the faithful but hostile surgeons and journalists who had publicly wagered against the outcome and whose professional reputations would be made by exposing a fraud; suppose each event is captured by sealed, independently maintained instruments, and is announced in writing in advance by named sceptics who are then obliged to report the success; and suppose there is no plausible mechanism for coordinated deception across a dozen mutually distrustful teams, and no naturalistic account that fits the data at all. What will he say, and what is he really committed to? I would like you to lay out the candidate rules his earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with — at minimum a literal prohibition on ever believing miracle testimony, and a proportional reading that weighs the improbability of the event against the improbability of the testimony being false — say what each predicts about my new case, give me your best guess as to which he is running and why, and identify the further case that would best tell the two apart. Tell me how confident you are in that prediction, and single out the step in your reasoning you are least sure of.
