I'm doing The Prestige for my metaphysics reading group next month, because it looks like it stages Black's two-spheres problem with actual people in it, and I want a reading of the film through the identity of indiscernibles rather than yet another essay about obsession and sacrifice, which everyone's already written.

My hunch is that the film gives us two different cases and only one of them is really Black's. Case one is Angier: Tesla's machine makes a perfect copy and leaves the original standing, one of them drowns in the tank under the stage every night, so are those two men indiscernible at the moment the machine fires? I suspect not, since one is on the balcony and one is in the tank, and if relational differences count then the principle is never really threatened and the horror is coming from somewhere else — from there being no fact of the matter about which one he wakes up as, which I think he more or less admits at the end, and which sounds closer to a bare thisness than to a quality.

Case two is the Borden twins, and this is the one nobody uses: they amputate matching fingers and share a name, a wife, a daughter, a whole life, so that they become indiscernable from the outside on purpose. The only thing that ever tells them apart is who loves whom. That seems like the better case to me — indiscernibility as a feat of upkeep, with a relational difference doing all the work and then destroying them.

So which case is the film actually about, and is it right about which one is worse? Please keep the identity of indiscernibles apart from the indiscernibility of identicals, since only one of them is controversial, and tell me whether the Leibnizian reply (a two-sphere world described with the spheres swapped is just the same world described twice) kills the Angier version of the puzzle or leaves it standing. And be honest about where this is just sleight of hand with the metaphysics instead of philosophy — plus what the film adds that the arguments don't, and which scenes I should show the group.
