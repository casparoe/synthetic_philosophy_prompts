I'm writing a feature on whether machine-learning systems could have minds, and I want to anchor it in a scenario rather than the usual Turing-test fare. Suppose a lab trains a single model and demonstrates that its learned control policy runs unchanged on three radically different substrates — a GPU cluster, a photonic chip, and a dish of cultured neurons — and then argues in its white paper that because one and the same abstract organization is realized in all three, the system's mental states are substrate-independent and therefore as real as ours. Now suppose we could show that white paper to Hilary Putnam, who introduced the multiple realizability argument against mind-brain identity theory, who did not live to see systems like this, and who in his later work concluded that computational functionalism had been a mistake; a philosopher I consulted for the piece adds that the lab's inference proves too much, since under a suitable mapping any physical system whatsoever can be described as implementing any program. What would Putnam have said about the white paper's inference? I want a definite verdict — would he treat the portability of the policy as vindicating his early argument, reject it as a non sequitur, or call the question ill-posed — along with the reasoning that gets him there, rather than a balanced survey of the possible readings.
