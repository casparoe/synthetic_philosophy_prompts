I've been stuck in an argument with a friend from the OR department for months now and we've each given up on convincing the other, so what I actually want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than another round of it. Here's the shape. Two players can jointly produce a surplus and each can guarantee themselves something if they walk away (the disagreement point); the question is which point on the efficient frontier they should land on. Nash's solution picks the point that maximizes the product of the two players' gains over the disagreement point. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution picks the point where the two gains are equal as proportions of the most each player could possibly have gotten — geometrically, where the frontier crosses the line running from the disagreement point to the utopia point. And the egalitarian solution just equalizes the two absolute gains. Each one is uniquely pinned down by a list of axioms, and the lists can't all be satisfied together, which is the part that keeps eating at me: the axiom set that pins down Nash includes an independence condition — you can add or delete options nobody would ever have chosen and the answer can't change — and Kalai–Smorodinsky is precisely the solution that violates that condition, while it's the one that satisfies a monotonicity condition Nash violates, namely that improving a player's best alternative outside the bargain can never leave that player worse off in the agreed split. So this isn't a case of nobody having proved the right theorem yet; the theorems are all there and have been for a long time. My working hypothesis, which I don't really trust, is that it's about what a solution concept is even for: Nash is doing predictive work (that's roughly where self-interested bargainers end up, and there's a non-cooperative bargaining story that converges on it), while the other two are doing normative work (that's the split a fair arbitrator should impose), and the axioms are just each camp encoding the ideal it already wanted. If that's right then the fight is two groups answering different questions under one heading. But the people who argue about it in print don't seem confused about prediction versus prescription, so I doubt that's the whole thing.

What I want is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal — the two sides mean different things by fair, or by rational, and the axioms are doing the work of stipulation rather than discovery? Is it substantive but formal — the ideals really are incompatible, so the question as posed has no answer and the honest move is to stop asking which solution is the correct one? Is it substantive and normative — a genuine clash about which distributive ideal is right, in which case no theorem could ever settle it? Or is it a hidden empirical question wearing a formal costume, about whether actual bargainers' behaviour tracks the independence condition or the monotonicity condition, in which case experiments could in principle decide it? If it's a mix, tell me which part is which and where the seams are. I'll add one thing I've noticed that may or may not matter: the egalitarian solution is usually dismissed on the grounds that equal absolute gains is an artifact of how you happen to scale the two players' utility functions, which suggests at least some of these arguments are really about which invariances we're willing to accept rather than about fairness directly.

And then the part I actually care about: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? A distinction, a reformulation of the question, a formal result, an experiment — or is the honest answer that this is a pluralist situation where you pick your solution concept by the job you want done, and the reason it feels stalled is that everyone keeps hunting for a single answer to a question that was never single? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says — the axioms and the bargaining stories behind them — rather than just surveying the solutions at me, because I know the definitions cold; it's the shape of the impasse I can't see.
