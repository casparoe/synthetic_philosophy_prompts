so next thursday i have to present at our decision theory reading group and im dreading the q&a. what im defending is a version of evidential cooperation in large worlds, and i want you to build me a prep sheet for the objections im actually going to get.

here's my thesis in my own words so you know what to attack. normally when i decide whether to do something costly that helps someone i'll never meet -- pay into a fund that only helps strangers on the far side of the planet, or even cooperate in a one-shot prisoners dilemma against a person i'll never interact with again and who'll never learn what i did -- the standard move is to ask: does my action change anything for anyone? if the answer is no, don't do it. i think thats too quick. my choosing to cooperate is also evidence about what other agents who are relevantly like me are choosing. if im the kind of reasoner who defects, then the other runs of that same reasoning process -- in other countries, in situations i'll never see -- tend to defect too; if i cooperate, they tend to cooperate. in a large world there are alot of such agents, and a good number of them sit in positions where their cooperating happens to benefit me, so the indirect payoff from my own cooperating can dwarf the direct cost. so sometimes i should cooperate even when i have zero causal influence on anyone. and i think the scope is bigger than people expect: it should change what i do about distant charities, about voting, about defecting in anonymous one-shot games, the whole lot. thats the claim.
