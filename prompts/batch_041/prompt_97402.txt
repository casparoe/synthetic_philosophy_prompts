For the past several weeks a colleague and I have been arguing about what, if anything, the word "disease" actually tracks, and at this point I want an outside number rather than another tour of the literature. Here is the view she has been defending, and which I would like you to price. Call a condition a disease in a population just in case two things hold at once: (i) it involves the depression of some biologically specifiable function below the range typical for the relevant reference class, indexed to age, sex, and similar factors; and (ii) a member of that population who was fully informed about the condition and its treatment, and who reasoned free of the distortions of present illness, fear, and social pressure, would prefer to have it treated rather than left alone — where that preference is fixed by the person's own values and not by the physician's or the wider society's. Her thought is that the first clause answers the charge of pure conventionalism, since it ties pathology to biology, while the second answers the charge that medicine merely stamps statistical deviation as disease, since it ties pathology to what actually matters to the person. My own suspicion is that one clause is always idle and the other silently does all the work, which would make the whole construction either a naturalism or a normativism wearing a disguise.

What I want from you is your credence that this two-clause analysis gets the extension of "disease" right across the cases that usually decide the naturalism/normativism dispute: conditions that were reclassified for plainly social or political reasons, conditions that are statistically normal yet treated as pathological, conditions that are statistically abnormal but that many people who have them decline to call diseases at all, and states such as aging that seem to fall awkwardly in between. Please decompose the number rather than asserting it. I want the probability that clause (i) alone already fixes the extension, so that the analysis is a naturalism in disguise; the probability that clause (ii) does independent and coherent work, including whether the idealized preference is well-defined enough to have a truth value at all; and the probability that the hybrid survives the hard cases without collapsing into one of its two halves. Then combine those into the final figure, and tell me which single term the number is most sensitive to and what argument or case would move it most.

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Item one should be the overall credence together with the multiplication that produces it; items two through four should be the three sub-questions above, each with a probability and a one-line justification; item five should name the term you are least confident in and say what would shift it.
