I'm a pension and casualty actuary by training, and most of my working life has been loss reserving, discounting to present value, occurrence versus claims-made triggers, retroactive dates, subrogation and contribution among jointly liable parties, aggregation and correlation across claims, and the recurring internal argument about whether a tail estimate is a fact about the world or a convention we have all quietly agreed to use. Lately I've been reading my way through the philosophical debate about reparations for historical injustice: what is owed for slavery, Jim Crow, and colonial expropriation; whether the descendants of the wronged can claim a loss when they would not have existed otherwise; whether people who never wronged anyone can owe anything; whether a long enough passage of time extinguishes the claim; and the difference between paying for a loss and acknowledging a wrong. I can follow the arguments well enough, but I think in actuarial terms, and I want to see how much of the debate survives the trip.

So please restate the case for reparations, and the main lines of resistance to it, as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: policyholders and beneficiaries, insured events, occurrence versus claims-made triggers and the retroactive date, incurred-but-not-reported losses, loss development triangles, subrogation and contribution, aggregation and correlation, discounting to a valuation date, adverse selection, moral hazard, indemnity versus punitive damages, and the underwriter's problem of pricing a risk for which there is no credible loss experience. In particular I want to see what the standard objections look like in that language — whether the objection that no living person committed the wrong turns into a question about who the liable policyholder is, and whether the objection that the claim has been overtaken by later events turns into a question about a policy that lapsed or a claim that is time-barred.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto something an actuary would recognize? Which translate only with distortion, where you have to posit a category that does not really exist in my framework? And which have no counterpart at all? I expect the failures to cluster in three places: the counterfactual baseline, since unlike a reserving exercise there is no agreed valuation date, no discount curve, and no uncontested picture of the world in which the wrong never happened; the identity of the claimant, since a loss has to be a loss to someone and that someone is frequently not the person who was wronged; and standing to adjudicate, since there is no insurer, no policy, and no tribunal whose authority the parties already accept. For each of the three, tell me whether the gap marks a real limitation of actuarial thinking — something it cannot represent and should admit it cannot represent — or whether it is a sign that the philosophical claim was less determinate than it sounds, so that my demand for a clean loss event is a legitimate demand the debate has not met. I would much rather the translation fail honestly than succeed by quietly importing actuarial assumptions — that every harm has a price, that there is always a liable party, that the book can be balanced — and calling that fidelity to the original.
