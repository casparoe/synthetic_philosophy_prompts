I write the ideas column for a mid-size daily — one of those "what would X have said about our moment" gigs — and this week it's Arendt on lying in politics, due Thursday. I've read Truth and Politics once, years ago, and the Pentagon Papers essay in grad school, so I'm at the dangerous stage of knowing just enough to sound confident.

There's a short piece in a small quarterly that lays her view out in a couple of paragraphs and it reads beautifully. I won't name it, I don't want to dunk on anybody, but I was going to build the column on it and now I have a bad feeling about it's central argument. Here's the gist, as best I can paraphrase it:

"Arendt's whole point is that politics and truth are natural enemies, and that the lie is basically the native instrument of political life — she notes that nobody has ever put truthfulness on the list of the political virtues, and that the truth-teller is helpless in the public realm, because facts have no way of compelling anyone the way a mathematical proof does. She draws a line between rational truth, which is what philosophers and mathematicians deal with, and factual truth, which is what historians and journalists deal with, and she thinks factual truth is the more fragile of the two, because it's contingent, it depends on testimony, and it can always be argued about. She runs through the two classic attempts to fix the problem — Plato's, who wanted the truth to rule whether the city liked it or not, and Hobbes's, who solved it by making the sovereign the one who decides what's true — and she's pretty clearly on Plato's side. The modern twist, the thing she saw in the Pentagon Papers, is that the old lie and the new one are different animals: the traditional liar had to keep one foot in the facts, because his lie was about a particular thing and he could be caught out, whereas the modern image-maker builds a whole consistent picture of the world that can displace the facts altogether, and that's why she thinks the modern political lie is far more dangerous than the old one. What disturbed her in the Pentagon Papers wasn't the deception as such, it was the self-deception — these were problem-solvers who had lost the ability to tell their own model of the situation from the situation. Her verdict on the whole thing is the banality-of-evil point, that these were just thoughtless men doing their jobs. And the upshot is that facts win in the end, because reality eventually reasserts itself — so the real danger isn't lying, it's the panic about lying, and the honest intellectual's job is to stay out of the fray, since you can't hold to the truth and play politics at the same time."

Can you go through that claim by claim? I want three buckets: what an Arendt person would nod at, what they'd wince at but wouldn't call false, and what's just wrong or something she denies somewhere. Part of what I'm after is which sentences are actually her and which are the writer's own editorializing wearing her clothes. And that last bit about reality reasserting itself — does it follow from anything true in the summary, or is it bolted on? The traditional-lie-versus-modern-image distinction is the part I wanted to build the piece around, so if that's off I need to know now.

Two practical things. Give me replacement sentences for the parts that are wrong, written so I could actually print them, and tell me which bits are genuinely contested among Arendt people, because I'd rather hedge in print than be confidently wrong. Please no page numbers or citations — I can't check them and my editor cuts them anyway, I just need to know what's safe to say in my own words. And don't quote her at me; paraphrase anything you want to convey, because if you quote her I'll end up quoting you quoting her, and that is definately how people get fired.
