Compare two cultural syndromes that are habitually set against one another: the American Dream, understood as an ethos that treats aspiration as praiseworthy and visible success as a mark of merit, and, on the other side, Jantelagen and the tall poppy syndrome, understood as Scandinavian and Anglophone norms that cut the successful down to size. I would like the comparison to be analytic rather than merely descriptive, so please work with the following distinction.

Separate two dimensions that the usual contrast tends to run together. The first is permission to rise: how far a culture licences ambition, self-promotion and the pursuit of standing beyond one's origins. The second is penalty for arrival: how far a culture resents, mocks or disciplines those who have already succeeded and are now visibly above the rest. These two need not move together, and I suspect the standard opposition conflates them.

With that distinction in hand, I want a definite verdict rather than a survey of positions. Which of the two syndromes actually governs which dimension, and does the familiar opposition survive the decomposition? Are there coherent social configurations that pair a high permission to rise with a high penalty for arrival, or the reverse, and if so what would they look like and what would they feel like from the inside? Which dimension does the greater explanatory work — for resentment of the successful, for the willingness of the unsuccessful to keep aspiring, or for the stability of the resulting order? And finally, taking the analysis as far as it will go, say plainly which of the two syndromes is the more defensible as an ethos, and why. Where you judge my two-dimension framing to be the wrong cut, say so and offer a better one.

Please write in British English.
