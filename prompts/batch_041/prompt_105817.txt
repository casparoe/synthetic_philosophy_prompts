I have recently taken over the 'Puzzles and Paradoxes' slot in the monthly newsletter of our local philosophical society, and next month's theme is the problem of logical omniscience. Instead of the customary riddle, I should like to open the column with a short joke — one that would genuinely amuse readers who know the territory, rather than a generic 'philosophers are hopeless' gag. I would be grateful if you would write it for me.

Some constraints, so that it lands. The humour must turn on the substance of the problem rather than on the word 'logic'. I should like it to exploit the fact that the standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge and belief presents an agent as knowing everything that follows from what it already knows — and, in the usual modal setting, as knowing every logical truth outright — so that such an agent cannot be surprised by the conclusion of a proof it has never run, and a single inconsistency among its beliefs would commit it to believing absolutely everything, including the negation of anything you care to name. Ideally the joke should dramatise that stipulation in some ordinary human situation rather than merely assert it. It may help to exploit the gap between what the agent is stipulated to know and what it could actually bring to mind: an ideal reasoner in the technical sense need not be a quick-witted one, and need not even possess the concepts required to entertain many of the propositions it is said to know.

Beneath the joke, please add a short note of a few sentences on where it takes liberties with the actual problem, or leans on a reading of it that is contested among people who work on epistemic logic, as distinct from what is genuinely settled. I would far rather have an honest note than a punchline that quietly passes off a disputed modelling choice as though it were simply how things are.
