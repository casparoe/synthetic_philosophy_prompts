I teach a graduate seminar that sits between metaphysics, tort law, and the ethics of automated systems, and this term's topic is causation by omission. What I need is a one-page handout in the form of a decision tree, not a literature survey.

Fix the starting point at the claim a student can genuinely accept or reject: an omission can be a cause of the outcome that followed it. From there, every branch node must be a question that separates the views beneath it, and every leaf must be a position on the status of omission causation, named descriptively by its central claim, stated in one sentence, and followed by one further sentence on the commitment that position has the hardest time defending.

The tree must work through at least these five questions, and may add others only if they do independent work:

1. Are omissions among the things that stand in causal relations at all, or is the word omission merely a way of describing an agent in the light of what they did not do?
2. Is counterfactual dependence enough for causation by omission, or is it only a symptom of it, so that the cause properly speaking is always some positive event or standing condition?
3. When we single out one omission from the indefinitely many conditions of a harm, does that selection answer to a further causal fact, or does it answer to the norms, duties, and expectations we bring to the case?
4. Does the difference between doing and allowing mark a difference in what happens in the world, or only in what we may demand of an agent?
5. Must a single account cover both failing to prevent a harm and failing to continue a course of action that had been keeping the harm away?

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space. Where two of my questions cannot vary independently, say so instead of multiplying leaves. Mark the cells that no position occupies, and diagnose each briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; if an unoccupied cell still describes a view someone could hold, label it unnamed and state the view.

Test the finished tree against these cases and say where each one lands:

- a landlord who never repairs the boiler, and a tenant who dies of the fumes;
- a doctor who does not order a test that would have caught a treatable cancer;
- a hospital team that switches off a ventilator it had been running for a week;
- an engineer who ships a system without running the test that would have caught the fault, and an outage follows;
- a moderation system that leaves up a post that is followed by a riot;
- a government that does not fund flood defences, and a town floods;
- a passer-by on the bank who does not throw the rope lying within reach;
- someone who stops paying for a medicine they had been paying for, and the patient deteriorates.

Two constraints on the leaves. Label each with the position's name, but do not attribute positions to authors or works; the handout has to stand on its own. And wherever the tree reaches the same position by two routes, or cannot separate two positions at all, flag it explicitly and say whether the failure is a defect of my five questions or a genuine collapse of the two views into one.

Keep the whole handout under 900 words. The audience is graduate students in law and engineering who have read no philosophy of causation, so every node must be answerable by someone who does not yet know which position it leads to, and any technical term must be defined in the branch where it first appears.
