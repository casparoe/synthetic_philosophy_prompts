The analogy I keep running into, whenever secession comes up among my friends, is divorce, and I would like you to put it under the microscope rather than argue the politics. The claim is this: a nation joined to a larger union is like a spouse in a marriage, so if the marriage has irretrievably broken down, the partner who wants out does not need the other's permission to go, and the terms of separation — the house, the savings, the debts, the arrangements over the children — are worked out afterwards, whether by negotiation or by a court. On this view, the demand that a referendum be authorised by the whole union, or that the terms of independence be settled before the vote rather than after it, is supposed to be as absurd as insisting that a husband obtain his wife's consent before he may file for divorce.

Please tell me what the analogy is doing and how far it holds. Spell the correspondences out in prose rather than in a table: what plays the part of the marriage, the spouses, the children, the jointly owned property, the family home, the maintenance payments and the court. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and sort the breaks into those that matter for the conclusion above and those that do not. Two things interest me in particular. The first is whether the analogy quietly presupposes a legal order with jurisdiction over both parties and the power to enforce its terms — which is close to the opposite of the situation between sovereign states — and what becomes of the sequencing claim if it does. The second is that divorce comes in a fault-based and a no-fault variety, and that these track two quite different positions about secession: that a group may leave only if it has been seriously wronged, and that it may leave whenever it chooses. Which of the two does the analogy actually deliver, and does that change what it can support about consent and sequencing? I would also like to know whether the children in the picture are really children, or rather adult fellow citizens who voted the other way and would be carried out of the union against their wishes, and what analogy you would substitute if this one cannot carry the weight. I want the argument itself, not a reading list.

Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables; the kind of long reply you would send to a friend who had asked exactly this.
