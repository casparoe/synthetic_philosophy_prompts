A friend of mine who reads a fair amount of epistemology has a habit of ending arguments with "you can't pull yourself up by your own bootstraps." The last time was in a dispute over whether I can know that my senses are reliable. My argument was a track-record argument: over the years I have checked particular perceptions against other sources — a second look, another person, a photograph, a measurement — and they have almost always held up, so I take myself to have good evidence that my perceptual faculties are reliable. He said that was precisely the sort of thing the proverb rules out, and treated the matter as settled.

My suspicion is that the slogan is doing several different jobs at once, and that some of them are claims he would not accept if I stated them plainly. Please lay out, as a menu I can bring back to him, the distinct precise claims that "you can't pull yourself up by your own bootstraps" could be making in this context, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each: platitude, substantive but defensible, or false.

The menu should separate the following axes, to the extent that they really are separate:

- What "can't" is doing in each reading: something mechanically impossible, something inferentially invalid, something that would fail to yield knowledge even if the reasoning went through, or something one is simply not entitled to do.
- Whether the prohibition falls on my conclusion (I cannot know my senses are reliable) or only on my argument (this reasoning cannot establish it), since these appear to come apart.
- Whether the objection is about circularity — a faculty being vouched for by its own deliverances — or about cheapness — the reasoning would let me know too much too easily, so something must be wrong with it — or about something else again, such as a demand that justification terminate in support that does not come from me.
- Whether the slogan is meant as a blanket ban on any faculty's being supported by its own deliverances, and if so how it avoids ruling out forms of self-support that he and everyone else accept, such as using reasoning to check reasoning or memory to vouch for memory.

Then tell me which reading or readings would actually support his "settled" conclusion, which are being smuggled in by the sound of the proverb, and which one he most likely had in mind. One further thing I would like explained: in ordinary speech the same phrase is a compliment, since pulling yourself up by your bootstraps is supposed to be admirable self-reliance, whereas in his mouth it names a mistake. Is that a genuine equivocation between two unrelated ideas, or do the two uses share a core that makes one of them a misuse of the proverb?
