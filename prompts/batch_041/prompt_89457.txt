The half-formed idea I keep circling: everyone says that when two people with deeply different value systems disagree, compromise is either impossible (no common currency to trade in) or corrupting (you sell out something you're supposed to hold unconditionally — the integrity objection). I think both objections are aimed at the wrong target. My hunch is we should split compromise into two very different things: trading across issues (I give ground on X to get ground on Y) and conceding within a single issue (I give up part of what I wanted on X alone). The across-issue case needs almost nothing — no shared scale, no commensurability, just the fact that the two parties rank the available bundles differently — so the standard 'incommensurability blocks compromise' argument doesnt touch it at all. The within-issue case is where the real philosophical action is, and I suspect the integrity objection is really an objection to a subspecies of it: concessions that are constitutive of the commitment, in the sense that making them changes what you'd be willing to accept next time in a way you couldn't in good faith endorse. So my candidate line is that a concession counts as a genuine compromise only if the conceder could still, in good faith, tell the people they speak for that they are pursuing the same thing; if not, it isn't a compromise, it's a conversion, and it's unclear the integrity objection even gets to call it that. A friend who does tax law keeps telling me 'that's just logrolling,' which is sort of my point and sort of not, and I haven't worked out which.
