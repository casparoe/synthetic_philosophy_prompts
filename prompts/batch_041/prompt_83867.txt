I'm a science and ideas journalist, currently doing a long profile of a Buddhist ethics scholar who's also been practicing for decades in a fairly precept-strict lineage. The hypotheticals are the best part of the interviews and I want to figure out what she's actually running before I file.

Where she's landed so far:

- A woman skims a few thousand from the clinic where she works to cover her kid's surgery. Her verdict: it's stealing, and the love in it doesn't launder it. The mind that says "just this once" is the mind that says it again.
- A man testifies truthfully against his brother-in-law in a fraud case. Prison, divorce, the family blown apart. Her verdict: he did the right thing, the truth wasn't his to sit on, and he shouldn't be carrying the fallout around as guilt.
- A soldier kills a man at close range, terrified, to save his squad. Her verdict: the killing is real and it'll follow him; "I had no choice" is just how the mind declines to look.
- A meditation teacher tells a student she's making real progress when she isn't, partly so the student won't quit, mostly because he likes being liked. Her verdict: wrong, and the flattery costs him more than it costs her.

Maybe you see my problem: every one of those is a case where the deed and the motive point the same way. Clean deed, clean motive. Bad deed, bad motive. So I cant tell whether she thinks an act is wrong because of what it is or because of what's behind it. She also keeps saying the tradition has one strand that lets you bend a precept for a good end and another strand that says that's exactly the loophole the precepts exist to close, and that she thinks the second strand gets it right — but then she'll float something like "is it true, is it useful, is it the right moment," and she only ever seems to apply the first one.

So here's the case I want to put to her. A man's brother is dying of pancreatic cancer, maybe a week left. The brother's daughter has made clear she isn't coming — not a dramatic estrangement, she just won't. Every few hours the brother asks whether she's on her way, and the man tells him yes, she's trying to get a flight, she loves you. Four days of this. The brother dies calm, believing it, holding his hand. Nothing mixed in the man's motive: he isn't squeamish about death, he isn't protecting himself, and he'd have told the truth if he thought it would do anything but torture his brother.

Is that lie wrong? Would she say so? Give me the two or three rules that fit everything above, what each one predicts here, which you'd bet she's running and why, and whether she's quietly answering two different questions — was the act wrong, and what does it do to the person who does it — without noticing that's what she's doing. Then tell me the next case to put to her to actually settle it. I figure it's probaly one where the deed and the motive come apart the other way, but you tell me.
