I am a software engineer by trade with no formal training in philosophy, and I have read enough around AI alignment to have absorbed a picture of ideal deliberation that I keep relying on in arguments without ever having checked whether its parts fit together. Before I keep leaning on it, I would like an audit.

The picture, as I actually hold it:

1. Correctness as an idealization. When I ask what I should believe or do, what I take myself to be pointing at is the limit of a process in which I deliberate for as long as necessary and, whenever I hit a subproblem I cannot make progress on, hand it to a fresh copy of myself who can do the same thing in turn. Whatever that process settles on is, by my lights, the answer. This is what I understand the humans-consulting-HCH construction to be getting at.

2. Factored deliberation. Deliberation of that kind factors into small, mostly independent subproblems, each of which I could either answer or at least check given enough time, with a small trusted checker combining the results. This is supposed to be tractable precisely because verifying an answer is generally easier than producing one, so the trusted core never has to be as capable as the system as a whole.

3. Amplification without conversion. The process extends my competence but does not replace my values or my standards for what counts as a reason. If it returned a verdict that struck me as alien, telling me to care about something I can see no reason to care about, I would treat that as evidence the construction had broken rather than as a truth I am too limited to appreciate.

4. No oracular deference. I do not think I should treat the idealization as something that overrides my present judgment. My present judgments are the only ones I actually have; the idealization is a regulative picture, and what it delivers is evidence about what I would think, not a verdict that settles what is true or right.

5. A bounded trusted base. The checker at the bottom of the factoring tree is something no more capable than I am. Whatever reliability the whole edifice has is inherited from mine. It buys me organization, memory and time, not a firmer basic grip on reasons.

6. Mind-independence. I also think there is a fact of the matter about what I ought to believe and do that does not depend on what I, or any copy of me, happens to conclude.

What I want is not a summary of these views but an audit of how they sit together. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be once you notice a distinction I am already entitled to make, and how severe is each real tension? For each real one, give me the cheapest revision that would remove it and say what that revision costs, in particular which of the six I would have to weaken and how much of the original motivation for it survives. Then tell me which single change would clear up the most trouble at the least cost, and what residual awkwardness it leaves behind.
