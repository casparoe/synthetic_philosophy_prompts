For two terms I have been running a small reading group in my department on the ethics of political violence, working through the just-war tradition and its medieval sources, and a colleague of mine — a historian of scholastic political thought who is finishing a book on the theory of tyrannicide — has sat in on most of our sessions. She is a patient reader of the sources and a menace in argument, and she insists that nothing she says is her own invention: every verdict she gives, she claims, is simply what Aquinas holds. I have been keeping a record of those verdicts, partly to test the claim. Three of them are stable. First: a general seizes power by coup, sets aside the legitimate dynasty, and rules by execution and confiscation; a private citizen, acting on his own initiative and with no one's authorization, kills him, and the old dynasty is restored without further bloodshed. She says the killer did nothing wrong and is not to be called a murderer. Second: a hereditary monarch of undisputed title turns oppressive — ruinous taxation, the arbitrary imprisonment of his critics — though the country stays orderly and the ordinary machinery of government still works; a private citizen assassinates him, and the realm falls into a succession war. She says the assassination was unlawful, and that the killer should have endured the oppression rather than take matters into his own hands. Third: take a monarch of the same kind, with the oppression now far advanced — whole families put to death, the realm coming apart — and let the standing assembly of the realm formally depose and condemn him and have him put to death by its officers. She says this was entirely permissible: an exercise of public authority, not an act of sedition.

Next week we take up a harder case, and I should like to know what she will say before she says it. A monarch of undoubted hereditary title has become a tyrant of the gravest sort and is preparing to hand the realm over to a foreign power that will reduce its people to servitude; the assembly has been dissolved, the courts are packed, and no public body is in any position to act; a private citizen, acting alone, assassinates him. What follows is not clean: the tyrant's younger brother, who is just and competent, does take the throne and rule well, but the killing hands the neighbouring power the pretext it wanted, and a long and devastating war follows. I am less interested in a verdict than in the shape of the reasoning behind it. Set out the candidate rules that fit her three earlier verdicts equally well — I assume they are compatible with more than one, and that they rule out others that would otherwise look natural — and say what each rule predicts about this case and why they come apart on it in particular. Then tell me which rule you think she is in fact running, and on what grounds, and what further case we could stage that would most efficiently force her to choose among the surviving rules; if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say what she would be committing herself to under each reading. And since she claims only to be reporting Aquinas, tell me where, if anywhere, the verdicts are her own.
