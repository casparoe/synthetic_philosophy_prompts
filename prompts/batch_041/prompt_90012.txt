I write a column on science and ideas for a mid-size daily, and I'm drafting a long-ish essay for our Sunday section on Chesterton's fence. It's suddenly everywhere — my tech friends cite it about deleting legacy code, my public-health friends cite it about scrapping old protocols, my politics friends cite it whenever someone proposes tearing down an institution — and I have a nagging feeling at least half of them are using it as a classy way of saying "I don't like change." I want the essay to sort that out.

Here's the angle I want you to take, and I'd like you to actually push on it rather than just agree with me. Everyone talks about the fence argument as if it's one argument, but I think it's doing at least three different jobs depending on what's behind the fence, and it's much stronger in some of those cases than others. Case (a): the reason for the fence is recoverable — someone knows it, or the records show it, and the reason still applies. Case (b): the reason is recoverable but has stopped applying; we know exactly why the fence went up and the thing it kept out is gone. Case (c): the reason is unrecoverable in practice — nobody alive knows, no records, and it's unclear whether there was ever a good reason or whether the thing just accreted.

My hunch, which I want you to attack if it's wrong: the fence argument is at its most persuasive in case (b), not case (c). In (c), the missing record isn't neutral evidence that a good reason exists somewhere — it's also evidence about how the fence got there in the first place (was it ever deliberated? was it ever load-bearing? or did it just happen to someone?). Treating our ignorance as a reason to preserve lets accident masquerade as inherited wisdom. And in case (a) the whole thing collapses into "look before you leap," which isn't conservatism, it's just diligence. If you think I've got (b) and (c) backwards, argue the other way — I'd rather the essay be right than be mine.

So: write the essay. Weigh the real case for the fence (dispersed knowledge nobody can reassemble in one head, unintended consequences, institutions that are load-bearing in ways the reformer can't see, the asymmetry between a thing that demonstrably works and a replacement that merely sounds good) against the real case against it (status-quo bias, entrenchment, the dead hand, the way "we've always done it" is often a feature of unaccountable institutions rather than of wisdom). Reach an actual verdict, not "both sides have a point." Give me at least one worked example where the reason behind a rule is genuinely lost and one where the rule's original purpose has quietly migrated somewhere else while the rule itself stayed put — the filibuster is an obvious candidate for the first, but your call, and please don't just recycle the same fence-across-the-road bit everyone repeats.

No citations or reading lists, I just need the argument. The Burkean material obviously matters, but don't burn 500 words summarizing Burke — I've read enough Burke for one lifetime. And go easy on the Chesterton cosplay; a little of that goes a long way. Also I've been thinking about this alot, so if you spot a distinction I'm missing — say, between fences that keep something out and rules that hold a system in a shape — throw it in.
