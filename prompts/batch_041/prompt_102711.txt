OK so every time somebody wants to explain Jain anekantavada they trot out the blind men and the elephant: a handful of guys each feel a different part, each comes back with a different description, so they're all partially right and none of them has the whole picture — hence many-sidedness, hence tolerance. It's a catchy story but I've got a nagging feeling it's dragging the doctrine somewhere it doesn't want to go. Can you do a real audit of it: what plays the role of the elephant, the blind men, the part each one grabs, and the king who supposedly sees the whole animal — then where does the mapping break? The thing I keep coming back to is that the actual view isn't "everybody's blind so everybody's equally right"; its that the object itself has endlessly many aspects, so any single claim is only true under a qualification (the syat thing), and the mistake is absolutizing one standpoint as though it were the whole. So does the story quietly smuggle in relativism, or a hidden God's-eye view (the king) that the doctrine actually denies? And does the tolerance conclusion people hang on the analogy survive once you swap the story out for what anekantavada actually says, or was the analogy doing all the heavy lifting the whole time? Skip reading lists and citations, I just want the audit.
