I run research at a small shop that sells macro and political-risk outlooks, mostly to funds and a couple of insurers who want to know a few years ahead which countries are drifting toward serious trouble. For about a year we've been leaning on Turchin's structural-demographic stuff — cliodynamics, secular cycles, elite overproduction, the whole picture where popular immiseration, too many elites chasing too few positions, state fiscal strain, and elite infighting pile up over decades and then some spark sets it off. It's been great for storytelling and much less great when a client asks what we actually did to get to a call. So what I want from you is a decision procedure my analysts can run on a country, roughly quarterly, in about two days, using only public numbers, news, and their own reading: a procedure that ends in one of a small number of verdicts — something like 'structurally stable', 'pressure building', 'pre-crisis window open' — rather than a score out of a hundred. Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, which steps are allowed to force a stop, and which steps are genuinely judgment calls that a person has to own rather than something the sheet decides. It has to survive contact with a client who has no background in any of this, and with our head of research, who signs off and will absolutely ask why step four isn't just vibes.

Two things I keep going back and forth on and I'd like you to actually take a position on. First, the theory tells you a society is getting brittle but is famously silent on what lights the match, so how do we handle triggers without pretending we can forecast them — is there a defensible way to keep 'pressure' and 'spark' seperate inside a checklist, or does that just relocate all the real work into an unmarked judgment call? Second, my colleague Priya thinks the honest move is to refuse the quantification altogether, because a scoring sheet turns a contested model into something that looks like a thermometer. If she's right, say so plainly instead of handing me a tidy-looking checklist; I'd rather hear it now than after we've sold three more quarters of outlooks off the back of it. And be straight with me about what this thing can't do: where it will misfire, which of the four drivers it handles worst, and what would have to happen for us to throw it out.

Then run the procedure end to end on two cases so I can see whether it earns its keep. Case A: bottom-half real wages flat for fifteen years while GDP grew steadily, the number of law and journalism graduates has roughly tripled with no new jobs for them, debt service now eats about a third of state revenue, both major parties have split since 2019, and there have been three large street protests — and yet nothing has actually broken, and it's been like this for eight years. Case B: incomes rising, state finances fine, no visible elite split, but three weeks ago the government postponed elections indefinitely and a mineral-rich region has started talking about autonomy. My gut says B is the one to worry about this year; I suspect your procedure will tell me B is structurally fine and that what I'm staring at is exactly the spark it was never built to see. Show me your work on both, including whatever the procedure forces you to say about A that I won't like, and mark the places where an analyst has to overrule the sheet.
