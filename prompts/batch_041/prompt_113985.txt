I write about cognitive science and AI, and I keep encountering the claim that a system—usually a large language model, sometimes a brain region or an animal—represents a proposition or understands a sentence because it can answer questions, track roles, or bind features. The philosophical worry I want to operationalize is the one behind Bradley’s regress and the unity of the proposition: attributing a unified content seems to require a relation that binds the parts, but then that relation itself must be bound to the parts, and so on. I need a reusable procedure for deciding when to attribute a unified propositional content to a system, rather than merely a collection of associated items. Write it as a checklist a journalist can run in an interview or while reading a paper, with questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a negative verdict, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is interpretive rather than empirical. It should handle cases where the system’s behavior is statistically robust but the internal story is a black box. Then run it on three cases: a transformer that answers multi-hop questions about a story; a split-brain patient who can report one object but not another; and a multimodal model that captions images with subject-verb-object triples. Say which steps the procedure cannot settle, and include one case where it returns an answer a journalist will not like.
