(c) A classmate claims the problem evaporates if we define a predicate "gred": something is gred just in case it is green when first examined before 1990 and red when first examined after. STUDENT ANSWER: Goodman's riddle starts from the fact that our evidence about emeralds — that every one examined so far is green — can be redescribed in a vocabulary that predicts something else. Call a thing grue if it is examined before some future time t and is green, or examined after t and is blue. Every emerald we've seen is both green and grue, so "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" have exactly the same evidence behind them, yet they disagree about the first emerald examined after t: one says green, the other blue. The symmetry isn't accidental — grue and bleen can be defined from green and blue and green and blue can be defined from grue and bleen, so the evidence on its own doesn't privilege either vocabulary. Goodman's answer is that the difference lies in the predicates rather than in the evidence: green is a natural predicate that marks a real kind in nature, whereas grue is an artificial gerrymander cooked up to fit the data, and only the former can appear in genuine laws. He cashes this out through entrenchment — green has been projected in countless inductions before and grue hasn't, so the habit of projection favours green — which shows the riddle is a real problem for anyone who thinks confirmation is a purely formal relation between evidence and hypothesis. One qualification: the entrenchment reply looks circular, since the only reason green is more entrenched is that we've been using it, which is the very practice the riddle was supposed to test. Goodman seems to accept this and ends up saying we can't justify our inductive practice, only note that it's ours. On the classmate's gred: I think they're right that it defuses the worry. The riddle only bites when the switch point is in the future, because that's the only case where the two hypotheses disagree about something we haven't yet seen. Once the switch point is in the past, gred has no future content of its own, so it's no longer a rival to "all emeralds are Greenland", which suggests the puzzle is really about our ignorance of the future rather than about the predicates at all.
