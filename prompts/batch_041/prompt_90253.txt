I am a PhD student in computer science working on algorithmic fairness, and our reading group has been stuck for months on the same exchange. One side treats the impossibility results—calibration and equalized odds cannot both hold except in degenerate cases when base rates differ, and similarly for predictive parity and equal opportunity—as a fundamental limit, concluding that any deployed system must trade one injustice against another. The other side treats those results as artifacts of an impoverished formulation: they assume a fixed predictor, a binary protected attribute, and a binary decision, and they ignore causal structure, individual fairness, and the fact that the decision threshold is itself a choice. Neither side moves. I want a diagnosis rather than another survey. Is this a substantive disagreement about the mathematics or its interpretation, a verbal dispute about the word fairness, or two groups answering different questions under one heading—one about the properties of a predictor, one about the justice of an allocation? My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not about the theorems but about what the theorems are taken to be about, and that the missing distinction is between fairness as a constraint on a model and fairness as a constraint on a decision policy, including the threshold and the utility function that turn scores into allocations. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress: a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
