For a couple of years now I've been locked in an argument with a friend who insists that the cyclical view of history — the whole family of theories, from the classical constitutional-cycle writers to the grand twentieth-century systems, that see societies rising and falling in recurring patterns — is nothing but the observer's taste for symmetry projected onto a fundamentally linear process. I've ended up defending a deliberately deflationary version of the cyclical picture, and I'd like you to help me see how exposed it actually is. The version I hold gives up the grand claims: no fixed period, no cosmic law, no suggestion that any particular episode has to recur on schedule, with the timing and severity of each one set by local contingencies. What I do claim is that a recurring and non-accidental dynamic is at work — that the very features which make an order stable and successful (a high capacity for coordinated action, low internal conflict, a broad sense among the governed that the arrangement is legitimate) tend to erode precisely as they succeed, because success lowers the cost of defection and raises the return to capturing the apparatus of the state; and because orders that resist the concentration of power tend to be outcompeted by those that permit it, competitive selection itself pushes societies toward the fragile configuration. The upshot is a sawtooth of consolidation and fracture rather than a pendulum, and it should show up in any society with enough internal complexity to concentrate power. My private worry is that this may be either a real insight or merely a dressed-up version of "institutions decay and are replaced," and I can't tell which from the inside.

Please tell me what would count against this position: which observations, arguments, historical episodes, or formal results should move me, and what the view predicts that a plain linear rival does not. Rank the considerations from most to least decisive, and be explicit about which of them a committed defender of the cyclical picture would concede as counting against them versus dismiss out of hand. Give the response as exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion — ordered from the defeater that should most move me to the one that should least, each item a sentence or two. Within each item, state whether the point is settled or genuinely contested among people who work on this, and whether it is something a defender would take as a hit or wave away; make the fifth item your honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that counts as a defect for a view of this kind or is simply the price of any large-scale claim about history. Where the people who actually study this disagree, say so rather than presenting a contested judgment as the consensus — I would rather have the genuine fault lines than a tidy story.
