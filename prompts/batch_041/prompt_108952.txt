I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether beauty is objective, and the argument kept returning to an analogy he would not give up. His view is that aesthetic judgment is like wine tasting. He concedes that there is no fact of the matter written into the wine, but says that is the wrong place to look for a standard. Palates differ in delicacy, training sharpens them, and people who taste seriously and repeatedly converge far more than beginners do; when they disagree, he says, we treat the disagreement as a fact about the taster rather than about the wine. He appeals to the story Hume retells from Don Quixote, in which Sancho's kinsmen taste from a cask and detect leather and iron that the others miss, and are vindicated when the cask is finally emptied. He also claims Hume already showed how to have it both ways: taste is sentiment, yet the joint verdict of the true judges supplies the rule. His conclusion is that beauty is objective in the only sense worth wanting — there is a better and a worse judgment, and the better one is the one made by the properly prepared judge.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than another round of the shouting. Please work through the following.

1. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the palate, the wine, the training, the competition, the taster's prior expectations, the other tasters in the room, and the cask at the bottom.

2. Say where the mapping breaks, and rank the breaks by how much they matter to his conclusion. I already suspect one break and want to know whether it is the load-bearing one: the kinsmen's delicacy is vindicated by a procedure that has nothing to do with taste, namely emptying the cask and looking. Nothing in the aesthetic case corresponds to that step. So the analogy may be borrowing its epistemic authority from a verification it cannot run. Is that right, and if so, how much of his position does it cost him?

3. Consider a disanalogy I have not seen raised in these arguments. A palate is a measuring instrument with a noise floor: below some threshold of difference, judgments are not reports of a subtle quality but noise. If that is so, the analogy predicts that certain aesthetic disputes are unresolvable in principle rather than merely unresolved — not because there is no fact, but because no judge could reliably detect one. Does that strengthen his case, by explaining away persistent disagreement, or undermine it, by making the convergence he relies on an artifact of easy cases?

4. Consider the aim. Tasting has a fixed and largely uncontested purpose, namely to detect what is in the glass. Art has contested purposes — pleasure, formal interest, truth, moral instruction, the articulation of a form of life. Does the analogy survive once the purpose is plural, or does the whole notion of a better palate presuppose a single thing the palate is for?

5. Separate the disanalogies that bear on his conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, and tell me plainly whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table.

6. If a better analogy is available — from measurement, from testimony, or from expertise in a domain whose aims are contested — say what it is, what it maps onto what, and what it buys that the wine-tasting picture does not.
