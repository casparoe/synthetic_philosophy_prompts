You are a historian of early modern science and a philosopher of science, with specialist knowledge of Kepler's astrology and of witchcraft prosecution in the Holy Roman Empire. Work carefully and in stages: open with a short preliminary section in which you state, in your own words, what you take Kepler's theory of astrological causation to be and what you take the legal and theological structure of a Württemberg witchcraft case to be, so that the analysis that follows is explicit about the theory it is applying. Argue from your own knowledge rather than looking anything up, and flag the points where your knowledge is thin.

I want the following episode analyzed through the lens of Kepler's own astrology, not through modern skepticism about astrology.

The episode: in 1615 Katharina Kepler, Johannes Kepler's mother, a widow in her late sixties living in Leonberg, was accused of witchcraft by a neighbor, Ursula Reinbold, with whom she had had a business dispute; Reinbold claimed Katharina had given her a bitter drink that made her ill. The accusation was taken up by the local magistrate, Lutherus Einhorn, who in his years in office prosecuted a number of women for sorcery and sent several to be burned. Katharina was arrested, then released; she was arrested again in 1620 and held for well over a year in Güglingen, guarded day and night, in chains for much of the time. Johannes, by then imperial mathematician, took over her defense, corresponded with legal and theological advisers, and traveled to be present. She was shown the instruments of torture and told what would be done to her; she refused to confess, and was released and acquitted in 1621. She died within a year. The trial cost Johannes more than two years of his salary, partly because the costs of her detention were charged to her family. The charges against her were of the ordinary neighborhood kind: a harmful drink, a curse, a look, a reputation for knowing things she should not know.

The lens: Kepler held that celestial configurations, and especially the geometrical relations among the planets that astrologers call aspects, act on the sublunary world and on the human soul in particular; that the soul, being itself geometrical, responds to these configurations by a kind of harmony; that the effect is on temperament, inclination, passion, and imagination; and that the stars incline rather than compel. He also insisted on a distinction between a reformed, physically grounded astrology and the judicial astrology that claims to read particular fates, and he spent a good deal of energy arguing against those who inflated astrology's predictive reach, including in his quarrel with Helisaeus Roeslin over the new star of 1604, where Kepler entered as a third party to a pamphlet dispute. He is often summarized as having likened astrology to a foolish daughter of astronomy who must nevertheless be kept because she earns her mother's bread.

What I want:

1. The mapping. Under Kepler's theory, who and what are the causal players in this case? What is supposed to be acting on what, through what medium, and with what degree of necessity? What would count as evidence for and against a claim of maleficent causation, on his principles? Be disciplined: say what the theory actually licenses and where it is simply silent, rather than filling the gaps with generic early modern background.

2. The collision. The prosecution needed a causal story connecting a word, a look, or a drink to a real illness. Kepler's defense, as far as it is recorded, tends to dissolve the causal claim: old women's talk, no natural mechanism by which a spoken curse injures, ordinary causes for the illness, interested accusers, irregular procedure. That is a defense any skeptic could mount. But it sits uneasily with a theory on which the soul is genuinely moved by celestial configurations and on which the imagination is a natural power capable of acting on bodies. On Kepler's own principles, is there a principled reason why celestial configurations can move a soul but one soul's imagination cannot move another person's body? If the boundary is not drawn by the physics, what draws it — theology, the pact with the devil, the law of proof, or something about who is entitled to practice natural knowledge? Notice that the prosecution's own reasoning may have been partly naturalistic, so that Kepler's naturalism was not automatically exculpating.

3. The return direction. What does this episode show about Kepler's astrology as a theory? Is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated? Consider, and adjudicate between, at least these readings: that the trial forced Kepler into a position more skeptical than his published astrology licenses; that his defense is fully consistent with a naturalistic astrology that denies only the demonic pact and not natural influence; and that the episode reveals his reformed astrology to be doing boundary work of a social kind, separating the respectable natural philosopher's astrology from the cunning woman's or the witch's magic, rather than a purely physical kind.

4. An honest accounting of the limits. Say where the theory is unhelpful, where the historical record will not bear the weight of the analysis, and what further evidence would settle the question you cannot settle.

On the epistemic status of your claims: separate throughout what is settled among people who know this material from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Treat the following as contested rather than settled: how far the surviving defense materials represent Kepler's considered philosophical position; whether the trial altered his views on astrology; whether his astrology contains a coherent boundary between natural and demonic causation; and how much this episode can tell us about his astrology at all. Where you are speculating, say that you are speculating, and say what would change your mind.

Do not cite sources, and do not supply a reading list. Do not invent titles, dates, or quotations; if you are unsure of a specific title or date, describe the work instead of naming it, and if you want to convey a formulation of Kepler's, paraphrase it and say that you are paraphrasing rather than producing a quotation. The deliverable is an essay in the four numbered parts above, written for a reader who knows the standard story about Kepler and the trial and wants the parts that the standard story leaves out.
