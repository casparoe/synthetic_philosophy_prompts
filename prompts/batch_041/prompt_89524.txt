I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences, and I am drafting this term's final examination. The unit is on path dependence, and I want a single essay question that cannot be passed by reciting the standard keyboard anecdote or by gesturing at increasing returns. To force the issue, I have written a case to be printed on the paper, and I want the question built on that case rather than on anything the students may already have read.

The case, in outline: a region adopted one of two incompatible technical standards very early, at a moment when the choice was driven by local conditions — terrain, say, or the cost of raw materials — and when neither standard was clearly superior in general. Over the following decades the early choice attracted complementary investments: trained workers, tooling, spare parts, and regulation. The rival standard survived only in a few enclaves. With hindsight the winning standard looks clumsy, and engineers periodically argue that the region would have been better off had the other one prevailed. A neighbouring region, facing broadly similar conditions but starting later, settled on the alternative and has been content with it.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable in roughly seventy-five minutes and should require students to do three things. First, say what would have to be true for the region's situation to be a case of genuine lock-in rather than efficient selection of a standard that happened to have real advantages. Second, distinguish the case from three look-alikes that students routinely conflate with path dependence: mere sensitivity to initial conditions; an ordinary coordination failure that could in principle be corrected; and an outcome that would have converged on the same result anyway under selection pressure. Third, take a position on whether the lock-in claim, as usually stated, is falsifiable, and on whether unfalsifiability would make it vacuous.

I would also like the question to make use of a distinction I plan to introduce in the lecture but which the students will not have seen applied to this material: the contrast between ergodic and non-ergodic processes — roughly, a process is non-ergodic when its long-run outcome depends on the particular path actually taken rather than averaging out across the paths that were possible, so that time-averages and ensemble-averages come apart. I want the model answer to say whether lock-in is best understood as non-ergodicity, or whether that equation obscures something important.

For the marking scheme: give five must-hit points, a mark for each, totalling twenty-five, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates miss it — for instance, treating any historical influence whatsoever as path dependence, or assuming that an inefficient outcome by itself proves lock-in. Separate a first-class answer from a solid second-class one by naming the specific moves that only the first makes.

Finally, in a short closing note, tell me how confident you are in the model answer's central claim about lock-in and non-ergodicity, and identify the single point in it you are least sure of.
