In preparing a graduate seminar on the ethics of deception, I have found that my students can rehearse the standard opposition between Kant's notorious verdict on the murderer at the door and a broadly consequentialist permission to lie, but that they lose the thread as soon as both interlocutors accept that the person in hiding should be misdirected. I want you to write the dialogue they cannot: a sustained exchange between two Kantians who agree on that verdict but disagree about what it reveals concerning the duty of veracity itself.

Let the first speaker hold what I will call the forfeiture view: veracity is a strict duty owed to rational beings as such, and the murderer's question is not a genuine request for information but an instrument of his wrongful end, so that a false reply does not wrong him and arguably is not a lie at all, since he has placed himself outside the relation within which truth is owed. Let the second hold what I will call the exception view: veracity really is a duty that is here violated, but it is a duty that can be outweighed, and the permission is a genuine exception rather than a redescription of the act; the first speaker, on this account, is engaged in a kind of moral bookkeeping that spares us the discomfort of admitting we sometimes do what we ought not.

Have each press the other on the cases where the two views visibly diverge. These should include at least: a questioner who asks the same question with no wrongful intent, so that there is no instrumentality to appeal to; a case in which the false reply is disbelieved and therefore deceives no one; a case in which the truth would not actually aid the wrongdoer because the intended victim is already beyond reach; and the question of what each view implies about false statements made under oath or to a lawful magistrate. Require each speaker to answer the other's strongest point rather than a convenient version of it, and to concede at least one genuine cost of their own position. Let them reach at least one real point of agreement before a final statement of what still divides them, and let neither be a fool.

Keep the exchange under nine hundred words, and begin in the middle of the argument rather than with an exchange of pleasantries.
