I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on ethics and economic life, and I'm writing a short argumentative essay on Keynes's conception of the good life. I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on both moral philosophy and the history of economic thought, and to construct the argument for me. The seminar has paired Keynes's memoir My Early Beliefs, in which he describes the outlook he and his Cambridge friends took from Moore's Principia Ethica — roughly, that the only things good in themselves are certain states of mind, such as the enjoyment of beauty, of understanding, and of other people, while actions are good only as means to such states — with his 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren and the closing chapter of the General Theory, where he looks ahead to a time when the economic problem has been solved and suggests that the remaining task will be to work out how to use the freedom that abundance brings, and worries that people have been shaped for so long by the need to strive that they may not know how to enjoy what they have won.

The position I'd like you to defend, and to make as strong as it can honestly be made, is that Keynes's mature conception of the good life is self-undermining. The thought — it came from a friend in the seminar and I haven't been able to settle it either way — is that the later Keynes quietly treats a certain kind of activity, namely purposive striving and the exercise of freedom, as itself a constituent of the good life rather than merely a means to enjoyable states; but the same texts also insist that the goods worth having are states of mind that we can only enjoy when they are not themselves the object of effort. On this reading, the more completely the economic problem is solved, the less there is to strive for, and the less able anyone is to be in the condition the Moorean goods require; abundance removes the conditions of the good life instead of delivering it. It may help to sharpen the contrast as one between a possession model of the good life, on which a life is good to the extent that it contains certain experiences, and an exercise model, on which a life is good to the extent that it is a certain kind of activity, and then to argue that Keynes cannot have both at once.

Before you commit to anything, spend a few sentences laying out the two readings in your own words so that I can see you have the material straight. Then give me the argument in continuous prose and in the voice of someone defending the thesis rather than surveying a literature; I don't want a reading list, citations, or quotations — please don't invent quotations from Keynes or Moore, and if you're unsure of some detail of their texts, say so rather than reconstructing it — and I don't need a summary of what commentators have said. After the positive case, state the strongest objection you can think of, for example that Keynes's remarks about striving and leisure are psychological predictions about how people will behave rather than claims about what is valuable, and then answer it on the thesis's behalf. Think carefully about the difference between what is valuable as a means and what is valuable in itself, and aim for something an advanced seminar would find genuinely contestable rather than a restatement of the standard view.
