Alright — I'm taking a longtermism module this term (final year philosophy) and I've basically been converted to the hinge of history idea: that we're living in the most influential period there has ever been, that the next few decades have more leverage over the long-run future than any century that came before, and that this makes what we do now matter in a way that nothing else ever did. I want you to try and break it for me, properly, and not with the standard line that every generation thinks it's special.

So: what would actually count against it? What would have to be true about the world for the claim to be false, and which of those things are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones? Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and definately be honest about which ones a defender of the view would already concede instead of waving away.

Things that nag at me, which I'd like you to take seriously along the way:

The claim needs a measure of influence or it isn't a claim at all. Say the hinge is whenever a small perturbation — one person, a modest budget, a few months of effort — makes the biggest difference to expected long-run value. Is that well-defined, or does it just shift around with whichever counterfactual baseline you pick? Can the view come out true under one operationalisation and false under another, and if so is that fatal, or just a fact about how to state it?

Earlier times have more branching (more ways the future could go), later times have more power (more capacity to shape the whole future). Which of those should count as influence, and does that settle the question? If it's the second, isn't the hinge almost certainly in the future rather than now — more people, better technology, and the chance to lock things in for good? Doesn't the future-is-bigger point that longtermists themselves like to make cut against the hinge being now?

Historians of institutions talk about critical junctures: short windows where a choice gets locked in and path dependence does the rest. Does that support the hinge claim (the window is now!) or undercut it (there are lots of junctures, and the ones that mattered are usually only visible afterwards)?

Is the claim even about epochs? If influence is really concentrated in a few individuals at particular moments, then it could be true of some people and false of the rest of us, which is a strange thing to assert of a whole generation.

And the self-fulfilling bit: if enough of us believe we're at the hinge, we act as if we are, which may raise our actual leverage. Does that make the claim partly true by assertion, and does it make it unfalsifiable in a way I should worry about? If it's been qualified into something no observation could touch — by adding, say, that it's about expected influence under our uncertainty — is that a problem for this kind of view, or is it just how any claim about the future has to be?

Also, what does the hinge claim predict that a rival doesn't? Compare it with a view where influence is roughly uniform across time, and one where it's cyclical, and tell me what would show up differently in the world — in the historical record, or in how well earlier generations did when they predicted their own moment was the decisive one.

And if you conclude the view is well insulated, say so — I'd rather have that then invented defeaters. British English please.
