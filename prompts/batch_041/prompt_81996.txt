I am a high school student preparing an essay for my history and philosophy seminar. I would like you to write an essay on whether path dependence means that history is genuinely locked in, or whether the same outcomes would have arrived anyway. The essay should weigh the main arguments on both sides, but it must not end on a non-committal note; defend a verdict. To make the discussion concrete, imagine a small coastal nation that adopts a particular rule for registering land because, during a single fiscal crisis, one influential clerk prefers one format over another. Over the next century, banks, courts, and families build their practices around that format, and switching to a more efficient alternative would now require coordinated changes across all of them. Some historians say the nation is trapped by an accident; others say that any comparable society would have converged on a similar rule because of the underlying needs of commerce. In your essay, distinguish between two senses of path dependence: first, sensitivity to initial conditions, where a small early event shapes later details without necessarily making the outcome worse; and second, lock-in, where a contingent choice produces an outcome that is inferior to a feasible alternative and is costly to reverse. Then propose and apply a test for distinguishing genuine lock-in from mere sensitivity. For example, you might ask whether a feasible alternative existed at the time, whether the chosen path is inferior by a defensible measure, and whether reversal costs are high enough that no individual actor has an incentive to switch. Also consider whether the persistence is driven by increasing returns, where each additional user makes the convention more valuable to others, or by sunk costs, where past investment matters only because it would be lost upon switching. Use these ideas to evaluate the hypothetical nation. Finally, reach a conclusion about whether the nation's rule is a genuine lock-in or a case where the same broad outcome would have arrived anyway, and explain what this implies for how we should think about historical explanation more generally. Please do not provide citations or a reading list; I am interested in the argument itself.
