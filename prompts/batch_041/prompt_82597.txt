I'm running the philosophy slot at my library's monthly reading group a week on Thursday -- 45 minutes, eight people, they mostly read novels, one is a retired engineer, nobody's done any math since school. Topic is supertasks and the toggling lamp. Dont explain the puzzle to me, I know it; I need you to plan the session.

I want to avoid the usual thing where I announce "the lamp's state is undefined", everyone nods, and nothing actually lands. My idea is to carry the whole hour on one made-up device instead of opening with the lamp: a machine with two dials, both updated at t = 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ... One dial is a ball that gets nudged a further 1/2^n metres along a track; the other is a lamp that flips on/off. At t = 1 the ball's position seems forced -- it's sitting at the end of the track -- and the lamp's seems to be nothing at all. Is that the right thing to build on, or does it smuggle in an answer? If it's right, tell me what it has to have and what it must not have; my worry is that the two-dial version makes the lamp look obviously different from the ball when the real work is saying what that difference is.

What I need: the order the ideas have to come in, and why each one depends on the one before it. I keep discovering I can't say what "the state at t = 1" means until they've got some prior distinction in place, and I don't know how to put that distinction to people who've never seen a limit. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. Ones I'm expecting: "you can't finish infinitely many steps so the whole setup is impossible"; "the answer is that the lamp is half on"; "the lamp is neither on nor off", said as if that's an answer rather than a dodge; "the switch would have to move faster and faster so it's physically impossible"; and someone saying it's just Zeno and calculus settled that. I also expect at least one person to insist there must be a fact of the matter about the lamp that we're simply not clever enough to see -- and I don't want to just tell them they're wrong, I want the sequence to have already done that work by the time they say it.

Also tell me what I should deliberately skip, and say out loud that I'm skipping it. I'm assuming anything about sequences, limits, or the difference between a limit and the value at the limit is off the table for this crowd, but tell me if that's wrong, or if there's something I think I have to include that I actually don't.

And give me one question to ask at the end that tells me whether they've got it, rather than whether they can recite that the lamp's state is undefined. Something where a person who followed the session answers differently from a person who just memorised the punchline.

No readings please -- they wont do them, and half of them would bail if I handed out a paper.
