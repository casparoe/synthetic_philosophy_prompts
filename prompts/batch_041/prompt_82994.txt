I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on Mill's political economy, and I've been asked to open a session with a thought experiment of our own devising: what Mill would have made of a universal basic income in a twenty-first-century rich democracy. The proposal we're working with is deliberately specific, so that nobody can hide behind generalities. A country of roughly forty million people pays every resident, children included, an unconditional monthly cash grant set at about a third of median income and indexed to a consumption basket rather than to GDP; the grant replaces most conditional transfers, including housing assistance, unemployment insurance and child benefits, while leaving public schooling, health care and old-age pensions untouched; and it is financed not by an income tax but by a tax on the unimproved value of land, together with a carbon dividend. The backdrop is something Mill could not have imagined in any detail: labour demand seems to be falling durably across a range of sectors, so that the old assumption of eventual reabsorption of displaced workers is at least contestable, and the grant is being sold as a response to that rather than to poverty in the ordinary sense. Assume for the sake of argument that he is brought fully up to date on the economics but keeps the moral and political outlook of his own writings, and that he died in 1873 and so never saw any of this.

I don't want a summary of Mill on socialism or a catalogue of his opinions; I want a prediction, argued from the parts of his system that would actually bear weight here, and I want you to tell me which parts those are. My own guess is that the pressure points are these: his Malthusian insistence on prudential restraint and his suspicion of relief that blunts it; the projected science of character formation he called ethology, which was meant to explain how institutions shape the desires, energies and habits of the people living under them, and which is exactly the sort of theory a permanent income guarantee would need; his distinction between the laws of production, which he took to be fixed by nature, and the distribution of wealth, which he took to be a matter of human arrangement; his hope that the labouring class would eventually cease to be a class of mere wage-earners dependent on the capital of others, and his consequent preference for transforming the relations of production through cooperative and worker-owned enterprise over redistributing income within those relations; his long-standing view that the rise in the value of land is produced by society at large rather than by the landowner and is therefore a fit object of special taxation; his fear of an uneducated and dependent majority wielding the franchise, together with the plural voting and educational qualifications he defended; and his late abandonment of the wages-fund doctrine, that doctrine which made a general and persistent surplus of labour over available employment impossible by construction. Please say what each of these commits him to, where they pull against one another, which ones would be doing the real work in his verdict, and whether there are considerations he would have treated as decisive that I have not listed at all. I am equally interested in the parts of the question he would have refused to settle from the armchair, and in what he would have said a utilitarian needed years of evidence to pronounce upon. I am not asking for references or a reading list; the reasoning is what I am after.

Finally, be candid about the limits of the exercise rather than performing ventriloquism. Distinguish the predictions his texts nearly force on him from the ones that are really extrapolations from his temperament, and mark the second sort as such. If the honest answer is that he would have had no determinate view because the question turns on empirical premises he never confronted, say so instead of manufacturing a verdict. And if any part of my framing is anachronistic or confused — if treating him as having a single view here flattens a real development in his thinking, or if the proposal I sketched assumes something about the wage relation that he would have rejected out of hand — tell me plainly rather than working around it, since I would rather know where the method of predicting a dead thinker's opinions is shakiest than receive a smooth answer that conceals the guesswork.
