I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the legitimacy of the postwar tribunals, and I want the standard objections to be weighed against one another rather than merely listed. Treat the following as a closed set of objections to the legal legitimacy of the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials, and sort them into tiers according to how much they genuinely threaten that legitimacy — from objections that, if sound, would be fatal to the whole enterprise, through ones that are serious but survivable, down to ones that are largely rhetorical. For each objection, give a brief justification of its placement and name the best reply available to a defender of the tribunals; where two objections are really the same objection in different dress, say so and rank them as a single item.

1. Retroactivity. The defendants were punished under law that did not exist when they acted, contrary to the principle that no conduct can be criminal, and no punishment imposed, absent a pre-existing prohibition.

2. Victor's justice. The tribunals were created by the victorious powers, which selected the defendants, drafted the charges, appointed the judges, and left their own conduct outside the dock.

3. Selective enforcement. Conduct by the Allies — unrestricted submarine warfare, the area bombing of cities, the treatment of prisoners on the eastern front — either fell within or closely resembled the offenses charged.

4. Superior orders. The tribunals rejected the defense of obedience to superior orders even though the domestic military law of several of the states represented at the bar had itself recognized such a defense.

5. The aggression count. Convicting leaders for a conspiracy to wage aggressive war required reading the 1928 treaty renunciation of war as having criminalized the conduct of individuals, an interpretation many jurists found strained.

6. Symmetry. If the tribunals' reasoning was sound, then a victorious Axis could, on that same reasoning, have legitimately tried Allied leaders for the bombing of Dresden and Hamburg; because we regard that counterfactual as plainly unjust, we should reject the reasoning that licenses it.

7. Procedural laxity. The tribunals are said to have admitted evidence, and applied standards of command responsibility, looser than ordinary domestic criminal process would tolerate.

Once the tiers are set, answer two further questions. First, if only one objection could be pressed, which would come closest on its own to showing that the tribunals were illegitimate? Second, is the strongest overall defense of the tribunals (a) to concede some objections and defend legitimacy on the remaining ground, (b) to grant that they were legally irregular but justified nonetheless, or (c) something else entirely? Give a short verdict with reasons, and be explicit about which objections a committed defender should simply accept rather than dispute.
