I am a third-year PhD student in political theory, currently writing a seminar paper on the moral equality of combatants, and I would like help auditing an analogy rather than merely reacting to it. At a workshop last week a colleague defended the doctrine with a comparison I cannot get out of my head. His line was that two opposing soldiers firing at one another are like two boxers in a ring: each has entered of his own accord, each is bound by the same rules, each is permitted to strike the other, and neither becomes a wrongdoer simply by throwing a punch, while the referee and the crowd exist to keep the contest fair rather than to take a side. From this he concluded that the received view -- that only the combatant on the just side acts permissibly and that his adversary does something wrong merely by fighting -- is a confusion imported from morality outside the ring, and that the moral equality of combatants falls straight out of the picture. I am not persuaded, but I do not want to dismiss the point with the usual slogans; I want to know precisely what work the analogy is doing and whether it can bear the weight he places on it.

Please give me a thorough audit. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly, in whatever form is clearest -- what plays the role of the boxers, the ring, the rules, the referee, the consent, the purse, the crowd, the decision -- and be candid about which of those have any genuine counterpart in war at all. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. I am especially interested in what the analogy quietly imports that war may lack: symmetric and revocable consent, a shared willingness to accept the verdict, the fact that both boxers want the same thing and can walk away, and the absence of third parties whose rights are at stake without their having entered anything. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion -- the moral equality of combatants -- survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along; and if it was, whether some better analogy governs the relation between two opposing combatants, or whether the relation resists analogizing altogether.
