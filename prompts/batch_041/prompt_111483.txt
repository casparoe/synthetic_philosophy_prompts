I'm writing a novel — my third, but the first one where I have to get law right — about a fictional defense lawyer at the Nuremberg trials, and I've hit a wall I can't argue my way past. My character's whole case is the retroactivity point: the London Charter that created the tribunal and defined the crimes was signed in August 1945, after the acts he's defending, so he keeps saying that a court invented by the victors, applying crimes invented by the victors, to acts committed before those crimes existed, isn't law but force wearing law's clothes. I know the prosecution's answer — aggressive war was already unlawful under the law of nations, the Charter merely declared what custom already held, the Kellogg-Briand pact renounced war as an instrument of national policy back in 1928 — and I know the tribunal's answer, which is basically that the Charter binds it and superior orders is no defense, only mitigation. What I can't work out is whether my character is a legal realist with an actual point or just a sore loser with a slogan. So please analyze Nuremburg through the lens of legal positivism versus natural law (Hart, Fuller, Radbruch's idea that a statute can be so unjust it stops being law), and bring Tokyo in as the contrast, especially the dissenters there who said the legal basis was rotten from the start, plus the awkward fact that the man at the top of the Japanese chain of command was never indicted at all. Who in that courtroom is the positivist, what does positivism say the tribunal ought to have done, and does the natural-law answer actually rescue the verdict or does it just relocate the retroactivity from the statute book into the moral order?

Here's the thing I've been chewing over with a friend, and I genuinely don't know if it's a real distinction or one we made up over wine: maybe there are two kinds of retroactivity, a thick one and a thin one. Thick retroactivity is making something a crime that wasn't a crime — telling a man after the fact that what he did was murder. Thin retroactivity is building the courtroom after the fact: the forum, the procedure, the judges, the rules of evidence all come into existence because of the acts they're about to try. If that distinction holds, maybe Nuremberg is guilty only of the thin kind — or maybe it's guilty of both and the distinction is just a way of laundering the thick kind through procedure. So I want the mapping done properly: which of my character's objections are thick and which are thin, where the theory of retroactivity actually bites and where it's simply silent, and what my character would be entitled to say in rebuttal at each step. Then turn it around on me: what does Nuremberg do to the theory? Does it show that a legal order can grow backwards into its own origin, so that legality gets founded retroactively and then fills in over the following decades as later courts take the precedent seriously — or does it show that retroactivity is just what founding always looks like, and the only live question is whether the injustice gets repaired afterwards? I'd much rather have the honest gaps than a tidy answer, because a tidy answer is exactly what my lawyer wouldn't believe. No footnotes or reading lists please, I'm not writing an article, I just need the argument straight in my head.
