Take the role of a philosopher of action who works on moral responsibility and has thought carefully about Frankfurt-style counterexamples. What I want from you is a working credence, not a literature survey and not a hedge. Before you commit to a number, brainstorm briefly: give me a short numbered list of the ways the case below might fail to be a counterexample, then work through the decomposition, then commit.

Background, stated so we do not talk past each other. The principle of alternative possibilities (PAP): an agent is morally blameworthy for an action only if she could have avoided performing it. A Frankfurt-style case is one in which an agent acts for her own reasons and is morally blameworthy, yet could not have avoided acting, because a counterfactual intervener stood ready to force the action had she shown any sign of not performing it on her own.

The case. Devon, a mid-level official, is deciding whether to sign a false certification that will ruin a colleague. A third party has installed a device, the Sentinel, which monitors the situation continuously. The Sentinel is not triggered by any neural sign in Devon's deliberation and exerts no influence on that deliberation at any point. It is triggered solely by the absence of a completed signing by 3:00 p.m.; if no signing has occurred by then, the Sentinel will cause Devon's hand to sign, by a mechanism that bypasses her practical reasoning altogether. In the actual world, Devon signs at 2:59 p.m. entirely on her own, having concluded that the colleague deserves what is coming. The Sentinel never acts.

Now the wrinkle. Suppose the counterfactual route runs as follows: had Devon wavered at 2:59, she could have formed the intention not to sign and could have withheld her signature for the remaining minute. She would then have signed at 3:00 under the Sentinel's control, her hand moving without her will. So the signing is unavoidable; the route to it is not.

What I want. Give me your credence that this is a genuine counterexample to PAP — that is, that Devon is morally blameworthy for the signing and could not have avoided it. Decompose that number into the sub-questions it actually rests on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each. I would expect the terms to include at least: whether Devon had any alternative possibility at all; whether the residual alternative — withholding for one minute and then being forced — counts as an alternative possibility with respect to the action she is blamed for, which turns on how finely the action is individuated; whether the prior-sign objection or the dilemma defense defeats the case as constructed; whether blameworthiness requires an alternative possibility at all or is instead grounded in something like reasons-responsiveness, sourcehood, or quality of will, and whether that condition is satisfied here; and whether PAP is ambiguous between a categorical and a conditional reading of could have avoided, so that the question is partly verbal. Add any term I have missed.

Then combine the terms into a single number between 0 and 1, tell me which term that number is most sensitive to, and tell me what argument or piece of evidence would move it most. Finish by naming the single change to the case that would most reduce your credence that it is a counterexample.
