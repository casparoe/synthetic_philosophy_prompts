In my philosophy of mind seminar, a discussion of epiphenomenalism spilled over into an argument between two classmates after class, and since I'd stayed out of it they've asked me to referee. I'd like a verdict that goes through their exchange claim by claim rather than a general essay on the topic.

Maya opened with this: if experiences never cause anything, then my conviction that I'm now seeing red was not caused by the redness. The neural state that produced the conviction would have produced it whether or not any experience came along with it. So the conviction gives me no evidence that the experience exists — and since convictions of that kind are the only evidence anyone ever has for consciousness, epiphenomenalism saws off the branch it's sitting on.

Dev replied that this confuses causing with indicating. A state can be a trustworthy sign of something without being acted on by it: the belief is caused by the neural state, the neural state reliably accompanies the experience, so the belief reliably signals the experience. Reliability is what licenses the inference, not causation.

Maya said that only pushes the problem back. On epiphenomenalist grounds the correlation is a brute coincidence with no explanation, so the "reliability" is cosmic luck. Worse, it doesn't even fix what the belief is about. The belief seems to be about the redness, not about the neurons; but if the redness never enters the process that forms the belief, nothing in that process can latch onto redness rather than onto the neural state that did enter it. At best the belief is evidence for a brain state, and Dev is left saying his belief happens to point at something it has no connection to.

Dev answered that representation doesn't require causation — lawful covariation can be enough for one state to carry information about another, and that's all "aboutness" needs. Maya replied that covariation only tells you the belief tracks whatever is always present alongside the neurons, and if the experience and the neural state are inseparable by hypothesis, nothing in the believer's position distinguishes a belief about the experience from a belief about the neurons. Dev then said he was willing to accept that the belief is really about the neural state and that "experience" is just a misleading label for it; Maya said that isn't epiphenomenalism anymore, it's eliminativism, and it concedes her point by giving up on the experience as the thing known.

Please adjudicate move by move — rate each of the main claims from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Say which party, if either, is right about the self-undermining charge, identify what each of them is missing (I suspect there's a distinction they're both sliding past, but I can't put my finger on it), and tell me who has the better of the exchange overall and on what crux it turns.
