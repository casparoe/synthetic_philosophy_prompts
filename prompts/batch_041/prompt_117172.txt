I am head of methods at a national food-standards agency, and two of our products put the same sign-off under strain. I want one procedure that covers both.

The first is an attribution report. A foodborne outbreak is traced to one of a closed list of sixty possible sources — a supplier, a batch, a handling step — and the design of the investigation guarantees that exactly one of the sixty is responsible. My analysts test each candidate separately and can rule it out at 99 per cent confidence. Every one of the sixty exclusions is therefore individually excellent, and the sixty of them together cannot all be right, because one candidate must be the source. Investigators want to publish the list of exclusions as a finding, since each exclusion is sound on its own. I want a rule that says when a set of individually sound exclusions may be asserted as a set, and what may be said once the set has grown too large to be safe.

The second is our annual assurance report, in which we state forty numbered findings about the state of the food supply, each cleared at 95 per cent confidence on the evidence we hold. The report is read as a whole — by ministers, by a public inquiry, increasingly by the courts — and each of our past five editions contained at least one finding that was later withdrawn. The accountable officer signs a single opinion covering the whole document, and I cannot get anyone to agree on what that signature commits us to: whether it asserts each finding, or all of them together, or only that the method was followed.

The two products pull in opposite directions. In the attribution report the trouble is that the claims cannot all be true at once, so accepting all of them is incoherent. In the assurance report the trouble is that the claims are almost certainly not all true at once, yet the report is worthless if every finding is hedged, and we would look dishonest if we did not admit somewhere that a document of this size will contain an error. We issue two or three attribution reports a year and one assurance report; the same officer signs both, often in the same week, and I do not want two standards.

Please write the procedure. It has to be something an analyst who has never read any philosophy of probability or of belief can run in under an hour, written as numbered steps with decision points and with the record to be kept for each claim. It must not contain the names of any theorists or works: I want the machinery, not the literature.

The procedure should do the following.

1. Give the questions to be asked, in order, of each candidate claim, and say what counts as a sufficient answer to each.
2. Say where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the matter to a person to decide.
3. State explicitly what becomes of the step from accepting each of several claims to accepting their conjunction, and defend either keeping it or giving it up.
4. Give a rule for claims that rest on the same evidence. In practice many of our findings share a laboratory method, a sampling frame, or a single expert panel, and I suspect this is where the arithmetic misleads us most often.
5. Give rules for what the sign-off may and may not say: whether the assurance report may state an expected number of errors, and whether the attribution report may name a source or only narrow the field.
6. Give rules for revision — what happens to the rest of an accepted set when one member is later shown false — and impose the constraint that the same evidence must not support opposite verdicts in two documents issued in the same month.
7. Say how thresholds are set where the costs are asymmetric, since failing to name a contaminated source and failing to flag a safety problem are not the same error, without letting anyone choose the threshold that yields the finding they wanted.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the sixty-candidate attribution report at 99 per cent each; the forty-finding assurance report at 95 per cent each; and a third in which twelve findings all rest on one method, so that confidence in the set collapses and the procedure obliges the officer to strike findings that are very probably true. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — I expect it to be the attribution report — and say plainly what I would have to give up in order to have a procedure that works.

Finish by stating what the procedure cannot do, which of its steps are genuine arithmetic and which are disguised judgement, and which bullet it bites: either that separately acceptable claims need not be jointly acceptable, or that a signatory may accept a set while expecting one of its members to be false, or some better option if you can find one.
