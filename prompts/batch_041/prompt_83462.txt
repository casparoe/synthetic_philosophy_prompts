I am assembling a casebook on the ethics of work and leisure for an undergraduate seminar, and I need a satirical piece that will make students argue about the assumptions behind slogans they already know. Please write a leaked internal strategy memo, roughly 800 words, from the fictional consultancy Ladder & Sons to the board of the Republic of Industria, which has spent decades trying to decide who has work and leisure right. The memo should be deadpan and professional, with a subject line, a brief executive summary, a numbered set of recommendations, and a closing next-steps section. It should advocate a policy called the Calling-Adjusted Workweek. Under this policy, the statutory 35-hour week is replaced by a vocational ledger: each worker's hours are weighted by a Calling Multiplier, so labor performed in a spirit of worldly asceticism counts as less than labor performed for mere wages; any shortfall against the 35-hour baseline is recorded as vocational debt and may be repaid through discretionary devotion, including unpaid overtime, weekend upskilling, and a voluntary martyrdom exchange on which firms purchase karoshi offsets to meet their annual productivity covenants. The memo should also propose that death from overwork be reclassified as terminal vocation and counted as a non-wage contribution to GDP, and that the 35-hour week survive only as a leisure subsidy for workers who can demonstrate, through a spiritual audit, that their rest is itself productive of future calling. The memo must never break character; it should present every recommendation as reasonable, evidence-based, and humane.

The humor should come from getting the underlying ideas right: the Calvinist notion of a worldly calling, the suspicion that leisure is idleness or theft, the statutory 35-hour week as a supposed civilizational bargain, and karoshi as the dark limit case of work-as-worship. Do not name or quote any real living person, and do not cite real books or articles. The piece should be legible to a reader who has never heard of the Protestant work ethic or karoshi, yet a specialist in the sociology of work should recognize that the author understands the material and find the satire fair. Keep the irony deadpan; no winking asides. End with a recommendation that follows the memo's own logic to a conclusion the reader will find appalling but that the memo presents as a best practice. Write in American English.
