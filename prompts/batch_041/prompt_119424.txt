I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about religious accommodation by going through cases rather than by choosing a doctrine, and the results fit no label I know. I would let a Sikh pupil carry a small ceremonial dagger to a state school if the blade were sealed, and I would let a Muslim pupil wear a headscarf there; I would let a teacher wear a large cross, though I feel the pull the other way. I think a public hospital is wrong to forbid a nurse to wear a headscarf, and I think a state that bans face coverings in every public space is wrong even though the ban is written to apply to everyone alike. Yet a state school that requires every pupil to remove conspicuous religious signs in class strikes me as defensible, and a religious community that wants to run its own schools on its own curriculum may do so provided its children can leave. I would not, however, let that same community exempt its members from an otherwise general law on the humane slaughter of animals. I have no quarrel with a state that asks new citizens to learn its language and the outline of its constitution, which I suppose makes me an assimilationist of one kind. Friends tell me the first verdicts amount to Anglo-American multiculturalism and the school-signs verdict to French laïcité, but I hold both at once, so the labels are no help; I am not asking which tradition I belong to, but what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

I have a half-formed principle and would like you to test it against the list and repair it. The principle is that a rule may be enforced against a person only if it is neutral in two senses at once: neutral in formulation, naming no religion and no practice, and neutral in incidence, falling with no disproportionate weight on the members of any one group. Where a rule is neutral in formulation but not in incidence, the burdened group has a prima facie claim to an accommodation, defeasible only by a pressing public interest that cannot be secured by any less burdensome means. I can already see one problem: the school-signs rule is neutral in formulation but falls almost wholly on Muslims and Sikhs, and I still think the school may keep it. I would like to know whether the principle can be repaired without its becoming a mere redescription of my verdicts, and what it says about a case I have not made up my mind about: a state that requires all its civil servants to be religiously neutral in dress, while letting private employers accommodate their employees as they see fit.

Please give me the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; between them the items should give me the repaired principle with the case that motivates each of its parts, the one verdict of mine the principle cannot keep, and its verdict on the civil-service case.
