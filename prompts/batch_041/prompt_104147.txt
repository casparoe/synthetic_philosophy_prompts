As the professor supervising a small foundations-of-probability group, I have one strong PhD student and roughly a year of her time to allocate to the epistemology of uncertain evidence — the cluster of problems that begins with Jeffrey conditionalization and radiates outward. I want to steer her toward the open questions that other questions depend on, rather than the ones that merely attract citations, and I would like your help in setting that agenda. Take the familiar candidates — whether Jeffrey's rule is the uniquely correct generalization of conditionalization to uncertain evidence, what to make of the fact that a sequence of uncertain updates need not commute, how much authority the rule retains once one notices that it is relative to a chosen partition, and how its credentials look when judged by accuracy rather than by a Dutch book — and add whatever question you think belongs on the list and is missing, including, if you judge it live, the question of what changes when the uncertain evidence is not about the world but about the agent's own evidential capacities. For each, tell me why it is or is not worth a year of a philosopher's attention, which other questions it would settle or leave untouched, how far it can be pushed with the tools of the field rather than with new mathematics, and what a result in the next twelve months would do to its standing. She works from first principles and has no library beyond her own head, so the agenda must stand on reasoning rather than on anyone's authority. Give me a defended ordering, with the trade-offs made explicit, not a survey.
