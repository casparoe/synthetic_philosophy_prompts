Ok so I've been reading about split-brain patients for a few months now and I've ended up with a pile of gut verdicts about which cases are one mind and which are two, and I can't get them to line up. I want the principle behind them, or a straight answer about which verdict has to go.

The classic one. Patient has the big bundle of fibers between the two halves of the brain cut, to control seizures. You flash a picture of a spoon into the left half of their visual field, so it only reaches the right hemisphere. Their left hand will reach into a bag and pick out the spoon. But ask them what they saw and they say they saw nothing, or they make something up -- the half that talks doesn't know about the spoon. My gut: one mind. Not two people sharing a skull, just one person with a weird blind spot.

Same patient, hands fighting: one hand buttons a shirt while the other unbuttons it. One mind, I think, though it's the closest I come to feeling like there's two agents in there.

The talking half, when it catches the left hand doing something it didn't order, invents a reason on the spot and reports it sincerely. One mind -- a mind lying to itself.

Blindsight: someone with damage to the visual cortex swears they can't see anything in part of their field, but make them guess whether a shape is an X or an O and they get it right way above chance. One mind.

Alien hand: the hand grabs a doorknob, the person grabs the hand with the other hand and pulls it off, says it feels like it belongs to someone else. Still one mind.

And a whole hemisphere removed: the person ends up living a pretty normal life, one mind, obviously. But then I have to say that nobody was killed by taking out half a brain, which is a problem, because if each half could be a mind, something got killed.

Now the two that break me. First: imagine two unrelated people whose brains are hooked together by a thin cable carrying only one kind of signal -- each feels the other's pain as a vague ache, no words, no images, no thoughts. Two minds. No question. Second: take the split-brain patient and hook their two halves together with a cable that carries the exact same thing, just a vague ache passing both ways. I still say one mind. Cut the cable again, still one mind, even though we're back to case one. So the only difference between my two-minds verdict and my one-mind verdict is that the second pair started out as one person. That feels like cheating.

Also worth knowing: split-brain patients can pass messages between the halves through the outside world -- left hand writes a word, eyes read it, done, the halves are talking again, just through paper. Doesn't change my verdict either way, which also worries me, because it suggests I'm not tracking anything about information flow at all.

My working theory is that I'm running two tests at once -- one about whether there's a single stream of experience, one about whether there's a single person/biography -- and they definately disagree on the split-brain case, so I have to give one up. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If I have to drop a verdict, say which and why. And tell me what the surviving principle predicts for the cases I haven't listed: hemispheres reconnected after twenty years apart, conjoined twins whose brains are joined by a band of tissue so each one feels what the other touches, and a robot body running two language models that disagree about what to do.
