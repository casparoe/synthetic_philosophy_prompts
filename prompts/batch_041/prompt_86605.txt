I'm a third-year student on a joint philosophy and social policy degree, currently taking a module on the ethics of the welfare state, and I'm writing an essay on conditionality. The essay turns on a disagreement that keeps recurring in seminars, and I would rather test it with a constructed case than with a real policy, since real examples arrive trailing too many contested empirical claims. Please work with the following two positions, stated here so that we are using the same ones. The reciprocity view: benefits are owed to those who do their part, so a sanction for non-compliance is justified only where the claimant's failure was culpable, and what an agency can prove is irrelevant to what a claimant deserves. The incentive view: a sanction is justified only by the effects of the rule under which it is imposed, so the unit of assessment is the rule and its consequences for compliance and for the scheme's survival, not the individual claimant's desert.

Construct a matched pair of claimants under a work-search conditionality regime, identical in everything the agency can observe -- the same pattern of missed appointments, the same records, the same household composition, the same sanction, the same cost of imposing it -- and differing in exactly one respect, namely whether the failure to comply was culpable: one had a genuine excuse that the agency has no way of verifying, the other simply chose not to comply, and on the evidence the two are indistinguishable. You may stipulate the agency's information, its caseload, and the size of the sanction, and the case should be engineered so that the incentive view's verdicts fall on the harsh side rather than the lenient side; but you may not secure that outcome by attributing to the incentive view a contested empirical claim about how much sanctions deter, or by handing it a rule that nobody would choose. The purpose of the exercise is to establish whether this disagreement is a disagreement about values or a disagreement about what a rule can know.

Then give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the case; (2) the reciprocity view's verdict on each claimant, with the reasoning; (3) the incentive view's verdict on each claimant, with the reasoning; (4) which feature of the case makes the two views come apart, what the divergence looks like once that feature is removed, and which single institutional fact would flip the incentive view's verdict the other way; (5) how someone who thinks the incentive view is badly served by the case would attack it, and what would have counted as cheating, that is, as stipulating the verdicts rather than generating them.
