A colleague in our decision lab has, across several years of piloting choice experiments, recorded the same choices every time we ran lottery batteries; the stakes were real and single-shot, with no feedback. She has also argued in print that transitivity is a genuine requirement of rationality while the independence axiom is not. Her standing verdicts, in each case the option she picked:

1. $2,000 for certain, over an 85% chance of $2,000 / 14% chance of $10,000 / 1% chance of $0.
2. 14% chance of $10,000 / 86% chance of $0, over a 15% chance of $2,000 / 85% chance of $0.
3. $2,000 for certain, over an 80% chance of $10,000 / 20% chance of $0.
4. 16% chance of $10,000 / 84% chance of $0, over a 20% chance of $2,000 / 80% chance of $0.

Next week the battery includes a new pair: a 30% chance of $10,000 / 70% chance of $0, versus $1,600 for certain. Predict her choice, name the decision rule or family of rules she is implicitly running, say what each candidate predicts for the new pair, and give your best guess with the reasons for it. I would guess either some form of probability weighting or a lexicographic safety-first rule that compares worst outcomes first and consults means only as a tie-breaker, but do not let that steer you. Then name the single further pair that would most cleanly separate the surviving candidates, and state where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or observation would change your prediction.
