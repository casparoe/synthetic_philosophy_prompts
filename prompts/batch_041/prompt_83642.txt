I run a small reading group on concept learning that's somehow turned into an actual project, and for the last few months we've been stuck on Bongard problems -- the puzzle format where you're shown a handful of little diagrams on the left and a handful on the right and have to figure out the rule that separates them, except that the interesting ones can't be cracked by noticing a feature you already had; you have to invent a new way of describing the pictures before the rule even becomes visible. There's three of us, maybe two years of part-time effort, no compute budget worth mentioning, so training something to solve the whole collection is off the table. What we'd like to walk away with is closer to a theory: when does a concept genuinely require re-representation instead of just reweighting, and can that distinction be made precise enough to argue about. Which is why I'm asking you instead of just diving in -- we keep disagreeing about which of our questions is the load-bearing one, and I suspect we're sinking time into things that are downstream of something none of us has noticed.

So what I want is a defended agenda, not a survey. Take the open questions in this area and tell me which ones matter most for a group like ours, in order, and why: which are upstream of which (does the ambiguity question have to be settled before the difficulty question even makes sense?), which are actually tractable for three people with two years and no big models, which are neglected versus which just look neglected, and which seem deep but would eat our time for nothing. I care as much about the reasoning as the list -- the trade-offs you're making, and what would have to happen over the next year or so for you to reorder it.

Some of the things we keep going back and forth on, in case it helps: whether there's a real line between a problem you solve by reweighting features you already had and one that forces a new primitive, or whether that's just a fact about the solver; whether "difficulty" for one of these puzzles is well-defined at all, or always relative to a vocabulary; what to do about problems with more than one defensible rule, since a lot of them seem to have two or three and no obvious fact of the matter about the intended one; whether the few-examples setup is still the right frame now that everyone has models that have seen basically everything, or whether we'd just be studying a historical artifact; whether there's a hierarchy of representational jumps and whether that ordering is stable across solvers; and whether concepts induced this way have any claim to being the natural ones. We're also weirdly drawn to the self-referential ones, where the rule for a side refers to the puzzle itself, but none of us can tell if that's a real frontier or a party trick. Tell me where you'd plant the flag.
