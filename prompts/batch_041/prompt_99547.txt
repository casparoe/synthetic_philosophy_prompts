Constitutional monarchs in Britain, Japan, and the Scandinavian kingdoms perform acts that constitute law and government: they assent to bills, formally appoint the prime minister after elections, and open parliament with a speech announcing a legislative program they did not write and may privately oppose. Assent is in practice never withheld; the speech is read exactly as written for them; and in several of these countries the monarch's public acts are valid only when a minister countersigns them or, as in Japan, only when taken with the advice and approval of the cabinet. Meanwhile the monarch is expected to hold no public political opinions at all, airing whatever views she has only in confidential audiences. What does this arrangement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of political legitimacy that lets laws be valid only through the monarch's assent while deriving their authority from an elected body; the implicit theory of agency and responsibility that lets a person utter commitments she does not hold, or sign statutes she thinks unjust, without lying or acting wrongly, and that shifts responsibility onto the countersigning minister; and the implicit account of what the office is, if anything, over and above the person who holds it. Is the package coherent, or does it depend at once on the reserve powers being real, so that the monarch is a genuine last-resort check, and on their never being exercised, so that she is genuinely above politics? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, say where it is underdetermined, and indicate whether a republican or a more openly political head of state could discharge the same functions on a different theory. State how confident you are in each claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
