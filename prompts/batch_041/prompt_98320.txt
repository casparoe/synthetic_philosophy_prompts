I am a philosopher of science finishing a paper that defends a question-relative account of verisimilitude, and in three weeks I have to present it to our department's philosophy of science reading group, whose regulars I know rather too well. The core of my proposal is this. Rather than comparing theories by their true and false content — the approach that ran into the trivialization results of the 1970s, on which no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other, because the false consequences swamp everything — I treat the space of possibilities as partitioned by the questions a scientific community is actually putting to nature (is this parameter in this range, does this mechanism operate here, is this entity fundamental), and I represent each theory as giving, for each cell of that partition, one of three answers: yes, no, or undecided. Truth is a point in the space. A theory's verisimilitude is then a weighted sum over the questions of credit for correct answers minus penalties for confident wrong ones, with a confident error penalized more heavily than a correct guess is rewarded and agnosticism scored in between, so that the measure is not monotone in content and a more cautious theory can outrank a bolder one. My claim is that this escapes the trivialization problem, preserves the realist's intuition that getting closer to the truth is both possible and gradable, and explains why the historical record looks the way it does — Newtonian mechanics scoring high on the questions of its day and disastrously low on the questions that came later.

The audience, however, is not friendly. There are two Popperians: one will say that any account on which a timid theory can beat a bold one has abandoned the entire point of verisimilitude and is really just a dressed-up accuracy score, and the other will press that I have quietly changed the subject from truthlikeness to something like informativeness-weighted reliability, since Popper's problem concerned theories as total descriptions of the world rather than as answer sheets. There is an objective Bayesian who will tell me I have reinvented a scoring rule and that everything interesting is already contained in the probability calculus, and a constructive empiricist who will say the whole notion is idle because empirical adequacy is all we can have and all we need. The most dangerous person in the room is our formal epistemologist, who will not care about any of the philosophy and will go straight for the mathematics: that the partition is not given by the world but chosen by us, so the ranking is not well defined; that the weights encode value judgements no measure can justify; and that any three-place scoring of yes, no, and undecided will be vulnerable to a representation-theorem or invariance objection of the kind that has sunk other aggregative measures. Prepare me for the question period, not for the talk. Give me the eight or nine objections I will actually get from these specific people, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way that particular person would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which two or three things I should simply concede at the outset to buy credibility, which single objection has to be answered well or the paper is dead, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that would force me to admit the account is doing less work than I claim.

Work as an expert philosopher of science with a formal epistemology background who has watched many of these sessions go badly; think it through carefully before writing, and begin by briefly sketching, for your own benefit, what the strongest version of my position would have to look like, so that your objections target that version rather than a straw man. I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to who said what — I want the arguments and the phrasing. Finally, be explicit about the limits of your advice: name the argument or consideration that would most change your verdict on whether this account is viable, say where in your own prep sheet you are most likely to be wrong (especially if you have misjudged what this audience cares about), and flag anything in my sketch that I have stated in a way that invites a cheap objection.
