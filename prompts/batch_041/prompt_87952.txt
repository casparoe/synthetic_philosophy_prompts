I'm a novelist — a third of the way into a book about a man who talks his way out of every obligation he's ever had — and my reading group has handed me an arguement I can't get out of my head, so I'm hoping you can tell me where it breaks. It goes:

(1) For Sartre, consciousness is free all the way down: whatever my situation is, how I take it up and what I make of it is mine to choose. (2) Bad faith is refusing to own that choice — telling yourself your temperament, your orders, or your circumstances decided for you. (3) So whenever someone says they had no choice, they're in bad faith. (4) And responsibility tracks choice: if it was genuinely open to me to do otherwise, then I answer for what I did. (5) So nobody is ever not responsible for what they do. (6) Which means the man who gives up his friends under torture and the man who doesn't are equally answerable, and a slave is as answerable for his servitude as the man who owns him. (7) But that's obscene and plainly false — a victim doesn't author what's done to him. (8) So something in (1) through (5) has to go.

My friend says (5) is just what Sartre beleives and existentialists are fine with the aftertaste; I'd rather keep (2) and (3) than (1) if I have to lose one, since my novel is about a man lying to himself, not about freedom in the abstract. So: which step is doing the damage, why does it look so innocent sitting there, and what does each repair cost me? If I keep radical freedom and swallow the price, what exactly am I swallowing; if I trade it for some thinner notion of freedom, do I lose the whole no-excuses force that makes bad faith a moral charge in the first place? And be straight with me about Sartre's own escape hatch — I dimly remember something in Being and Nothingness about a rock being only unclimbable relative to a project of climbing, plus a distinction between choosing what my situation means and actually being able to get out of it. Does that plug the hole, or does it just relabel it?
