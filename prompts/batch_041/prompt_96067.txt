I am the outgoing chair of a small community music school, and next month I have to give the speech at our annual dinner. I would like you to write it. The difficulty is not the delivery; it is knowing what is true.

Six years ago our landlord raised the rent in a way we were advised breached our lease. We fought it. The fight ended in March and we lost: the rent stands, our legal costs came to a good deal more than the whole sum that was ever in dispute, and in the summer we leave the building that has been the school's home for forty years. The sum was never large. What we told ourselves was at stake was the principle, and each year the principle grew.

What makes the speech hard is that at no point did any of us decide anything that looked foolish at the time. Our rules required a unanimous vote of the board for every new round of expenditure, and we took that vote eleven times. There was never a single moment at which we could lose everything at once; there was always one more letter to write, and each round cost a fraction of what we had already spent. Our treasurer warned us against the very first letter, and was right, and voted yes from the fourth round onward, on the grounds that by then the money was gone and stopping would only make the loss final. I know there is a name for this pattern; I have read the usual accounts of it, and none of them has ever explained to me why eleven people who are not fools, one of whom saw it coming, kept choosing it. I want the speech to say what actually happened to us, and to leave the room with something it can use.

Some constraints. The speech must not use the phrase "sunk cost," and it must not deliver a lesson about throwing good money after bad; the room has said that to itself many times and it has helped no one. Nor should the speech fall back on our having been unlucky, or congratulate us for having fought. What I want in place of those is an account of the machine we were standing in. The room will be mixed: board members who are still raw about it; members who barely followed the dispute and would like an evening that is not about it; partners who thought we had lost our minds; our treasurer in the front row; a former board member who resigned in protest at round seven and is coming back for the first time; and our lawyer, who told us at the outset that we would lose and was right. It is a dinner after a long year, so the speech should be warm, should be capable of a joke, and should be a speech rather than a lecture. Ten minutes at most. Please write it.
