Since retiring from a career in experimental physics I have begun graduate work in philosophy, and this term I grade for a second-year undergraduate survey in epistemology in which the students have read Nozick alongside the standard treatments of the Gettier problem and of Cartesian scepticism. I am new enough to the material that I would like a careful second assessment before I return the paper. Below are the question I set and one student's answer to it. Please assign a letter grade and supply the comments that justify it, written so that I could hand them back to the student. I am asking for a second-year standard, not that of a research paper.

Question 3. (a) State Nozick's conditions for knowledge, and say what work each is meant to do. (b) Nozick is generally read as holding that we can know ordinary things, such as that we have hands, while denying that we can know the denial of the sceptical hypothesis that we are brains in vats. Explain how his account yields that asymmetry, and say what it implies for the principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment. (c) Consider the following case. Tomás lives in a flat with thin walls and can hear his neighbour, Mrs Iqbal, playing her radio through the wall. On that basis he believes she is at home. She is at home. But Mrs Iqbal leaves her radio on whenever she goes out, which is most afternoons; had she been out, Tomás would have heard exactly what he hears now, and would have believed just the same thing. Does Tomás know that Mrs Iqbal is at home? Answer with reasons.

Student's answer: "Nozick's conditions are (i) p is true, (ii) S believes p, (iii) if S were to believe p, then p would be true, and (iv) if p were true, S would believe p. The point of the conditions is that knowledge is not merely true belief that is well supported: the belief has to track the fact across counterfactual situations. In this respect the account is a form of reliabilism, since what ultimately matters is whether the belief was produced by a reliable method; Nozick accordingly states his conditions relative to the method by which the belief is formed. A further consequence is that Gettier cases are excluded: in the stopped-clock example the subject's belief is true and supported, but it does not track the time. It is also worth noticing that justification plays no essential role in the account; on Nozick's view a knower need not be able to justify what he knows. On the sceptical asymmetry: because the conditions are counterfactual, they can be satisfied for 'I have hands' while failing for 'I am not a brain in a vat'. The belief that I am not a brain in a vat fails condition (iv): were it true, I would not believe it, since a brain in a vat believes it just as firmly as I do. So Nozick can accept that I know I have hands while denying that I know I am not a brain in a vat, and he therefore denies that knowledge is closed under known entailment, since the second proposition is entailed by the first. On the case: Tomás knows. He is right, and his way of finding out — listening through the wall — is a reliable one in that building, as he has verified on many occasions. That the radio would have been on whether or not Mrs Iqbal was in is a fact about one particular afternoon, and it does not undermine the general reliability of his way of telling whether she is at home."

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no preamble and no closing summary. The first item should state the grade and, in one clause, the strongest single reason for it. The remaining four should each take one step of the answer above, say whether it is correct, mistaken, or correct but misleadingly put, and state what the student should have written instead.
