I'm drafting a position paper for a metaphysics seminar on material constitution, and before I defend it I'd like an audit of the whole package rather than a survey of what other people have said. Here is where I've landed. (1) When a lump of clay is molded into a statue, there are two numerically distinct material objects in the same place at the same time, the statue and the lump, sharing all their parts; constitution is not identity. (2) The test for distinctness is Leibniz's Law: if two things differ in any property, they are not one thing. (3) The statue and the lump do differ modally, since the lump would survive being squashed flat and the statue would not, and it is that difference that earns them separate status. (4) Modal properties are not brute; they are grounded in what a thing is by its kind, so the statue is essentially a statue and the lump essentially a lump. (5) At every moment the two are intrinsically identical: same mass, same shape, same microstructure, same arrangement of atoms. (6) What makes something a statue is historical and institutional, requiring that it have been made, or taken up, under an appropriate artistic intention, whereas anything with the right cohesion and composition is a lump of clay. (7) Whether there are two objects on the pedestal is a fact about the world, not a matter of how we choose to count or describe. (8) On persons I am a plain animalist: I am the human organism, there is no additional person-object constituted by it, and my persistence conditions simply are the organism's.

Two things I take to be non-issues, though I would like to be told if I am wrong about that. First, the counting objection: I see no absurdity in the statue and the lump each weighing ten kilograms while the shelf bears ten, since the shelf rests on the same atoms either way, and I treat the charge of double counting as a bookkeeping confusion. Second, ordinary speech: I am happy to say that the statue is the clay in the way we ordinarily talk, and I do not think that concedes identity. The case that pushed me to write all this down is one a studio-mate raised: a bored assistant in a sculptor's studio shapes a lump into a recognizable face for about a minute as a practical joke and then flattens it, and nothing is exhibited, sold, or meant as art. I want to say no statue existed at any point, but the only thing I can point to as missing is the intention, and that makes me uneasy, though I cannot tell whether the unease is well placed or whether I am just noticing that my views are doing real work.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture: which of these commitments are actually in tension with which, ordered by severity; which pairs only look like they conflict and why they do not; for each genuine tension, the cheapest repair and what that repair costs elsewhere in the position; and then, at the end, which single change would clean up the most while leaving the rest intact. Please be specific about which of my numbered claims would have to give, and do not tell me it all hangs together unless you can show me why the appearance of trouble is misleading.
