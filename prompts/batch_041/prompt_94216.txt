Ok so a bit of background before the actual ask. I'm on the board of a small housing co-op — 14 units, we decide everything at a monthly general meeting — and I have zero formal background in any of this (I did a fine art degree, I just read a lot), but for the last couple of months I've been low-key obsessed with something that happened at our March meeting and I think it has a name, something like "the discursive dilemma" or "judgment aggregation"? Anyway, here's what happened. We had a report on our ancient boiler and we voted on three motions that came out of it: (1) the boiler is past the point where repairing it is worth it; (2) if the boiler is past that point, we should replace it with a heat pump rather than another gas boiler; (3) we should replace the boiler with a heat pump. Every single one of us voted a coherent package — nobody endorsed something that contradicted something else they'd endorsed — and yet when you tallied the three motions separately, the meeting as a whole came out endorsing (1) and (2) and rejecting (3), which flatly contradicts itself, and the chair just banged on to the next item like nothing happened. I've been chewing on it since and I've convinced myself this isn't people being sloppy or the chair being bad at her job: it's built into deciding each motion separately by majority, and no amount of better facilitation or more information fixes it, because the problem is in how you glue the individual votes together, not in the votes themselves. So next month I want to make the case that we change our process: put only a small set of genuinely independent questions to a vote and let the rest follow from those, and if a set of winning motions turns out not to fit together, drop the one with the weakest support until the remainder is consistent, instead of adopting the whole pile as-is. My partner thinks I'm going to get eaten alive and she's probably right.

So what I want from you is a prep sheet for the question period, not a defence — I don't need you to convince me, I need to know what's coming. The room will be: about twenty members, mostly people who've been here 10+ years and who are genuinely attached to our consensus process and to direct democracy, they think one-member-one-vote on each proposal is the fairest and most legitimate way to run a co-op, several of them have done facilitation trainings and are a bit proud of it, and they get suspicious fast of anything that sounds like handing more discretion to the board or the chair. There's also two retired professionals (a lawyer and an accountant) who care less about philosophy than about whether my proposal is consistent with our bylaws and whether it's gameable, and one guy who will just say this is all over-thinking and we should get back to item 4. And they'll phrase things in co-op language, not philosophy language — "that's just a slippery slope to the board deciding everything", "we don't have this problem because we talk things out", "you're fixing something that isn't broken", "so who decides which questions are independent?", that kind of thing.
