I am assembling materials for a seminar on anachronistic philosophy, and I need a worked example. The figure is Kant; the debate is the contemporary dispute between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws of nature, which took its present shape only in the twentieth century, well after his death in 1804.

Take the debate as follows. On the Humean side: laws are not features of the world over and above the total distribution of local, non-modal matters of fact (the mosaic), but rather the axioms or theorems of the deductive system that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit with the objective chances; everything supervenes on the mosaic, and there is no further modal glue. The anti-Humean side comes in several varieties: laws as second-order relations of necessitation holding between universals, as Armstrong, Dretske, and Tooley maintain; laws as primitive, irreducible entities, as Maudlin maintains; laws as grounded in the dispositional essences or powers of properties, as Bird and Ellis maintain; and a more recent proposal on which laws are meta-laws, that is, constraints on which first-order generalizations are eligible to be laws at all. The familiar objections cut both ways: that a mere regularity cannot explain its own instances or support the counterfactuals we expect laws to support; and that a relation of necessitation does no explanatory or epistemic work, since it tells you nothing about what happens, so that positing one is positing a governor with no governor's office.

What I want is a prediction rather than a survey. How would Kant have reacted to this dispute? Reconstruct the position he would most likely have taken, using his own apparatus rather than a modern paraphrase of it, and be explicit about the following:

First, the distinction between the transcendental laws that the understanding legislates to nature and the empirical laws that are contingent and must be systematized by reflective judgment. Given that distinction, would Kant regard the Humean's standards of simplicity, strength, and fit as a secular cousin of his own regulative principle of the systematicity of empirical laws, or as a category mistake, namely a maxim of the reflecting subject mistaken for a feature of the object?

Second, the necessitarian's governing relation. Would Kant accept it, or deny that any necessity in nature could be a worldly relation between universals? If the latter, does he thereby count as a Humean, despite rejecting Hume's account of the origin of our idea of necessary connection?

Third, whether his own combination is stable: an a priori and strictly necessary causal principle, as in the Second Analogy, together with empirical laws that are contingent and discovered by systematization. Does the necessity of the one tier leak into the other?

Fourth, chance and uninstantiated laws. Could Kant admit irreducibly probabilistic laws, or laws whose antecedent conditions never occur, given his commitment to the thoroughgoing determination of empirical nature?

Fifth, an invented hybrid to be assessed on its merits: a two-tier view on which the causal principle is transcendental while every particular law is a Humean regularity. Would Kant call this coherent, or does the upper tier collapse without a necessitarian lower tier? And would he think a Humean who abandons the causal principle can still make sense of there being laws at all?

Where the texts underdetermine the answer, say so, and mark clearly which parts of your reconstruction are extrapolation. You need not supply citations or a reading list; argue from the positions themselves.
