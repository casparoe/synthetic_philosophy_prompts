I am a novelist, some two hundred pages into a book that I cannot finish until I know whether the moral picture I have built into it holds together. The setting is a small coastal republic — invented, though closely modelled on places I have spent time in — in which a mid-level official in the ministry of works is pressed by an international lender to accept a procurement code while his extended family presses him from the other side. In defending the book to friends I have found myself defending a set of views about corruption, and I would like an audit before I write another scene on top of them.

What I believe is this.

(a) Corruption is not a natural kind. The formula one meets everywhere — entrusted power, private gain, and the abuse that links them — has never done any work for me, because every term in it is the contested one. What counts as private, what counts as entrusted, and above all what counts as abuse are settled differently in different places; the line between a gift that obliges and a bribe that buys is drawn by convention rather than discovered in the act.

(b) When an office-holder's conduct is wrong, what makes it wrong is that he has converted an office into a private resource. The test is not the size of the payment, nor whether the payment changed his decision, nor whether anyone was harmed, but whether he treats the office as something he is at liberty to dispose of. I once thought the mark of a bribe was simply its addressee — that a gift is offered to a man and a bribe to an office — and I no longer know whether I meant that as a definition or as a moral claim.

(c) The wrong, where there is one, is a betrayal of trust. It is done to those who entrusted him with the office, and it is unintelligible except against a background of expectations that he has himself accepted.

(d) Some conduct is wrong whatever the local conventions say. The money allocated for the clinic that was spent instead on a wedding is a wrong in a village where everyone agrees that a man's first duty is to his sister.

(e) The international anti-corruption apparatus — conditionality, league tables, the procurement code that must be signed before the loan is disbursed — mostly exports the moral tastes of the lender while relocating discretion upward, from the clerk at the counter to the consultant in the airport hotel.

(f) As a novelist I will not write villains. Every character in the book should be acting on an obligation he would be ashamed to break, and the reader should be able to see that each of them is right about something.

In the draft as it stands, the protagonist's brother-in-law is awarded a contract at a price a competitor would have matched, and the work is done well; the protagonist signs, because his sister's children's school fees depend on it, and he would be ashamed to refuse. I have written this as not corruption — nobody is harmed, the road gets built, the man is doing what his family requires of him — but I am not easy about it.

Please treat this as an audit rather than an essay on corruption. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension with which, and which pairs only appear to conflict? Rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, say what would have to give, what it would cost me in argument, and what it would cost the book, since some of these are load-bearing for scenes already written. I would rather have three ranked tensions than a list in which everything is said to pull against everything else, and I would rather you take a position than lay out the options: if one commitment has to go for the rest to stand, tell me which, and defend the choice. If you think the set is consistent and I have merely been muddled about one of the items, say so and say which.
