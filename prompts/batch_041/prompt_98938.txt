My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the common prior assumption and went to bed without settling it. She takes the line that two rational agents who start from a single shared prior simply cannot agree to disagree: once each one's posterior is common knowledge, the numbers are forced to coincide. My objection is that this only bites when the disagreement is fully common knowledge, and that the common prior is a substantive stipulation rather than a theorem about rationality — she thinks I'm just refusing to accept that persistent disagreement is a failure. I'd like to answer the evening with a pantoum, written in the voice of one of the two arguers, that carries the argument through its own form: use the interlocking repetitions — each stanza's second and fourth lines returning as the first and third of the next — to enact the ladder of common knowledge, so that the two voices' posteriors are held apart in every stanza where the knowledge is merely mutual, and only collapse onto a single shared line once the repetition has been driven all the way down. Let the closing quatrain's return of the opening line mark the moment Aumann's agreement theorem takes hold, and let the poem be candid about how much that moment leans on the shared-prior stipulation rather than on rationality alone. It should be accurate enough that someone who has taught the theorem would recognize the machinery and not merely the vocabulary, and legible to someone who hasn't. Five or six quatrains is plenty. When you've finished, add a short note saying how confident you are that you've represented the theorem and its critics fairly, and flag the single place where you're least sure the philosophy survives the meter.
