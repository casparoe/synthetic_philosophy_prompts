The World Happiness Report publishes an annual ranking of countries by "happiness," and I'd like you to treat it as a test case for the philosophy of wellbeing. As I understand it, the headline number comes from a survey question that asks people to picture a ladder with the best possible life at 10 and the worst at 0 and to rate their own current life on it, and the country scores are averages of those ratings; these rankings get widely reported and are often used to argue that governments should be trying to climb them. Using the standard trio of accounts of wellbeing -- hedonism (wellbeing as pleasant experience), desire-satisfaction (wellbeing as getting what you want), and objective-list theories (wellbeing as comprising certain goods whether or not you want or enjoy them) -- tell me which account the ladder question actually operationalizes, and whether what it measures deserves the name "happiness" on each of the three. Then run the analysis the other way: what does the prominence of these rankings show about the theories themselves? Does a country have reason to try to top the ranking, and does that answer change depending on which account is correct -- in particular, is there an account on which climbing is plainly the right goal, and one on which chasing the score could raise it while lowering wellbeing? Be specific about what the survey question captures and what it leaves out, and say where the theories are silent rather than making them speak.
