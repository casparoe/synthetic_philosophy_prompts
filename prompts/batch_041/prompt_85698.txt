I run the smallholder agriculture portfolio at a mid-sized foundation -- irrigation, seed, market access, about 30 grants a year across the Sahel and South Asia -- and I write the recommendation the board votes on, so if its wrong it's on me. Two weeks ago one of our trustees came back from a holiday with her head full of the argument that the agricultural revolution was the worst thing that ever happened to us: that foragers worked a fifteen-hour week, ate better than the first farmers, and that farming is what bought us hierarchy, famine and bad backs. Her challenge to me was "if farming was a mistake, what exactly are we funding?" She's a former epidemiologist and she doesn't accept vibes. I don't have a framework for this, I've just been pointing at the pumps. Her sharpest point, and the one I can't answer, is that farming may not have made anyone better off, it just made more of us -- and if that's the shape of the thing then half the metrics we use are measuring the wrong quantity.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposal in half an hour, from my desk, using only what's in the proposal and what I already know. No reading list, no citations, I'm not going to go do a PhD. Take whatever is genuinely defensible in that debate -- I know the romantic version is contested, so don't hand me the romantic version -- and turn it into tests I run in order: what each test is asking, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, which tests can end in a flat no, and which ones mean I have to stop and hand the board a values question instead of pretending I can settle it. Then run the procedure on two real-shaped cases. First: a solar irrigation scheme that would roughly triple dry-season yields per household, but it raises the labour each household has to put in, and last time something like this went in the older kids vanished from school for the harvest. Second: a cooperative of women farmers switching a chunk of their land to an export crop -- income up a lot on paper, but the inputs are debt-financed with land as collateral and there are two buyers. I'll tell you now that I want the first one to pass, so if your procedure says otherwise, say so plainly and show me the step where it happened.

Finish by telling me where this procedure is most likely to be wrong: which step is doing the real work while pretending to be neutral, and what single consideration, if it went the other way, would flip your verdicts on those two cases. And flag anywhere I'd be better off just saying "this is a judgement call, board decides" rather than dressing it up as a test.
