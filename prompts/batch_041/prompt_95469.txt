I am setting the final examination for my upper-level seminar "Rationality, Information, and Choice." We have just finished a unit on the value of information, organized around I. J. Good's result that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer's expected value of information is non-negative. Too many students leave the unit able to recite the result — "information cannot hurt, because you can always ignore it" — without seeing how much work the assumptions are doing or what happens when they fail.

Draft one essay question, worth 40% of the exam and answerable in about 75 minutes, that forces them past the recitation. I want the question to open with a concrete decision scenario (invent one; do not reuse the familiar umbrella, insurance, or urn examples) in which a rational-seeming agent's expected value of information is negative, and then to require students to (i) locate exactly which premise of Good's result the scenario violates, (ii) explain why Good's proof nonetheless stands as a theorem about the restricted class it actually covers, and (iii) take a position on whether the theorem deserves its reputation as a vindication of the slogan "more information is always better."

For the scenario, I want something that breaks the theorem in an interesting way rather than by simply attaching a cost to acquiring the information — an acquisition cost just rewrites the payoffs and leaves the theorem's logic untouched. Make the agent's preferences, or the agent's decision rule, the source of the trouble. A case I would find especially useful to see handled: an agent who is an expected-utility maximizer with respect to a fixed action set but whose updating rule is not conditionalization, or whose preferences over the objects of choice are non-additive (for instance an ambiguity-averse agent who evaluates acts by their worst-case expected utility across a set of priors). Feel free to design something sharper, provided the failure is genuine and the diagnosis is exact.

Please give me three things. First, the question itself. Second, a model answer of roughly 800 words. Third, a marking scheme worth 40 marks that, for the top band, names the specific moves an A answer must make and the specific errors that should cap a student at a B — in particular I want to distinguish a student who concludes "Good's theorem is simply false" or "this refutes Bayesianism" from one who says precisely which idealization the scenario relaxes and what that does and does not show. The marks should be allocated so that a student cannot earn full credit by describing the scenario correctly without drawing the distinction the question is testing.

One further request for the model answer: keep separate the parts of this that are settled from the parts that are genuinely contested among decision theorists and formal epistemologists. That the proof is valid for its stated assumptions is not in dispute, and I do not want it presented as though it were. But whether the theorem supports the informal "information is good" slogan, whether the non-expected-utility agents who can violate it are irrational, and how much the result should be thought to vindicate the practice of gathering evidence are live disagreements — say where the consensus ends and the disagreement begins, and do not present a contested position as the settled view.
