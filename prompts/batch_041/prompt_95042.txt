Please give a conceptual explanation — not a formal derivation — of why the thermodynamic arrow of time is standardly traced to a special low-entropy boundary condition rather than to the laws of motion themselves, and say precisely what that boundary condition supplies that the laws cannot. Then consider a universe whose microphysics is time-reversal invariant and whose entropy is low at both temporal extremes and maximal in between. How many thermodynamic arrows would such a universe have, and on what does the answer turn? I am chiefly interested in whether an observer situated near the middle could be justified in calling one direction "past" rather than the other, and in whether the standard account must treat the two low-entropy extremes asymmetrically. If it must, what licenses the asymmetry; if it need not, what picture of time's arrow results?
