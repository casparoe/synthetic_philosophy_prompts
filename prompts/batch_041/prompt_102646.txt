okay so I've fallen down a rabbit hole on suffering risks — s-risks, the whole idea that the worst thing that can happen to the future isn't us going extinct but some vast, stable setup that cranks out astronomical amounts of suffering instead.

Here's what I actually want explained, and please don't just define the term at me. Everyone talks about s-risks as if its a single quantity you can compare — this future is a worse s-risk than that one, spending effort to reduce s-risk here trades off against extinction risk there, and so on. But the examples people reach for are all over the shop: a galaxy-wide totalitarian dystopia, a modest but permanent number of minds in endless torture, a sim running beings that suffer, wild animals still suffering on a terraformed planet forever, an AI that's indifferent to suffering but locks in some lousy equilibrium. These don't seem like the same kind of bad at all. Some are 'worse than extinction' on one reading and arguably not on another.

So the conceptual issue I want you to walk me through: are s-risks really one comparable thing, or a grab-bag of incomparable outcomes wearing a single label? What work is the phrase 'worse than extinction' actually doing, and can you cash it out without already smuggling in a particular axiology (suffering-focused, totalist, whatever)? And if you can't, does 's-risk' collapse into just 'the stuff my ethics says is really bad', or is there something left over once you strip the axiology out?

One more thread I want woven in. Someone described an s-risk to me as a 'suffering lock-in' — bad, stable, and hard to exit — as opposed to a 'suffering transient', bad but self-limiting. Does that stability distinction do real independent work, or is it just a rough proxy for how much suffering there ends up being in total? I suspect it's doing more than people admit but I can't articulate why.
