I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been writing about people who cross borders, which has left me with a question I cannot answer from inside the fiction. Nearly every liberal state treats the freedom to leave its territory as a right — no one needs permission to emigrate, and no one is punished merely for departing — while treating the freedom to enter as a privilege the state may withhold at will. That asymmetry is not the puzzle by itself. The puzzle is how the surrounding apparatus is arranged. Entry is lawful only under a purpose the state has already named and recognized: a visit, a course of study, a job, a marriage. At the point of entry an officer assesses not only whether the traveler fits a category but whether the traveler's stated intention is sincere, so that the state polices a state of mind before any act has occurred. Enforcement is largely delegated: airlines are fined for carrying a passenger later found inadmissible, so a check-in clerk becomes the first border officer; employers and landlords are conscripted to verify status, so that ordinary private transactions become checkpoints. And the law does not treat all unlawful presence alike — in the United States, I am told, entering without authorization and overstaying a permission fall under different bodies of law, the one closer to a crime and the other closer to a civil infraction, though the two people are identically present on the ground.

What I want from you is the theory this machinery presupposes. I am not asking you to defend or condemn open borders. I am asking what view of movement, membership, territory, and wrongdoing is baked into the way things are actually done — and I would like you to be honest where the machinery turns out not to have a single theory behind it. Assume I have no philosophical training: define any term of art when you first use it, and do not send me to a reading list, since I have no library at hand.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

**The Practice in Plain Terms** (150–200 words). Restate the machinery as one institution with parts, so that we agree on what is being explained. Separate what is common to most states from what is peculiar to a few.

**What the Machinery Presupposes** (500–600 words). Reconstruct the implicit theory. Address at least: what kind of right the right to exclude is taken to be, and in whom it is vested; why exit and entry are treated as different in kind; what makes a person's presence legitimate, such that a stated purpose rather than the bare fact of being a person is what licenses it; who bears the burden of justification; and what kind of wrong unauthorized entry is taken to be, given that the penalty attaches to the manner of entry rather than to the resulting presence. Say plainly what the apparatus would look like if these commitments were not held.

**Presupposition or Accommodation** (300–400 words). Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with. Name at least one theory of the right to exclude that the machinery accommodates without needing, and explain how you can tell the difference.

**What It Quietly Rules Out** (250–350 words). Which rival views of movement and membership cannot be held consistently with the machinery as it stands? Be specific about which part of the apparatus does the ruling out.

**Where the Machinery Is Silent** (250–350 words). Identify the questions the practice leaves open, so that several incompatible theories would license the same arrangements. Explain what additional evidence about the practice — not about the theory — would settle each open question.

**The Other Theory, and What It Would Dismantle** (400–500 words). Suppose the presupposed view were replaced by the straightforward claim that a person's freedom to move is a right against every state, not only their own. Walk through the apparatus part by part — the purpose-bound visa, the judgment of sincerity, the delegated carrier check, the private verification, the distinction between unlawful entry and overstay — and say what would have to go, what could survive in altered form, and what would replace it.

Close by telling me whether the machinery is unified or whether it is a bundle of accommodations that only looks like a single doctrine from the outside. I would rather have the honest reading than a tidy one.
