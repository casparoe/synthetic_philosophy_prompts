'Simultaneous discovery is the rule in science, not the exception. Darwin and Wallace, Newton and Leibniz, and dozens of other pairs show that once the time is ripe the idea gets made by whoever happens to be standing there. So those discoveries were inevitable — if Darwin had died on the Beagle, Wallace would have published the same theory, and if neither of them had, somebody else would have gotten there within a few years. You can even measure the ripeness of a field: take the fraction of its major discoveries that had multiple independent discoverers, and that number tells you how inevitable each one of them was. Which is why the lone-genius story is a myth, and why priority disputes are basically vanity — the individual's contribution is negligible, so the credit really belongs to the community, or to the era. The Newton–Leibniz fight over the calculus is the perfect illustration of how empty it all was: neither man had really seen the other's work, they arrived at the same results anyway, and so there was nothing to settle.'
