Consider two rival views about the structure of value. On the commensurable view, enjoyment and suffering lie on a single scale and every episode of suffering has a finite price: for any suffering, however intense, some finite quantity of enjoyment makes a world containing both at least as good as a world containing neither. On the lexical-threshold view, this fails at the extreme: there is a level of suffering at or above which no finite quantity of enjoyment, however large or widely distributed, can compensate.

What is your credence that the lexical-threshold view is correct? Decompose it rather than merely asserting it: (a) the probability that enjoyment and suffering are commensurable at all, so that trade-offs between them have a determinate answer; (b) conditional on commensurability, the probability that the scale is non-Archimedean at some point, i.e. that some amount of disvalue cannot be matched by any finite amount of value; (c) conditional on that, the probability that the break falls on the side of suffering, giving the avoidance of suffering priority over enjoyment rather than the reverse or a symmetric arrangement. Give each a number with one line of justification, then combine them. Then say whether the view so understood is committed to a single maximally intense instant of suffering outweighing any finite enjoyment, however long that enjoyment lasts, and whether that consequence counts for or against the view. Which term is your number most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move it most? For what it is worth, I put myself at about 0.5 and a colleague at about 0.15; tell us which of the two is more likely to be mislocating the disagreement. Answer in British English.
