You are a philosopher of probability with a specialist's command of the principle of indifference, invariance arguments in the foundations of probability, and the geometric paradoxes that go by Bertrand's name. I am preparing teaching material for a graduate reading group whose members are philosophers with rusty measure theory, and I need a diagnostic instrument, not an essay. Please think carefully before you write, and follow the specification below closely.

The subject is the family of arguments in which a quantity — for instance the length of a "random chord" of a circle — admits several apparently legitimate constructions, and the constructions assign different probabilities to the same event (a third, a half, a quarter, depending on whether one randomises the endpoints, a radius, or a midpoint). I do not want a retelling of the paradox or a survey of its literature. I want a decision tree that takes a reader who already accepts the paradox as a genuine problem and walks them, by a sequence of concrete questions, to a named position on what the paradox shows and what should be done about it.

Requirements:

1. Build the tree from the logical space first, positions second. Begin with the questions that actually separate views — for example, whether "a random chord" has a truth-value in the absence of a stated randomising mechanism; whether the proper remedy is to demand a physical procedure or to impose an invariance condition; whether indifference constrains rational credence or merely helps to construct it; and, if a reference class is required, what could fix it — and only then locate the named positions on the branches those questions generate. Do not assemble a list of positions and attach one question to each.

2. Phrase every node as something my reader can answer without knowing where it leads: a concrete case, a claim to endorse or reject, or a choice between two described procedures. No node may read "do you accept the invariance approach?" or "are you a subjectivist?".

3. The following positions must appear as leaves, named however you judge clearest: the view that the problem is well posed and that a symmetry or invariance argument of the kind Jaynes pressed selects a single answer; the view that it is ill posed because no randomising mechanism has been specified, and that this is a defect in the question rather than in the principle; the view that the principle of indifference survives in weakened form as a constraint on priors within a fixed parameterisation; the strict subjectivist view that nothing beyond coherence constrains prior probabilities, so that the paradox dissolves; the reference-class relativist view that every such probability is relative to a chosen reference class and there is no further fact of the matter; the view that the paradox is a symptom of the Borel–Kolmogorov phenomenon concerning conditioning on events of probability zero; and a decision-theoretic view on which the interesting question is not what the probability is but what an agent should do when several symmetries compete.

4. My three supplied cases must be placed by the tree, and each leaf must state what it does with each of them.

(a) A needle-dropping machine: a long straight needle is released above a large floor on which a circle is painted; the needle's centre lands uniformly in a large square containing the circle, and its orientation is uniform. Does the tree route this to the same leaf as the classical uniform-midpoint construction, or does the dependence of the induced distribution on the size of the square change the routing?

(b) A length-first procedure: a length is chosen uniformly between nought and the diameter, and a chord of that length is then chosen uniformly at random from among all chords of that length (orientation uniform, and the choice between the two mirror placements made by a fair coin).

(c) A three-dimensional analogue with two rival specifications: first, a plane is taken perpendicular to the radius through a point chosen uniformly in the ball; second, the plane is taken to be the one through three independent points chosen uniformly on the sphere's surface. Do the tree's branches, built for the circle, transfer, and where exactly do they fail?

In addition, state briefly whether the tree's branches survive the substitution of a square for the circle, and what that substitution reveals about the role the size of the symmetry group is playing.

5. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say why — for instance because two formulations collapse into one another, or because the positions differ only in motivation rather than in what they say about cases. Also identify combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, and diagnose each: is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

6. Do not supply citations, references, or reading lists; I have those. Any technical term you use (invariance, reference class, conditional probability given an event of probability zero, and so on) should be unpacked in a clause of plain language, since not everyone in the room will follow it otherwise.

7. Audience and size: eight to twelve leaves, no more than ten internal nodes, and between roughly 1,200 and 1,800 words for the whole deliverable.

8. Structure your answer as follows. First a short scratchpad in which you list the questions you think genuinely separate positions, note where two candidate questions are really the same question, and check that your leaves are mutually exclusive. Then the tree itself as a numbered, indented outline. Then a table of leaves with four columns: leaf name; one-line statement of the position; its most awkward commitment; and what it does with cases (a) to (c). Then your remarks on inseparable pairs, unoccupied branches, and the square-for-circle substitution.

9. Write in British English throughout.
