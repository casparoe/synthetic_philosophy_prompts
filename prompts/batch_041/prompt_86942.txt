I'm in the upper sixth and I run a small philosophy reading group at my college. Our teacher has asked me to write a mock paper for the session we're giving over to advance directives, because the practice paper we were handed was made up entirely of questions that could be answered by reciting two or three standard positions, and the whole group scored more or less the same on it. What I'd like from you is one essay question, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme. The session runs for fifty minutes with no notes and no internet access, so the question must be answerable from reasoning and general knowledge alone: please don't require students to cite anyone, and don't hang the question on a named philosopher's book or on a particular court case, since half the group will half-remember the names and the other half will never have met them.

Here is the case I want the question to turn on. I've invented it, so that nothing about it can be recalled rather than thought through. Mrs Fairhurst is sixty-three and was diagnosed with a progressive dementia five years ago, while she was still running her own business. That same month, with her solicitor present, she wrote out a statement of her wishes in her own words. In it she refused antibiotics for pneumonia, artificial nutrition and hydration, and any move to a hospital; she added that the statement was to stand even if she later appeared to ask for treatment; and she also asked to be kept at home rather than in a care home, that her two sisters take turns to stay with her so that she would not die among strangers, and that her grandson be brought to see her every Sunday. Both sisters have since cut their working hours and one has remortgaged her house, and the grandson, who is eleven, has begun to find the visits distressing. She now has pneumonia. She is cheerful most of the time, does not always recognise her grandson, and in a lucid half-hour last week said she was happy and asked for the tablets that would clear her chest; the next morning she seemed not to remember having asked. We are not interested in what the law says; the group is only interested in the moral authority of the earlier self. The question I have in mind is something like "whose decision is this, and what makes the earlier Mrs Fairhurst's word authoritative?" — but please sharpen the wording yourself, and make sure the question forces students to confront whether that authority is the same in kind when it is used to demand something burdensome of other people as when it is used to refuse something for oneself, and what a lucid interval in the middle of it all is supposed to show. I would rather it were a question on which a student who has read a summary of the debate still cannot see straight away how to answer.

The model answer should be committed rather than even-handed: it should come down on one side on the demand question, give its reasons, and say plainly what it costs to hold that side, instead of laying the options out and leaving the reader to choose. For the marking scheme, I'd like five must-hit points worth four marks each, with a one-line note against each saying how candidates typically get that point wrong, and a closing sentence naming which of the five actually separates an A from a B.
