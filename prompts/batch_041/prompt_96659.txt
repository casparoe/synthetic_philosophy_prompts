Right, so I'm putting together something for my science-and-ideas column — a staged dialogue, the sort of thing where two clever people go at each other and the reader gets to eavesdrop. Two philosophers of science, both of them sharp, neither one a strawman, arguing about underdetermination: the idea that the evidence can never quite force us to pick one theory over its rivals.

The setup in my head is that they're at the hotel bar after a conference and the row kicks off because one of them reckons the whole debate is a bit of a fuss over nothing — that underdetermination is basically a transient problem, it dissolves the moment someone builds a cleverer instrument or thinks up a new kind of experiment, and the people who go on about it are just dressing up a temporary gap as a deep fact about science. The other one thinks it's permanent and ineliminable, and that what actually does the deciding, once the evidence has run out, is all the stuff that isn't evidence at all — taste, values, who's got the funding, that sort of thing.

And here's the twist I'd like the second one to push, because I find it genuinely interesting: that the real problem isn't choosing between two theories you've already got sitting on the table, it's the rivals nobody has even thought to formulate yet — so the whole business of agonising over which of two empirically equivalent theories to adopt is, in a sense, agonising about the wrong thing entirely.

Can you write me that dialogue? Keep both of them equally competent, I don't want one of them playing the idiot, and make them actually respond to each other's points rather than just taking turns delivering speeches. Somewhere in the middle let them sink their teeth into a concrete case — dark matter versus modified gravity, or the fact that nobody could have anticipated the rival theory that eventually turned up — so it doesn't just float off into the abstract. Ta.
