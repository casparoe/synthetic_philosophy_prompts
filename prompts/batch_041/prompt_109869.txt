You are a philosopher of biology with a firm command of the formal theory of signalling, and you have been asked to help a colleague work through a passage that has defeated them. Before you commit to a reading, set out in a few lines the two or three candidate interpretations of the passage that strike you as live; then give me your considered answer. I would rather have a definite verdict, defended, than a survey.

I teach a seminar that puts nineteenth-century social theory next to contemporary evolutionary work on communication, and I keep coming back to Thorstein Veblen's The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), specifically the chapter on conspicuous leisure:

In order to gain and to hold the esteem of men it is not sufficient merely to possess wealth or power. The wealth or power must be put in evidence, for esteem is awarded only on evidence. And not only does the evidence of wealth serve to impress one’s importance on others and to keep their sense of his importance alive and alert, but it is of scarcely less use in building up and preserving one’s self-complacency.

And a little later, in the chapter on the pecuniary standard of living:

The standard is flexible; and especially it is indefinitely extensible, if only time is allowed for habituation to any increase in pecuniary ability and for acquiring facility in the new and larger scale of expenditure that follows such an increase.

I can gloss the period vocabulary well enough — "invidious comparison", "reputability", "pecuniary emulation" are not what defeats me. What I cannot say precisely is what Veblen is claiming in that first passage, and whether the apparatus of signalling theory genuinely illuminates it or merely dresses it up in modern clothes.

Here is where I get stuck. "Esteem is awarded only on evidence" reads like a claim about an inference: the observer infers something about the possessor from what the possessor displays, and the display works only because it is somehow credible. That invites the comparison with Zahavi's handicap principle — the idea that a signal is reliable precisely because it is costly, in the way that Grafen's 1990 signalling model formalises, and that Spence's job-market signalling model had already anticipated in economics. But I can also see a quite different reading on which Veblen needs no such machinery at all: burning resources in public is not merely costly evidence of wealth, it is more or less constitutive of it, so that the signal is unfakeable for free and no handicap is required. That is the distinction, standard in the animal-signalling literature, between indices and handicaps, and I cannot tell which side Veblen falls on, or whether the question is even well posed for him.

I have four further worries, and I would like them addressed rather than set aside.

First, what Veblen has the display establish is relative standing — rank among emulators — whereas the handicap literature is usually about absolute quality of a kind (viability, condition) that a receiver can do better or worse at detecting. If the thing signalled is a positional good, then the "evidence" requirement generates an arms race rather than an equilibrium, and the sentence about an indefinitely extensible standard looks like Veblen's acknowledgment that his account has no rest point. Does the absence of an equilibrium disqualify his account from being a signalling account at all, or is it a signalling account of a different species than Grafen's?

Second, is the receiver of the signal really another person? Veblen says the display is of "scarcely less use in building up and preserving one's self-complacency". If the primary beneficiary of one's own evidence is oneself, we seem to have signalling addressed to the signaller, which sits awkwardly with models in which signals exist because receivers act on them.

Third, Veblen's economy runs on vicarious consumption: the wife, the liveries, the servants consume on behalf of someone else. The cost is borne by one party and the repute accrues to another. Does the handicap logic survive that delegation, or does it need the signaller to bear the cost himself?

Fourth, I would like to know what work "evidence" is doing for Veblen at all — whether it is an epistemic notion (what makes the display credible to an observer), a causal notion (what compels the display regardless of any observer's inference), or a juridical metaphor he never cashes out. If the passage equivocates between these, say so, and tell me which one the surrounding argument actually requires.

Assume I know Veblen's book and I know the biology at the level of an interested outsider, but I have not internalised the game theory, so explain the models in words rather than in notation. I do not need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; tell me plainly what you think, and where the text will not settle the matter, tell me that too. I am asking for something I can hand to graduate students, so define each technical term the first time you use it.

Finally, and I mean this as a condition on the answer rather than a courtesy: say what would change your mind. Tell me which feature of the passage, or which result in the formal literature, would lead you to a different verdict, and tell me where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
