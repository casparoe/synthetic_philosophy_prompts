I host a monthly reading group — mostly long-time rationalist-blog readers, no philosophy coursework — and next session is Hume's essay on suicide. Don't explain the essay to me; plan how I should teach it in fifty minutes.

Start with what has to be in place before the group's question ("is suicide wrong?") even becomes Hume's question ("is it a transgression of duty?"), and the order those prerequisites go in. Then the sequence: he canvasses duties to God, to our neighbour, and to ourselves — give me the order in which those three must be handled, why that order rather than the obvious one, and the single example that should carry the whole session, whether it should be Hume's own (a house that falls under its own weight versus one pulled down by men) or one of ours, and what that example must have and must lack. Then the misconceptions, each pinned to the point in the sequence where I should head it off: that the argument only works if you're already an atheist; that his concessions to theism are a rhetorical trick rather than the load-bearing move; that "no duty violated" amounts to an endorsement of suicide; and whatever else this group will reliably produce.

How will I know at the end whether they've grasped the shape of the argument — as I read it, that he grants his opponents their entire framework and defeats the conclusion anyway — rather than merely formed an opinion about suicide? Give me one closing question that separates those two outcomes, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this length.
