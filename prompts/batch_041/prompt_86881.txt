I'm a PhD student working on the epistemology of higher-order evidence, and I keep colliding with a slogan that I've come to suspect is doing several different jobs at once: you can't believe what you take yourself to have no business believing. It surfaced again in a reading group last week, where a colleague used it to wave off the entire literature on epistemic akrasia. His reasoning was that a subject who genuinely judges her first-order belief to be irrational simply no longer holds that belief, so the alleged phenomenon is incoherent and the papers about it are chasing a contradiction. I don't think that is obviously right, but I can't yet say precisely where the inference fails, and I'd rather arrive at the next meeting with the options mapped out than with a single rebuttal. Could you give me a menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing? I want each reading stated as a claim I could accept or reject on its own, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive but defensible, or false — and, for each, a note on whether it would actually license my colleague's move of declaring epistemic akrasia impossible rather than merely irrational. I'm especially interested in whether any of the readings turns on a distinction between occurrent and dispositional belief, or between the impossibility of holding a belief and the impossibility of holding it rationally, and in whether the slogan is being asked to carry a much stronger thesis than it can bear — something on the order of a requirement that a subject's first-order attitudes be closed under her own higher-order assessments, or a picture on which first-order and higher-order attitudes are simply not permitted to come apart. Lay the readings out as a numbered list with the verdicts attached to each, and finish by saying which reading my colleague most plausibly had in mind and whether that reading does the work he wanted. Please skip citations and reading lists; what I need is the disambiguation and the verdicts.
