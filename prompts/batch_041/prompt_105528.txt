I serve on the national board that decides whether to license new pesticides, industrial chemicals, novel foods, and medical devices, and I have spent a year trying to work out what rule one of my fellow members is running. Everyone on the board says she is the one who takes the precautionary principle seriously. Here is her record, as compactly as I can put it.

She voted against licensing an insecticide to be applied by aerial spraying over inhabited areas, where the only safety signal was a single high-dose animal study, no human data existed, and the harm, if it came, would be permanent. She voted against releasing a self-propagating soil organism to clean up contaminated land, where containment failure was judged unlikely but possible and an escape could not be undone. She voted against adding a compound to the municipal water supply, where the evidence suggested an unquantified but plausible risk of a serious illness that would be treatable and would resolve once exposure stopped. She voted against allowing a chemical to be used in children's mattresses, where the developmental risk was plausible but unquantified.

She voted for a drug for a rare and otherwise fatal disease that carries a known, quantified risk of a serious complication in roughly one user in five thousand, where the complication is treatable and patients consent after counseling. She voted for a permanent, non-removable implant for consenting adults with a known and quantified risk of a complication requiring follow-up surgery. She voted for a novel food additive whose only flagged risk was a theoretical, unquantified chance of transient digestive discomfort in adults, disclosed on the label.

She once summarized her own position by saying that no one should have a serious risk imposed on them that no one has measured, a sentence that leaves it open whether it is the imposition or the measurement that does the work for her.

The file now before us is a first-in-class gene therapy for a disabling but not life-threatening condition. The treatment alters the patient's own tissue in a way that cannot be undone, and the long-term effects are genuinely unknown: there is a plausible, unquantified risk of a serious and permanent complication. The patients are competent adults who have been told exactly this, no third party is exposed, and nothing comparable has been approved anywhere.

Tell me how she will vote. I want the rule she appears to be running; at least one other rule that fits her record just as well; what each of them predicts about the gene therapy; which prediction I should bet on, and what makes you prefer it; and, the part I care most about, the case, not this one, that would settle the question between them.
