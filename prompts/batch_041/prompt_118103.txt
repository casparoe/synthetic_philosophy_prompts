Ok so I've been chewing on this wasp thing for a while and I want to watch two people argue about it properly.

Here's the wasp thing, in case you haven't run into it. A digger wasp (genus Sphex) paralyses a cricket and drags it back to her burrow, but before taking it inside she drops it at the entrance and goes in to inspect the nest first. If you, the experimenter, nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's inside, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the entrance, and then goes in to inspect the nest again. Nudge it again while she's in there and she does the entire routine again. People have run this dozens of times in a row and she never just carries the cricket in. Hofstadter coined the word sphexish for behaviour like this, stuff that looks purposeful from the outside but is really a fixed routine that a trivial change in the environment can reset. Dennett leaned on the example in his free will writing. Though I should say there's a philosopher called Keijzer who argues the wasp is alot more flexible than the anecdote makes out, that she adapts after enough repetitions, and that the story survives in the literature for rhetorical reasons rather than empirical ones. Also, checking the nest again after finding your cricket moved is arguably just... sensible. Anyway.

The part I actually care about isn't the wasp. It's that I can't see a principled difference between her loop and mine. I deliberate, I make plans, I change my mind, I step back and reflect on my own reflection. But all of that could be the same kind of machinery with a longer loop and a bigger input space, and the fact that a reason rather than a nudge is what resets me doesn't obviously move me into a different category. I have a nagging suspicion that I'm sphexish and no way to check from the inside, which is probaly exactly what a sphexish thing would think.

So write me a dialogue between two people who disagree about this, and make them both good. One thinks the wasp story generalises all the way up: that human deliberation is a loop too, one that happens to take reasons as input, and that our sense of being able to have done otherwise is evidence of nothing at all. The other thinks there's a real difference in kind and wants to actually say what it is, something about representing your own loop, or holding a goal as a goal rather than as a trigger, or what it means to be interrupted by a discrepancy rather than by a stimulus. Neither of them should be a strawman and neither should be a mouthpiece for the obvious objection of their side. Every reply has to engage with the actual last thing the other person said; if one lands a good hit, the other has to absorb it or answer it rather than slide past it. Let them get into the weeds: whether reset is even well defined for a system that represents the interruption, whether the wasp evidence cuts either way, whether the difference between us and her is one of degree or kind or whether that distinction is doing any work, whether being arguable-out-of-a-routine by a reason makes us freer or just differently wired. It's fine if they don't resolve it. I'd much rather have a genuine standoff than a tidy verdict.

Around 800 words. Keep the ending open, no final paragraph telling me what we've learned or who came off better. And no formatting: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no stage directions in brackets about what someone does with their eyebrows. Just the two of them talking, one turn after another, in continuous prose the way you'd write it in an email. Speaker labels are fine, that's all I need.
