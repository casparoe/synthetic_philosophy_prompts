I'm a third-year PhD student working on the rule-following considerations, and I've come to think the debate they generated has been spinning in place for forty years. The skeptic claims that no fact about a speaker — dispositional, phenomenal, or otherwise — determines that she means one function rather than another that agrees with every use she has actually made and diverges only on some novel case. Dispositionalists answer that the fact is just her disposition to respond in certain ways, typically under idealized conditions; the objection comes back that a mere disposition cannot be correct or incorrect, so it cannot be what meaning consists in, and that idealizing the conditions quietly smuggles in the very notion of a rule being followed. Anti-reductionists hold that grasping a rule is a primitive normative state not analyzable into anything else; critics reply that this relabels the mystery rather than dissolving it. Both sides know each other's moves, the same exchanges recur, and nobody concedes anything.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than assumed, is that two questions are being run together under one heading: a constitutive question about what makes it the case that an expression has this meaning rather than that one, and a justificatory question about what a rule demands of a speaker and when she is entitled to her confident application of it. If that is right, the dispositionalists and their critics may not even be contradicting one another — they may be answering different questions in one vocabulary, with the word normativity doing double duty for prescriptive demand and for correctness against a standard.

So diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate it. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or two questions under one name? Where does each side's real argumentative burden sit, and which of the two readings of normativity is each side actually relying on? Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected; and say what would move you to a different diagnosis and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail.
