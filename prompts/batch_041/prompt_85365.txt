I teach a second-year undergraduate course on philosophy of biology and I've got one 75-minute session, about 16 students, for the unit on the species problem. My problem is that when I just lecture through the different species concepts they nod along and then hand in essays saying some version of "species are just human constructs, it's all arbitrary," which is definately not what I want them to take away. So I want to run an activity instead, where they do the classifying themselves and walk into the trouble rather than being told about it. Assume I've done no lecturing beforehand on this topic.

You're a philosopher of biology who also designs active-learning sessions for a living, so please think carefully about the mechanics before you write anything up. Start by brainstorming the specific ways a sorting exercise like this predictably fails (they finish in six minutes, they reach for "it's all subjective" as an escape hatch, one loud student decides for the whole table) and design against each of them. Here's the shape I have in mind, though change whatever needs changing: I hand out roughly 20 specimen cards for a made-up genus of beetle (or lizard, your call) from an island chain, and each card carries a mix of data -- what it looks like, where it was collected, whether it bred with specimens from other localities in captivity, a genetic distance figure, what it eats, when it was collected. I want the data deliberately rigged so the criteria come apart: two almost identical-looking populations that won't interbreed, one variable population with two wildly different forms that happily do interbreed, a chain of neighbouring populations where each interbreeds with the next along the chain but the two ends won't interbreed with each other, a lineage that reproduces asexually so the interbreeding question is simply inapplicable, a hybrid zone, and a second drawer of specimens from the same localities collected a long time earlier or later that I can spring on them halfway through. Groups of four recieve the same cards but a different job: one group is writing a field guide for ecologists, one is doing a phylogenetic reconstruction, one is a museum curator deciding how to label the drawers, and one has to decide whether a particular population is distinct enough to qualify for legal protection before a development permit gets granted. Each group commits to a number of species and defends it, we compare numbers across the room, and then the whole room has to make the permit decision together, so the disagreement stops being academic. Please give me the actual card contents (at minimum a fully worked set of the nasty ones), the timing, the roles, the sequence of reveals, and a debrief that maps each moment of friction onto a real piece of the debate: what each criterion is actually for, why the disagreement is not just missing data, the difference between a definition and a criterion for applying it, and what it costs to say "they're all equally valid" or "there's no fact of the matter" once a protected listing is on the line.

Two more things I need from you. Be honest about what the activity can't do. My worry is that it will leave them thinking the mess is an artifact of a small dataset, when the real claim is that more data makes it worse, and I'll have to say that part myself -- so tell me exactly where the facilitator has to step in and supply what the exercise can't show. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, in specifics, not generalities (someone will try to settle everything with the genetic distances and I need a card that blocks that, someone will try to split the difference by calling the ring species two species "in transition," etc.), plus what to do when each of those happens. Materials printable, no tech beyond a projector, and the whole thing has to fit in 75 minutes with real time left over for the debrief, which is always the part I run out of time for.
