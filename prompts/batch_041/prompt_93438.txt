I sit on the editorial board of a general mathematics journal — the sort that publishes analysis, combinatorics, and operator theory, and whose referees are rarely set theorists. Two submissions have forced a policy question onto the agenda for next month's meeting, and I need to circulate a written opinion beforehand.

Paper A proves a theorem about the structure of certain families of sets of reals under the assumption that the continuum hypothesis holds; the assumption is flagged in the abstract, and the referee recommends acceptance. Paper B proves a theorem about the same families whose conclusion contradicts A's, under a combinatorial principle known to imply the negation of the continuum hypothesis; its referee also recommends acceptance. Both referees agree that each paper is a correct conditional result. The board is split. One faction holds that a theorem resting on a hypothesis which can be neither proved nor refuted from the standard axioms should not be printed without a loud caveat, and perhaps not at all. A second faction replies that this is no different from publishing a theorem conditional on the Riemann hypothesis. A third holds that A and B do not conflict at all, because the continuum picks out a different object in different models of set theory — as if one asked whether the first coordinate of a vector is even when no basis has been chosen.

Please act as the board's logician and philosopher of mathematics and write the opinion I will circulate. Think the problem through carefully before you draft, and begin by setting out for yourself the space of possible policies, ranging from refusing such papers outright, through publishing them under a standardised caveat, to publishing them with a commissioned companion note. Then commit to one.

The opinion should:

- open with a clear recommendation and an implementation timeline — what we do about A and B specifically, and what the standing policy will be from the next submission cycle onward;
- state that policy in terms a non-set-theoretic referee can actually apply when deciding whether to recommend acceptance;
- give my colleagues the strongest case for the position you reject. In particular, engage seriously with the thought that there is a fact of the matter about the continuum, that our axioms are merely incomplete, and that the discipline's task is to discover the right additional axioms rather than to relativise results to whichever universe a paper happens to inhabit — and with the related distinction between justifying a new axiom by its intrinsic plausibility and justifying it by the fruitfulness of the mathematics it yields;
- and say plainly what you take to turn on the choice: whether a dependence on the continuum hypothesis is a defect in a theorem or merely a location.

No citations or reading list, please — I cannot check them and they will not help the board decide. One to two pages is enough.
