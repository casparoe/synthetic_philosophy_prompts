I have been writing novels for some fifteen years and teaching fiction writing to graduate students for the last four, and I have come to think that the philosophical literature on skill has been asking a genuine question about the wrong moment. I have drafted a short essay on this and would like your comments — on the argument, on the examples, and on whether it is worth anyone's attention.

The essay's target is the familiar opposition. On one side is Gilbert Ryle's contention that knowing how to do something is not a matter of knowing any collection of true propositions; the skilled performer is not silently reciting instructions to herself. On the other side is the intellectualist rejoinder, most associated with Stanley and Williamson, that knowing how to swim simply is a species of propositional knowledge — knowledge, held under a practical mode of presentation, of a way to swim. I grant both sides their central insight and argue that the dispute is under-described, because both sides are describing a state the agent is in: a standing capacity, on Ryle's account, or a standing propositional attitude on the intellectualist's. Neither describes what I call occurrent craft knowledge — the knowledge a practitioner has only in the act, in the interval of doing, which is neither a standing disposition nor a proposition held before the mind, and which is extinguished the moment the practitioner steps back to describe it.

The essay's central example is a novelist midway through revising a sentence. She knows that the sentence must end on the word "nevertheless" rather than "still," and she knows this as firmly as she knows her own address; but she cannot say why, cannot derive the choice from any rule of style she could state, and if interrupted and asked to justify it, the knowledge evaporates — she can reconstruct a rationalisation afterwards, but the rationalisation is a post hoc artefact rather than the thing itself. I claim that three properties distinguish this from Rylean know-how. It is occurrent rather than dispositional. It is indexical, indexed to a particular sentence at a particular moment, so no general way of writing can capture it. And it is prospective, directed at what the next move must be rather than at what has already been done. I further claim that it is not the intellectualist's practical-mode proposition either, because it is not grasped under any description at all: the practitioner is not representing a way of proceeding, she is proceeding, and her knowledge is the proceeding's own self-correction.

The essay draws two conclusions, of which I am less sure. The first is pedagogical: if occurrent craft knowledge is a distinct kind, it cannot be transmitted by testimony, and the standard workshop apparatus of stated rules and explained choices addresses only the two standing kinds — which would go some way toward explaining why so much writing instruction produces competent imitators and so few writers. The second concerns testimony more generally: it would explain why we trust a practitioner's judgment within her domain while discounting her own account of it.

I am aware of two objections and have answered them only partially. The first is that I have merely renamed flow, or automatism, or absorbed skill — states that are not knowledge at all but rather the absence of the deliberation that knowledge would require. The second is that the "extinguished by interruption" criterion proves too much, since many ordinary occurrent beliefs vanish when one attends to them, and no one supposes that this makes them a new epistemic category.

What I want from you is not encouragement but surgery. Tell me whether occurrent craft knowledge is a genuine third kind, or whether it collapses into one of the two standard positions once stated carefully — and if it collapses, say precisely where the collapse happens. Tell me whether the three properties I list are independent, or whether the second and third are doing all the work while the first is decoration. Tell me whether my central example does any evidential work at all, or whether I am illicitly generalising from my own drafting habits; if the latter, propose an example that would test the thesis rather than merely illustrate it. Tell me whether the pedagogical conclusion follows or is a non sequitur. And tell me, finally, whether the essay stripped of its more excitable claims says anything a philosopher would not already have said. I would rather publish a smaller true thing than a larger false one.
