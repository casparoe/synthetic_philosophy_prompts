Write a short story, third person, under 1,500 words, set in a near-future clinic that offers a service it calls reconception: from a stored tissue sample it reconstructs the exact sperm and egg that produced a given person and implants the resulting embryo. The service is sold to bereaved parents as a way of having their child again. Your protagonist is the clinic's senior embryologist, who takes a new case — a mother whose seven-year-old daughter died and who wants the daughter back.

The story should stage, rather than settle, a disagreement about personal identity. On one side: the view that a particular person's identity is fixed by the particular sperm and egg she came from, so no other gamete pair could have produced her, and what the clinic makes will be a different child however closely it resembles the first (the position usually called origin essentialism). On the other: the view that the gametes are incidental, that what the mother is asking for is coherent if anything is. Give both positions their strongest voice through what the characters do and say, not through lectures, and do not let the narrator adjudicate.

Two further elements should be in play. First, the dead daughter was herself conceived by IVF from a batch of embryos, and her parents chose which one to implant; the embryologist has to work out what that fact does to the clinic's story about the same child. Second, a scene in which the mother asks the embryologist to guarantee something the procedure cannot guarantee.

The philosophical question, not the technology, should be what the story turns on, and the ending should leave a reader able to see the pull of the view the story has been leaning against. Characters may use philosophical vocabulary, but the story must work for a reader who has none.
