Quick background: I do mechanistic interpetability work, and I've drifted into being a realist about it — I think that when we find a feature in a network and can steer behavior with it, we've found something the network genuinely represents, not just a story we tell. A philosopher friend says that's naive, and a labmate says the whole position is unfalsifiable, and now I want to know if I'm fooling myself.

So here's the task. You're a philosopher of science who actually understands how these models work — not someone who'll just shrug and say it depends what you mean by representation. I want you to treat my view as a real scientific hypothesis and tell me what would count against it. Start by brainstorming the whole space of possible defeaters before you commit to any, because I don't want the four obvious ones and nothing else.

Here's roughly where I stand. First, there's a fact of the matter about what a trained network is doing, and it isn't just whatever's convenient for us to say. Second, when a probe decodes some property, or a sparse autoencoder pulls out a feature, and especially when we can intervene — ablate it, or add a steering vector and watch the behavior change the way the feature's content predicts — we've got evidence the network represents that content. Third, so 'the model represents X' can be true or false, and the read-then-write test is at least a fallible way to find out.

What I want from you: the observations, arguments, formal results, or experimental outcomes that should move me, sorted from this-would-basically-sink-the-view down to annoying-but-survivable, and be concrete about which of my three claims each one hits.

A few worries I already have, so tell me how bad they really are. A labmate keeps saying that for basically any distinction you care about, you can train a probe that decodes it and find a direction that steers it — so passing the read-then-write test doesn't show the network uses that feature, it shows the test is too easy. Meanwhile someone else in my group thinks the discreteness we find with sparse autoencoders is an artifact of the sparsity penalty — if the true structure is continuous and heavily overlapping, then 'the network has these features' is just false. And a deflationist friend says representation-talk here is like the intentional stance: fine for prediction, no commitment to inner content, and no experiment could ever tell the difference. If that's right, I'd like to know it's right.
