I am a third-year philosophy student serving as the undergraduate representative on my university's dining services advisory committee, which votes next week on a proposal to print an animal-impact figure beside every dish in the dining halls: a single number estimating how many animals are killed per thousand calories, summed across every animal involved, vertebrates and invertebrates alike. Before the vote I have to circulate a short written opinion recommending a concrete course of action — adopt the figure as drafted, adopt some modified version of it, or drop it — with a timeline for implementation and a candid account of the strongest case against whatever I recommend. The committee is mostly administrators plus a staff dietitian, so an argument that turns on naming the right papers will not land; it needs to stand on its own reasoning. The trouble is that the drafters' own spreadsheet produces a ranking most of the committee finds absurd: because cattle are large and chickens small, chicken dishes come out far worse than beef on deaths per calorie, even though beef consumes far more cropland, and cropland itself kills field animals and displaces wildlife. One member has added, to general discomfort, that if the lives of wild animals are on balance more suffering than flourishing, then shrinking their populations is not obviously a harm — which would reverse the ranking a third time. Tell me what the number should actually measure, which of these contested judgments it has to take a stand on and which it can honestly present as a range, whether any single composite figure can be defensible at all, and what I should move that we do.
