I'm a TA for an upper-level ethics seminar and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on supererogation that can't be answered by defining the term and trotting out the usual saint and hero examples. The thing I keep trying to get students to see is a puzzle I've started calling the shoelace problem. You walk past a stranger whose shoelace is untied; you could tell them, it costs you nothing, it saves them a possible fall, and it seems morally better than saying nothing, but nobody thinks you've done something wrong if you don't. Is that act supererogatory? If yes, then supererogation doesn't require sacrifice, which messes with the standard idea that it's about going beyond duty at significant cost. If no, then why isn't it just a tiny duty? And either way, what does the answer say about whether supererogation is a distinct deontic category at all, or just the top end of the permissible, or maybe a kind of aretaic praise rather than a deontic status? I want the question to make them distinguish the claim that an act is morally better from the claim that it is required, and to say something about the difference between no one being able to demand it and it not being required. There's alot of standard material they could just recite, so the question needs to force them to take a stand on the shoelace case and defend it.

For each point give a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong. The exam is closed-book and they have 60 minutes, so keep the question tight enough to answer in that time, and don't make it depend on naming any particular philosopher or work — I want the argument, not a bibliography.
