Ive been developing a view about rationality under logical uncertainty and I want to know what it's exposed to. The picture: an agent holds genuine credences in undecided mathematical claims -- not just "I don't know" but "probably true", "0.7, call it", etc. -- and updates them the ordinary Bayesian way on whatever it manages to prove and on computational evidence it gathers. Rationality for such an agent isn't a special logic of uncertainty: it's the usual package (accuracy, no sure loss to a cleverer bettor, calibration, convergence to the truth on anything eventually decidable, sensible reflection on its own credences) relativized to what the agent can actually compute. So logical uncertainty isn't a different epistemic predicament, it's ordinary probability with a computability constraint baked in.

What would count against that? I want the defeaters ranked by how much they should actually cost me, and I want you to say which ones I'd have to concede rather than wave off. The ones I keep worrying about:

- The truth value of the conjecture is already settled, right now, there's a fact of the matter I just haven't got to. Does that make every non-extreme credence objectively inaccurate, and my whole framework a fancy name for sanctioned ignorance? Or does that objection quietly assume an agent with unlimited computation, and so beg the question?
- If keeping the criterion from being vacuous means restricting the hypothetical bettor to some complexity class, how arbitrary is that choice, and does the answer to "what is rational" change when the class changes? Is there a principled reason to stop where I stop?
- A criterion that only constrains the infinite limit of a sequence of credences may say nothing about what I should believe on Tuesday. Is that fatal for a theory aimed at real agents, or just an acknowledged limit of the formalism?
- Does the view predict anything a boring rival doesn't -- the rival being "do the maths, and dont pretend to have probabilities about claims you haven't settled"? If I can't say what's wrong with someone who simply refuses to assign credences to unproved claims, what work is the view doing?
- Any case where it licenses something plainly silly -- a credence that marches to 1 on a false claim, or that refuses to budge no matter what evidence arrives?

The first one is what would genuinely move me. And at the end, tell me honestly whether this position can be defended against every objection that comes along -- i.e. whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability -- and if it has, whether that's a real problem for a view of this kind or just the price of doing business.
