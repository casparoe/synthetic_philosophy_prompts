I teach a 200-level seminar on belief and evidence — eighteen students, mostly juniors, a mix of philosophy majors and people who wandered in from psychology — and I have ninety minutes next Thursday for the Clifford and James session. The reading is done: they have had Clifford's essay and the first half of James's lecture, and I do not want to spend the hour talking. What I want is a game they run themselves, in which the thing James is claiming becomes a live problem rather than a thesis they can paraphrase back at me, and in which Clifford's objection has teeth rather than being the position everyone politely agrees with for five minutes before returning to James. Concretely, I want them to feel two things they can only get by being inside the situation: that withholding judgment is itself a decision with consequences when the truth of the proposition depends on what people do about it — James's point that refusing to decide is a decision, taken at the same risk of losing the truth — and that a policy of staking nothing without sufficient evidence can lose a truth that a policy of precursive faith would have reached. But I also want the opposite pull felt from the inside: that a belief can make a proposition true without making it true about anything, and that staking a bet is not the same as holding a belief. James himself thought we cannot will ourselves into a hypothesis that is dead to us, and I would like the activity to leave them bumping into that limit rather than dissolving it.

Here is the shape I have been imagining, though you should replace it if you have something better. Two rounds, both built on a proposition whose truth is partly constituted by how many people commit to it: in one, the proposition is worth making true (something about the room's willingness to act together, or about a person's competence — the friendship and athlete structure of precursive faith); in the other, the proposition is a rumour that becomes true only because enough people act on it, a seminar everyone assumes will be cancelled or a bank everyone assumes will fail. Each student marks a card yes, no, or abstain, where abstaining is the safe option paying a small amount regardless, and the tally settles the proposition, with abstainers counted in the denominator so that withholding is not neutral but actively keeps the proposition false. Two wrinkles I want in if they work. First, some device that separates believing from betting after the fact: I am thinking each student privately writes the smallest change in the payoffs that would have flipped their mark, and these are read out anonymously, so the room can see how many of its believers were bettors. Second, two students serve as evidence officers who keep a public board of everything that could count as evidence for the proposition before each round, and who are paid a bonus only if the room's marks track the board; I want the friction between that bonus and the staking bonus to be visible. No real money, small stakes (coffee, the department's terrible chocolate), nothing that makes anyone's mark embarrassing to have made.

Give me the rules in a form I can hand out, the exact wording of the propositions, a scoring scheme that survives being run by someone who has not read your notes, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes including when the two rounds happen and how much time the debrief gets, and a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the part of the argument it is meant to make vivid — James's conditions for a genuine option, his claim about suspension of judgment, the difference between a live and a dead hypothesis, and Clifford's worry about the habits that a licensed belief-formation encourages. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I am particularly worried about the room converging on abstention out of politeness, about one student objecting that the game makes staking instrumentally rational regardless of evidence and concluding that the whole thing is rigged, and about the rumour round reading as an endorsement of believing whatever is convenient — and how to handle each. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: I suspect it cannot produce a hypothesis that is live for some students and dead for others, and cannot show that a mark on a card is a choice in the way a belief is supposed to be. Say so, and tell me what I have to supply in the debrief that the game leaves out.
