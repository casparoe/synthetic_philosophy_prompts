In most jurisdictions a patient's consent to a medical intervention counts as valid only if it is informed: the clinician must disclose the material risks, benefits, and alternatives, and the patient must be able to understand that information, appreciate how it bears on her own case, reason about it, and communicate a choice. That is the practice. Now set beside it the class of choices that are transformative in the sense made familiar by L. A. Paul — becoming a parent, receiving a cochlear implant, entering a monastery, enlisting, or taking a drug that may reorganize what one cares about. For such choices the patient cannot know from the inside what the outcome will be like until she has undergone it, and undergoing it may change the very values by which she would afterwards judge it. What philosophical commitments does the practice of informed consent presuppose about the possibility of informed choice?

Reconstruct the implicit theory. What view of understanding, of preference, and of the deciding self does the doctrine require, and does the requirement that the patient appreciate the significance of the choice for her own life survive contact with transformative decisions? Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say which rival views it quietly rules out, and say where it is underdetermined, so that several theories of decision and of the self would license the same rules. Note as well an apparent inconsistency worth explaining: the doctrine treats understanding as a precondition of valid consent, yet for the most consequential decisions it accepts consent that cannot be fully informed, and it does not class these with the exceptions it already grants, such as emergency or therapeutic privilege. Then consider a remedy some clinics have adopted — supplementing standard disclosure with testimony from people who have already undergone the transformation: lived-experience counselors, implanted adults who speak with candidates, those who have reversed course. Does hearing from the already-transformed supply the understanding that disclosure cannot, or does it merely relocate the difficulty, and if it relocates it, what does that reveal about what the practice was presupposing all along? Finally, if we took the transformative character of these choices seriously, what would have to change — the standard of disclosure, the assessment of decisional capacity, the doctrine of substituted judgment for patients who can no longer speak for themselves?
