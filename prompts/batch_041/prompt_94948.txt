Ok, so I'm finally writing the intro handout for the animal minds seminar and I want to do it in the right order for once. Every year I open with the usual menu — IIT, higher-order theories, global workspace, predictive processing — and then we spend three weeks watching each of them give a confident verdict on the octopus and completely flounder on the bee, and only at the end of week three does someone notice that nobody ever said what the theories were supposed to deliver in the first place. So this year it's spec first, theories after. Write me the desiderata for a theory of the distribution of consciousness across animals.

Fix the purpose like this: the theory should be good enough to hand to a legislator who has to decide whether to extend welfare protections to farmed insects. Which means it has to give usable guidance under uncertainty, not just a verdict, and it has to be usable by someone who can't measure the thing directly.

Go through the requirements one at a time, each with the case or consideration that puts it on the list, and push back if you think one of mine isn't a real requirement:

- It has to have something to say about animals that get rebuilt or dismantled. The caterpillar's nervous system is largely taken apart and reassembled inside the pupa — is the butterfly the same subject, and is the theory even obliged to answer? The sea squirt larva swims around with a small brain and an eye and then, on settling, reabsorbs most of it. Does consciousness switch off there? Is that a prediction or an embarrassment?
- It has to locate the subject in a cephalopod, where the great majority of the neurons aren't in the brain at all, and a severed arm will still go on doing arm things.
- It can't let the verdict turn on neuron count as such. A bee has around a million and does things we'd call clever; a mouse has orders of magnitude more. "More neurons, more consciousness" is a proxy wearing a lab coat.
- It has to be honest about split brains, and about cases where the number of subjects might not be exactly one.
- It has to square with what's actually known about nociception without a cortex, and with convergent evolution — if the proposed mark of consciousness is a structure that birds and cephalopods each built independently, the theory can't rule them in or out by definitional fiat.
- If it makes consciousness depend on a threshold — enough recurrent processing, enough integration, a feedback loop closing — it owes an account of where the line falls among real animals, and can't get away with "somewhere in there."
- It has to fail in a way that's at least in principle detectable. If no possible evidence could count against it, say so and say what that costs.
- It shouldn't be allowed to answer by re-describing the question (is the species conscious, is the clade conscious). I want to know what it says about individuals.

Then the part I actually care about. Flag any pair of these you don't think can be satisfied together — if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, that's the most interesting thing on the page and I want it stated as a result. Tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding the popular theories, and which of my items are just the prejudices of one camp dressed up as adequacy conditions. No citations needed, I know the literature, I want the argument. And go easy on the scorecard at the end, we'll do that in class.
