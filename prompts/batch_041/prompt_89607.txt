I am a chartered accountant with eleven years in forensic and audit work, and last night I spent the better part of three hours arguing with a close friend — a philosophy graduate student — about Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument. He put it to me as a reductio: begin from any distribution you like, let people exchange freely and voluntarily, and you arrive at a lopsided outcome that nobody can object to without also condemning the very choices that produced it. I found myself defending the argument almost entirely in the vocabulary of my own trade, and he kept telling me that I was changing the subject rather than defending it. I want to know whether he was right.

So: restate Nozick's entitlement theory of justice — the three-part structure he builds around acquisition, transfer, and rectification; his distinction between historical principles and end-state or patterned ones; and the Chamberlain argument, which is supposed to show that any patterned principle must either be enforced continuously against people's choices or quietly abandoned — in the vocabulary of accounting, auditing, and asset tracing, as faithfully as you can. Assume I have that vocabulary at my fingertips and use it precisely: the ledger and the audit trail; cost basis, carryover of basis, and the resetting of basis when assets pass at death; historical cost against fair value, mark-to-market, impairment and lower-of-cost-or-market writedowns; substance over form, and the willingness of the standards to look through a sequence of individually valid transactions; tracing assets through commingled accounts, including the rule that a traceable claim is capped at the lowest balance the account ever fell to; taint, and the ways taint gets cleansed, whether by statute of limitations or by the practical impossibility of following funds any further; restitution, constructive trusts, and unjust enrichment; and the fact that an audit takes the opening balances as given and certifies process rather than outcome. If I have put one of those terms in the wrong place, tell me and put it where it belongs.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which of Nozick's claims come through the translation intact? Which survive only in a distorted form, and what precisely is lost in the distortion? And what has no counterpart at all — I suspect the proviso attached to his account of acquisition, the one about no one being made worse off by an appropriation, and the idea that entitlement is a moral fact about a person rather than a status recorded in a system, are my two candidates for the gaps, but I may be misjudging where the seams are. I am particularly interested in two things. First, whether a discipline built on historical cost and audit trails can even generate the objection his critics press, namely that a ledger which reconciles at every step, with a defensible entry at each one, can still leave an indefensible closing balance. Second, whether the pragmatic hybrids accountants actually live with — fair value for some things, historical cost for others, impairment as the trigger for writing history down — are an unprincipled muddle or the outline of a serious answer for which Nozick leaves no room. Say which way you think the residue cuts: a defect in my framework, or evidence that his claim was less clear than it looked.

Be explicit about your confidence and about what would change your mind: name the single argument or consideration that would move you to a different verdict on whether the translation succeeds, and say where your reasoning is likeliest to fail. If you are unsure whether you are using one of my professional concepts correctly, flag it rather than papering over it.
