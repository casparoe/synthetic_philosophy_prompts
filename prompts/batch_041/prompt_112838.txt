I spent my working life in a natural-science laboratory and have come to philosophy only in retirement, with no training in decision theory. Two problems have been occupying me. In the first, an eccentric trillionaire gathers twenty strangers and offers a billion dollars to whichever one of them alone sends him a telegram, charges reversed, by noon the next day, coordination among them being forbidden. In the second, which was actually run by a magazine, a prize of one million dollars is divided by the total number of entries received and awarded to the sender of one randomly chosen entry, each reader being free to send in as many entries as he liked; the editors' published answer, which they called the superrational one, was that a reader should send a single entry with probability equal to one over the number of entrants he expects, though in the event readers sent in numbers so large that the prize became worthless. Please describe the most important implications of these two problems for how I should think about my own small decisions in matters where the outcome depends on how many other people do what I am contemplating — whether to keep refereeing manuscripts, whether to keep flying to meetings, how much of my income to give away — including where the analogy to my cases misleads. Reply with exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, and no introduction or conclusion.
