I am setting the examination paper for a final-year undergraduate option on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds, and I have hit a wall with the essay question. The course divides neatly in two, and that is precisely the problem. The first half is ontology: whether the objects inside a persistent virtual world are real, what category of thing they would be if they are, whether their existence is exhausted by their being instantiated on a running server, and whether the fact that they are intersubjectively accessible and causally efficacious — many players can see the same object and act upon it, and it in turn causes their experiences — is sufficient for realism of any interesting kind. The second half is ethics: whether in-world events can constitute genuine harms, and if so to whom. My students can reproduce the stock line on each half in their sleep, and several of them have already worked out that a good answer is supposed to say something about consent and about the fiction of the game. What I want is a question that forces them to put the two halves together, and to say out loud whether the ontology is doing any work in the ethics at all.

Here is the case I want the question built around, and I would rather you did not substitute a tidier one. Aldermere is a persistent virtual world that has run for eleven years. Over that time a group of players has built, and continually maintained, a memorial garden for a member of their guild who died in real life; the garden is visited, weeded and added to, and the players speak of it among themselves as a grave. Aldermere's operator, without warning, discontinues the legacy shard on which the garden sits and deletes it, along with everything else on that shard; the players are offered a partial transfer of inventory to the new shard, but the garden cannot be transferred, because the terrain it occupies does not exist there. Separately, and under the ordinary rules of the world, one player kills another's avatar in a duel that both entered freely; the rules make that death permanent, and the loser's inventory, accumulated over years, is destroyed. Please write the examination question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question is worth 25 marks and should be answerable in about forty-five minutes of writing; it must require a verdict on both limbs of the case, must make the candidate state explicitly whether the claim that virtual objects are real is load-bearing for the claim that a genuine harm has occurred or whether the ethics can be settled without it, and must not be answerable by summarising the debate we have already covered in lectures. The model answer should be around 800 words and should itself reach a verdict rather than survey the options. The marking scheme should consist of five must-hit points with the marks each is worth (totalling 25), and, for each, a single line on how candidates typically get that point wrong — I am especially keen to catch the students who help themselves to the move from the reality of the objects to the reality of the harms without noticing the gap, and those who think that because the duel was fictional nothing whatsoever happened to anyone.

I have deliberately made the two limbs of the case vary consent while leaving the metaphysical status of the objects unchanged, and I should like the question to make that visible without giving the game away. Everything — question, model answer and marking scheme — should be in British English, and please keep the model answer within the register of a strong undergraduate rather than that of a journal article; I want to be able to show it to the class without intimidating them.
