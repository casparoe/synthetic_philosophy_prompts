The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution to a two-person bargaining problem is the point at which the segment joining the disagreement point to the utopia point — the vector formed by taking each player's best payoff in the feasible set, which is in general not jointly attainable — meets the Pareto frontier. It is characterized by Pareto optimality, symmetry, invariance under positive affine rescaling of each player's utility scale, and a monotonicity condition in place of independence of irrelevant alternatives. It is a normative solution concept rather than a model of the bargaining process: it takes the players, the feasible set, the disagreement point and the utopia point as given, and returns an allocation.

Bring that concept to bear on a real episode: the Antarctic Treaty of 1959. Twelve states negotiated it in the wake of the International Geophysical Year. Seven of them claimed territory, each claim drawn as a sector converging on the South Pole, and three of those claims — the British, the Chilean and the Argentine — overlap across the Antarctic Peninsula. The United States and the Soviet Union recognized no claim at all and reserved their own positions. What the treaty did with the claims was neither to divide nor to adjudicate them but to freeze them: no new claims, no enlargement of existing ones, no acts that could serve to assert, support or deny a claim, and no prejudice to any party's existing position. The same instrument demilitarized the continent, banned nuclear explosions there, guaranteed freedom of scientific investigation, gave the parties a right to inspect one another's stations, and placed decisions with the consultative parties acting by consensus. A generation later the parties negotiated a regime to regulate mineral extraction, abandoned it before ratification, and adopted instead a protocol prohibiting mineral resource activity outright.

Analyze the episode through the solution concept, and run the analysis in both directions. First the mapping, which I want done exactly: who the players are, what the feasible set is, where the disagreement point lies, what each player's utopia point is, what the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution therefore predicts, and how that prediction compares with what was actually agreed. Note that the characterization above is for two players, and say what an extension to twelve would have to look like and whether the result survives it. Then the other direction. What is the surplus the parties were dividing, if there was one? What does the theory do with a stake that cannot be divided or transferred, with decisive players whose ideal outcome is that the stake be allocated to no one, with a settlement that is a rule for future conduct rather than a division of a fixed stock, and with a geography in which what counts as a party's share depends on whether shares are measured by area, by coastline or by degrees of longitude? Separate failures of the axioms from failures of the domain restrictions the theory presupposes, and say which of these the case actually exhibits. Treat the freeze itself as a conception of fairness and compare it with the proportionality the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution embodies. Close with a verdict: is the Antarctic settlement a counterexample to the solution, a problem outside its scope, or a case it should have anticipated? Where the concept is silent, say so rather than extending it by force.
