Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I'm the staff lead for a city heritage and naming commision — 15-20 petitions a year, rename a hall, pull down a bust, move a plaque, or the reverse and put something up. Our commissioners are a retired archivist, two councillors, a local historian and three residents; none of them are philosophers and neither am I. We get roughly thirty minutes per case in public session, I have to write a one-page finding for every decision because anything spicy gets appealed to full council, and we have no research budget — we work off the file plus whatever the local history society hands us, so the record is often thinnest in exactly the places that matter.

What's eating us is the man-of-his-time defence. Some commissioners treat it as decisive — everybody in 1750 thought X, what do you want from the guy. Others say it's an excuse for literally anything. Right now our decisions come down to who shows up that night and talks loudest, and the chair wants something written down so decisions dont just turn on that.

So give me a procedure my staff can run before a hearing. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a clear no, and where it hands the thing over to the commission as a values question history can't settle. It needs to handle: whose time we mean when we say his time (the household, the county, the empire, the whole world — the answers come out different); whether anyone around at the time objected and what it cost them to say so; the difference between excusing what someone believed and excusing what they did; the worry that if the defence covers everyone it does no work at all; and the fact that our file usually has nothing either way on what contemporaries thought, which I suspect we've been quietly treating as a pass.

One more thing. It seems to me two questions are getting mashed together — was this person a rotter by the standards available to them, and does honouring them now commit the city to something. Tell me whether the procedure should treat those as one question or two, and what to do when they come apart.

Run it on three of our live petitions — Jefferson, Churchill, Columbus — including at least one where it spits out an answer our commissioners will hate, and say where the procedure can't help and a human just has to decide and put their name to it.
