I've ended up in a fortnightly reading group on democratic theory and we keep circling back to lottocracy — you know, replacing or at least supplementing elections with randomly selected citizen bodies. Last week a mate of mine, a bit of a crank but in a good way, made the case that the whole point of sortition is descriptive representation: the chamber should be a statistical mirror of the electorate, so you sample on demographics — age, class, region, education, that sort of thing — and you don't fuss about what people actually believe, because the mirror takes care of that for you. I think thats backwards and I want to be able to argue the other side properly instead of just spluttering. Please make the argument that an allotted body should be built to maximise diversity of opinion and cognitive style rather than to mirror the population's demographic proportions — that the mirror ideal is really a defect, because it reproduces the same tight correlations between background and viewpoint that elections already hand us, whereas a good lottocratic chamber would deliberately over-sample dissenting and outlier views and put a ceiling on the largest ideological cluster. And since I don't want to be the person who asserts a principle and then has nothing to say about machinery, take it further: what would the sampling procedure actually have to look like if you took that seriously — what you'd stratify on, what you'd have to measure before you could even draw the sample, how you'd stop the opinion data (which is much noisier and more gameable than a census) from being captured by whoever designs the survey, and whether the resulting body can still honestly call itself democratic once it's no longer a mirror of anyone.

A friend in the group reckons this whole line just collapses into "pick the cleverest cranks and hope", so give that objection a proper paragraph and either absorb it or concede it.
