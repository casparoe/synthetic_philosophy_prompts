I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on formal epistemology and the logic of knowledge, and I'm trying to get a short paper on the surprise exam paradox into shape for a workshop next term. The draft is rough, but the core claim is this: the paradox is an artifact of treating the teacher's announcement as a proposition with a fixed truth value. The announcement is about the students' epistemic state, and that state is partly constituted by their own reasoning about the announcement, so the announcement is better modelled as a function from credence distributions to truth values — what I have been calling an announcement-schema. Read that way, the backward induction is valid, but its major premise is inconsistent with the very announcement it reasons from: the students need the announcement to be common knowledge as a fixed proposition, whereas "surprise" denies exactly that. The conclusion that no exam can occur is then not a discovery about surprise but a symptom of a schema being mistaken for a proposition. I want to insist that this is not just the familiar line that the students' proof is self-defeating: I need not say the announcement is false, or that the students are irrational, or that "surprise" is vague, and I can route around the whole detour through the KK principle (roughly, that knowing entails knowing that one knows); all I need is that the induction's premise misdescribes its object.

To put pressure on the diagnosis I have been using a variant that I have not seen discussed. The teacher announces not only that there will be a surprise exam this week, but that if the students ever become able to prove on which day it will fall, she will move it to another day. The students run the standard induction and conclude that no exam can occur; intuitively, though, the teacher can still catch them out on Wednesday. I take this to support the schema reading, since the moving clause makes the announcement's dependence on the students' credences explicit rather than leaving it implicit. A colleague in the logic group thinks I am reinventing a wheel, and that the variant is just the original paradox dressed up, and I would like to be able to answer her.

What I would like from you is the kind of comment a sharp referee would give: whether the central distinction is actually doing work or whether it collapses into something more familiar under another name; whether the claim that the induction's major premise is inconsistent with the announcement is circular, as she says, on the ground that I am simply assuming what I set out to prove about common knowledge; whether the moving-clause variant is a genuine test of the view; and whether I overreach when I extend the account to probabilistic credences, where I claim the induction fails for any non-degenerate distribution. I would rather hear that something is not yet earning its place than have it go unmentioned.
