I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy and have been asked to chair the closing discussion at our department's workshop on perception. The visitor will present work from a book she is writing in defense of disjunctivism about perceptual experience, and her central claim is that the argument from illusion is a distraction: once one separates perceiving the stick from the way the stick looks, the illusory case lends no support to the idea that veridical and illusory experiences are of a common fundamental kind, and the only serious pressure on her position comes from hallucination. I do not want a session in which she simply restates the familiar dialectic. Please draft eight to ten questions for me, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

The set should include at least one question on whether conceding that the stick looks the same way in the illusory and in the veridical case reintroduces the common factor she wants to reject, and whether that concession can be confined to illusion without becoming ad hoc; at least one on what becomes of her view if the argument from illusion does no work at all, whether disjunctivism still needs the possibility of perfect hallucination in order to pose its contrast with the common-factor view, and how she proposes to explain the indistinguishability of the good and the bad case without treating it as a brute fact; at least one pressing the demand for a positive characterization of the hallucinatory case, with a follow-up that anticipates her replying that a purely negative account of the bad case is all the view requires; at least one on whether her disjunctivism is a thesis about the nature of experience or about the epistemic standing of the perceiver, and what the epistemic version loses if the metaphysical version is confined to successful perception; and at least one that tests her account of illusion as misperceiving an object against a case in which no object is misperceived, such as seeing something in a mirror.
