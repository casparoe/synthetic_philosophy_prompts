I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been stuck for months on an argument in a small reading group that keeps circling without either side moving. It began with the usual spectrum setup: at one end a life that is only just worth living, at the other a rich and genuinely good life, and the claim that we can travel from the first to the second by a long chain of tiny improvements, each of which seems obviously to make things better, until we are apparently forced to say that a vast number of barely-worth-living lives, or the good life with one extra grain of rice added, is better than the good life as it stands. Half the group bites the bullet and insists that "better than" must be transitive -- if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C -- so that anything that feels wrong here is a defect in our intuitions that we should train away. The other half refuses and says betterness simply is not transitive, or that certain pairs are "on a par," or incomparable, or roughly equal, so that the relation is not a strict ordering at all. Everyone now knows the other side's moves by heart, and yet nobody has shifted an inch, which makes me suspect we are not actually disagreeing about what we think we are.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I'd like it to be concrete about the structure of the impasse. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the fight looks like it is about transitivity but is really about whether "better than" is one single binary relation at all, or a family of context-dependent and possibly vague predicates -- so that each side quietly reads the other's terms ("better," "on a par," "incomparable," "roughly equal") in its own sense and then reports a contradiction. I would like that tested rather than assumed. In particular, I'd like you to untangle transitivity from the assumptions it usually travels with and that I suspect we have been conflating: the assumption that any two options are comparable at all, the assumption that small improvements always compose without a threshold or a jump, the assumption that every step in the chain is a strict improvement rather than a mere non-worsening, and the assumption that the same relation is in play when we compare single lives and when we compare whole distributions of lives.

Please finish by telling me what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis -- a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change in the question, or an honest argument that none should be expected -- and separate clearly what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, without presenting a contested position as though it were the consensus.
