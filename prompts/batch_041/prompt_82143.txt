I serve on my university's research-integrity committee, and at our last meeting a colleague tried to settle a long-running dispute with the witness's oath. Our draft policy distinguishes a false statement from a misleading one and treats the first as the graver offense. My colleague says that distinction is spurious, and that the oath everyone knows — the one in which the witness swears to speak "the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth" — proves it: no honest reading of that formula separates telling the truth from not misleading, so our policy is inventing a difference that neither law nor morality recognizes.

I am not persuaded, but I cannot say precisely why. Please act as a philosopher working at the intersection of the philosophy of language and normative ethics — someone who has thought carefully about assertion, implicature, and the ethics of deception — and help me see what my colleague's slogan could actually be claiming. Think it through slowly and in stages.

Before laying anything out, spend a few lines brainstorming the distinct readings, because the sentence is short and I expect there are more of them than either of us currently sees. Then give me a menu rather than a verdict. For each reading: state it as a precise thesis; classify it as analytic or trivial, substantive and defensible, or plainly false; and say what else it commits its holder to — for instance, whether lying requires an intention to deceive, whether a literally true answer to a question can be a lie, or whether the wrong of deception lies in the false belief produced, in the breach of a warrant, or in the violation of trust.

I am especially interested in readings that turn on the internal structure of the formula: whether "the whole truth" imposes a duty of completeness, and if so whether it is even satisfiable given how much any speaker leaves out; whether "nothing but the truth" forbids only false assertions or also true statements that invite a false inference; and whether the three clauses name three duties, one duty stated three times, or one duty plus two intensifiers. I would also like the reading on which the wrong-maker is deception as such, so that lying and misleading differ only in technique.

Then tell me which readings would actually license my colleague's conclusion — that misleading and lying are morally on a par, so our policy's distinction should be dropped — and which readings, if true, would undermine it. Note separately any reading on which the two are equally wrong but different in kind; I suspect that is where the dispute really lives.

Two requests. First, do not pad the answer with references to a literature; I have no access to it and want the analysis, not a reading list. Second, if the honest answer is that the oath is doing rhetorical work no precisification can rescue, or that the lying/misleading distinction is a verbal dispute not worth having, say so plainly rather than producing a tidy taxonomy just because I asked for one.
