(H) Whenever two people are in fact equally well informed about a question, equally competent at reasoning about it, and have fully exchanged their evidence and their reasoning with one another, and they still disagree about the answer, at least one of them has made a mistake. Their disagreement is not a case of two rational people rationally holding different views.

Some background on why this is not a textbook exercise for me. The formal result I have in mind is that two agents who are both perfect Bayesians and who start from a shared prior cannot rationally end up with different posterior credences in the same proposition once those credences are common knowledge between them, where common knowledge means not just that each knows the other's credence, but that each knows that the other knows, and so on. I understand why the proof goes through. What I cannot settle is what follows for people.

The case that started the argument: two colleagues, same prior, both know this, and both know it is known. A discloses her entire information set and reasoning; B receives it, updates, and replies; and at the last step neither can fully rule out that the other's final message never arrived. So each knows the other's final credence only up to a small chance of error. Read strictly, the antecedent fails outright, and my colleague says that settles it: the theorem is mathematics about an unreachable ideal, so it tells us nothing about whether a persistent real-world disagreement between well-informed people is evidence of error. I suspect that is a technicality rather than the point, and that the interesting question is whether the conclusion survives in the limit, with real disagreement still counting as evidence of error, just less of it. Here is the distinction I have been trying to press on her, and I do not know whether it is the right way to carve things up: some ways the antecedent fails come in degrees (a small chance that a message was lost, priors that are merely very close, mild ambiguity in the agents' own credences), while others fail in kind (an agent being unaware of a possibility the other takes seriously, information that does not fit the partitional picture at all, or genuinely different partitions that no exchange of evidence could align). I think only the second sort would be structurally fatal, but I would not defend that in print yet.
