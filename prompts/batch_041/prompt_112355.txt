You are a philosopher of moral psychology with a second life as a long-form interviewer. I am recording an episode with a guest who defends a position she calls the division of moral labour, and I need you to draft my questions. Begin with a few lines of brainstorming in which you work out the two or three moves she is most likely to make in order to avoid the hard version of each question; I want those notes, and then the questions.

Her position, as I understand it, is this. There are two ideal types of folk morality. Order-morality treats the primary virtue as compliance with shared, publicly knowable rules; its paradigm failure is deviation, and its paradigm achievement is doing one's duty unobserved. Conscience-morality treats the primary virtue as sincerity of individual judgment; its paradigm failure is unreflective rule-worship, and its paradigm achievement is refusing an unjust demand at personal cost. She holds four further theses. First, the burden-of-proof thesis: the two moralities do not differ over which acts are permitted but over where the burden of proof sits, order-morality placing it on the deviator and conscience-morality on the enforcer. Second, the parasitism thesis: conscience-morality is a second-order practice that functions only against a thick background of order-morality, so that where that background is thin, following one's conscience becomes hard to distinguish from doing as one likes. Third, the administrative-virtue thesis: the characteristic vice of order-morality is not cruelty but the substitution of correct procedure for judgment, producing the type she calls the flawless official, and its characteristic blind spot is moral progress, which always arrives first as a deviation. Fourth, the cost-bearer problem: order-morality can only treat the conscientious objector as an exception to be scheduled around, while conscience-morality cannot explain why anyone else should bear the cost of his refusal. She concludes that neither morality is self-sufficient, which is what the division of labour is for.

I want nine or ten questions, and I want at least one that does each of the following: presses the burden-of-proof thesis, asking whether a difference that lies only in who must argue leaves the dispute merely verbal or whether a substantive disagreement sits underneath it; presses the parasitism thesis, asking what becomes of it in a society where the background is thin and whether the thesis is circular; forces the cost-bearer problem into the open with a concrete case, for which I suggest a single dissenting member of a cooperative scheme who refuses, on conscience, to take part in a practice everyone else regards as harmless; asks whether her claims about order-morality producing stable coordination are empirical and what would count against them; asks whether the German and American labels are doing explanatory work or are a polite way of essentialising two nations, and what would show that she has mapped them onto the wrong pair; asks what makes a morality folk rather than a philosopher's reconstruction; asks the domain's own question, which of the two has the better of it, and what she says when they conflict; asks whether her account can explain moral progress without making the reformer initially vicious; and asks whether the division of labour is a description or a normative proposal in disguise, and whether it collapses into relativism.

For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and for three of them a second follow-up that anticipates the standard deflection. The questions must turn on the content of the position as stated here; no biography, no warm-up questions, no invitations to restate the view she has already stated, and no requests for reading. If a question depends on a term she has not defined above, define it inside the question. Think carefully about which of these questions she can answer comfortably and revise until none of them is comfortable.
