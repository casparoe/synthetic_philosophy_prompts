I serve on the advisory board of a small research center on mass atrocities. We can support roughly four people for three years, and we have deliberately chosen to fund conceptual and normative work rather than fieldwork, case documentation, or quantitative conflict research. What I need from you is a defended research agenda: which open questions in this area deserve the money, and in what order, with the reasoning made explicit.

Two clusters keep dominating our board discussions. The first concerns what the category 'genocide' is actually for. It began as a moral and political diagnosis of a distinctive wrong, was later hardened into a legal offense with a demanding mental element, and is now used at once as a finding by tribunals, a verdict by historians, and a speech act by states and activists; these roles pull in different directions, and it is unclear whether a single definition can serve all of them. The second concerns the recurring politics of naming: whether an atrocity is called genocide often seems to depend less on the facts than on who is doing the naming and what duties the label would trigger, and yet the label is also supposed to track something real and independent of the namer.

I want two proposed reframings treated as serious candidates rather than curiosities, because they are the reason I am asking. The first is that the operative idea of destroying a protected group as a group should be replaced or supplemented by an account on which the distinctive wrong is the destruction of a group's conditions of social existence, so that forced assimilation, the suppression of a language, and the dismantling of a group's institutions could count even in the absence of mass killing. The second is that the deepest problem is not the definition but the act of naming itself, and that the productive questions are about when such an act should be performed, by whom, with what authority, and what it allocates -- standing, duties to intervene, permission to use force, or merely memory.

Please prioritize the open questions in this area and defend the ordering. Use criteria such as how upstream a question is (how many other questions depend on settling it), how tractable it is for conceptual and normative researchers rather than lawyers or empiricists, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and how much the answer would actually change practice. Say which questions you would deprioritize because their answer would not change much downstream. For each top question, say what evidence or argument over the next few years would move it up or down the list.

Finally, be explicit about the state of play: separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
