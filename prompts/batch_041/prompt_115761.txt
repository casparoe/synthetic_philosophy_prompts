Our little art school has this tradition where a faculty member gives the commencement address, and this year they've handed it to me. I teach letterform design there — twenty-two years now — and this year's graduating class has six type-design students in it who have spent four years fighting with me about what actually makes an 'a' an 'a'. I want to build the speech on that fight, because it's the truest thing I know and, weirdly, it's the right thing to say to a room full of people about to go out and make things. So write the speech for me.

Here's the argument the way I'd put it, so you know what you're working with. There's no feature that every 'a' has. The two-storey one — the kind you're reading right now, bowl with a little hook arched over the top — and the one-storey one, a circle with a stem down the right side, the way most people write it by hand, have no shape in common. Some 'a's have a tail, some are open at the top, some are one unbroken stroke. What makes them all 'a' is a job, not a shape: an 'a' is whatever can do what 'a' does among the letters — stay unmistakably itself and not an 'o' or an 'e' or a 'd', hold its place in a word at any size, still read when it's smudged or enormous or six pixels tall. Style is the other half. Hand a good type designer four letters of a new face and she'll draw you the other twenty-two and they'll all sound like one voice — not because they share some feature but because they share a way of making decisions. There's no essence sitting inside the letter waiting to be discovered. There's a job, and there's a way of doing the job.

What I need it to do. It has to land for the parents and grandparents in the room, who dont know a font from a typeface and who will start worrying about four minutes in that I'm just a woman who cares alot about the letter a. It has to land for the six type students, who know this argument cold and will hear it instantly if I soften it into something safe — if I turn there's no essence, there's a job and a way of doing it into just be yourself, man. Those aren't the same thing and I don't want to say the second one. It also shouldn't come out as it's all arbitrary, none of it means anything, because I don't believe that either — the job is real, that's the whole point. And it can't be a lecture: no jargon, no family resemblance talk, no diagrams, nothing that sounds like a seminar. Give the non-designers something concrete to look at or do for a second — a letter on a page, whatever you come up with — so they're not just listening to me talk about letterforms.

The thing I actually care about, under the surface, is that a voice is only audible against constraints. You can't be distinctive at nothing. I'd rather the graduates feel that than be told it. Five or six minutes out loud. And please dont end on some go-change-the-world note, I'd have to sit down.
