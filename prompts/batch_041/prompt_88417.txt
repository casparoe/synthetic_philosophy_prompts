I am assembling a dissertation prospectus in the philosophy of physics and would like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a full proposal. The idea concerns the argument, associated with Gödel's rotating cosmological solution, that time is not objective. As it is ordinarily presented, the argument goes: general relativity admits a solution describing a rotating universe in which closed timelike curves pass through every point and in which there is no global division of events into earlier and later; since the theory we take to describe the actual world permits a world with no global time order, our conviction that reality is stratified into successive presents cannot be a report on the world's structure, and time in the robust sense is ideal. My dissatisfaction is that the conclusion appears far stronger than the premise. Three claims are usually run together under the heading of the objectivity of time: that events stand in objective before-and-after relations; that time has a distinguished direction; and that there is an objective present, or a passage from moment to moment, common to the whole world. A closed timelike curve plainly disrupts something, but it is not evident that it disrupts all three alike — local temporal order survives in such a spacetime, and so, arguably, does a local arrow. I suspect the argument's real target is the third claim alone, and that this has a consequence both its friends and its critics miss.

The reconstruction I want to test, and which I have not seen made explicit, is this: the step from some solution of the field equations having a feature to time in the actual world being ideal is not a physical inference at all but a transcendental one, resting on a premise about what must hold for us to have the experience of succession. If that is right, the argument's force depends entirely on whether such a premise can be defended without assuming the conclusion, and the interesting question becomes whether any such premise can be.

Please turn this into a prospectus containing: a statement of the question precise enough to have a determinate answer — and, if my formulation does not, the nearest question that does; an account of why the two standard positions, the one treating the argument as decisive and the one dismissing rotating solutions as unphysical curiosities, leave my question open; the method, where I would prefer conceptual analysis of the argument's premises and inferential steps to any further physics, along with an honest assessment of whether a purely conceptual treatment can settle the matter or whether it will require a verdict on a physical question; what a successful outcome would look like, what would count as the project failing, and what the deliverable is at the end of a single semester of full-time work; and a realistic schedule. Do not supply citations or a literature review; I will fill those in myself.
