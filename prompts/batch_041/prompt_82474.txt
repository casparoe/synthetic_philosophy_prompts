For the better part of a year my reading group has been stuck on the boundary problem, and I would like to force us to produce the document we should have written months ago rather than relitigating the same three examples over cheap wine. Our disagreement is not about who should vote in any actual country; it is about what makes someone belong to a demos in the first place, given that any answer has to be enacted by a body that already presumes a boundary — the circularity is the whole difficulty, and we agree on at least that much. We have settled into two camps. One of us holds the all-affected principle: whoever is significantly affected by a collective decision has a claim to a say in making it, so the demos ought to be drawn wide enough to take them in. The other holds the all-subjected principle: what grounds inclusion is not being affected but being authoritatively bound — having one's rights, duties, or legal standing determined by the decision — which is narrower, less indeterminate, and, he insists, tracks the distinctive wrong of being governed without a voice. I want a joint statement the two of us could sign in good faith, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than papered over.

Keep it concrete and in the weeds by building around the case we keep circling: an upstream state proposes to dam a river; two downstream states will be badly affected by the changed flow but are bound by nothing the upstream state decides; a fourth state is bound by the decision only because a pre-existing trade treaty incorporates the upstream state's environmental standards. Use that case to press both principles, and also test a hybrid that neither of us currently holds — graduated inclusion, under which affected-but-not-subjected parties get a voice without a vote while subjected parties get full membership — as a possible point of convergence or as a device that exposes what each principle is really committed to. Do not assume the two principles coincide in this case; if you think one of them collapses into the other, say so and show where.

Format the document as follows, in this order. First, "Agreed Claims": a numbered list of eight to twelve single-sentence claims both sides could sign, including at least one about why the boundary problem is a genuine problem rather than a confusion and one about what would count as a decisive objection to either principle. Second, "Residual Disagreements": a table with one row per contested question and columns for the question in neutral wording, the all-affected answer phrased so its own side would accept the phrasing, the all-subjected answer phrased likewise, and the crux that row turns on. Third, "Cruxes": three or four short paragraphs, each naming a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side, and saying which side and why. Fourth, "Prospective Tests": for each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about how the river case or the graduated-inclusion hybrid should come out. Fifth and finally, "Verbal Disputes": a short paragraph identifying any part of the quarrel that is merely terminological, if there is one, and why the rest is not.
