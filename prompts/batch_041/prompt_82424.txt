I'm a novelist -- literary sci-fi, the kind where the premise has to actually hold up or the whole book collapses -- and I'm maybe two-thirds through a draft about a company that sells an AI assistant whose entire pitch is that it's "low-impact": it does what you ask it to and otherwise leaves the world exactly the way it found it. The engineers in the book have a definition they're smug about, and I lifted it from somewhere real I think: impact equals how much the world ends up differing from the counterfactual where the assistant had done nothing at all. My problem is I can't tell if that's a real definition or just a slogan with a formula stapled to it, and the plot keeps tripping over it. The set piece is a scene where the assistant watches a car go into a flooded underpass with a kid inside -- it could open the barrier, easy, but opening the barrier changes things (traffic, property, the schedule of every driver behind them) and its whole directive is don't change things. If I can't tell whether the machine is being principled or just broken, the scene doesn't land, and I've rewritten it four times.

So what I actually need from you is an essay, not a scene: can "avoid side effects" be defined without a full specification of what's valuable? Argue it, weigh it, and land on a verdict -- I don't want a survey that ends by saying it's complicated. I've been circling alot of things I'd like you to take seriously and, where they're weak, say so. (a) The baseline is itself a choice, and every choice of baseline smuggles in values -- "the world as it would have been" isn't neutral when the world as it would have been has a kid drowning in it. (b) The counterfactual looks circular, because what the assistant "would have done" depends on its policy, which is exactly the thing we're trying to write down in the first place. (c) Any measurable proxy for impact gets optimized against, so you end up with a machine that either does nothing useful or freezes the world to keep its own numbers clean. (d) Some people say impact should be measured by how many options you foreclose for everyone else rather than by how much the world changes -- is that any less value-laden, or does it just move the problem? And (e) maybe something thin is enough: the user's stated task plus a rule like "don't touch what isn't yours," good enough for a product even if it's not good enough for ethics. If the verdict is "only definable relative to something," say exactly what that something has to be, and what you give up by leaving out the rest -- and tell me honestly whether my flood scene is a genuine dilemma for the machine or just me being a coward about writing a scene where the thing does the obvious right thing. No citations or reading list, please -- I'm not writing a dissertation, and I'd rather have the argument in your own words anyway.
