In my graduate seminar on natural kinds, a colleague keeps settling disputes with the phrase "carving nature at its joints." Last week he used it to insist that a taxonomy embedded in a clinical protocol is correct because the underlying biology carves nature at its joints; this week he used it in the opposite direction, to dismiss as "merely pragmatic" a classification that its defenders had supported with inductive and explanatory considerations. I want a disambiguation I can hand to the seminar. Set out the distinct precise theses the phrase might be expressing, arranged as a menu from the nearly tautological to the fully metaphysical: which of them are claims about the world, which about our concepts and kind terms, and which about scientific practice; which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or unfalsifiable; which are compatible with one another and which are mutually exclusive; and, for each defensible reading, what would have to be true of a classification for it to satisfy that reading. Then say which reading is actually doing the work in each of my colleague's two uses, and whether the use survives on that reading.
