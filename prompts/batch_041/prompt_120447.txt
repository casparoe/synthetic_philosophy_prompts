I'm a retired physiologist — thirty years of lab work on motor control, and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy of mind slowly and in the evenings. I've ended up fairly convinced by the causal exclusion argument. The lecturer who runs the undergrad philosophy of mind course I've been sitting in on has now asked me to take one session, and I'd rather run it as a game than stand at a whiteboard. I want them to feel the squeeze themselves, before anyone says the word "exclusion".

Here's what I've got. Knock it into a runnable 90-minute session for about 18 students — second-years, they've read a bit of Descartes and not much else.

Everyone gets a card. Each card names one candidate cause of a single outcome: a student reaching for a glass of water. Some cards are physical (a motor neuron fires, the flexor contracts, the retina registered the glass), some are mental (her desire to drink, her intention to reach, her belief that the glass is in front of her). Each card has a dial on it. There's a judge — me, or a student I've briefed — with a rule sheet, and the rule is: your card counts as a cause only if you can point to two settings of your dial that give different outcomes while every other dial is held fixed. I've got lookup sheets saying what happens at each setting.

The sting is that every mental dial is bolted to a physical dial. Turn the physical one and the mental one moves with it; you can't move the mental one on its own. So the mental players can never produce the required difference, and the physical players always can. They'll fight it, and I want them to fight it, because each escape route they try is one of the argument's premises in disguise — "it's the same thing really" (identity), "then it's caused twice over" (overdetermination), "the mental isn't fixed by the physical" (fine, show me two worlds identical down to the last ion with different mental states — and I hand them the rule sheet that says there aren't any).

So: the rules, the materials, the exact wording of the judge's sheet and the lookup sheets, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps each failed escape route onto the piece of the argument it stands in for. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I've got a feeling its going to be somebody announcing the whole thing is rigged and refusing to play, and I want to know what to do when that happens.

Two things I want you to be straight with me about. First, if this only works because I've written the conclusion into the judge's rules — if what the students "discover" is just my premise wearing a costume — say so plainly, and tell me whether that's fixable or whether I should just own the rigging and make it part of the debrief. Second, tell me honestly what this format can't get across at all, the thing I'll have to stand up and say myself. And if 18 is too many for it to work, say that too rather than padding the design.
