I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy or economics. Recently I sat in on a reading group about uncertainty, and two members got into an argument about Keynes that I could not adjudicate. I stayed out of it and am reporting it as a bystander. I would like you to go through their exchange and give a point-by-point verdict: who is right about which specific claims, where each is overreaching, and what both are missing.

The first member, Maya, opened like this: Keynes's central insight is that probability is a logical relation between evidence and hypothesis—a degree of partial implication—and that not every pair of propositions has a determinate probability. Keynes also emphasized cases, such as the prospect of a war or the long-run price of a commodity, where there is no scientific basis for a calculable probability. So the lesson is that we face genuine uncertainty, not merely risk. Expected utility theory assumes probabilities that often do not exist.

The second member, Dev, replied: That sounds dramatic, but I think it is a confusion. If you cannot assign a single probability, you can still assign a set of probabilities, or a second-order probability over your first-order estimates. What Keynes called the weight of argument is just the amount of relevant evidence, and that can be modeled as the precision of your credence: high weight means low variance in your posterior. So Keynesian uncertainty is just imprecise or high-variance probability. It does not escape the expected utility framework; it enriches it.

Maya answered: That is exactly the translation Keynes would reject. Weight is not the variance of a subjective posterior. For Keynes, probability is a logical relation, not a psychological degree of belief. Weight is the balance of relevant knowledge and relevant ignorance. You can have high weight for a probability of 0 or 1, and low weight for a probability of one-half. More importantly, some evidence-hypothesis pairs have no probability relation at all—not a wide interval, but none. Turning that into a second-order distribution assumes a probability space over the very thing Keynes says may not be probabilifiable.

Dev came back: But if there is no probability relation, what does it mean to say one option is more rational? You still have to act. And any coherent decision rule—maximin, minimax regret, avoiding irreversible commitments, keeping options open—can be represented as expected utility maximization with a suitably chosen utility function over sets of outcomes. So even if the probabilities are indeterminate, the choice problem is not outside decision theory. It is just a different decision theory. And if you say the rational thing is to avoid irreversible commitments, that is a substantive risk attitude, not a refutation of expected utility.

Maya replied: That is the vacuity objection. If any behavior can be represented as expected utility by tweaking the utility function, then the representation does no explanatory work. Keynes's point is not that people have unusual utility functions; it is that the rational basis for action is sometimes a judgement of the weight of evidence, not a calculation of expected value. In those cases, the rational thing may be to postpone action, diversify, or rely on convention, precisely because the probabilities are not determinate enough to support maximization. Saying that this is just a utility function loses the distinction between cases where we have a solid basis and cases where we do not.

Dev said: I agree the distinction matters practically. But you are treating the absence of probability as a metaphysical fact when it is often an epistemic limitation. If a perfectly rational agent with all the evidence still could not assign a probability, then maybe the event is genuinely non-probabilistic. But in most real cases—war, commodity prices—we do have some information, and imprecise probabilities capture that. Keynes's rhetoric about there being no scientific basis is about the limits of frequentist statistics, not about the nonexistence of credences. And his logical theory has its own problems: if probability is a logical relation, why should it guide action at all? The bridge from partial entailment to rational choice is never fully built.

Maya answered: The bridge is exactly what the weight of argument supplies. You do not need a precise probability to have a rational basis; you need enough relevant evidence to make a judgement. And Keynes's logical theory is not meant to be a psychological description; it is normative. The fact that it is hard to formalize does not mean the alternative—treating every uncertainty as a probability—is better. In fact, treating non-probabilistic uncertainty as imprecise probability can be dangerous: it gives a false sense of precision and encourages the same mathematical hubris Keynes was warning against.

Please adjudicate their exchange. I want a point-by-point verdict, not just a declaration of a winner. In particular: (a) Is Dev's second-order or imprecise-probability reconstruction of Keynes's weight of argument faithful, or does it miss something essential? (b) Did Keynes actually hold that some events have no probability, or is that an overreading of his remarks about uncertainty? (c) Is Dev right that any decision rule under uncertainty can be represented as expected utility with a different utility function, and if so, does that make Maya's appeal to a non-maximizing rationality vacuous? (d) What is the strongest version of each side's position, and what is each side missing? Where the dispute turns on a technical point, explain it in terms I can follow.
