I've got two mates who have been arguing about Goodhart's law for about a year now and I want to force them to actually write something joint instead of scoring points past each other. One of them (call her the unificationist) thinks Goodhart's law names one real phenomenon: optimise a proxy hard enough and it stops tracking the thing you care about, and that this is structural, a feature of proxy-optimisation as such, so it turns up in evolution and in RL training and not just in human institutions. The other (the deflationist) thinks the name is a ragbag covering things we already understand perfectly well -- measurement noise and regression effects, selection on a correlate, incentives to misreport, adversarial gaming, plain model misspecification. Shes fond of saying that any half-decent decision theorist with the feedback loop written into her causal model would have predicted every single case, so the law carries no predictive content of its own.

Write the joint statement the two of them could both sign. It should have:

- a numbered list of claims they agree on, including claims about what the phenomenon is *not*, and about which of my four cases below they classify the same way
- the genuine disagreements, each one phrased so that the side it belongs to would accept the wording as fair
- the cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling it would actually move at least one of them
- for each crux, a case or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction, written down in advance
- a note on any part of the dispute that turns out to be purely verbal, and why

Things I want them to fight over explicitly rather than dodge:

1. Is there a unified core, or does everything fall out of one of: noise, selection, incentive misalignment, adversarial gaming? If the four aren't seperate -- if the noise one and the extremes one are really the same thing seen at different scales -- say so, and say which is basic.
2. Does the law need an optimiser, or does it need an adversary? Test case: one agent, deterministic environment, no noise, nobody else in the world, but a metric that only covers part of what she cares about. Does she still get Goodharted?
3. In social cases, does the target exist independently of the metric at all? School quality, research excellence, patient outcomes -- if the target is partly constituted by the practice of measuring it, is the claim that the measure stopped tracking the target even well-formed?
4. Drift versus capture: is the slow decorrelation of a metric the same phenomenon as the metric getting built into the definition of the goal itself? Is the second one part of the law, or a seperate political process wearing its clothes?
5. Is there a scalar worth calling the pressure -- roughly, how much target-relevant variation the optimiser can move that the metric is blind to, multiplied by how much it pays her to move it? Is that measurable even in principle? Does the law only bite above some dose of it, and if so is the degradation smooth or does it come in steps?
6. Practical upshot: does the law license advice beyond get your causal model right and dont optimise a bad metric? Keep slack in the system, rotate the metric, use several incommensurable ones, keep it partly hidden from the people being measured. The deflationist should say whether any of that is more than good decision theory; the unificationist should say what a perfectly well-specified optimiser still gets wrong.

My four cases, for the classification bit: (a) a university funds departments by citations per paper and papers get shorter and more numerous; (b) a health service bonuses hospitals for lower readmission rates and hospitals start reclassifying patients as not yet admitted; (c) a firm manages to quarterly earnings and R&D quietly thins out; (d) a government pays clinics per patient seen and clinics start seeing more patients for less time each. One phenomenon four times, or four different things? Say where the two of you classify them differently.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements as a table with a column for the question, a column for each side's position in its own words, a column saying whether it's a crux, and a column for the agreed test with both predictions. It should read like something both of them would actually put their names to -- no cheap wins for either side, and no formulations so hedged they say nothing.
