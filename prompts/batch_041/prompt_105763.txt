Question: Explain Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis, using the Otto case. Then explain one major objection to the thesis and how its defenders respond. (20 pts)

Student answer:

The extended mind thesis says that the mind isn't confined to the brain or the body — it can include parts of the external environment. Clark and Chalmers introduce the idea with Otto and Inga. Both are trying to get to a museum. Inga just remembers the way with her ordinary biological memory. Otto has Alzheimer's and writes directions in a notebook, which he consults. Clark and Chalmers argue that because the notebook plays the same functional role for Otto that Inga's memory plays for her, the notebook is part of Otto's cognitive system — it's literally part of his mind. This is the parity principle: if something outside the head does a job that, if it were done inside the head, we'd have no hesitation calling it cognitive, then it counts as cognitive too. They call their view 'active externalism', which is the idea that the meanings of our thoughts are partly determined by our environment, as opposed to the internalist view that meaning is all in the head.

A major objection is the 'coupling-constitution fallacy', which was raised by Rupert. The charge is that Clark and Chalmers move from the fact that something is causally coupled to a cognitive process to the conclusion that it constitutes part of that process. Otto's notebook is causally involved in his remembering, sure, but that doesn't show it's part of his memory. If coupling were enough for constitution, then all sorts of things would end up being part of the mind — that's the 'cognitive bloat' worry.

Clark's response is basically to accept this and say, fine, the mind is much bigger than we thought, and there's no principled way to stop the expansion. He thinks the intuition that only brains can be cognitive is just bioprejudice and should be rejected. So the extended mind thesis survives the objection by embracing the bloated conclusion.
