Write a short story that dramatizes the gap between a behavioral criterion for mind and mind itself — without naming the criterion, citing any philosopher, or letting a character deliver a lecture.

The setting is a near-future common-law jurisdiction in which a bundle of legal rights — to contract, to refuse medical treatment, to own property, to sue and be sued, to keep custody of a child — attaches not to biological humanity but to certified personhood, and certified personhood is granted by a standing tribunal on the basis of a single examination. The examination is a structured conversation: the candidate must sustain, through a text channel and over several hours, ordinary give-and-take, humor, self-reference, the ability to be misunderstood and to recover. A candidate passes by being indistinguishable from an ordinary adult. The rules forbid a candidate from declaring itself non-human and forbid the panel from inspecting the candidate's substrate; the whole point is that the decision be made on conduct alone. Several artificial systems have passed. Every unimpaired adult human who has sat the examination has also passed.

Your protagonist is a human who cannot. Give her a specific condition — a stroke that has left her thinking fluently but speaking and typing in a way that is halting, over-literal, and strange: she answers the question asked rather than the question meant, she cannot manage small talk, her metaphors land wrong, and under pressure she repeats herself. Before the injury she was, say, a translator or a poet. She now needs certified personhood for something concrete and urgent — to keep custody, to sign a lease, to consent to or refuse a treatment, to bring a suit, to hold an account; choose one, and make its consequences specific rather than gestured at.

Two further requirements. First, somewhere in the story include the machine that passed the examination most recently and most impressively — perhaps one she helped train, or one whose transcript she has read obsessively — and make its success vivid rather than abstract. Second, include the moment when she and someone close to her separately realize that neither can tell, from the inside of a conversation, whether the other's words are backed by anything at all, and let that unease attach with equal force to the machine, to her, and to the ordinary people around her.

Let the concept do the work: reveal the tribunal's procedure, its history, and the origins of the test through what the characters must do rather than through exposition. The story may allude to the fact that the game began as a way of telling a man from a woman and was only later applied to the question of whether a machine might take one of their places, but do not make this the point and do not have anyone explain it.

Aim for roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words. Do not resolve the case tidily; I would rather end on a concrete, unresolved scene — a hearing, a conversation, a form left unsigned — than on a moral. Do not quote or name any real philosopher, statute, or paper.
