I teach a second-term logic unit and the Russell paradox is the point where everyone nods along and then quietly decides the whole thing is a swindle. I want a one page decision tree I can hand out that starts from the most naive position anyone actually holds — every predicate I can write down determines a set, and you get to run the derivation on the set of all sets that aren't members of themselves — and then splits that starting point into the positions you can land on, where every branch node is a question a student can answer on they're own without knowing which label sits at the end of it. The distinctions I care about are the ones that genuinely come apart: first, do you keep classical logic and the steps that turn the paradox into a contradiction (contraction, excluded middle, reductio), or do you cut back the comprehension schema; and if you keep the logic and cut the schema, do you cut by size (nothing collects too much), by syntactic form (only stratified predicates, or only positive ones), by predicativity (no impredicative definitions), or by level of language (that predicate just isn't in the base language)? Then: does your repair keep a universal set, does it keep the offending collection around as something (a proper class, an atom, a non-set object), does it keep extensionality, and does it keep one universe of sets or go structuralist, or plural-quantifier and say there are no sets at all just pluralities? Aim for 9 to 12 leaves, name them where a name exists (ZFC-style iterative, NBG-ish, simple types, ramified types, NF-style stratified, positive, paraconsistent/dialetheist, intuitionistic/constructive, structuralist, no-sets error theory), give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, mark the combinations of answers nobody has occupied and say briefly whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any leaves my tree cant really separate.
