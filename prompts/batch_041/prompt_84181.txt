I chair the protocol review committee at a psychiatric research institute, and we meet on Thursday to decide whether to approve a trial that has already passed scientific review and is otherwise sound. The investigators want to test deep brain stimulation for severe, treatment-resistant anhedonia, in patients who report that almost nothing gives them pleasure anymore. The primary endpoint is a single number on a fifteen-item scale the team has adapted from a pain inventory; they call it a hedonic tone score, and their power calculation treats every patient's reported pleasure as the same kind of quantity. In the pilot, patients described their improvements in ways that do not obviously line up. One said music now had a buzz it had never had. Another said what she felt was mostly relief that the flatness was gone. A third said he wanted to do things again but still did not much enjoy them. A fourth said he liked things again without feeling pleasure in them. The investigators say these are simply different routes to one underlying state, that pleasure is a single positive hedonic tone, and that our committee is not the place to settle philosophy. One of my members, who works on the philosophy of emotion, says the endpoint is incoherent: if you ask what the buzz, the relief, the wanting, and the liking have in common, you get either nothing or something so thin it cannot anchor a measurement, and a single scale will average together states that are not the same. She wants us to require a multi-dimensional measure that separates sensory pleasure, relief, wanting, and liking, and to strike the word pleasure from the consent form. The investigators reply that they do not need a common felt quality: what unites these states is that the patient likes them and is disposed to seek them out, and that is what the scale tracks. My member counters that the fourth patient, who liked things without feeling pleasure in them, shows that liking and pleasure can come apart, so liking cannot be what the scale is measuring if it claims to measure pleasure, and that if the scale is really measuring liking then the consent form and the paper should say so. The investigators also warn that a multi-dimensional endpoint would leave the trial unpowered and effectively kill it. I have to circulate the committee's written opinion before Thursday. Please recommend a decision — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and give the reasoning the investigators can actually respond to, including the strongest point on whichever side I end up rejecting. If the right answer is a condition, say exactly what it should require and what it should forbid, and tell me what conception of pleasure the committee is thereby committing itself to. Write it as continuous prose with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables, the way I would write a formal letter to the committee.
