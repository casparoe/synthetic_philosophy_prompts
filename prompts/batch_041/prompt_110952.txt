I'm a science-and-ideas journalist assembling an application for a one-year residential fellowship, and the foundation wants a proper research proposal. If I get the fellowship, the deliverable would be a long-form piece for a general readership plus a short conceptual framework I can circulate to editors as a companion; I am not asking you to draft the article, only the proposal that would win the year.

My half-formed project concerns the claim, familiar from Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self, that a person is a pattern rather than a substance and therefore persists, to some degree, in the minds of others. I find the picture moving and suspect it is doing two jobs at once that ought to be kept apart. The first is the ordinary, undeniable fact that other people carry models of us: partial, lossy, and shaped by their own minds. The second is a survival claim, that this carrying is itself a form of continued existence. The strange-loop view seems to license the second, but only if the pattern lodged in the other person is genuinely the same pattern, and I doubt it usually is. My working idea is that the distributed-self thesis equivocates between a brain's holding a model of someone and its re-running that someone's loop, and that once the two are prised apart, nearly all of the intuitive pull attaches to the model side while the survival claim quietly depends on the re-instantiation side. What I want the project to answer is narrow enough to be answerable: what would have to be true of a second brain's state for it to count as an instance of the first person rather than a representation of them, and which of the candidate conditions — fidelity, causal power, first-person perspective, or some further feature of the loop — is doing the real work? A test case I keep turning over: two people who each knew someone well hold two partial models; if the models were merged, or if each were made faithful enough, would we have survival, and if not, what is missing that more fidelity could not supply?

Write the proposal for me. State the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions — the copying-and-uploading debate, the memory-and-identity tradition, and the strange-loop picture itself — leave it open; set out the approach and why a journalist's method (reporting among researchers, people who build digital replicas of the dead, and bereaved people who describe the dead as surviving in them) does conceptual work here rather than merely gathering color; say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and what the honest scope is for twelve months. I want the framing to be mine, not the standard one, so please resist the temptation to retreat into the familiar uploaded-mind hypothetical. Do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation; I will add references myself once the argument is settled, and I would rather the proposal refer to positions and arguments than to titles.
