So I've been chewing on the Moore stuff — Principia Ethica, the beauty-and-friendship parts — and I think I've basically talked myself into the view. Roughly: the things that are good in themselves are enjoying beautiful things and loving particular people, friendship and affection and that whole family; neither one is good because of what it gets you, and you can't cash either of them out as pleasure or as desire-satisfaction without losing something. And then the part I find weirdly convincing even though it looks like it should be a mess: wholes aren't sums. A friendship isn't the total of the good moments inside it — the whole can be worth more, or less, than all the parts added up, and the value of the parts can change depending on what else is in the whole. Same trick with a beautiful object and someone's awareness of it.

Now: what would count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd actually be, from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which of them a committed Moorean would themselves concede as a real cost and which they'd just wave off. A few things I keep circling, but don't feel limited to these:

Does the view really imply that loving someone gorgeous is a bigger good than loving someone plain? If it does, is that a bullet to bite or a reductio? And if the move is to say beauty means something thinner there, is that a dodge or a legitimate disambiguation?

The landscape nobody ever sees. Moore's isolation method says a world containing just a beautiful landscape beats a world containing just an ugly one, even with no one around to appreciate either. Is that credible on its own, or does it only fall out of the method? Which way does it have to go for the view to stay standing — because it feels like the two answers each cost him something.

The method itself. Does asking what would be good in a world with nothing else in it actually test anything, or does it just read off intuitions we already have? Can it ever come out against the view, or is it a machine for confirming? If it can't disconfirm, is that a defect or just what any intuition-based ethics looks like?

A modern one I can't get out of my head: a long, deep, mutually-shaped relationship with an AI companion, where the thing on the other end isn't a person at all. The view says personal affection is the good. If it says the value is the same as a real friendship, is that a problem? And if it says the value is zero, can it explain why without quietly smuggling in a claim about the nature of the object — which is exactly what it said didn't matter?

Same family: a friendship you never find out was entirely one-sided, or was performed. Does the view track the relationship as it seemed from inside, or the facts about it? Which one does it have to track to stay coherent, and does that commit it to something ugly?

What does the view predict that hedonism or desire-satisfaction or a plainer objective-list view doesn't? What's the observation or case that should actually make me drop it — and be honest about whether the answer is nothing, because organic unities plus good being unanalyzable plus intuition as the only court of appeal might mean the view can absorb literally any result. If it can, tell me whether that's a fatal flaw or just the price of the position, and don't fudge it.

What would move me most is either a case where I'd have to call something monstrous or absurd to keep the view, or a rival that handles the same intuitions more simply. I don't need cites or a reading list, just the arguments and the cases.
