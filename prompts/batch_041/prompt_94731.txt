You are a philosopher of social norms with a specialisation in coordination problems and the morality of convention — someone equally at home in analyses of convention as a solution to recurrent coordination problems, in the literature on how moral obligations arise from, attach to, or fail to attach to social practices, and in the mundane anthropology of how strangers actually behave in queues, restaurants, and crosswalks. Work carefully and systematically. Do not answer from a general impression, and do not decline to give numbers.

I am building an argument about (i) whether everyday conventions are moral obligations at all and (ii) how far they bind people who do not share them. Before rating anything, write one short paragraph — 150 words at most — setting out what would have to be true for a convention to be obligatory, so that your ratings are anchored in a view rather than in intuition. Then produce the ratings.

Rate the following eleven everyday conventions:

1. Leaving a tip of roughly the customary percentage after table service at a full-service restaurant in a country where the practice is entrenched.
2. Tapping the suggested tip option on the payment tablet at a counter-service café.
3. Not stepping into a gap in a single-file queue, even when the gap would let you through faster and nobody would object.
4. Standing on the right and leaving the left for those who walk, on an escalator, in a country where that is the going convention.
5. Crossing mid-block against the light on a street with no traffic in sight.
6. Waiting for the pedestrian signal while a handful of strangers wait alongside you at the corner, on the same empty street.
7. Not taking the last item from a shared plate at a table of near-strangers.
8. Holding a door open for the person a few steps behind you.
9. Not taking a phone call on speaker on a crowded train.
10. Giving up your seat on a full bus to someone visibly older or visibly pregnant.
11. Keeping a one-seat buffer — not sitting directly beside a stranger when other seats are free.

Stipulated taxonomy, to be used as given rather than replaced with a familiar one. Call a convention a schedule convention when its value lies in everyone doing the same thing and not in which thing is done (which side of the escalator you stand on). Call it a contribution convention when its value lies in compliance, whichever variant is complied with, because each act adds to or subtracts from a shared good (speakerphone on the train). Call it a regard convention when its value lies in the attitude the act expresses toward a particular person (the seat, the door, the last item). Many items will be hybrids; say so rather than forcing a single label.

For each item, give: (a) insider bindingness — the strength of the moral obligation for someone who grew up with the convention, from 0.00 to 1.00; (b) outsider bindingness — the strength for a visitor who does not share the convention, does not know it, and has no stake in it, on the same scale; (c) the visitor multiplier, (b) divided by (a), flagged where it exceeds 1.00, with an explanation of what could make a convention bind a foreigner more tightly than a native; and (d) the type label, with a note on any item that is genuinely hybrid.

Then run three tests and report what each does to (a) and (b):

- The empty-street test: rerun the ratings with all bystanders removed. Which items survive, and which turn out to have been sustained entirely by an audience?
- The overnight-flip test: every convention on the list is reversed overnight by everyone else. For which items does the strength of the obligation change, and for which is it untouched?
- The all-visitors test: everyone in the town is a visitor, no one an insider. Which conventions retain a point at all?

Finally, assess the invariance hypothesis: the outsider bindingness of a convention is proportional to its convention-independent component, that is, to how much of its moral force would survive if the practice vanished and no one expected anything. Does your table support the hypothesis? Name the single item that is the strongest counterexample to it, and explain why. Close with a tier list of all eleven items from most to least plausibly a genuine moral obligation binding on anyone, state in one sentence the most defensible general principle you can extract about when a local convention binds a visitor, and name the one placement in the tier list you are least confident about and why. Where a rating depends on a factual assumption — the customary tip percentage, whether a service charge is already included, what the local traffic code says — state the assumption and rate under it rather than hedging around it. If two of your ratings cannot both be sustained, say which and revise one; do not paper over the tension. Present the per-item results as a table with one row per item.
