I'm a novelist — 300-odd pages into a historical novel set in the 1840s in the north of England — and I've been chewing on that Nietzsche essay about the uses and disadvantages of history for life, the monumental / antiquarian / critical thing, for about a year now. My editor is definately sick of hearing about it.

Here's what I want you to argue for, and argue it properly, not as a vibe. Two claims, and they're connected:

First: for a novelist, the antiquarian mode is the most dangerous of the three. More dangerous than the critical mode, which is the one everybody I know assumes is the dangerous one. I want the argument to turn on what antiquarian history actually does to somebody who's trying to make a thing — not just that too much research is bad, which I already know.

Second: I think what I'm doing in this book isn't any of the three, and isn't a blend of them either. I've started calling it the accomplice mode. I go looking for where the past dead-ended — the strike that broke, the scheme that got shelved, the person who was right too early — and I give it a second run at the world. Not wish fulfilment, not the South winning, but a counterfactual that's meant to tell you something true about how it actually went. Argue that this is genuinely a fourth thing: not critical history with a sympathetic face, not monumental history with a loser where the hero goes.

Then tell me where the argument is weakest, and what would have to be true for the accomplice mode to collapse back into one of the other three. If you think the whole distinction is a distinction without a difference, dont humour me — say so and argue that instead.
