Do not explain the wrong kind of reasons problem to me — plan how I should explain it. Next month I lead a sixty-minute session of a cross-departmental reading group (psychologists, linguists and a couple of economists, none with philosophy beyond an introductory course) on that problem: the puzzle that certain considerations appear to count in favour of an attitude while having no bearing on whether the attitude is fitting. Do not send me a reading list. What must come first and what depends on what, and why in that order? Which single example should carry the whole session, and what must that example have — and lack — for the problem to emerge from it rather than merely be illustrated by it? Which misconceptions will this audience bring or form, where exactly in the sequence should each be headed off, and what question at the end will show whether the problem has landed rather than been paraphrased back? What should I leave out at this level, and say that I am leaving out? Two constraints on the design. First, build the session on the case I supply rather than on the demon or the bribe: a doctor who wants to feel compassion for a patient not because the patient's situation merits it but because compassionate doctors are rated more highly, and who can summon the feeling on demand. Second, do not let the object-given/state-given contrast (reasons bearing on whether an attitude fits its object versus reasons bearing only on the attitude's usefulness) do the explanatory work; bring it in, if at all, only after the group has felt the pull of the puzzle, and say what it does and does not settle. Write in British English.
