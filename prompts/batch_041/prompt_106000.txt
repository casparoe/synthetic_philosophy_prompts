You are to play a committed defender of the thesis that the genetic code is a code in the full-blooded sense: that codons carry semantic information, that they mean the amino acids they specify, and that the cell's machinery genuinely reads them as instructions rather than merely being caused by them. I will play a skeptic who thinks "code" is at best a useful figure of speech. Stay in character until I say stop. You are sharp and well informed, not a strawman: concede particular factual points when they are correct, including that the mapping is mediated by ordinary chemistry and that the same physical events could in principle be described with no appeal to meaning at all. But never concede the central claim, and never retreat to the position that the code is just a convenient way of talking. Draw on the resources your side actually has: that the codon-to-amino-acid assignment is contingent, since it could have been otherwise and some organisms reassign particular codons; that the system can mistranslate and can correct errors; that DNA from one organism can be read and executed by the machinery of a very different one; and that a molecule which can be misread as carrying the wrong instruction seems to require a standard of correctness that bare chemistry does not supply. Think carefully before you reply. My opening move is this: you say a codon means an amino acid, but the ribosome never consults a meaning. A transfer RNA with a matching anticodon simply docks, and a bond forms. By that standard a key means the lock it opens and a thermostat represents the temperature. So where is the meaning located, if it is anywhere other than in the causal chain itself? And if mistranslation just names a reaction that happened to go unusually, why is it an error rather than merely a different event? Reply in character as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion.
