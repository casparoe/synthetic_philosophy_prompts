On Saturday I have to give the toast at the retirement dinner for my doctoral advisor, who has spent thirty-eight years working on one obscure genus of eyeless cave beetles. She described her first new species at twenty-six and her last at sixty-three, and in between the count of species in that genus went like this: twelve, in her dissertation; thirty-one, in a revision by her second student, who split aggressively; nine, in a revision by her fourth student, who applied a criterion that merged almost everything; and now, depending on which criterion you favour, somewhere between nine and thirty-four. The dinner is in the natural history museum where she has had the same office since before I was born, and the room will be roughly half systematists — colleagues and former students who have been arguing with her about those numbers for decades, some of them fondly, some of them not — and half family, including her sister, a high-school maths teacher who has asked "so how many kinds of bugs is it now?" at Thanksgiving every year for thirty years and has never once received the same answer twice.

What makes this hard is that the thing everyone is celebrating is precisely her demonstration that the question she spent her life on has no single correct answer. There is no criterion that partitions the living world into species across the whole of it: interbreeding fails for the asexual and for anything known only from fossils; morphology fails both for the animals that look identical and turn out to be six things and for the animals that look wildly different and turn out to be one; any genetic threshold is a number somebody chose. Her line, which I have heard her give in about nine different registers, is that we should stop asking whether a species concept is true and start asking what work we want the word to do — a unit of evolution, a unit of field identification, a unit of legislation are three different jobs, and the same beetles can honestly be nine species or thirty-four depending on which job the word has been hired to do. And yet her decisions escaped the seminar room. A quarry that never opened and a cave system that got fenced and gated exist because a population in one wet hollow was named as a species of its own; a later worker argued it was the same animal as one two hundred kilometres away, and she never pretended to be at peace about it. She also used to say, with real irritation, that her field's worst habit is treating a preferred criterion as a moral commitment rather than a tool.

So please write the five-minute toast. It needs to be funny in the places where the field is genuinely funny, and affectionate rather than clever about her; the systematists will forgive a joke at their own expense but not an inaccurate one, and her sister has to be able to follow the whole thing and laugh in the right places. It cannot claim she settled anything, cannot treat the disagreement as a charming quirk rather than the substance of her contribution, and cannot turn into a lecture on the species problem — the philosophy has to do structural work, deciding what can be said and in what order, rather than decorating a warm anecdote. Specifically, I want you to find a way to say the hardest thing in the room, that the line she drew was a decision and not a discovery, and that this makes her work less like map-making and more like law-making, in a form that lands as praise rather than as faint damnation. Don't invent biographical facts about her; where you need a concrete detail, use what I've given you or leave a bracketed blank for me to fill. And if you conclude that some part of this cannot be done in five minutes for that particular room, tell me which part, why, and what you would trade away to keep the rest.
