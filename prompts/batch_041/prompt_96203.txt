I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so I have framed this the way I would frame a specification problem, and I would like the answer to respect that framing even if it turns out to be the wrong framing.

In most U.S. jurisdictions the legal standard for death offers two disjunctive criteria: irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. What I cannot make sense of is whether these are two definitions of one thing, one definition with two tests, or two different things sharing a name. A patient whose whole brain has been destroyed can be maintained with a beating heart and working organs for years, and in the cases that get cited, such bodies heal wounds, grow, and can even carry a pregnancy to delivery. A patient whose heart has stopped can sometimes be restarted, and we do not then say they were briefly dead. So the circulatory criterion appears to require only that we have decided not to try, whereas the neurological criterion is supposed to be irreversible in a stronger sense. If both are sufficient conditions for the same predicate, the predicate looks incoherent.

I would like your credence in the following claim: there is a single fact of the matter about when a human being dies, and the correct account is that a human being dies if and only if the whole brain, including the brainstem, has irreversibly ceased to function, with the circulatory criterion counting at most as a practically reliable indicator that this condition has obtained or is imminent.

Please give a number, then decompose it into the sub-questions it depends on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each. The terms I would expect to matter, though you should add or drop them as you see fit: (1) whether death is a real biological kind at all, as opposed to a legal or social status that we stipulate; (2) whether the bearer of death is the organism or the person, and what follows for the brain criterion if a body can remain somatically integrated after the whole brain has died; (3) whether irreversibility should be read as impossible to reverse in principle, impossible with present technology, or merely not going to be reversed because we have chosen to stop; (4) whether the two legal criteria can be reconciled or whether one of them is silently doing all the work; and (5) how much weight the empirical facts about maintained brain-dead bodies should carry against the brain's traditional role as the integrator of the organism.

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, what evidence or argument would move it most, and whether the question I am asking is well posed in the first place. My own credence is around 0.6, but I suspect I am smuggling a definitional question into a place where an empirical one belongs.
