I'm putting together the final for my upper-level epistemology seminar (third and fourth years, plus a couple of grad students sitting in), and I want the one long essay to be on the dogmatism paradox. Every year they can recite the Gettier stuff in their sleep and I'm bored of it. But I keep getting the same non-answer: they state the paradox, say "so knowledge is dogmatic, spooky," and stop. I want the question to force them to actually locate where the thing bites.

The paradox as I teach it: if you know P, then you're in a position to know that any evidence against P is misleading, since P's being true means the evidence can't be right; and someone who knows a piece of evidence is misleading can rationally ignore it; so knowing P licenses you to ignore all counterevidence. My own view, which I want the exam to *test* rather than announce, is that the weak joint is the word "misleading" doing two jobs. Sense 1: the evidence isn't genuine at all — bad data, a lying witness, a misprint. Sense 2: the evidence is perfectly genuine, it just gets outweighed by what you've already got. To reach the scary conclusion you need sense 1; to make the premise feel obvious you need sense 2, because of course you may disregard a lying witness. The whole thing slides from one sense to the other. In my notes I've been calling that slide the misleader slide, for whatever thats worth.
