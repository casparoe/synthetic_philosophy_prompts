A friend and I have been arguing for weeks about whether you should defer to your own future opinions, and it has degenerated into a list of cases I keep rearranging on the back of an envelope. The principle we started from — one I have seen attributed to van Fraassen — is roughly that my credence in something now should already equal my expected credence in it later, so that if I am sure I will believe it tomorrow, I am committed to believing it today. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts actually support, stated as precisely as you can manage (a formula is welcome if it helps), together with what that principle predicts about the cases I cannot settle and about a case I have not thought of at all.

Here are the verdicts. Suppose I spend a week working out which of two urns holds the coin and arrive at 0.8; tonight I swallow a pill that erases the week's evidence, and tomorrow I will sit at 0.5. I think I should stay at 0.8, not slide to 0.5. Suppose instead that tomorrow morning a colleague who has read the full dataset tells me her credence, and I know I will land at 0.35 the moment I hear her; I think I should move to 0.35 tonight, before she opens her mouth. Suppose that I am drunk now, think I can drive perfectly well, and will be sober tomorrow with exactly the same information and a much lower credence: I think tomorrow's number is the one I should hold now, even though no new evidence will have arrived. But suppose I am at 0.4 tonight and know that tomorrow, having slept well, I will feel 0.9 about the very same question on no new evidence whatsoever; I do not think I should adopt the 0.9, and I think that is precisely because nothing about my evidence will have changed. I also do not think I am bound by what I believed a year ago, when I had read a fraction as much, so whatever the rule is, it does not run backwards. And if I knew a hypnotist would spend tonight planting a belief in me that I would mistake for my own conclusion, I would not move toward it.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the sobriety case and the mood case. Both leave my evidence untouched; in one I defer, in the other I do not. The only thing I can say is that being drunk is a handicap and being in a good mood is a bias, which feels like a distinction I invented after the fact to save my verdicts — a good mood does not make my arithmetic worse. My tentative principle is this: adopt the future credence exactly when the future self will be at least as good as I am at settling this particular question, meaning she will assess at least the same evidence with at least as much competence. Test it, repair it, or replace it. Two cases it seems to break on. First, the one I keep going back and forth on: tomorrow I will have the referee reports in hand, which is genuinely new evidence, but I will also be badly jet-lagged and will, as I know from experience, weigh them carelessly — do I move now toward whatever jet-lagged me will conclude, or do I hold my present view until I have recovered? Second, if I know that tomorrow I will be at 0.9 and that in a week, having thought it through and with no new evidence arriving in between, I will be at 0.1, then I seem licensed to adopt both, which cannot be right. Please tell me which of my verdicts, if any, the principle you land on forces me to abandon, and say how confident you are in it and which single step in your reasoning you are least sure of.
