Ok so I've got a 90-minute slot with my decision-theory reading group next week — about 15 people, a mix of philosophy and econ grad students plus one or two who do ML — and I want to do something that makes them feel the Ellsberg thing in their gut instead of nodding along, because right now I think they've all filed it under "yeah yeah, people hate ambiguity, whatever" and moved on. So: no lecture up front, and please don't just hand me the plain two-urn demo, everyone's seen that one and it's dead on arrival. Design me a runnable activity — real choices, staged in stages, fake stakes (points they cash in at the end for coffee or beer, nothing that needs actual money). I want them to walk into the usual pattern without being told that's what's happening — paying to avoid the urn whose mix nobody knows, even though there's nothing in the unknown urn that's obviously worse — and then I want to pry it open. Two moves I'm keen on. First, a later stage where the "unknown" urn's composition is itself decided in public, by a coin flip or a wheel, between two mixes everyone can see, so the second-order uncertainty is sitting right there on the table, and I can watch whether people still flinch away from it or whether the flinch only shows up when the unknown sits next to a nice clean risky option. Second, a stage where the ambiguous-looking bet is the one people actually reach for, so nobody can leave thinking ambiguity aversion is just a stable personality trait they happen to have. A friend of mine who does ML keeps saying "just put a prior on the mix and stop being weird about it," and honestly I want the activity to make that move feel either obviously right or obviously glib — I'm not sure which it is yet.

The thing I most want them to collide with is the move where the two-stage setup — first the mix gets fixed, then a ball gets drawn — is supposed to collapse into a plain one-stage bet for the purposes of rational choice, and to notice, out loud, whether their own choices actually collapse that way or whether they're quietly pricing the two stages seperately. So build in a moment where everyone commits numbers to paper before anyone compares (what they'd pay for each option, written down, no conferring), because I suspect the comparison is doing alot of the work and I want to catch them before they can coordinate on an answer. I'd rather not say the E-word until the debrief.

Then the debrief: map each stage onto a distinct moving part — the independence/sure-thing idea, the collapsing move, and the point that the aversion only shows up when the clean option is sitting right there next to the ambiguous one — and be straight with me about what the activity can't show. I'm already suspicious that it can't show the aversion is irrational, only that it violates an axiom people might cheerfully drop, and that a coin-flip between two known mixes is a model of ambiguity rather than ambiguity itself, so the second-order stage is a stand-in, and the cleverest two students will say exactly that. Tell me how to handle that in the room, rather than pretending it doesn't come up. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm guessing people anchor on the first stage and refuse to revise, someone demands to know the real composition and won't accept "there isn't one," and the whole thing overruns by 30 minutes — and what to do about each. A materials list, a rough timeline per stage, and whether to split them into groups or keep everyone choosing privately at the same time would be great.
