I am a fifth-year PhD candidate in the history of economic thought, and in three weeks I will present a dissertation chapter at a joint workshop of our economics and philosophy departments. The thesis I am defending is that Keynes's weight of argument, from the Treatise on Probability, is not a second dimension of evidential support standing alongside probability, as it is normally read, but a measure of how far a probability estimate would move if further relevant evidence were added — which would make it a forerunner of modern robustness and imprecision-based accounts of confidence rather than a forerunner of subjective Bayesianism. A corollary I am also committed to, and which I expect to be the more contentious half, is that the post-Keynesian appropriation of Keynes as the prophet of an ontological fundamental uncertainty gets him backwards: on my reading, the uncertainty passages of the 1930s mark a limit on the applicability of the numerical part of the logical theory, not a repudiation of the theory of rational belief itself.

The audience is not homogeneous, and the prep sheet needs to be organized around who is who. Roughly eight people will do the talking. Two are senior historians of interwar Cambridge economics who have worked on the Treatise and on its reception by Ramsey and who care intensely about whether there is continuity or rupture between the Treatise and the General Theory. Two are decision theorists who work on imprecise probability and who will regard the logical theory as unnecessary metaphysics, on the grounds that anything Keynes wants is already available as a set of probability functions. Two are post-Keynesians from our macro group, for whom uncertainty is an ontological feature of a non-ergodic economy and who will hear my reading as defanging Keynes. The last is a philosopher of science who works on confirmation and who will care only about whether weight is doing any work that a likelihood ratio or the width of an interval does not already do.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I will actually get in that room, grouped by camp and phrased in that camp's vocabulary: the historians will press me on textual evidence and on the arc from 1921 to 1936; the imprecise-probabilists will ask why I need a logical relation at all when I can have a set of distributions; the post-Keynesians will ask whether my Keynes can still explain why investment collapses; the confirmation theorist will ask what weight adds to a likelihood ratio. Order the objections by how likely each is to be raised times how much damage it does if I fumble it, and say plainly which ones are close to fatal. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, then one sentence on where that reply is weakest and what I should concede rather than defend. Tell me which objections I should simply grant, and where my thesis as stated above is ambiguous enough to invite a cheap shot. Do not pad the list with objections nobody in this room would raise, and do not summarize the Treatise back to me.

Keep the whole thing self-contained: no citations and no reading list, since I will not be able to check anything during the session. Paraphrase rather than quote, so that I do not end up with a misquotation in my notes.

Close with the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that would force me to give up either the stability reading of weight or the continuity claim if it were pressed.
