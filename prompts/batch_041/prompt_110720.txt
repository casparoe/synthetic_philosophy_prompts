A friend of mine has spent the last two years on a project I cannot get to the bottom of, and I would like your help either in talking him out of his conclusion or in accepting that he is right.

He took recordings of the nerve cells of a small invertebrate — the sort of creature you find under a log — as it was touched with something hot and tried to pull away. From those recordings he built a program that, for any input he gives it, reproduces the pattern of activity the animal's nervous system produced. Run side by side, the numbers coming out of his program and the numbers coming out of the animal are, so far as he can tell, indistinguishable. The program runs on an ordinary laptop.

He now says that he can no longer bring himself to close the laptop or delete the file, because he believes that while the program is running it is feeling pain. His argument is simple: the only difference between the laptop and the animal is the material it is made of. The activity is the same and the organization is the same, so the pain must be the same. He adds, as though it settles the matter, that he could have obtained the same outputs from a quite different arrangement of parts — a large enough table of pre-computed answers, say, or a set of water valves — and that he would then have to say the same thing about those as well.

He is not a crank; he is an engineer, and he will listen to an argument if it is a good one. I suspect that something has gone wrong in his reasoning, but I cannot say where. I am not asking for a general essay on machines and minds. I want to know whether his program is in pain, and if not, which precise step in his reasoning fails — whether the trouble lies in his claim that the laptop is doing the same thing as the animal, or in his claim that doing the same thing would be enough. And, practically: what should I tell him to do with the laptop?
