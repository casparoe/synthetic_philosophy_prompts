Write an essay on the following controversy, and reach a definite verdict.

Dutch book arguments are standardly used to show that an agent's degrees of belief must satisfy the probability calculus on pain of irrationality. The core of the argument is this: if an agent's degrees of belief violate the axioms of probability, then there is a finite collection of bets, each of which she regards as fair or favorable by her own lights, whose combined effect is to guarantee her a net loss whatever happens. (An agent who assigns degree of belief 0.4 to A, 0.4 to B, and 0.7 to their disjunction, where A and B are mutually exclusive, can be made to lose a fixed sum in every outcome.) A bookmaker who knows her degrees of belief can therefore construct a sure loss for her.

Reconstruct the argument with enough precision that its premises are explicit, and then determine what it does and does not establish. Your essay should adjudicate between two readings of its conclusion. On the pragmatic reading, it shows only that incoherent degrees of belief leave an agent open to exploitation, so that coherence is a prudential good for anyone who wishes to avoid sure losses. On the epistemic reading, it shows that incoherent degrees of belief are defective as representations of how things are, quite apart from any betting behavior. Address at least the following:

(a) The argument seems to presuppose that the agent is willing to gamble, that her utility is linear in the stakes, and that she will recognize the sure loss when it is put to her. Assess how much these presuppositions cost, and whether the argument can be repaired by weakening them.

(b) Consider an agent whose degrees of belief are incoherent but who neither gambles nor cares about money, and who therefore cannot be induced to accept any bet. Does the bare existence of a set of bets that she would regard as individually acceptable, and that guarantees a net loss, show that her credences are irrational? If not, can the argument be recast so that its conclusion concerns an internal defect in her credences -- their distance from the truth, say, rather than her expected financial loss?

(c) The same style of argument has been used to support the diachronic requirement that an agent update her degrees of belief by conditionalization on her evidence. Explain how such an argument would run, and consider whether it faces a difficulty peculiar to the diachronic case: the bookmaker must exploit the agent's own future degrees of belief, which she may not foresee or may later disown. Does the diachronic argument inherit the strengths of the synchronic one, or does it expose a flaw common to both?

(d) Some hold that an agent's degrees of belief may be imprecise -- represented by a set of probability functions rather than a single one -- without irrationality. Can a sure-loss argument be constructed against such an agent, or does imprecision provide an escape? What does your answer imply about whether the argument establishes a requirement of precision as well as of coherence?

Do not end on a non-committal note. State clearly whether Dutch book arguments establish a genuine requirement of rationality, and, if so, whether that requirement is pragmatic, epistemic, or both, and justify your verdict.
