I write code for a living and haven't read any philosophy since a compulsory module at uni, so go easy on the jargon. My mate Tom has got deep into sortition — the jury-service idea, a random sample of the population instead of elected MPs, they legislate for a year or two and then go home. I'm definately into it. But he posted this in our group chat and I've been chewing on it for a week, because it seems to prove a lot more than he thinks, and maybe more than I can live with.

(1) When a decision affects a lot of people, the only fair way to make it is to give every one of them an equal chance of being the one who decides. Nobody's interests count for more than anyone else's, so nobody should get a bigger share of the deciding than anyone else.
(2) Elections give a much bigger share to the photogenic and the well connected; expert panels give it to the credentialed. Both are unfair ways of deciding something that affects all of us.
(3) So decisions that affect all of us should be made by bodies picked at random. That's lottocracy, and that's the bit I agree with.
(4) But there's nothing in (1) about politics. Whether the estate gets knocked down or the bypass rerouted affects all of us just as much as a vote does, and the fairness reason in (1) doesn't care what the decision is about — only who gets to make it.
(5) So by the same reasoning the panel that decides the bypass should be a random sample, and so should the one deciding which new scanner the hospital gets, and — same reasoning again, it's a decision affecting several people — the one deciding whether to replace my mum's hip.
(6) So: a lottery for surgeons. Which is obviously wrong. She wants the person who's done two thousand of them.
(7) So (1) is false.

Tom's line is that the honest thing is to accept (5) all the way down: if a random panel is good enough for tax policy it's good enough for a hip. I don't believe he believes that. But I really don't want to give up (3), and (1) feels like the whole point of the thing to me.

Where's the flaw? Is it one of the steps rather than (1) itself? And whatever I give up, what's the price — if I drop (1), what am I left with as a reason for lottocracy, and does the fix need a principled line between political and non-political that I'd then have to defend forever? Something else Tom said in the same thread, which I didn't really follow: that a crowd of people with different ways of going about things beats a crowd of individually better people when nobody knows the right method, but not when somebody does. No idea if that's relevant or if he was changing the subject. Use it if it helps.
