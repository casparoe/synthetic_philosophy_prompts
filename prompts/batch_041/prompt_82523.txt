I'm the instructor of record for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and I am currently working through the final exams. Three of the four short-essay questions are settled; I would like a careful second opinion on the fourth, which is the only one where I cannot decide between two marks. Some context on what the students were taught, because the question presupposes it. The unit on cluelessness was organised around a distinction I introduced with my own labels, since the names used in the secondary literature kept confusing them. Input cluelessness is the ordinary predicament of an agent who lacks evidence or computing power: in principle better information or a better model would close the gap, the facts are there to be had, and the only interesting question is how much deliberation is worth its cost. Ripple cluelessness is different in kind: the consequences of any act propagate indefinitely and depend on contingencies so remote and so arbitrary — a stranger's mood, a norm's slow spread across a century, which of two nearly identical histories the world turns out to be — that no accumulation of evidence or computation would ever close the gap. We then considered two replies. The first is that the ripples are individually negligible, so that the total is dominated by the near effects and our ordinary judgments are safe. The second is that even if no one can compute the expected value of an act, the expected value is still well defined, being a sum over possibilities weighted by their probabilities, so the theory's criterion of rightness can be stated without any reference to what agents can know. Students were explicitly told they could take any line they liked on whether either reply succeeds; on this question I graded the argument, not the verdict. The paper is worth 30 marks, and I allocated them as follows: 8 for the distinction between the two kinds of cluelessness, 10 for the treatment of the two replies, 6 for the section on rules and dispositions, and 6 for overall coherence and command of the material.

The question as it appeared on the exam was: 'Distinguish the two kinds of cluelessness discussed in lecture, and say which, if either, is a serious problem for act consequentialism. Then consider whether the two replies canvassed in lecture succeed, and whether a consequentialist could escape the problem by evaluating rules or dispositions rather than individual acts.' The student's answer, which I reproduce in full and unedited, runs as follows. 'Both kinds of cluelessness arise from the same fact — that the consequences of what we do extend further than we can follow — but they differ in kind and have very different implications for act consequentialism. Input cluelessness is the familiar predicament of an agent who lacks the evidence or the computational power to work out the expected consequences of her options. It is a practical obstacle rather than a theoretical one: the facts are there to be discovered, and a sufficiently informed and sufficiently fast reasoner would see them. The remedy is more research or more careful deliberation, and the only interesting question is how much of each is worth its cost. Ripple cluelessness is not like that. The causal ripples of any act propagate indefinitely — the stranger nudged into a different mood, the norm strengthened or weakened, the child who is not born because two people met at a party I did or did not attend. Because these chains are sensitive to contingencies that are arbitrary from any evaluative point of view, no accumulation of evidence would ever let us close the gap, and it is this second kind that is the serious problem. It is serious because it threatens the connection between the theory's criterion of rightness and any procedure an agent could actually use. The first reply holds that the ripples are individually negligible, so that the total is dominated by the near effects and our ordinary judgments are safe. This reply succeeds, and it is worth seeing why. Since the ripples of an act spread out in every direction, the favourable and the unfavourable ones offset one another, and the expected sum converges on the effects we can see. Ripple cluelessness is thereby reduced to input cluelessness: the far future is not a source of a special kind of ignorance, only a place where our ordinary ignorance is stretched thin. The second reply is more modest and, I think, plainly correct. Expected value is a sum over possible outcomes weighted by their probabilities, and whether we can compute that sum has no bearing on whether it exists. The criterion of rightness for act consequentialism therefore does not depend on what any agent knows, and ripple cluelessness cannot show the theory to be false. Since the theory survives intact, cluelessness is not really a problem for the theory or for the agent, and the anxiety it provokes is misplaced. It is also worth asking whether the problem can be dodged by shifting from acts to rules or dispositions. A rule consequentialist evaluates the rule rather than the token act, and the consequences of a rule are more predictable than those of an act, because the law of large numbers smooths out the idiosyncratic effects of any particular application. So a consequentialist troubled by ripple cluelessness should prefer rules, or at least a dispositional version of the theory, which promises guidance where the act-based version falls silent. There is one further point, which I think is the deepest. Ripple cluelessness is at bottom a case of moral uncertainty: if we cannot trace the consequences of an act into the far future, we cannot say which of our theories has jurisdiction over the strange states of affairs we may have brought about, and so we cannot say what would even count as getting it right. The cluelessness is therefore not merely about the facts; it reaches the standards by which the facts would be assessed. The upshot is that ripple cluelessness is a genuine and distinctive problem, but not a fatal one. It tells us that act consequentialism is not a decision procedure, and that the interesting work lies in the theory of what an agent with our limitations ought to do.'

Please grade the answer out of 30 and, more importantly, supply the point-by-point comments I can attach to the paper: for each substantive claim, say whether it is correct and, where it is not, exactly what has gone wrong — a conflation, a premise that does not support the conclusion drawn, a reply that has been strengthened or weakened in the retelling, a claim that sits uneasily with something else the student says. Fluent answers of this kind tend to be read more charitably than they deserve, so I would rather have the argument's joints tested than a general impression; where a claim is defective, tell me what the student would have to say to repair it, and where a claim is defensible even though it is not a move I made in lecture, say so and explain why, since I do not want to penalise a student for being right in a way I did not anticipate.
