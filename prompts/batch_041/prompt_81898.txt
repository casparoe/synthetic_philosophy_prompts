I'm a clinical psychologist who runs an alcohol research lab, so my working vocabulary is the one you would find in a treatment-and-mechanisms journal: dose-response curves, expectancy effects, cue reactivity, alcohol myopia, state-dependent learning, disinhibition as a contested construct, ecological momentary assessment, controlled-drinking paradigms, assessment validity and social-desirability bias, iatrogenic effects of intervention. A colleague in the classics department recently accused me of treating Plato's discussion of drunkenness in the Laws as a quaint forerunner of modern harm-reduction policy, and I want to answer him properly rather than rhetorically. What I remember of the relevant stretch is this: three old men, an Athenian stranger, a Cretan, and a Spartan, are walking to the cave of Zeus and talking about legislation; the Cretan and Spartan codes, as I recall, forbid drunkenness and the symposium outright, and the Athenian argues the opposite of what one would expect, namely that wine is a drug a lawgiver might require rather than ban. I remember him treating the drinking party as a kind of instrument: wine makes the drinker's soul more supple and shows what is actually in it, the way a mirror does; he compares the procedure to testing courage by exposing a man to frightening things, so that the corresponding test of self-control is exposure to the pleasure that most enslaves us; he insists that the party needs a sober commander and a set of rules; and I believe there are age provisions, with wine withheld from the young, restrained in the middle years, and prescribed to old men as a remedy for the harshness and forgetfulness of age. I also recall, more vaguely, that all this hangs together with the claims in the first book about every man being at war with himself and about education consisting in the correct training of pleasure and pain. I may be misremembering details and order, so correct me where I am wrong, but do not bother with references or a bibliography; I need the argument, not a reading list.

What I want from you is a translation, not a summary. Take the position that emerges from that discussion — wine as an instrument of civic education and of the soul's self-disclosure, the drinking party as a supervised institutional test, the legislator as prescriber rather than prohibitor — and restate it as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, using its actual constructs: pharmacokinetics and dose, tolerance and sensitization, expectancy effects where they come apart from pharmacological ones, the trait-state distinction, the psychometrics of an instrument that is supposed to reveal a stable disposition, demand characteristics, therapeutic window, and the ethics and feasibility of a mandated intervention. Then give me the residue report. Which of Plato's claims survive the translation without loss — for instance, does the idea that wine reveals character come out as a defensible validity claim about a diagnostic probe, or as a claim that contemporary alcohol research would simply reject? Which survive only in a distorted form? Which have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary — the parts of the soul and their proper order, the war within oneself, virtue as the measure, the political and theological framing of lawgiving — and for each of those, tell me whether the failure is a defect of my framework, something it cannot express and ought to be able to, or a sign that Plato's claim was doing less work than it appears to, or was not a well-formed empirical or normative claim in the first place.

Finally, I would like your view on what seems to me the sharpest point of contact. The whole proposal appears to require that acute intoxication be a valid test of a stable trait, and I suspect that is precisely where a modern researcher would dig in. Tell me whether Plato has any resources to answer that objection, and whether my vocabulary makes his position look better or worse than it does in its own terms.
