I am putting together a 90-minute session for a mixed group of about fourteen people — half philosophy graduate students, half technically minded adults from a local reading group with no metaphysics background — and I want an activity that makes them collide with the best-system account of laws of nature rather than hear it described. I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and the failure mode I keep seeing in activities of this kind is that participants get to watch an idea instead of being caught by it, or else they perform the correct answer the facilitator has already signposted. Neither is worth the ninety minutes.

The account I want them to feel the force of: take a complete inventory of what happens in a universe — the whole mosaic, every local matter of fact — and consider the deductive systems from which that inventory can be derived. The best system is the one that optimally balances simplicity (few and short axioms, simple vocabulary) against strength (how much of the inventory it entails). The laws are the regularities that turn up in that best system. Nothing governs and nothing necessitates; whether a regularity is a law depends on the whole mosaic and on how the tradeoff is scored.

What I need:

First, they commit. Put six or seven regularities from a small toy universe in front of them and have them mark each as law or accident, in writing and out loud, with no hints. Do not mention best systems yet.

Second, they do the work. Hand them the complete history of that universe — small enough to check by hand, say a grid with a few particles over a dozen ticks — plus a restricted language for writing axioms and a scoring rule that operationalizes simplicity and strength. Teams build candidate systems and score one another's. No prior metaphysics should be needed to play, and no equipment beyond paper, handouts, and a whiteboard.

Third, the rule's verdicts have to come apart from their commitments. Engineer at least one case where the best-system verdict contradicts a regularity they had already defended, and at least one pair of universes that differ only in a far, causally isolated corner, so that a regularity among the near particles is a law in one and an accident in the other. I want the demotion to land on something they said out loud.

Fourth, let them move the simplicity/strength weighting and watch the verdicts flip. The suspicion that lawhood is then interest-relative should be felt in the room, not announced from the front.

Have the activity commit to one reading — laws as the axioms of the best system, or as its theorems — and say in the debrief how that choice changes the answers.

Deliverables: the materials, including the toy universes and the axiom language; the scoring rule; the rules of play; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the account and its rivals. Do not name the view or its critics until the debrief. Two positions must still be alive when they leave: someone holding that the best-system verdicts track what lawhood really is, and someone holding that the pre-theoretic verdicts were the data and the rule is a bad analysis of them. I would rather they leave with that disagreement unresolved than with a tidy takeaway; the bookkeeping is a means, not the point.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time — I expect at least one participant who already knows the account and spoils it, and at least one team that games the scoring rule instead of the question — how to handle both, and, frankly, which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce. In particular I doubt it can reproduce the anti-Humean's positive intuition that laws are doing something, and I want to know whether stipulating the scoring rule begs the question against them.
