ok so ive been trying to figure out my actual views on punishment and i keep failing at it, so im dumping my gut calls here and hoping someone can tell me what principle im implicitly running, or which call is the odd one out.

roughly:

- guy tortures cats for fun, gets caught, is genuinely never going to do it again, nobody else will ever hear about it. i still want him to suffer. not for any deterrent reason, theres none. it just seems like he owes something.

- drunk driver kills a kid. other driver, equally drunk and equally careless, gets home fine. i want the first punished a lot and the second barely at all, and honestly i think the first deserves more even though the only difference between them is luck. this one bugs me.

- flip it: guy attempts murder, fails only because the gun jammed. i want him punished almost as much as if hed succeeded. so here luck barely matters, but in the driving case it does? i cant tell if those two are consistent or not.

- town is about to have a riot, framing one innocent guy and jailing him would stop it cold and save lives. gut: no. absolutely not. even though the math says yes.

- somebody commited a serious crime 35 years ago, is now a beloved old man, zero risk, fully reformed, feels terrible about it. i lean toward letting it go. but the cat guy above i wanted to suffer... is the difference just that the old man already suffers?

- rich guy and poor guy commit the same fraud. a 5k fine ruins the poor guy and is pocket change to the rich guy. i want it scaled to income, which is weird if the point is that they did the same thing.

- dead person: we find out a now-dead famous person did something horrible. i have some urge to "punish" them — pull the statue down, damn the name. but theres no one left to suffer. is that punishment at all or just norm-expression?

- a person who desperately wants to be punished for what they did (not suicidal, just wants to pay). does punishing them still count as punishment? does the consent change anything?

my tentative theory, and i want you to try to break it: ive been reading a bunch of game theory / repeated-games stuff and i half-believe punishment is basically a commitment device — the point is to make it common knowledge that defecting costs more than its worth, so cooperation stays stable. under that view the cat guy is a weird case (no future game), the innocent framing is obviously wrong (it breaks the very norm its supposed to protect), the old man is a waste (no future game), and the dead guy is just norm-expression.

but the cat guy still feels like he owes something, and the dead guy still feels like he owes something, and i dont think thats just me being confused. so either my theory is wrong or my guts are.

question: what principle, or small set of principles, actually fits the calls above? which of my calls has to go? and what does your principle say about two cases i havent listed: (a) punishing a wrongdoer by doing to them exactly what they did, literally an eye for an eye and no more and no less, and (b) punishing someone in a way that also incidentally teaches them and everyone else a useful lesson vs. punishing them in a way that teaches nothing but is exactly what they deserve.
