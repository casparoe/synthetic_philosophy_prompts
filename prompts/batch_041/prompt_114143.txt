I'm putting togther a seminar on Nietzsche's eternal recurrence and I need a case for the session on whether the doctrine has any real practical bite. Build me one.

Construct a single thought experiment, set in ordinary contemporary life — no supernatural messenger, no time travel, no sci-fi physics — in which a person ends up fully convinced that the eternal recurrence is true (she's been handed an argument she can't find a hole in; how she got there isn't the interesting part), and in which the two main ways of reading the doctrine deliver opposite verdicts about what she has reason to do about a decision she was already facing. By the two readings I mean the cosmological one, where the recurrence is just a fact about how the world is, and the practical one, where the whole point of the doctrine is the thought of it as a test of whether you can affirm your life. Keep the person and the decision ordinary (a job, a friendship, a move — nothing melodramatic), and don't let the divergence rest on her doubting the news or being weak-willed; it has to fall out of the two readings themselves — definately not just be announced.

Then do the check: state the decision, say what each reading tells her to do and why, and name the exact feature of the case that pulls them apart. Say what happens if you take that feature out.

Then the part I actually care about: change the setup so the recurrence is selective — only some lives recur, and nobody can tell in advance whether theirs is one of them. What does each reading say now, and does the practical reading survive at all once affirmation is no longer something everyone is on the hook for? Finish with how a Nietzschean who thinks I've rigged the case would try to defuse it. No citations needed, I'll handle those.
