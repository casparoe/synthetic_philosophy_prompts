I'm a philosophy professor who has somehow ended up as the academic member of our hospital trust's new-interventions panel (dont ask). Twice a term a proposal lands — fund a nurse-led clinic, roll out some remote-monitoring kit — and the meeting always stalls on the same question: decide now, or spend six months running a local pilot and collect more data first? Everyone nods along to 'more evidence can only help' and I mutter that it can't, but I've never once managed to say anything they could actualy use in the ten minutes we've got.

So write me a procedure the panel can run on a proposal, grounded in what decision theory genuinely says about the value of information — Good's result about free information being worth having, and the places where it quietly stops applying. I want it in order: what to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it says 'decide now, more data changes nothing', where it says 'don't look', and which steps are value judgements I shouldn't dress up as arithmetic. Keep the machinery to what four people can do on a whiteboard in ten minutes — nothing beyond 'rough chance of each outcome' and 'rough cost of being wrong'.

Things it has to handle, because these are the ones that bite us: information that costs money or delay; the gap between what a pilot would actually tell us and what we'd like it to tell us; the case where running the pilot changes the thing being measured, because everyone behaves differently once they know they're being watched; the case where we've publicly promised to act on the result — I have a suspicion that this quietly destroys the very thing that makes free information free; cases where finding out is bad for the patients whatever we then do about it; and, if it can be done without a blackboard, the case where the fact that we chose to look is itself a signal about what's there to be found.

Then run it on two proposals: a pilot of a new triage protocol in A&E, and a survey of staff before we decide whether to change the rotas. One of them should come out 'don't bother gathering more', and I'd rather it wasn't the one I already like.

Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do — where it gives up and the panel has to argue it out like grown-ups. British English throughout, please.
