I'm a politics-and-ideas journalist, and I've been invited to submit a proposal for a six-month research fellowship at a university's democracy institute — they want a plan for a project, not a pitch for a story. My half-formed idea is this. Compulsory voting, a lower voting age, and voting rights for long-resident non-citizens are invariably argued one at a time, each on its own favoured ground — unequal turnout, the claims of the young, taxation without representation — and the coalitions behind the three are treated as interchangeable. I suspect they actually presuppose different and not obviously compatible answers to a prior question: who counts as a member of the demos at all. The two principles usually invoked to answer that question pull in opposite directions on precisely these cases. One says the demos is everyone subject to the law, which would exclude the long-resident non-citizen while admitting the fifteen-year-old; the other says it is everyone affected by the decision, which would admit the non-citizen and, pressed hard, the foreigner abroad. If that is right, the three reforms cannot all rest on one foundation, and the standard defences of each are quietly borrowing from the other's. Turn this into the proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer, say what the existing defences of each reform leave open, set out the conceptual method and why it does better here than a survey of the empirical literature on turnout, describe what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and give a scope that is honest about six months of part-time work. Skip the bibliography and reading list — I'll do the reading and add citations myself.
