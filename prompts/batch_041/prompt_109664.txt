Ok so I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a book, and I've wrote myself into a hole. My narrator dies around the 60% mark — no afterlife, no ghost, she's just gone — and the last third is about what happens to everything she left behind. Her notebooks get edited by someone who badly misreads them, her one good idea ends up credited to her ex, that kind of thing. Every beta reader says some version of: she's dead, she doesn't know, so who exactly is harmed? Which I recognize as the Epicurean move about death, and what I want to know is whether it still works when you push it one step further, to what happens after.

So build me the case. Not a survey — an actual scenario I can steal, and I will steal it.

Rules for the scenario:

- The harm has to be to her, not to the people who loved her. Their grief is a different thing and it's cheating.
- Contemporary and ordinary. No afterlife, no ghosts, no time travel, no cryonics, no sci-fi.
- Whatever happens has to happen entirely after she's dead, and she has to have had no anticipation of it: she never worried about this particular thing, never formed a desire or a fear about it, so I can't be smuggling the harm back into her living life through dread.
- She can't have set it up herself or signed off on it in advance — no instructions to burn the notebooks, no contract, no will — otherwise I can't tell a harm from a deal she made.
- It has to be the kind of thing where an ordinary reader says yes, that was bad for her, without hedging — but where the Epicurean has a real answer available, not a cheap one.

Then, what I actually need:

1. Show me it works. Go through each rule and say why the scenario meets it, and flag any place where it only just meets it.
2. Give me your verdict, not the menu. Is she harmed by what happens after she's dead? And can someone hold both that death is nothing to us and that a thing like this is a genuine harm to the dead — or does one of them have to give? Commit to an answer. I'd definately rather you be wrong and definite than lay out four positions and hand me the shovel.
3. Walk through what the deprivation account, the straight experience requirement, and a desire-satisfaction account of posthumous harm each say about the scenario, and why they part ways.
4. Name the one feature doing the work — the thing that, if I changed it, flips your answer. Then change it and show me the flip. Related: does the harm need a subject who exists at the time it happens, and if not, whose harm is it — her at the moment she died, or her as she was in the middle of her life? Pick one.
5. Tell me how a defender of the Epicurean attacks my scenario. I want to know where it's soft before my editor finds it.
6. If you're leaning on a contested reading of any of this, say so in a line. No citations, no reading list — I'm writing a novel, not a dissertation.
