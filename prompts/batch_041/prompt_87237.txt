My nani died three weeks ago and I've somehow ended up as the one giving the eulogy on Saturday at the crematorium — mainly because I'm the only grandchild who can get through it without dissolving, which is a low bar, but there we are. Everyone back to the house afterwards.

She was Gujarati, came over in the sixties, married my granddad, who was a lapsed Methodist from Yorkshire, so the room is going to be a proper mix: her sisters and their families and a load of aunties and uncles who actually know the tradition properly, and then my granddad's lot, the neighbours off her road, a few of her old colleagues from the health centre, and my mates from work who never met her and are coming anyway.

Since she died I've been reading up on anekantavada, which she used to bang on about when I was small and which I have definately always got wrong. I thought it meant 'everyone's a bit right about everything' — relativism with good manners — and my uncle, the one who actually knows this stuff, put me straight: it's a claim about the thing itself having endless sides to it, not a claim that we can't know anything. And the famous seven-fold thing, the saptabhangi, is a discipline for saying things properly: in a way it is, in a way it isn't, in a way it is and isn't, in a way it can't be said, and then the three combinations of those. The 'syat' at the front of each one means 'in some respect', not 'maybe' — that's the bit I'd got wrong my whole life. Its not a shrug.

What I want from you is the eulogy. About five minutes. British English please — I'm reading it out in Solihull and I don't want it sounding American.

The catch: I can't say anything she'd have thought was false. So no heaven, no 'she's up there with Granddad now', no 'she's looking down on us', and please no 'all religions are really saying the same thing at heart' — she hated that one, said it flattened everything into mush. Also, I'm not religious myself and I don't know what I think happens when we die, so I can't stand up there and assert rebirth or liberation on her behalf either.

And the doctrine has to actually do the work in the speech, not just get a name-check. That's the hard part, because half that room will wince if I turn her into a nice multicultural lady who thought every opinion was as good as every other (which, again, is not what she thought), and the other half will be completely lost if I stand there explaining the seven modes like a lecture. So I suppose what I'm really asking for is a eulogy shaped by the idea that every true thing you can say about a person is true in a respect and never the whole of it, without me having to say that out loud. Say something true about her, then say the other thing that's also true and seems to contradict it, and let the two sit next to each other.

Stick a few lines at the end, after the speech, about what you were doing and what you left out, so I can check I'm not about to embarrass myself in front of the aunties.
