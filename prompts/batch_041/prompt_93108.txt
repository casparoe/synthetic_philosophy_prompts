I'm taking a seminar on divine foreknowledge, and after class broke up, two classmates stayed behind and kept at it for twenty minutes while I mostly listened, unable to decide who had the better of it. The topic was middle knowledge — the claim that, before God decides anything about which world to create, God already knows what every possible free creature would freely do in any circumstances it might be placed in, so that this body of knowledge sits logically between God's knowledge of necessary truths and God's knowledge of what he actually creates (the professor calls the whole picture Molinist). Priya opened by saying the doctrine is impossible. If God knows that a creature would, in circumstances C, freely do A, then there must be a fact about what that creature would do; but a fact like that, settled before the creature exists and before C ever arises, means the creature cannot do otherwise in C — so the act isn't free, and what God is supposed to know isn't a free act at all. Daniel said she was sliding from "there is a fact about it" to "it is determined," and that this is just the old mistake about foreknowledge: my knowing now what you'll choose tomorrow doesn't make you choose it. Priya said the analogy breaks precisely where it matters, because in the actual world the choice itself is what makes the prediction true and it comes later, whereas a counterfactual about a world God never creates has no choice anywhere to make it true, only a brute fact about someone who does not exist. Daniel came back with two points. First, the truth-maker is still the free choice — the choice the creature would make in the world where the antecedent holds, created or not; and if she insists that a free choice needs some further truth-maker beyond itself, she will have to say that about the actual world too, which puts freedom in jeopardy everywhere rather than only in the counterfactuals. Second, he said her "brute fact" complaint proves too much, since plenty of truths are brute, and in any case truth is not causation — that a proposition is true because a free agent would choose a certain way doesn't let the proposition push the agent around.

Priya then tried a different tack, and this is the stretch I've been unable to settle on my own. She said the trouble isn't the metaphysics of truth-making but the order of explanation. Suppose God surveys the counterfactuals, sees that a world in which Alex freely helps a stranger is slightly better than one in which Alex freely walks past, and creates the first world partly because of what Alex would do. Then a creature who never existed and never chose anything has determined something about God's decision — and she pressed a dilemma: either those counterfactuals are brute facts God simply has to work around, in which case his sovereignty is compromised, or God somehow settles them himself, in which case Alex's helping isn't free after all. Daniel answered that "reason" is not "cause": God's choice remains free and self-determined, the creature doesn't cause it, and God could have decreed a world in which such counterfactuals carried no weight at all; Priya, he said, is smuggling in an assumption that anything a decision depends on must be something that controls the decider. She replied that this just relabels the problem, since a decision that tracks a fact outside the decider is answerable to that fact however politely we describe it.

What I want is a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually went, not a general survey of the debate: which of Priya's and Daniel's specific claims are correct, which are mistaken, and which are open questions that people who work on this still dispute. In particular, please address (a) whether Daniel's "foreknowledge doesn't cause" reply actually answers Priya's first argument or whether the disanalogy she presses is fatal to it; (b) whether Priya's demand for a truth-maker is a fair requirement or an extra one she'd have to apply to free choices in the actual world as well; (c) whether her dilemma about the order of explanation is a genuine incoherence or a confusion between explaining a decision and controlling the decider; and (d) what, if anything, both of them are missing. Be explicit about which of your verdicts reflect something settled among people who study this and which are contested, and don't hand me a contested position as though it were the consensus; where you're genuinely unsure, say so rather than rounding off to a verdict.
