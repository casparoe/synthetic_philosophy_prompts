I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and there's an argument at work I cant win and its eating me. Colleague A: our queueing model assumes arrivals are independent and Poisson, that's not true, so the model is garbage. Colleague B: who cares, it predicts the p99 fine. I'm sort of B, but I know B is too glib. Someone in my book club told me economists had this exact fight seventy years ago — Friedman, and then a guy called Samuelson who named the bad version of the position the F-twist, because if you say a theory's assumptions never need to be realistic, you've just said any old falsehood is fine as long as you like the predictions, which can't be right.

Here's the part I want the poem to be about, and it's neither of those two positions. I've started to think there are three kinds of false assumption in a model, and we argue past each other because nobody says which kind they mean. One, idle ones: you could delete them and the number wouldn't move, they're scaffolding nobody bothered to take down. Two, locally harmless ones: false, but the error they introduce is small in the regime you actually care about — Poisson is wrong at peak, fine at 3am, and 3am is all we use it for. Three, load-bearing ones: the model only says anything because the assumption is false. No friction isn't a claim about surfaces, it's a way of isolating what's left once you delete everything that isn't the mechanism. Assume everyone is a profit maximizer and you get a theory of competition that survives being told people aren't calculators. Type three is the one I believe in and the one that scares me, because that's exactly where the F-twist objection bites: if falsity can be a virtue, what stops it from being a free pass.

So: take this on as a philosopher of science who also writes poems, and give me a villanelle — the one where two lines keep coming back and finally meet at the end. I know that's a lot to hang on one form, but the form is doing the work here, so think about the shape before you write a single line. Brainstorm a bit first; maybe write out for yourself what each of the three positions actually claims, and which one a poem can enact rather than state.

Specs. The two refrains should be, first, a flat statement of a false assumption, said as though it were obviously true, and second, a flat statement of what the model gets right. They have to rhyme with each other, because I want the falsity and the hit rate tied together by the form — that's the whole point, they're the same poem. The false assumption should be the second refrain, the one that closes the poem, so the last thing we hear is the thing that isn't true, still standing, unretracted. Never tell the reader which refrain is which; someone who knows this debate should be able to label them by the third stanza, and someone who doesn't should still feel it. Somewhere in the middle let the objection in — that this defense would equally excuse any falsehood you happen to like — and don't refute it. I don't want resolution. I want the two of them left standing next to each other.

Keep the concrete stuff boring and close to home: one load-bearing assumption, from my world if you can (a cache, a scheduler, a queue, a hash), not frictionless planes or spherical cows or the line about all models being wrong but some being useful — I've heard those, that's the part I already know, and I want the poem about the part I don't. Don't have the poem name economists to do the work; if the idea isn't there without the names, it isn't there.

Format: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists — continuous prose, the way you'd write an email. Give me the poem first, then a few sentences of plain prose on why you made the formal choices you made, and then stop.
