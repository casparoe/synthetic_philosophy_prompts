my friend and i got into it for like three hours last night about Keynes and the good life and i want to turn the argument into a poem, partly because i lost it and partly because i think the poem could say the thing i couldn't get out loud.

his line was: the whole bit about everyone working fifteen hours a week once the economic problem is solved is just optimism with nice prose, and the good life he's pointing at is just leisure, i.e. not working, i.e. nothing. my line was that the interesting part isn't the fifteen hours, its what he says the real problem is once we get there: we've been trained for generations to keep striving and we have no idea how to enjoy anything, and the prospect of having nothing left to strive for is genuinely frightening, and the old gods -- avarice, usury, loving money as a possession instead of as a means -- have to stay our gods a little longer precisely because they're what carries us out of the tunnel, even though he thinks they're ugly and a bit diseased. so you get this awful shape: the virtues you need to reach the good life are the ones that make you unable to enjoy it, and the people who keep them going are the ones who never get there. and then theres the other strand, the late memoir about what he and his friends got from Moore, where the good life is states of mind -- love, friendship, the enjoyment of beautiful things, the pursuit of knowledge -- each taken as an end in itself and never as a means, plus a flat rejection of the whole measuring-pleasure-and-adding-it-up approach to ethics. he says he and his circle were maybe the last generation to actually believe a good life like that was possible, and that Moore's version left out the passions, and that they were complacent about how dark people can be.

so: write me a villanelle about this. the two refrains should be (a) the postponement line -- the old gods stay our gods a little longer, the good life is always just past the next round of accumulation -- and (b) the good-life line, the art of living, things enjoyed as ends and not as means. the form has to do the work: the refrains keep coming back no matter what the tercets try to say, so the poem should feel like the postponement is the only stable thing in it and the good life only ever shows up as a rhyme. in the closing quatrain the two refrains end up side by side and i want that adjacency to be the whole point -- pick the order yourself, but the order has to be an argument, and if the good life gets the last word i want the poem to have earned it, and if the postponement gets it i want to feel why.

don't let it turn into a poem about economics jargon, and don't let the good life collapse into a vague list of nice things -- the Moorean part needs to be concrete enough that someone who knows that memoir will recognise it (love, friendship, beautiful objects, knowledge, and the refusal to treat any of them as instruments), and the fear of leisure should show up in at least one tercet. keep it first person plural if you can, the we who are keeping the old gods going for somebody else's benefit. no title needed. i'd rather it be sad than clever.
