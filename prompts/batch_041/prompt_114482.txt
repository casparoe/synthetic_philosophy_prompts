I sit on the research committee of my university's internal Wellbeing Initiative, which disburses small pilot grants across departments and, as a condition of funding, requires every grantee to deposit its data in a shared repository. The committee meets on Thursday and must dispose of a single proposal that has split us badly, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion beforehand.

The proposal, from a joint psychology-and-philosophy team, is to build and validate what the applicants call a Unified Hedonic Inventory: a single 0–100 self-report scale, administered by every project the Initiative funds, with its midpoint defined by one reference experience (the applicants propose "the pleasantness of a warm shower on a cold day") against which subjects are asked to place all their other pleasures. The stated aim is interoperability: with one instrument, a taste-preference study, a trial of an analgesic, and a study of meditative absorption would all report in the same units, and the Initiative could pool them into a running estimate of the net pleasure its portfolio has produced.

Three things are in dispute. First, the applicants hold that the Inventory measures one thing; a colleague on the committee, a philosopher of mind, holds that it measures an artifact, because the states we call pleasures — the taste of something sweet, the relief when a headache lifts, absorption in a proof, the warmth of being greeted — have no common phenomenological or functional core, so a number purporting to compare them is an average of incommensurables. Second, the pilot data are ambiguous in a way both sides cite. In the pilot, participants rated the relief of nausea and the enjoyment of a favorite meal within a few points of each other, yet in a choice task would have surrendered enormous quantities of the first for a little of the second; the applicants read this as measurement noise, my colleague as evidence that the scale tracks something other than felt pleasantness. Third, and most practically, we must decide what our data-sharing rule commits us to if we approve: requiring every future grantee to use an instrument whose validity is contested makes the Initiative a party to the dispute in a way a one-off grant would not.

My colleague proposes that we decline and instead require modality-specific scales with a Pareto rule — one project counts as better than another only if it is at least as good on every sub-scale — while our statistician favors abandoning self-report altogether in favor of preference-based aggregation. I think both alternatives are worse than they look, but I do not want the memo to be a brief for my own view.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a definite course of action — fund, fund with conditions, or decline, with any conditions and their timeline spelled out — and give its reasons in a form the applicants could actually respond to. It should engage the heterogeneity objection on its merits rather than dismissing it as philosophical scruple: state clearly what would have to be true about pleasure for the Inventory to be defensible, and whether the applicants' own pilot data bear on that. It should also put the strongest case for my colleague's position as fairly as I would put it myself, and then explain why the recommendation stands anyway. Finally, it should be explicit about the cost of the recommendation — what the Initiative would be asserting, and what it would be forgoing, if it takes the route you propose. Assume an audience of non-specialists; about a page and a half; no citations are needed, since the committee wants the argument rather than the literature.
