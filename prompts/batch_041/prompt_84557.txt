I've been going around in circles on self-referential sentences and I want to know if there's a principle behind my verdicts, because right now I'm pretty sure I'm just making it up case by case.

Rough map of what I think, so you can see the machinery running: "Snow is white" true, "2+2=5" false.

"This sentence is false." Neither true nor false. I won't call it false — if it were false it'd be right about itself, which is absurd — and it's not some exotic flavor of false, it's a sentence that never got a truth value at all.

"This sentence is true." Also neither, same reason. I know people want to say this one is obviously true. I don't see it. Nothing in it hooks onto anything.

"This sentence is not true." Here's where I get off the bus. I want to say this one is true. If it's not true then it's right, so it's true; and if it's true then, well, yeah, I know. I can't get it to come out true without tripping over my own feet, but I also can't call it false or neither. True is where I land, and I'm not happy about it.

"This sentence is false or 2+2=4." True, and it bugs me how easy that was.

"This sentence is false or it's raining." True if it's raining, neither if it isn't. Same sentence, and the weather decides whether it's a paradox or a plain truth. I'll live with that.

"This sentence is true and 2+2=5." False, plain false. The self-referential part doesn't rescue it, the other conjunct sinks it.

"The sentence written on the blackboard is false" — and the blackboard is blank. False. There's no sentence there for it to be about, so it's just wrong. Doesn't feel gappy to me at all.

Two sentences on a card: A says "B is false," B says "A is true." One of these has to be false, I think. I can't tell you which, but it doesn't feel gappy — it feels like there's a fact of the matter I just can't see.

Three sentences: S1 says "S2 is false," S2 says "S3 is false," S3 says "S1 is false." This one feels gappy to me, all three neither. Which is wierd, because it's the same shape of problem as the card and I've got different verdicts for the two.

So here's the principle I'd guess is doing the work — try it on and see: a sentence is true if it comes out true on every way of assigning true and false to the sentences involved that doesn't contradict what any of them say; false if it comes out false on all such assignments; and neither if there's no such assignment at all, or if the assignments disagree about it.

Does that get my list? It seems to get the liar, the truth-teller, the blackboard, the disjunction with 2+2=4, the conjunction with 2+2=5, and the weather case. It does not get "this sentence is not true" (it says neither, I say true) and it does not get the card (it says neither, I say one of them is false). So either I give up those two verdicts or the principle needs a clause I haven't thought of, and I'd rather hear which. If the honest answer is "no single principle covers this," fine, but then tell me exactly which two of my verdicts are the ones that can't live together.

Also tell me what the thing predicts for a few I haven't decided: "This sentence is true and this sentence is false"; two sentences each saying the other is false; and a sentence that says no one will ever truly believe it. And where is your reasoning most likely to fall apart — what would actually change your mind about which verdict has to go?
