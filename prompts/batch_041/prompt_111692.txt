I hung up my lab coat about three years ago — thirty-one years as a physical organic chemist, mostly mechanism and kinetics — and since then I've been reading my way through history and philosophy of science the way I used to read the literature. I've talked my way into a small six-month fellowship at the university's centre for history and philosophy of science. The itch is this. Almost every time the historians in that room talk about how my field's history gets told, someone says it's whig history — everything was always leading up to the current picture, the losers were simply wrong, the winners saw further ahead than they could possibly have seen. And the scientists in the room (me included, I've caught myself doing it) shrug, because we do it on purpose. When I teach, I *want* the narrative where the evidence gradually forced the right answer, because the point of the class is to get a student to the working picture as fast as possible, and a full accounting of every dead end that looked reasonable at the time would be useless. So my instinct, as a scientist, is: this is an idealization, and idealizations are fine when they earn their keep. We use them everywhere. The question is whether that instinct survives contact with the historians' objection, and I don't think either side has actually said what "earning their keep" would mean for a narrative about the past.

Here's the half-formed idea I want you to turn into a real proposal, and it's the part I actually care about. I think both sides are treating progress as one thing, and it isn't. In my own field, at least, there are layers that behave completely differently. The instruments and techniques ratchet: nobody is going back to determining structures without the modern spectroscopic tools, nobody is going to un-learn how to purify a compound properly, and the standards for what counts as a clean result have gotten stricter in a way that I don't think is reversible. The theory layer is not like that at all — the explanations come and go, and the fact that a picture won in 1975 tells you very little about whether it will be standing in 2075. So a single narrative can be genuinely, non-whiggishly true of one layer and a self-flattering myth about another, and I suspect a lot of the shouting past each other happens because nobody separates them. Then there's a second twist I keep circling: the progress story does real work outside the classroom. It's the thing we point at when we tell the public to trust science — look, it corrects itself, it converges. If the narrative is an idealization rather than a record, does the argument for trusting science survive de-idealization, or was it always leaning on the flattering version? That one worries me and I'd like the proposal to at least get it in view, even if it doesn't settle it.
