I am grading a stack of essays for an undergraduate seminar on famine, entitlement, and political economy, and I would like a careful second read of one of them. I want my comments to be defensible, so that I neither let a genuine error pass nor penalize a reading that is in fact defensible. Please give me a specific, point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression.

The essay prompt was as follows:

Consider the claim that famines are never natural disasters but always political crimes. Critically assess it, drawing on Sen's entitlement approach and comparing three cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the late 1840s, and the Chinese famine associated with the Great Leap Forward (roughly 1958–1962). In your answer: (a) state precisely what the entitlement approach implies about the causal role of food availability; (b) for each case, distinguish the trigger (if any), the mechanism that converted a shock into mass starvation, and the political decisions that determined the famine's scale, and say which of these the entitlement approach is equipped to explain; and (c) say whether the Irish and Chinese cases, in which the food supply genuinely fell, tell against the entitlement approach or can be accommodated by it.

The student wrote:

Sen's entitlement approach, set out in Poverty and Famines, is meant to displace the older food-availability-decline (FAD) account of famine. On Sen's view, whether a person starves depends not on how much food exists in the aggregate but on the food they can command through their entitlements: their endowment (what they own, including their labour power) together with the exchange entitlement mapping that converts endowments into food through production, market exchange, and transfers. Starvation occurs when this mapping collapses for a large section of the population. Since command over food, and not the size of the harvest, is what matters, the entitlement approach shows that food availability is essentially irrelevant to famine causation.

Bengal in 1943 fits the account closely. Aggregate food availability in Bengal was not markedly lower than in preceding years, yet something on the order of two to three million people died. For Bengal the trigger was the war; the mechanism was the collapse of exchange entitlements. The loss of rice imports after the fall of Burma, the colonial denial policy that removed boats and stocks from the coastal districts, and above all the inflationary boom created by the war drove food prices far beyond the wages of rural labourers, fisherfolk, and others with weak endowments. Speculative hoarding by traders and the colonial government's refusal to intervene completed the catastrophe. This is a famine of entitlements, not of nature.

Sen draws a further and stronger conclusion. Because famines are failures of command rather than of nature, they are avoidable through public action, and democratic governments with a free press reliably undertake that action; hence no functioning democracy has suffered a famine. This is the central conclusion of the entitlement approach, and it follows directly from the analysis of entitlements rather than from any separate empirical thesis.

Ireland is where the political reading looks strongest. Ireland in the 1840s was part of the United Kingdom, with seats at Westminster and a press at least as free as Britain's, so by Sen's own criterion it counts as a democracy — and yet a famine killed about a million people and drove as many to emigrate. Ireland is therefore a decisive counterexample to the democracy claim. The potato blight was, moreover, something of a red herring: what killed people was the government's laissez-faire relief policy and the punitive workhouse system.

The Great Leap Forward famine is different, because there grain output really did fall. That makes it a food-availability famine, and so it lies outside the entitlement framework altogether. Even so, its causes were political through and through — procurement quotas set above what the harvests could bear, exaggerated reports by local officials, continued grain exports, and the absence of a free press — so the case still supports the claim that famines are political crimes. The claim, then, is correct in substance: nature may trigger a famine, but the famine itself is always politically produced.
