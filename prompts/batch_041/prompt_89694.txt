I'm in my final year of a joint philosophy and economics degree, and I've been asked to give the student address at our graduation ceremony next month: five minutes, about forty graduates, their families, and the faculty, including the professor who taught the game theory unit and who will notice immediately if I fudge a step. I want the address built on the centipede game and the backward-induction argument rather than merely mentioning them, and I want the speech itself to have a centipede's shape — a sequence of increasingly generous offers to the audience, each of which they could cut short. The guests who have never heard of the game have to be able to follow it, and the people who know it must not hear anything false. In particular the address has to be honest that when the last node is known to both players and their rationality is common knowledge, the unraveling really does run all the way back to the first move; I don't want the standard commencement moral that cooperation is obviously the rational thing. What I want the address to turn on is one specific repair, and I want you to choose it and say precisely what it does and does not buy: either the game has no last node at all, so the induction has nothing to anchor on, or there is a small chance that the person across from you is simply the type who never takes, so that passing early can be worth it. Don't run the two together as if they were one idea, and if neither actually rescues the early move as I've described it, say so in a sentence or two rather than letting a false claim stand inside the speech. Write the address itself, in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — as something I could read aloud to that room.
