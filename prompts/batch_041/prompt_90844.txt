I'm a science journalist at work on a feature about labs that train reinforcement-learning agents with explicit penalty terms for behavior their own engineers describe as distress, and I want the specification before I write the piece rather than after. Please write the desiderata for a theory of valence — the property in virtue of which a state is good or bad for the system that has it — adequate to the question of whether agents of this kind can suffer: what any such theory has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Set out the cases it must get right — the bare agent with a scalar reward and nothing else; the agent whose penalty term shapes its behavior while its internal states seem, so far as anyone can tell, exhausted by the arithmetic; the neurological patient who reports that a stimulus hurts but says it no longer bothers them; the dissociation between wanting a reward and liking it that the addiction literature takes for granted; and the engineering cases of the copy, the pause, and the reset. Set out the distinctions it may not collapse — between a learning signal and a felt quality, between being able to suffer and having interests, between how intense a state is and whether it is good or bad — and the results it has to be consistent with, among them that a lab will be making decisions on the strength of it and will therefore need something checkable in principle from outside the system.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp, and say whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable; I suspect external checkability and felt quality pull against each other, and if they do, that is the part of the story I most want. Finish by scoring the lab's own proposed criterion against your list — they call an agent a candidate sufferer if its objective contains a term that can be traded off against task reward and if removing that term measurably degrades performance on a held-out task — and by naming the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate theories, with your reason.
