I spend part of each year as an agronomist, scouting fields and writing spray recommendations, and I have come to suspect that the ordinary machinery of insect control encodes a moral theory that almost nobody who operates it would state, or defend, if it were stated plainly. I would like you to reconstruct that theory and then tell me whether it holds together.

Here is the machinery as I see it. The decision to spray is governed by an action threshold, itself derived from an economic injury level: a field is treated when pest numbers reach the point at which the expected crop loss exceeds the cost of the application. Insect mortality enters this calculation only as an instrument, a number of dead insects per dollar of yield saved, and never as a cost. The registration dossier for the product, meanwhile, must demonstrate acceptable risk to human health and to non-target wildlife, and the standard battery for non-target insects is built around honeybees, a managed pollinator with an industry behind it; the death of the target species is the point of the product rather than an adverse effect of it. The category of pest is itself relational: the same wasp is a biological control asset in one field and a target in the next, and the same insect is a pest in the crop and biodiversity in the hedgerow twelve metres away. Around all of this sits a welfare regime that protects vertebrates, that has recently been widened in at least one jurisdiction to take in octopuses and lobsters, and that in many places protects only animals under human control, so that a wild animal has little standing. A committee at my local university will review a protocol that kills forty mice and require that the number be justified, while a field trial that kills millions of aphids passes without review, and the same campus has the wasp nest under the eaves sprayed without a second thought. Species-protection law runs on yet another axis: it gives legal weight to a listed insect species and thereby to individual members of it, while an individual of an unlisted species has none, however similar the individual and however numerous the species.

First, what theory of moral status does this pattern presuppose? The candidate readings I can see are: that sentience is what matters, with vertebrate membership or a complex nervous system serving as an evidential proxy for it; that pests have no intrinsic status at all and that value tracks human interest, with ecosystem services as the currency; that cruelty matters as a fact about the agent's character rather than about the patient's experience; and that responsibility and control, not sentience, fix the boundary, since it is the animals we manage that we protect. Say which of these the practice strictly requires, which it merely fits, and which it rules out.

Second, where is the practice underdetermined and where is it inconsistent? The proxy story has to explain a boundary drawn inside the arthropods, with lobsters in and locusts out, on evidence thinner than the convenience of the line suggests. And a welfare law that protects an animal under human control but not a wild one of the same species cannot be applying a sentience criterion at all; it looks like a criterion of responsibility. Tell me whether the pattern is best read as one theory with rough edges, as a division of moral labour in which welfare law handles individuals, conservation law handles species, and pesticide regulation handles human health and ecosystem services, each with its own currency and no single theory underneath, or as an incoherent patchwork whose coherence we should stop trying to save.

Third, suppose the presupposed view were replaced with one on which insect mortality is a cost to be weighed. What would have to change concretely, in the threshold rule, in the registration battery, in what a field trial has to clear? Is the required conversion between insect suffering and crop yield a coherent thing to ask for, or does it collapse under the numbers, so that any serious version of the policy implies that agriculture as currently practised is not permissible? And is the operative criterion in the real world simply organized interest, with the honeybee protected because it has an industry and the monarch because it has a constituency, in which case the practice's implicit theory is a sociology of attention rather than an ethics, and the numbers are simply too large for us to picture, which is a fact about us rather than about insects?

Please give me a committed answer rather than a survey of options. Pick the reading you think does least violence to the pattern, state it as a position, defend it, and say plainly which part of the practice you would have to give up in order to hold it. If you think the pattern is simply incoherent, say so and name the piece that should go. No citations or reading lists, please; I am interested in the reconstruction and the argument, not the literature.
