I teach an upper-level seminar on consciousness — a mixed room of philosophy students and cognitive scientists, which keeps me honest — and I'm drafting the take-home essay section of the final. I want a single question on illusionism about phenomenal experience, and I want it to be unanswerable by reciting the debate's standard furniture. My students already have the standard furniture: the realist who insists that its seeming to one that one is in pain is the one datum no theory gets to deny; the illusionist who replies that the seeming is precisely the thing to be explained rather than the explanation; the reframing of the hard problem as a question about why creatures like us issue the reports we do; and the two-pronged charge that illusionism is either trivial (a thesis about our concepts of experience) or self-undermining (it denies experiences while leaning on how things seem). What I want to test is whether they can do anything with those pieces beyond shuffling them, so I've built the question around a move a student made in our last meeting.

The student's objection, which I'll state here in the form I want the exam to use. Call it the correction-asymmetry problem. With an ordinary perceptual illusion, the pull survives but the belief does not: the lines still look unequal after you've measured them, and what changes is that you stop believing they are. The alleged illusion of phenomenality is supposed to be stranger than that — it is supposed to persist through full theoretical conviction, so that the committed illusionist who has held the view for years still finds it seeming to him that he is undergoing experiences, and cannot get the seeming to weaken by understanding the theory better. In the ordinary case, better understanding weakens the grip on belief; here it is supposed to be inert. A student's way of putting it: the more thoroughly you understand a real illusion, the less like an illusion its target looks, so an illusion that gets more convincing as the theory gets clearer is a strange candidate for the title. The question should force candidates to say whether illusionism can account for this asymmetry rather than merely restate it, and if it can, what the account commits them to about the mechanism that produces the illusion — is the introspective process cognitively impenetrable in something like the way perception is, is its content somehow self-referential, or has the illusionist quietly abandoned the analogy with illusion and moved to a metaphor of the mind as a user interface, in which case they owe us an argument that the metaphor still carries the explanatory weight? I also want the question to press the weaker and stronger readings apart — the weaker one on which phenomenal properties exist but are not what we take them to be (not intrinsic, not ineffable, not private in the sense the tradition wanted), and the stronger one on which there are no phenomenal properties at all, only quasi-phenomenal ones that we systematically misrepresent as phenomenal — since I suspect the objection lands very differently depending on which is on the table, and I'd rather reward a candidate who commits to one and defends it than one who hedges across both.

So: please write the essay question exactly as it should appear on the exam sheet, self-contained and assuming no reading list, no citations, and no access to anything but what we covered in the course — the prompt itself should carry whatever apparatus it needs. Then give me a model answer of about 700 words, the kind of thing that would earn full marks, written so that a candidate who reads it can see the ladder they were supposed to climb. Finally, a marking scheme out of 25 points built around five must-hit moves, with the marks attached to each, a one-line note per move on how candidates typically lose those marks, and a closing line on what separates an A from a B — I care most about the difference between a candidate who diagnoses where the objection actually bites and one who merely names the objection and then reports that illusionists have responses.
