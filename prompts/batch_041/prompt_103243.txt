You are an expert in analytic jurisprudence with a deep working knowledge of the Hart–Fuller debate and of the later literature on legal positivism and natural law. I am a PhD student in legal philosophy, and I am drafting the take-home component of an upper-level seminar's final assessment. Please think carefully before you write, and begin by briefly brainstorming to yourself the possible cruxes of the topic before committing to one.

Produce three things: (1) a single essay question, answerable in about ninety minutes of writing; (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme worth 20 marks that names the specific moves a top answer must make, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on the error that typically costs students each mark.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard summary (Hart: law and morality are conceptually distinct, validity turns on the rule of recognition; Fuller: law has an "inner morality" of eight principles, so sufficiently unjust rules fail to be law). I want the crux to be a tension the students must resolve rather than merely name: an authoritarian regime can arguably satisfy every one of Fuller's eight principles of legality — generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, constancy, and congruence between official action and declared rule — and still enact monstrously unjust laws. If Fuller's inner morality is genuinely moral, how can that be; and if it is not, what work is it doing in his case against positivism? Pair this with the transitional-justice problem: when a post-authoritarian court refuses to treat a wicked statute as having been law, is it better described, in Hart's terms, as applying a new rule retrospectively, or, in Fuller's terms, as declining to recognise a non-law? Require students to say whether that dispute is substantive or merely verbal, and to take a side and defend it.

Keep the question self-contained: assume the students have read Hart and Fuller, but do not assume they have read Radbruch or the transitional-justice literature, and do not ask them to cite anything. No bibliographies, reading lists, or dates.
