I am planning a single seventy-five-minute session for an advanced undergraduate seminar in metaethics — twelve students, one prior course in ethics, no prior exposure to metaethics — on the family of views that locate what an agent ought to do in what an idealized counterpart of that agent would endorse, where the counterpart is improved along some axis such as information, coherence, or concern for others. Do not explain the view to me; give me the plan for explaining it. Begin with the dependency order: which components must come before which, and why, including which prior problem has to be live for the idealization move to look like an answer rather than a stipulation, and which of the standard objections cannot even be stated until some later piece is in place. Then specify the one case that should carry the session — what it must contain so that the actual agent and her idealized counterpart come apart for a reason other than the agent's ignorance, what it must leave out so that the divergence cannot be dismissed as the agent simply being misinformed, and why the obvious candidates, such as a person who is wrong about the facts or a person who has not thought long enough, will not do. Next, the misconceptions, in the order the students will actually form them, with the exact point in the sequence at which to head off each: that idealized means thinking harder and longer; that the idealized verdict is a procedure the agent could follow; that idealization can only push an agent toward greater selfishness or only toward greater impartiality; and that the whole view is a dressed-up universalizability test. I also want the session to end with the students holding two things that look inconsistent — that the idealized counterpart is still in some sense them, and that its verdict can demand the surrender of something they actually care about — so tell me where in the sequence that tension should be allowed to surface rather than be resolved. Give me one closing question that would reveal whether they can separate the authority problem, why the counterpart's verdict binds the actual agent, from the determinacy problem, whether the idealization picks out a unique verdict at all, with a note on what a strong answer and a weak answer would each look like, and one exchange to have ready for the student who asks which idealization is the correct one. Finally, say what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving it out.
