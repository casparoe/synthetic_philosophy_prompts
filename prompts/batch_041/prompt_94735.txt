I'm a philosopher working on the ethics of markets, and in a fortnights time I'm giving a paper at our department's work-in-progress seminar arguing that a regulated scheme of compensation for kidney donors should be legal — not an open free market, but a state-run scheme with fixed (not negotiated) prices, a waiting list that doesn't privilege the rich, and long-term follow-up for donors. The audience is going to be awkward for me. The seminar is mostly moral philosophers who lean Kantian and have alot of time for the corruption-of-goods line (the idea that some things are degraded or changed in character by being bought and sold), plus two health economists who'll want to talk about incentives and the crowding-out of altruistic donation, and one transplant surgeon who mostly cares about whether the scheme would actually reduce deaths on the waiting list. A couple of the philosophers are the sort who think the very fact that I'm attaching a price to a body part is already the objection, and that nothing I say about regulation touches it.

My argument, roughly, is that the commodification objection is really two objections wearing one coat, and only one of them is any good. The bad version says a price on a kidney is intrinsically degrading, full stop; I think that version can't survive the fact that we already price plenty of body-related things (labour, surrogacy in some jurisdictions, plasma donation in the US) without anyone seriously thinking the participants are thereby degraded. The good version, which I want to concede is serious, is about the terms of the trade rather than the existence of one: it's objectionable when the price is set by the desperation of the seller rather than by anything about the good itself — what I've been calling, maybe too cutely, 'ransom pricing'. My positive claim is that a fixed, non-negotiated, state-set compensation is precisely designed to avoid ransom pricing, so the serious version of the objection never gets off the ground against *my* scheme, even though it demolishes an open market.

What I need from you: act as a philosopher who's spent a lot of time in rooms like this one and knows how these objections actually land in real time, under pressure, with a chair trying to move things along. Don't give me a general survey of the literature and don't pad it out. Give me a Q&A prep sheet — the eight or so objections I'll actually get from *this* audience, not the ones I'd get from economists in the abstract or from a libertarian think tank. Order them by how much damage they'd do to me, and for each one phrase it the way the person who'll ask it would phrase it (so the Kantian ones should sound Kantian, the crowding-out one should sound like an economist), give me a two-minute reply, and flag honestly where the reply is soft or where I'm quietly bluffing. Somewhere in there, include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Before you write the sheet, brainstorm for a moment about what each faction in this audience actually cares about and what a win looks like for them — I'd rather you spend a bit of effort working out where they're coming from than rush straight to the list.
