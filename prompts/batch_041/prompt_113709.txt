Here's the list. These are my actual reactions, not the ones I think I'm supposed to have.

1. A guy glances at the clock, sees 3:00, believes it's 3:00, and it is 3:00 — except the clock stopped twelve hours ago. Justified true belief, no knowledge. This one lands completely. It feels like the case itself is doing the work, not the wording.

2. A neuroscientist knows every physical fact there is about colour vision but has spent her whole life in a black and white room. She walks out and sees a ripe tomato. Does she learn something new? Doesn't land on me at all. I think I'm being asked to imagine someone who knows absolutely everything physical and then be surprised that she's surprised. The surprise is smuggled in with the stipulation.

3. A runaway trolley will kill five people; you can divert it onto a side track where it kills one. Most people say divert it. Then: the only way to stop it is to shove a large man off a footbridge into its path. Most say no. The first case feels like it shows something real, though not very much. The second I don't trust at all — I think my "no" is just squeamishness about putting my hands on someone, because if the same thing were done by pressing a button on a remote control I'd feel differently, and I doubt the moral facts change with the remote.

4. You can plug into a machine that gives you experiences indistinguishable from the best life you can imagine. I say no thanks, and I think that verdict is real: it shows me I care about more than the contents of my experience.

5. A man who speaks no Chinese sits in a room with a rulebook and passes out correct answers to Chinese questions slipped under the door. He doesn't understand Chinese, so neither does the room, so no computer program understands anything. Doesn't land, and I think I can say why it feels rigged: the whole force comes from being told to imagine being the guy inside. The conclusion is about whether the room understands, and I'm being made to settle a question about the room by consulting my sense of what it's like to be part of it.

6. You get divided into two people, each with half your brain and all your memories, both carrying on your life and both insisting they're you. Here I have a strong verdict, and it's for exactly the same reason as in the Chinese room: I'm imagining being one of them. Same move, opposite verdict. That bothers me.

7. You wake up surgically attached to a violinist who needs your body for nine months; detach and he dies. I think it's clear you're allowed to detach, so a right to life doesn't by itself include a right to use someone's body. That lands, but it only kills one claim, not the whole position it's usually aimed at, since in the case I was kidnapped and in the real dispute nobody was.

8. A lightning bolt in a swamp assembles a molecule-for-molecule copy of me, which walks into town and does everything I'd do. Does it mean what I mean, does it know my friends? The pump as usually told doesn't work on me — I feel like the word "meaning" is doing all the work. But if you redescribe it as "a copy of me appears and finds its way around town exactly as well as I do — is that the same as my knowing it?", I do have a verdict. So my problem is with the packaging, not the case.
