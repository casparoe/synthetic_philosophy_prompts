Hi — I teach a senior seminar on decision theory and risk (mixed philosophy/econ kids, a few CS), and I'm putting together the take-home final. I want one big essay question on the unilateralist's curse, but the whole point is that they can't just define it and coast. Last year everyone wrote basically the same two paragraphs and I could've graded them without reading.

Here's what I want the question to make them do. Set it up so they have to (a) actually reconstruct the mechanism — why having more agents, each acting on their own private read of the situation, makes it more likely that somebody takes an action the group as a whole would have rejected — and then (b) make them square that with Condorcet. Both stories have independent noisy individuals and both ask what happens as the group gets bigger, yet one says the group gets better and the other says it gets worse. They should have to pin down exactly what differs. I want them to see it's about how the individual verdicts get combined — a majority that averages out idiosyncratic noise versus a setup where any single yes is enough to trigger the thing — and not about the number of people per se. If a student just says "Condorcet is about voting and the curse is about acting," they should get partial credit but not full.

Then add a couple of twists that I don't think they'll have seen in whatever summary they read. Twist one: suppose the agents can all share their evidence before anyone acts. Does the curse go away, and if so what does it turn into? Twist two: suppose the private signals are correlated — everyone's read is skewed by the same shared bias. Does that make it better or worse, and why? And a last part: have them propose a fix and defend it against the obvious objection that any rule strong enough to stop the curse also kills the good unilateral actions, the ones where it's genuinely better that somebody went first. I want them to notice the fix can't be free.

Keep it one essay question with maybe three or four labelled parts, doable in 90 minutes, aimed at a strong senior or a first-year grad student. Please write the question, a model answer of about 700–800 words, and a marking scheme worth 30 marks that tells me which specific moves separate an A from a B, plus the two or three mistakes that most often cost people. It shouldn't lean on any particular paper or name — I dont want them rewarded for having read the one article, and I don't want it to be unfair to the ones who haven't. No citations needed, they recieve the question cold.
