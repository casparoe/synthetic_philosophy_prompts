I am designing a year-long research programme around program equilibrium, and I would like your help deciding where the intellectual effort should go. The basic construction will be familiar: instead of modelling agents as choosing actions, we model each as submitting a program, allow the programs to read one another's source code, and look for profiles from which no agent could have profited by submitting a different program; in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma this yields cooperation without repetition or external enforcement, and the provability-logic refinements that make the cooperation robust to one agent's uncertainty about another's reasoning are what first drew me in. I have taught the material twice now in a graduate seminar on rational choice, and the more I teach it the less satisfied I am with my own answer to the question of what the construction is ultimately for.

Let me be more precise about the dissatisfaction, because it is the reason I am asking for a prioritisation rather than a survey. The field seems to me to run together two ambitions that are only contingently connected. The first is technical and comparative: program equilibrium is a solution concept, and the interesting questions are how it relates to Nash and to the standard refinements, which games admit which profiles, how robust the results are to the details of the code-reading assumption, and whether the provability machinery is doing essential work or merely happens to deliver the results. The second is normative and, I think, far more ambitious: program equilibrium as a formal model of the Kantian thought that agents who recognise one another as reasoners can arrive at cooperation simply by asking what each could will as a rule for all — no enforcement, no repeated play, no contract. My working hypothesis, which I would like you to treat as a hypothesis and not a premise, is that the technical literature has largely answered the first set of questions while the second has been left as a suggestive slogan, and that the second is where the philosophical value now lies.

I have roughly one doctoral student and one master's student for a year, my own time perhaps one day a week, and no appetite for empirical or computational work beyond what is needed to keep the formalism honest. So: what are the open questions here that matter most, and in what order should we take them? I care less about a list than about the reasoning — for each candidate, why it is upstream of the others, whether it is the kind of thing a philosopher or a logician rather than an engineer can actually settle, how neglected it is, and what a result would even look like. I am especially interested in whether the normative ambition can be made precise enough to be assessable: whether the program a reasoner could will as universal law can be given a determinate content, whether the robustness results are a feature of rationality or an artefact of shared proof systems, and whether anything in the construction survives once we drop the assumption that the agents can inspect each other's code. Tell me which of these you would drop, which you would put first, and what evidence over the next year would move a question up or down the list. I know the surrounding literature well enough for present purposes; what I lack is the argument for the ordering, so please spend your effort there rather than on anything resembling a reading list.
