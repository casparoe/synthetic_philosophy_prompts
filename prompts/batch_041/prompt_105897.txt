Hey, so I owe my philosophy of law reading group a short story next week — something that actually dramatizes a puzzle about self-amending rules instead of just explaining one, because we've all read enough explanations. I've been circling this for days and getting nowhere, so I'd rather you take a swing at it.

Here's the setup I want you to use (please stick to it, don't swap in your own machinery): a town of about four hundred people runs itself off a single written rulebook. Any rule in the book can be changed by a simple majority at the monthly assembly — including the rule that says so. But the founders built in a wrinkle: the rule that sets the threshold for changing the *threshold rules* is itself protected, and it can only be touched when two things are true at once — a three-quarters vote, and a certification that the change is "not retroactive," issued by a standing committee. That committee's membership, though, is defined by an ordinary rule that the majority can rewrite any time it likes. So there's a little knot: the committee that guards the meta-rules is appointed under an ordinary rule, the meta-rules guard the amendment threshold, and the amendment threshold governs the committee.

I want the story to be about the first time the town actually hits the knot. Somebody moves to change the protected threshold, and the whole thing starts eating its own tail. What I care about is the *texture* of the paradox — the moment it stops being clear whether a vote succeeded, whether a rule is still in force, and who even has standing to say. Please don't resolve it with a clever lawyer or a wise elder who cuts the knot; I want the story to sit in the indeterminacy and make the reader feel why "just amend the rules so this can't happen" isn't obviously available to them.

Two asks. First, keep it a story and not an essay in a trenchcoat — characters, a concrete scene, dialogue, some stake for whoever's caught in it. Second, end with a short afterword, a paragraph or two, telling me honestly where the story takes liberties with the actual logic of self-reference in rule systems, and which reading of the puzzle it ends up assuming. And if the setup I handed you is incoherent in some way — if I've accidentally made one of the rules do no work, or the paradox quietly dissolves under a reading I didn't notice — say so in the afterword instead of papering over it. I'd genuinely rather find out now than in front of the group.
