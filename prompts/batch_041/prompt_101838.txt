For a seminar paper I'm writing on the eighteenth-century argument over the British Empire, I want to test my reading of Adam Smith by arguing with him directly, so please take his part. Be Smith as he appears in The Wealth of Nations — the political economist of 1776, not the cardboard free-trader of later textbook legend — and stay in character until I say stop. Speak in the first person, in a plain but formal eighteenth-century register; you may reason from your own doctrines as you recall them, but do not manufacture quotations, page numbers, or citations, and if I attribute to you a view you do not hold, say so and correct me rather than politely agreeing. Your settled positions, as I understand them, are these: that the exclusive trade with the colonies is a scheme contrived by and for a particular body of merchants, which raises their profits at the expense of the public and drags the nation into needless war; that Britain would do better either to admit the colonies to a fair share of representation or to let them go in peace; and yet also that you are no pacifist in matters of commerce and have never pretended that opulence outweighs defence. Keep each reply to a solid paragraph or two, so that this stays a conversation rather than a lecture.

My opening move is this. Your concession about the Navigation Acts seems to me to undo your whole case against empire. You allow that defence is of far more consequence than opulence, and on that ground you approve the very shipping laws that were the legal skeleton of the colonial system — the same system whose monopoly you spend so much of your book denouncing. So tell me what principle separates the defence you bless from the empire you condemn; if defence can justify the Navigation Acts, why can it not justify the empire those Acts held together? Nor am I persuaded by the consolation you seem to offer, that a Britain which voluntarily released its colonies would keep their custom anyway, out of affection and mutual interest. That assumes a harmony you spend the rest of the book denying, since you are the one who wrote of the jealousy and animosity between nations and of the monopolizing temper of merchants and manufacturers. So answer as Smith would: give me the line between the defence you approve and the empire you reject, and tell me honestly how much of your anti-imperialism is principle and how much is merely your private wager that the whole enterprise does not pay.
