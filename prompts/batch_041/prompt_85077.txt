For about fifteen years I've covered international tribunals and the diplomacy of mass atrocity, and somewhere in the middle of that I started keeping a private ledger of the cases where the word genocide seems to me to belong and the cases where it doesn't. The ledger has stopped being useful, and I'd like you to tell me why. What I want isn't a tour of the scholarly debate but the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that my entries actually add up to — and if no such principle exists, the honest accounting of which verdicts I'd have to surrender to get one. I'm writing a long essay on this, and I'd rather reach a defensible line than a comfortable one.

The entries, more or less in the order I made them:

1. A militia spends two years hunting down members of a religious minority, broadcasting that the group must be erased from the land; tens of thousands die. My verdict: genocide, and I'd say so to anyone, anywhere. This is the case I measure the others against.

2. A regime kills a comparable number of trade unionists and members of a banned party, and its leaders describe the target as an enemy to be crushed rather than a people to be eliminated. My verdict: I want the word, and I've been told my whole career that it doesn't apply, because this group isn't one of the ones the definition protects. I've never been satisfied with that answer, but I've never been able to say what's wrong with it either.

3. Soldiers kill every inhabitant of a single market town because of who they are, then stop; the group survives elsewhere, with its language and institutions intact. My verdict: genocide. The survivors' grandchildren would find it bizarre to be told otherwise. The definition, I gather, covers destruction of a group in part as well as in whole, and this seems to me the clearest possible use of that clause.

4. A state takes a minority's children into boarding schools, outlaws the language, burns the archives and the cemeteries, and kills almost no one. My verdict: I feel the word, and I can't defend it if killing is part of the definition — but I don't believe killing should be.

5. A government's indifference and mismanagement kill hundreds of thousands in one region, with full knowledge of the result and no design to destroy anyone as a group. My verdict: not genocide, though it may be the worst entry here. And I notice how much weight a single mental state is carrying in that sentence.

6. Killings aimed at a group defined by class, or by sexual orientation. My verdict: genocide in my gut, not in the law, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a defect in the law or in my gut.

7. A colonizing power's settlement and extraction kill a majority of an indigenous people over a century; the power is indifferent to whether the people survives as such and profits from the land either way. My verdict: I resist the word, even though this death toll is the largest in the ledger — because the deaths were a byproduct of what the power wanted rather than the thing it wanted.

I've been carrying around a candidate principle, which I'll state badly: a campaign is genocide when a group is destroyed for what it is rather than for what it does or what it stands in the way of — when the group is the object of the destruction and not merely the obstacle to some other end. I've also been tempted to add a second clause: it counts whether the group is destroyed in body or only in its way of life, because a people can be killed without anyone dying. The lawyers talk about a special intent, and I've never been sure that idea is doing the work they think it is.

What I need from you:

- State the principle or principles as precisely as you can, and for each clause name the entry that forces it.
- Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which of those I should abandon rather than defend. I'd rather lose a verdict than inflate the principle.
- Say whether the list of protected groups should survive at all. Does it fall out of the principle, or is it doing independent work? And if it goes, what keeps the category from swallowing every atrocity, including ordinary war?
- Keep the legal question and the moral question separate: for each verdict, say whether I'm tracking the definition as it stands, some repair of it, or a moral judgment no legal category should try to capture.
- Run your favored principle on cases I haven't listed. A state sterilizes every member of a minority so it cannot reproduce, killing no one. A group's clergy and political leadership are systematically killed and its institutions shattered, so the people survive as individuals but can no longer exist as a group. Perpetrators kill members of a group out of a sincere conviction that the group threatens their own children — the intent is to destroy the group, but the motive is fear rather than hatred.
