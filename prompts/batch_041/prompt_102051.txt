I recently left software engineering for a PhD in cognitive science, so the claim that the brain is a computer has stopped being a harmless metaphor and become something my own work has to take a stand on. Please give me your credence in the following claim: that the brain performs computations in the same non-trivial sense in which a laptop does — that is, that there is a fact of the matter, independent of our descriptive interests, about which computations a brain implements, and that citing those computations does genuine explanatory work rather than merely redescribing the phenomena. I don't want a tour of the positions; I want a number with its decomposition. Give a rough probability for each of the following and say how they combine: (i) that implementing a computation is a well-defined property of a physical system at all, given that any system can be described as implementing some computation under some mapping; (ii) that the physical processes the brain exploits are Turing-computable, rather than continuous, analog, or otherwise non-computable dynamics doing essential work; (iii) that the computational level of description earns its explanatory keep by yielding predictions that lower-level descriptions do not; and (iv) that the implementing system is the brain rather than the brain-body-environment loop. My own number is about 0.65; tell me where the disagreement between us most likely lives, which term you are least confident in, and what argument or evidence would move that term the most. Answer in American English.
