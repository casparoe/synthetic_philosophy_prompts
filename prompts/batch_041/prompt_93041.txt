I'm in a philosophy of biology course and we've been reading about free-floating rationales — the idea that evolution can put a reason into the world, like the reason the bird flies south is that food is scarce up north, without the bird or anyone else ever entertaining that reason. In seminar people keep sliding between three things: (1) a trait is adaptive, (2) a trait has a function, (3) there's a free-floating rationale for a behavior. I want a thought experiment that separates (3) from (1) and (2), and also from a genuine reason that a reasoner has.

Build me a case with these specs: it has to be realistic (a real animal, plant, or everyday human-built system; no aliens, no far-future tech, no demons); it has to involve two systems that do the same observable thing in the actual world, where one has a free-floating rationale and the other has a genuine reason (or at least a minimal internal state that represents the reason); and the two must come apart in a nearby possible world, so the difference isn't just a label. Also, the case must not turn on consciousness or language — if you need those, you've cheated. And make the two systems related by descent or co-option, so one is a modified version of the other; I dont want two totally unrelated examples.

Then give me the analysis. I want exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion. Item 1: the scenario in one paragraph. Item 2: state which system has the free-floating rationale and which has the genuine reason, and why. Item 3: run the counterfactual test — change one feature of the environment and say what each system does, and what that shows. Item 4: say which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you remove it (does the distinction collapse?). Item 5: how a defender of the side that comes out badly would attack the case — what would they say is rigged or question-begging?

Please write in American English. And don't give me citations or reading lists; I just want the case and the reasoning.
