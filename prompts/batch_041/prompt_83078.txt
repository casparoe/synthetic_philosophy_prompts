I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past two years auditing a graduate seminar in epistemology, and I would like you to answer as a professional philosopher with a thorough command of the contemporary literature on knowledge attributions. The seminar leader has been kind enough to rule on cases I have brought her from my old lab, and her verdicts are these. (1) A postdoc reads the instrument log and concludes that the spectrometer was calibrated on Tuesday; nothing of consequence turns on it, no doubt has been raised, and we are chatting idly — she says he knows. (2) The same log and the same evidence, but he must decide whether to consume the last of an irreplaceable sample, a loss that would also ruin her own project; with the possibility of a misfiled log on the table, she says he does not know. (3) That same misfiling worry is raised in a case where little is at stake, and the postdoc then rules it out conclusively from the backup record; she says he knows. (4) He has only a secondhand, hedged report of the calibration; nothing is at stake and no one doubts anything; she says he does not know.

Now the new case: our technician needs the calibration fact only to complete a routine log entry, so nothing of his own turns on it, but he is being asked whether he knows in a conversation with me, in which I am deciding whether to sacrifice the last sample, and in which the professor is now a collaborator who would share the loss. No doubt about the log has been raised. Predict her verdict. Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm the several rules that fit her four verdicts equally well — among them the two positions a fellow student put to me, pragmatic encroachment and contextualism, and any others that fit at least as well — say what each predicts here, give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and name the further case or cases I should put to her in order to separate the surviving rules.
