I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or economics, and I've been trying to think my way through a puzzle about markets and knowledge that I keep running into in my work on forecasting systems. Here is the puzzle. There is a well-known result, the so-called no-trade theorem, which says roughly the following: if a group of traders share a common prior, if it is common knowledge that each of them is rational, and if the status quo allocation is already efficient, then no trade will arise from private information alone. The intuition is that any trade you propose reveals something about what you know; once everyone draws the correct inference from the mere fact that you are willing to trade, the price moves so that there is nothing left to gain. This result sits oddly next to two other things people say about markets: first, that markets are epistemically valuable because they aggregate dispersed information (the Hayekian picture of prices as condensed, decentralized knowledge); and second, that real markets churn with enormous trading volume. If the theorem is right, at least one of the assumptions behind those claims must be failing somewhere.

I want the essay to be about what all this tells us about the epistemic value of markets. Please cover the following:

- Lay out the theorem's setup and the standard escape hatches — for instance, agents not sharing a common prior, agents differing in their incentive to acquire information in the first place, or non-optimizing participants such as noise and liquidity traders.
- Then take seriously a scenario I've been chewing on: a prediction market populated entirely by copies of the same well-calibrated forecasting model, all trained on the same data, differing only in the private information they are fed at run time. Would such a market trade at all? What does the no-trade theorem predict here? And what does the answer suggest about whether the information-aggregation story of markets is fundamentally a story about aggregating private facts, or instead a story that quietly depends on prior disagreement?
- Consider the objection that even a market with zero trade might still aggregate information through prices, and ask whether aggregation that no one acts on deserves to be called aggregation in the epistemically interesting sense.
- Weigh whether the epistemic reputation of markets survives once we notice how much it appears to lean on agents holding genuinely different priors — that is, on a kind of irreducible disagreement — rather than on the pooling of private facts.
- Reach a verdict: do markets deserve their reputation as truth-tracking institutions, or is their epistemic power more limited, or more parasitic, than the Hayekian slogan suggests? Take a side and defend it.
