I am a secondary-school student preparing an independent-study paper on the question of what intelligence is, and before I compare any of the accounts I have come across — the psychometric one, the general-problem-solving one, the one that ties intelligence to success in an environment, the one that ties it to efficiency in acquiring new skills — I want a clear specification of what any such account would have to deliver. Please write me that specification: a list of desiderata that a theory of intelligence must meet, together with the consideration or case that motivates each one.

I am especially interested in requirements that arise from cases where our pre-theoretical judgement is pulled in two directions at once. Some cases I would like the list to be built around, though you should add others if they do the work better:

- a pocket calculator that is faster and more reliable than any human at arithmetic but that nobody would call intelligent;
- a chess engine that plays far beyond any human yet cannot say why it made a move, or transfer what it has learned to anything else;
- a slime mould or an ant colony that appears to solve a routing problem with no nervous system at all, alongside a group of people who together solve a problem that none of them could solve alone;
- a person who is exceptional at one narrow activity and helpless outside it, and a person who is unremarkable at every particular thing but handles novel situations well;
- a device, which I stipulate for the sake of argument, that returns the correct answer to any question put to it, including questions about its own past and future answers, but which has no memory, no goals, and cannot be wrong.

For each desideratum, state what it requires and what motivates it, and then classify it: is it a genuine constraint that any theory must respect, or is it a preference belonging to one camp — for instance, that intelligence be a single measurable quantity, that it be realizable in any substrate, that it be comparative across species, that it match ordinary usage, or that it correspond to a natural kind? Please tell me which of the requirements you believe cannot all be satisfied together, and why; if the list itself turns out to contain an inconsistency, that is the result I most want. Finally, say which single requirement does the most to rule out the accounts people actually defend, and why that one rather than the others.

You need not score the accounts against the list; I will do that myself. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and the cases behind them. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" naming the single requirement you judge to exclude the most theories.
