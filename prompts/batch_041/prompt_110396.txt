I am in the upper sixth and I am writing an extended project on the units of selection debate. My supervisor insists that the essay be built around a case I have designed myself rather than a summary of the familiar ones, so I should like you to construct one to my specifications and then demonstrate that it does what I need it to do. Please write your answer in American English, and please do not give me citations, references or a reading list of any kind: I have no access to journals, and I want the argument rather than the bibliography. You may introduce terminology of your own, provided you define it.

What the case is for. I want to know whether the disagreement between the gene's-eye view, the individual-as-unit view and the group-as-unit view can ever be a substantive disagreement about what will happen, rather than three ways of keeping the same set of books. A case that simply stipulates that groups containing the behaviour out-survive groups lacking it, and then labels the result group selection, will not do: every verdict must be generated from the framework to which it is attributed, not from my description.

Specifications.

1. A realistic setting — a colonial bird, a social insect, or a human village. If you invent an organism, keep its biology mundane and easily imagined.
2. A behaviour that is genuinely costly to the individual performing it, in survival or in offspring, and the cost must be shown rather than asserted.
3. The scenario itself is to be no longer than two paragraphs.
4. The behaviour must be inherited with high fidelity between groups and with no heritability at all among individuals within a group: whether an individual performs it depends on the group it was raised in, and not on its parents or its genes. Inside any single group there must be no heritable difference between performers and non-performers.
5. Everything else is held fixed — same ecology, same cost, same within-group dynamics — so that the inheritance channel is the only variable.

Then, in order:

(a) give the verdict of each of the three frameworks — does the behaviour spread, decay, persist, or does the framework decline to say anything — with the reasoning shown from the framework itself. I expect the gene's-eye and individual-level accounts to be unable to account for the behaviour's persistence in the way the group-level account can; if you find that they can, then the case has failed and you should say so rather than rescue it;
(b) identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work;
(c) restate the case in one paragraph with that feature reversed, and say what becomes of all three verdicts, in particular whether the case then collapses into the situation in which the three frameworks are merely keeping the same books;
(d) say how a defender of the framework that comes out worst would attack the case, which of those attacks is strongest, and whether any version of the case survives it;
(e) finish with your own verdict on whether the case shows what I want it to show, and what would make it fail.
