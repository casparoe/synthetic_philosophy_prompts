I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or the social sciences, and I have arrived at a view about the left/right distinction that I would like you to attack rather than defend. I will state it as precisely as I can, because I suspect the precision is where the trouble lies.

The view: left and right name a single real dimension of political disagreement rather than two coalition labels that happen to travel together, and the content of that dimension is the legitimacy of existing hierarchies — whether the present distribution of wealth, status, and authority is taken to be justified or taken to be something to be flattened. I mean real in an operational sense, and I am willing to be held to it. If the dimension is genuine, then three things should hold. First, knowing a person's positions on a small set of issues should let you predict their positions on issues they have never been asked about, better than chance and better than treating each issue as independent. Second, a person's position on the axis should remain roughly stable as the salient issues of the day turn over; if the axis is a standing disposition rather than a snapshot of which issues happened to get bundled together in one era, it should survive that turnover. Third, whatever remains after the axis is extracted should look like noise or idiosyncrasy rather than forming a second coherent pattern — if the residuals are themselves interpretable, I would take that as evidence of a second real dimension rather than of one dimension plus error. I also hold the stronger claim that what binds each side is shared content rather than a shared opponent: people on a given side agree with one another about the substance of the dimension, not merely about whom they oppose.

What I want is the case against this, weighted by how much it should actually move me. I do not need a reading list; I need the kinds of findings and arguments that would do the work. Which results would show that the axis is a coalition artifact, or a historical accident in which unrelated issues got bundled together? Which would show that the apparent one-dimensional structure is really several dimensions that happen to correlate in one population and come apart in another? Which would show that the binding is opposition or identity rather than doctrine? And which would show that the dimension is real as a description of what people say while doing nothing — failing to predict anything outside of survey answers?

I would also like one specific pressure test addressed directly. Suppose we found a population in which the pattern inverted: those most committed to economic leveling were consistently the ones most committed to social and cultural hierarchy. Would that falsify the view, or would a defender simply redescribe it as the same dimension wearing different clothes — and if the latter, is there any conceivable population that would falsify it? If the view can absorb that case, say so plainly, because I would rather be told that my formulation risks nothing than be handed a list of defeaters that a sympathetic defender would shrug off.

Finally, separate what a defender would themselves concede counts against the view from what only a critic would say, tell me whether any surviving version is weakened to the point of no longer doing the work I want it to do, and rank the objections from the ones that would end the discussion to the ones that are merely inconvenient.
