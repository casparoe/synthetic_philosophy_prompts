I have no formal training in biology or economics, but I have been reading popular treatments of the handicap principle and costly signaling, and I have constructed an argument that appears to prove something I cannot accept. A friend of mine, who enjoys grand formulations, describes the handicap principle as a kind of conservation law of trust: honesty in a signal must be paid for somewhere, so there is no such thing as free trust. I am not certain that is right, but it has helped me see why the following chain bothers me.

(1) A signal is honest only if it is differentially costly: a lower-quality sender must find it more expensive to produce than a higher-quality sender does.
(2) In human social life, kindness, generosity, and public moral commitment function as signals of quality or trustworthiness.
(3) Therefore, such behavior is honest only if it is differentially costly in the same way.
(4) But a self-interested faker can absorb any finite differential cost if the reward for being trusted is large enough, so the only fully reliable signal is one that ruins the sender.
(5) Therefore the most trustworthy person is the one who sacrifices the most, and the most honest kindness is the least useful kindness.
(6) That proves too much. It makes effective, well-targeted generosity suspect by definition, and it makes the saint who destroys herself more credible than the friend who quietly helps without wrecking her own life.
(7) I do not believe that, so one of the earlier steps must be wrong.

Which step should I reject, and what would rejecting it cost? I would rather keep the core of the handicap principle than accept the conclusion, but I want to know what I am giving up. Is the trouble in the idea that honesty requires differential cost, in the idea that human morality is a signal, or in the move from reliability to moral worth? Please explain why the step I should reject looked plausible in the first place, and what a repaired version of the argument would look like. I am not asking for a bibliography or for names of theories; I want the diagnosis.
