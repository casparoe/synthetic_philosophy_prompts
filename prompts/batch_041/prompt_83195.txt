I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and next month I'm giving a talk to our city's effective altruism discussion group defending the hinge of history hypothesis: the claim that we may be living at the most influential point in human history, where present actions have the largest expected effect on the long-run future. I want to give the argument a quantitative spin of my own — I define a "hinge index" for a given decade as the variance in total expected future value that can be traced to actions taken in that decade, divided by the historical average of that variance, and I claim the present index is orders of magnitude above one. The audience is about twenty people: most are forecasters and data scientists who care about base rates, calibration and falsifiability; a few are software engineers who read a great deal of effective altruism material; and two are academic philosophers, one working on decision theory and one on population ethics. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the six to eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold, name the one question I am privately hoping nobody asks, and finish with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names the single objection most likely to sink the talk.
