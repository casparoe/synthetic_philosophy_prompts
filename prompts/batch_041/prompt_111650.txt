I teach the final-year undergraduate seminar in the ethics of war, and I am drafting this year's final examination. The unit that reliably disappoints me is the one on the moral equality of combatants: candidates arrive able to reproduce, almost word for word, the traditionalist claim that soldiers on both sides are permitted to kill and liable to be killed irrespective of whether their war is just, and the revisionist rejoinder that this cannot be right because liability must track responsibility for an unjust threat — yet they cannot say what actually divides the two camps once the slogans are stripped away, and they treat the phrase as though it named a single thesis rather than a cluster of separable ones. What I want from this year's paper is a single essay question — ninety minutes, twenty-five marks, closed book — built around a distinction I pressed all term but which the textbook treatments blur: the difference between a combatant's being justified in killing and his being merely excused for doing so, and, running alongside it, the difference between equality of permissions (both sides may kill), equality of liabilities (both sides may be killed), and equality of accountability (neither side is culpable). I want the question to force candidates to see that these can come apart — that one might hold unjust combatants are excused rather than justified, and that this concession might either rescue a robust moral equality or deflate it into a merely conventional or legal one — and to take and defend a stand on which reading, if any, the thesis can survive on. The question must not be answerable by reciting the traditionalist and revisionist labels or by naming the relevant authors; the work has to be in the disambiguation and in the argument that follows from it. Assume the candidates know the jus ad bellum and jus in bello vocabulary, and that they have been told about the independence thesis (the claim that the justice of a war does not bear on the justice of how it is fought), but do not assume they have thought about how that thesis interacts with an excuse-based defence of equality.

Please write the essay question itself; a model answer of roughly six hundred to seven hundred words, written as a strong candidate would write it; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth summing to twenty-five. The marking scheme should be explicit about what separates an A answer from a B answer — naming the specific moves the former must make and the latter typically omits — and should include, for each of the five points, a one-line note on how candidates usually get it wrong. I am especially keen that the model answer take a determinate position on whether the justification/excuse distinction is a genuine rescue or a face-saving manoeuvre, and that the marking scheme reward candidates who notice the case of the combatant who kills on the unjust side while believing, on good evidence, that his cause is just.
