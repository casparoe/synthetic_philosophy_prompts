I moved to Germany for work last year, and the small moral disagreements I keep having with my partner and my colleagues have stopped feeling like quirks and started feeling like a real philosophical problem. Here are my gut verdicts. At 3 a.m., on an empty street with no car in sight, my partner will not cross against the pedestrian signal, and I have come to think she is right — not merely law-abiding but right — though a year ago I would have said the opposite and felt nothing was lost. When a clerk refused a document that had expired by one day, in a case where the office itself had caused the delay and a supervisor could plainly have waived it, I thought this was not conscientiousness but a failure to care about the person standing in front of her. When I once stayed past closing to finish helping a customer, against a rule I had been told to observe, I felt I had done the right thing; when a friend falsified a date on an official form to keep a benefit she badly needed, I felt uneasy, and I still cannot say why, since both were small rule-breaks on behalf of a person. And I find something admirable, not merely efficient, in a colleague who sorts his waste with what looks to me like religious seriousness.

The principle I have been trying out is this: a rule binds just insofar as noncompliance would be legible to others as a refusal of a shared expectation, so that a break done where no one could be affected is nothing, while a break done where it quietly weakens a practice others rely on is serious; conscience is the right tribunal only when it answers to that reliance rather than to the agent's own good intentions. I suspect the real difference lies between rules that are mere coordination devices, which ought to yield to the point they serve, and rules whose observance is itself part of what it is to belong — but I cannot make that distinction do the work, because the clerk's rule and my partner's signal both look like coordination devices to me.

Is my principle coherent, and does it actually yield my verdicts, or do I have to give some of them up? State the principle or a small set of rivals precisely, say which case motivates each clause, tell me what it predicts for cases I have not listed — including one where keeping a rule would cost someone a great deal and one where breaking it would cost no one anything I can see — and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would also like to know whether the principle ends up vindicating the German style or the American one, since I am no longer sure which I want it to.
