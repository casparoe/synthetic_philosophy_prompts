I write about science and ideas for a metropolitan daily, and over the past decade my beat has drifted toward the science of exhaustion and the politics of working hours — the thirty-five-hour week the French wrote into law, the Japanese category of death from overwork that exists because people kept dying at their desks, the four-day-week trials that consultants now sell to school boards. Ten years ago I covered the nine-week strike at the Delmar Tool & Die plant outside Dayton, Ohio, where the local traded a scheduled raise for a four-day week of thirty-two hours. That local has now asked me to give the after-dinner speech at the dinner marking the anniversary, and I would like you to write it.

The room is three rooms at once. There are the old-timers, men and women in their seventies and eighties who spent thirty years on the floor at fifty-five hours a week, some of them proud of it, some with ruined shoulders and unfinished arguments at home; anything that lands as a verdict on how they spent their lives will be heard as contempt. There are the members who were in grade school when the strike ended, who treat the fifth day as an inheritance and will not sit still for a lecture about gratitude. And there are two executives from the parent company at the head table, who have traveled a long way to hear a good story about a plant that learned to do more with less. I do not want to hand them that story. The local's president has asked me, in effect, to be honest and not to be a jerk about it.

Here is what my reporting turned up, and it is why I cannot simply write a cheerful anniversary speech. Within three years of the strike, overtime on the four remaining days had eaten back most of the gain; hours per member fell by less than two a week. The fifth day did not stay empty. It filled with elder care, with grandchildren, with second jobs, and with what people kept calling projects — a woodshop, a rebuilt truck, a certificate program — and more than one member told me, half joking, that they felt guilty on a Tuesday when they had not done anything with the day. Meanwhile the plant's injury rate fell, and its defect rate fell with it, and those two numbers are now the entire public case for the four-day week, in the company's recruiting material and in the union newsletter alike. The best argument anyone has for the fifth day is that the other four days went better.

I have been carrying around a distinction that I want the speech to test rather than to preach, and I want it to shape the whole arc of the speech rather than sit in it as one paragraph. There are two ways to defend time off. The first is restorative: the time is owed back to you because you will work better for having had it, and it is justified by what it returns to the job. The second makes no such promise — the time is yours because a life is not a shift, and it does not have to pay anyone back. Delmar looks to me like a clean natural experiment in which of those two defenses survives contact with a payroll department, and I suspect the answer is uncomfortable. Deaths from overwork are the limit case at the other end, and I want the speech to touch that without turning it into a sermon aimed at the people in the room who did the hardest years.

What I need: roughly a thousand words, six to eight minutes out loud, in American English, holding all three audiences without flattering any of them. Honor the old-timers without blessing the fifty-five-hour week that wore them out; be honest with the younger members that a shorter week can be absorbed by the very work ethic it was meant to escape; do not use the phrase work-life balance; and do not quote anyone or attribute any claim to a named scholar, since I will be the one standing at the microphone and cannot risk a line that turns out to belong to somebody else or to nobody. Do not end on a resolution I do not believe. Give me something I can actually say out loud to these people.
