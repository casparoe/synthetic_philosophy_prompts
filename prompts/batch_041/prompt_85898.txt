I'm an undergraduate currently taking a course on philosophy of cognitive science, and I've run into a snag that I suspect is my own confusion rather than a genuine paradox, though I can't locate the error and it's starting to bother me. I'd like your help finding it.

In last week's seminar the instructor put two claims on the board and endorsed both. The first was a formal point about implementation: for any physical system, however ordinary, there is some mapping of its states onto the states of a given finite-state automaton, so that the system can be described as implementing that automaton. The instructor called this the triviality of implementation and presented it as a standing embarrassment for computational accounts of the mind — a constraint on how those accounts must be stated, not a proof that everything computes. The second was a functionalist claim: what fixes a system's mental states, and therefore its moral status if we grant that only beings with the relevant mental states are moral patients, is its computational organization rather than the material it happens to be made of.

Here is the inference I keep making, in the instructor's own spirit. Consider a pot of soup simmering on my stove. By the first claim, there is a mapping under which the soup implements the same abstract machine as a brain in pain. By the second claim, mental states are fixed by computational organization. So the soup, under some description, has the mental state; and by the moral premise, the soup is a moral patient. That is absurd, and I take it as a reductio — but I can't see which step to deny without denying something the instructor would affirm.

I've tried two repairs and neither satisfies me. The first is to say that the offending mapping is gerrymandered and so doesn't count as a genuine implementation; but I can't say what makes a mapping gerrymandered rather than merely unusual without assuming what I'm trying to prove. The second, suggested by a classmate, is to require that the implementation be causal rather than merely formal; but I don't see what "causal" rules out that "formal" didn't already rule out, since the soup's states plainly do stand in causal relations to one another.

What I want is not a verdict on whether machines can be conscious, but a diagnosis of my error: where exactly does the argument go wrong, and what unstated condition on implementing a computation is doing the real work in the functionalist position? If the triviality result really does show that any physical system implements any computation, does that empty the functionalist criterion of moral status, or does it only show that the criterion must be stated far more carefully — and if the latter, what would the careful statement look like?
