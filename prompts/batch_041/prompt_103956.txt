I teach early modern political thought, and a slogan I keep encountering has come loose from any text: 'fortune favors the bold.' A colleague invoked it in a faculty meeting last month — as though it were Machiavelli's settled doctrine — to argue that we should stake the department's resources on an ambitious and risky restructuring, on the grounds that fortune rewards those who seize the initiative and punishes the cautious. In the same week a graduate student used the phrase to license ignoring what she dismissed as mere structural constraints on political action. I want to set those two disputes aside and get at the prior question: what could the one-liner even be claiming?

Lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing, as a menu ordered from the trivial to the radical — from the near-tautology that hesitating sometimes forfeits an opportunity, through the decision-theoretic claim that boldness is the optimal policy under uncertainty, to the metaphysical claim that the world is fundamentally responsive to the will of the daring. Distinguish the senses of 'fortune' the claim might presuppose — blind chance, fate, providence, or a personified force that can be coerced — since the thesis shifts with the term. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and note where an empirical objection such as survivorship bias threatens it.

Because the slogan is routinely fathered on Machiavelli, also say which of these readings, if any, he actually held — taking seriously that his considered position seems to grant fortune a large and independent share of human affairs, and to treat virtù as something closer to adaptive responsiveness to circumstance than to sheer force of will (compare his images of the flooding river and of the woman to be subdued). Finally, identify which reading my colleague and my student would each need in order to reach their conclusions, and say whether any reading on your menu actually does the work they want. You needn't supply citations; I care about the analysis.
