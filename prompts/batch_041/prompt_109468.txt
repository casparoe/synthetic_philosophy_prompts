I'm in a year-long seminar on the ethics of memory — we've been working through Margalit on the duty to remember and Rieff on the case for forgetting — and the conversation keeps collapsing because everyone grabs the single word "memory" and then argues past each other. So I've been trying to do it the honest way: write down my gut verdicts on particular cases first, then look for whatever principle they add up to. Here is what I have. (1) A village keeps an annual walk to a mass grave in the woods; the walk should continue, and I feel this as an obligation, not a taste. (2) A city wants to take down a monument to a massacre because the neighborhood around it has changed; it should stay up, even though I have no attachment to it and even though it makes daily life a little worse for people who had nothing to do with the crime. (3) A survivor asks that her recorded testimony be sealed until everyone named in it has died and then destroyed; honor her request, though I can't say why I feel so sure. (4) The granddaughter of a man who committed a wartime atrocity and then lived quietly, whose family has never told anyone, should be free to publish what she knows, and I think she has something close to a duty to. (5) That same survivor's private diary, which she burned before she died: entirely fine, nobody wronged anyone. (6) A town's registry of who denounced whom during an occupation, destroyed in a flood and never reconstructed: acceptable — I don't think anyone was obliged to rebuild it. (7) The same registry, quietly shredded decades later by people who wanted to spare the denouncers' families: not acceptable at all — and I notice the outcome, no registry, is identical to (6). (8) A wrongdoer who has genuinely changed asks his victim to stop telling the story in public: he has no standing to ask, though I'd want her to be kind about it. (9) A town where the descendants of both sides still live side by side decides to end the public commemoration but keep the records open: allowed, maybe even right, even though in (1) I said the walk should continue.

My own guess is that the duty isn't about remembering at all but about not erasing — that what's forbidden is a deliberate act of suppression, not a state of not-remembering. That would explain why (6) and (7) come apart, why (5) is fine, and why (8) is a non-starter, since he's asking for an act of suppression and has no standing to. But it makes (1) and (2) mysterious, since keeping a walk going or a monument standing is an act of remembering rather than a refusal to erase, and it doesn't yet explain why (4) is a duty while (5) is merely permitted. And (3) nags at me: if the duty is not to erase, honoring her request looks like licensing exactly the thing I called forbidden in (7). A friend in the seminar offered a distinction that seemed promising — between custody of a record and acknowledgment of a wrong, on the thought that you can lose custody without losing acknowledgment, and hold custody without ever acknowledging anything — but I can't get it to do all the work either.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets all nine verdicts or as close as anyone can reasonably get. State it precisely; tell me which clause each case is doing the work for; say what it predicts about cases I haven't listed (give me at least two, and make one of them a case where your principle appears to cut against my verdict, so I can see the cost); and be honest about which of my nine verdicts has to go. I'm also stuck on the case I keep going back and forth on, and I'd like you to settle it if you can: a family has kept a benign founding lie alive for four generations — a story about how they came by their land that is false in a way that flatters them and quietly obscures a dispossession — and everyone involved is long dead, while the current generation would be hurt by the truth. No duty to correct it, a duty, or something in between? Please answer in American English, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, and don't tell me the cases are underdescribed — if a case needs a stipulation, make it and say that you've made it.
