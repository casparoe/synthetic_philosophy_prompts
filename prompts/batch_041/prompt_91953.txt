I'm in a film-and-philosophy reading group and it's my turn to lead on Metropolis, the Fritz Lang one, and I want to read it through Marx on exploitation and alienation — but I'm nervous about the obvious version, the usual "machines crush the workers, rich live up top, poor live below" thing that everyone's already heard a hundred times. So here's the puzzle I keep going back and fourth on. The film shows alienation up to its eyeballs: Freder, the boss's son, is the most estranged person in it — he can't stand the Eternal Gardens or the Yoshiwara, he's sick with guilt, he watches the M-Machine turn into Moloch eating men alive. But where's the exploitation? We never see a wage, a working day, a surplus, a rate of profit. The workers are just down there, toiling, and the film barely seems to care wether they're paid or how much of their labor Fredersen pockets. So what I want is a reading that takes the film's own picture of the underground seriously and takes Marx seriously, and tells me whether that underground is really Marx's real subsumption of labor to capital — workers remade into living appendages of the machine — or just a pile of drudgery imagery, and whether the two Marxian ideas, exploitation (which is about the extraction of surplus) and alienation (which is about estrangement from species-being), actually come apart here, and if they do, what that tells us about which one the film is really about.

And I want you to deal with the ending, because it bugs me. Rotwang's robot wearing Maria's face whips the workers into wrecking the machines and drowning their own children, and then it all gets patched up when Freder becomes the heart that mediates between the head (his father) and the hands (Grot). My suspicion is that this is the film doing ideology rather than resolving anything — capital and labor reconciled by a son's love, which is exactly the kind of fantasy Marx would call out, maybe even a Feuerbachian one where the "heart" is a projected human essence doing the work that politics is supposed to do. But I don't want you to just agree with me. If there's a reading on which the film is genuinely critical of its own ending, or on which the false Maria is doing something more interesting than standing in for false consciousness, tell me, and tell me where I'm wrong. No citations, no reading list, I don't need you pointing me at texts — just argue with the film. And be straight about how confident you are in the reading, and flag the one spot where you're least sure you've got either the film or Marx right.
