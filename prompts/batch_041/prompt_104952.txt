I teach a mid-level undergraduate course on rationality and choice for philosophy students who have had no economics, and next week I reach the unit on solution concepts. Two questions from this term's students have left me unsure what I should be telling them. The first came with a payoff table for a single player choosing among three rows against two columns: the top row pays 2 in the left column and 0 in the right, the middle row pays 0 and 2, and the bottom row pays 0.9 in both. The student observed that no pure row does better than the bottom row against both columns, so on my own account the bottom row is not dominated; but she pointed out that flipping a fair coin between the top and middle rows pays 1 in either column, which beats the bottom row everywhere. She wanted to know whether my claim that dominance reasoning eliminates exactly the strategies that are never best responses was simply false. The second student, having noticed that one can sometimes delete one strategy first and reach a different surviving set than if one had deleted a different strategy first, asked whether the order of elimination is arbitrary and therefore whether the whole procedure is ill-defined.

I had been teaching the method as a well-defined one: the surviving set is invariant under the order of deletion, and iterated dominance elimination is the same thing as iteratively discarding strategies that are never best responses. Now I am inclined to demote it in the lecture to a rough heuristic rather than a solution concept, since it appears to depend on choices the students can see are arbitrary and to fall apart the moment mixtures enter. Is that the honest thing to do?

I would like to know which of the claims I have been making are actually true, which need qualification, and how to present the qualification to students who have the example in front of them and will not be satisfied by being told that the issue is merely technical. Please be specific about where the fragility actually lies and where it does not, since I would rather repair the lecture than quietly drop the topic.
