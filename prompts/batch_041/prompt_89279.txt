For a graduate seminar handout on the ethics of agriculture, write a taxonomy of the positions one can hold on insecticide use and pest control, in the form of a branching decision tree. Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names: the branch nodes come first, and the named or unnamed positions get located at the leaves afterwards. The nodes I need are: whether insects and other invertebrates are sentient enough for their deaths to register morally, and what a view does with that question while it remains unsettled; whether death is itself a harm or only suffering is, which decides how a fast-acting contact spray compares with a slow-acting poison the insect outlives for days; whether killing a pest as the intended means differs from killing non-target arthropods as a foreseen side effect of the same application; whether the value of a pollinator population or of an ecosystem is anything over and above the welfare of the individuals composing it; and whether the fact that we bred the pest by planting the monoculture changes what we owe it. Phrase every node as a question a reader can answer without already knowing where it leads. Keep one running case throughout: a greenhouse manager choosing among a broad-spectrum foliar spray, a targeted biological control, and destroying the crop. Each leaf should be labeled with the position's name, or marked "unnamed" if no one defends it, and should carry one sentence naming its most awkward commitment; note any two leaves the tree cannot distinguish, and briefly diagnose any combination of answers that no position occupies — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
