I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and for a piece I'm working on I've been arguing for a fairly uncompromising version of origin essentialism. Stated a little more carefully than it usually is: an individual organism — a human being, say — could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones it did, and arguably could not have originated from any different zygote at all; its actual origin is part of what it essentially is, rather than a merely causal fact about how it happened to get started. The familiar intuition pumps have convinced me, and the view seems to do real explanatory work elsewhere, so I'm inclined to keep it.

What I want from you is a map of its exposure. What would count against it? Not only the stock objection that it makes accidental-seeming facts essential, but the full range: which intuitions I'd have to surrender, which possible cases the view cannot describe without strain, which results in neighboring areas — the metaphysics of persistence, of kinds and essences, of vagueness — it stands or falls with, and above all which real or imminent developments in reproductive biology it has to accommodate. Two of those interest me especially. The first is mitochondrial replacement, in which a child is made from the nuclear DNA of two parents but the mitochondrial DNA of a third donor, so that the organism's causal origin is, quite literally, split. The second is the prospect of gametes manufactured from ordinary body cells, which threatens to loosen the tie between an individual and the particular sperm and egg that, on the standard telling, are supposed to fix it. I'd also like you to take the twinning case seriously — an early embryo that could divide into two individuals — since it seems to put pressure on the idea that a single zygote is what an individual essentially originates from.

Sort what you give me by how much it actually threatens the position, from the defeaters that would be fatal down to the ones that are merely awkward, and say in each case whether a committed origin essentialist would concede the point or deflect it. Tell me, too, what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it risks anything at all or has been insulated from risk by the usual qualifications — and if it has, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or just the price of doing metaphysics.

I want a committed answer rather than a survey: tell me which of these defeaters you think actually lands, whether the position as I've stated it survives, and if you think it survives only because it has been quietly weakened into something else, say that plainly.
