I run a philosophy-of-economics reading group — 12 people, a mix of grad students in philosophy and econ, one 90-minute session. I want the whole session to be a game, no lecture up front, and the thing I want them to run into is the no-trade result: the claim that if everyone is rational, and that rationality is common knowledge, and everybody knows everybody else's prior, then purely informational trade shouldn't happen at all — you shouldn't want to trade with someone who wants to trade with you, because their willingness to trade is itself evidence about what they know. I want them to feel that, not hear it.

Here's the setup I've half-designed, and I need you to turn it into a runnable activity plus debrief. Please keep these pieces, they're doing real work:

- The asset pays off the total of 12 secret numbers. Each participant draws one card with a number on it and sees only their own. Everyone starts out holding one identical unit of the asset, so nobody has any reason to trade except what they privately know. That's the point — it should feel like a level playing field at the start.

- There's a market maker who has to post one single price and honor it for anyone who wants to buy or sell. That person will almost certainly lose points and should.

- Before any trading opens, there's a public round where everyone has to say out loud whether they intend to buy or sell at the posted price, and only then does trading open. I want this round to be the moment the whole thing curdles, so build the payoff scheme so that the public announcement actually matters.

- One participant is a plant. Tell them, privately, to trade unconditionally no matter what the price is — no reasoning, just take the other side of whatever comes. The group shouldn't know who it is. I want them to discover, in the debrief, that this is the one thing that re-opens trade, and to argue about which assumption it broke.

Give me: the materials (cards, envelopes, a price board, whatever you need), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the payoff scheme in points that convert to snacks or candy at the end (no real money), the exact instructions you'd read aloud for each phase, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the pieces of the argument — the common prior, common knowledge of rationality, the inferential feedback from other people's willingness to trade — and it should get them to see why the price itself is doing epistemic work, i.e. that a market price is a claim about what other people know. Don't let them hear the name of the result until the debrief.

Two more things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I assume people will trade anyway out of boredom or politeness, and I want to know how to handle that without scolding them, plus any other failure modes you see. Second, be honest about what the game can't reproduce: I already suspect we can't actually instantiate common knowledge of rationality in a room of 12 people, and that real markets have gains from trade the theorem just abstracts away, but I want you to say clearly which feature of the idea the activity simply cannot deliver and that I'll have to supply by talking.

Keep the tone practical, like you're handing me a lesson plan you've actually run.
