Play the role of a committed accuracy-first epistemologist — an unnamed proponent of the view that the sole fundamental epistemic good is accuracy, understood as closeness to the truth; that credal norms such as probabilism and conditionalization are to be vindicated by expected-accuracy and accuracy-dominance arguments; and that any admissible way of scoring a credence function must be strictly proper, so that an agent can never do better, in expectation, by misrepresenting her own credences. Hold this position across the whole exchange, until I say "stop." Be a serious opponent rather than a caricature: concede a minor or merely verbal point when it is genuinely forced, but do not abandon the core commitment that accuracy, and nothing else, is what makes credences epistemically good. Write each reply as a single substantive paragraph, so that the exchange reads as a conversation rather than a treatise, and do not step outside the role to comment on the exercise.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to meet it rather than talk around it. Suppose we extend the framework from precise credences to interval-valued ones, as anyone sympathetic to imprecise probabilities will want. Take the Brier score for a precise credence c on a proposition whose truth value is t, namely (c − t)², and define the score of an interval [l, u] optimistically, as the best score achievable by any precise credence inside it: zero if the truth value lies in the interval, and otherwise the squared distance from the truth value to the nearer endpoint. Now notice what follows. The maximally vague interval [0, 1] contains every truth value, so it scores zero on every proposition no matter how the world turns out; nothing can beat it, and every more determinate state is at least as badly off. So if accuracy is the fundamental good and norms are whatever maximizes expected accuracy, your framework appears to recommend total vagueness as the epistemically ideal state. I can hear the first reply — that the optimistic score is not truth-tracking, because its minimum is not located at the truth. But truth-tracking for an interval has an obvious reading too: the interval contains the truth value, and under that reading [0, 1] is perfectly truth-tracking and still wins. So you must either accept that accuracy-first endorses [0, 1], or produce an admissibility constraint strong enough to kill my score; and I claim any such constraint will also kill every genuine interval credence, leaving you with a framework that is precise-credence-first in disguise rather than accuracy-first. Which horn do you take, and why is it not fatal?
