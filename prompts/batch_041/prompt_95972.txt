I'm in a second-year course called Global History: Whose Past? and there's an argument the seminar leader sketched in week three that I've been carrying around ever since like a stone in my shoe. She kept insisting we hold two things apart -- having a position and being privileged -- and I honestly can't tell whether that distinction is doing any real work or is just her way of dodging the hard question, so maybe you can settle it for me. Here's the arguement, cleaned up as best I can: (1) There's no view from nowhere; every account of the past is written from some place, by someone with a position in the world. (2) A position isn't just a filter on what you can see, it also ranks it -- writing from somewhere makes some things central and others peripheral, and 'central' is never a neutral description. (3) So every history, simply by being written from somewhere, privileges some lives over others; the Eurocentric histories we spent the first weeks dismantling are only the loudest instance of something unavoidable, not a special case. (4) If privileging is the disease, the cure can't be swapping one privileged standpoint for another, so it has to be an account that privileges none -- she had a name for this, the choral constraint: an account is adequate only if no voice in it is a soloist, i.e. the past told from everywhere at once. (5) But an account with no soloists has no way of deciding what to include, since mattering is always mattering-to-someone, so it isn't a history at all, it's just everything that happened. (6) And the demand for a standpoint-free account is itself voiced from a standpoint -- the one that believes it can survey all the others -- which is precisely the imperial standpoint we were supposed to be escaping. So the critique of Eurocentrism seems to eat itself, and my friend says fine, that only shows history is impossible, which can't be right because she's three chapters into a dissertation on exactly this. I'd rather not give up (1) or (2) if I can help it; (6) I could probably live without.

So where does it actually go wrong -- is this a real dilemma or is there a slip, and if there's a slip, why did it look so smooth going past? Whichever step you'd drop, tell me what dropping it costs, because the two options I can see are both worse than the argument: either where you write from doesn't really matter (which throws out everything we've done all term), or nobody can write history responsibly, which I don't believe. And here's the case I keep chewing on and can't settle, which might be a test of whatever you propose: an 1890s colonial campaign where the officers' letters home describe a punitive patrol against bandits, and the villagers' descendants describe soldiers burning the village and shooting people who couldn't run. If I give both accounts equal narrative weight I can't call it a massacre, and that feels like exactly the cowardice the critique was meant to prevent; if I call it a massacre I've picked a winner, which (4) forbids. I cant tell if that shows the argument is right and my instinct is soft, or the other way round. What am I actually committed to here?
