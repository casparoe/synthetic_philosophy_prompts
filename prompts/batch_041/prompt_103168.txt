For most of my working life I was an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind. Again and again I meet an analogy that seems to carry an enormous amount of weight while being treated as though it were self-evident: that the brain is a digital computer, and more pointedly a stored-program machine of the kind the engineers of the 1940s specified, in which a single memory holds both the data and the instructions, a processor fetches and executes those instructions one step at a time, and everything is synchronized by a clock. Von Neumann, whose name the architecture bears, wrote a short book near the end of his life, The Computer and the Brain, comparing brains with the machines of his day; as I read him he was markedly more guarded about the comparison than most of the people who invoke it now. I would like you to audit the analogy for me rather than restate it.

Begin with a correspondence table. For the machine side—processor, memory, instruction set, stored program, clock, buses, the designer or programmer, the input and output devices—say what, if anything, plays that role in the brain, and mark honestly where the correspondence is exact, where it is loose, and where it is simply absent.

Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the use people make of the analogy from those that are merely picturesque. Several disanalogies seem to me serious. First, the machine separates the store from the processor and the instructions from the data; the brain appears to do its storing in the very structures that do its computing, and to have no program laid out anywhere that a homunculus could read. Second, the machine is digital, clocked, and largely serial; the brain is massively parallel, has no central clock, and signals by graded potentials, spike timings, and population rates depending on where you look—so it is not clear that the brain's elementary signalling is digital in the relevant sense at all, whatever the textbook slogan about the all-or-nothing spike. Third, the machine's parts are precise and its reliability is engineered; the brain's parts are sluggish and noisy, and whatever reliability it achieves is bought statistically, by redundancy—and von Neumann is supposed to have turned to precisely this problem, of building reliable computation out of unreliable components, which cuts both ways. Fourth, the machine has a designer, a specification, and a purpose; the brain has a history. Fifth, a general-purpose computer is a device for manipulating symbols, whereas a brain is the organ of an organism that must eat, flee, and reproduce. I would add a worry of my own: the word computer has meant rather different machines in each generation—von Neumann's serial digital engine, the connectionist network, the graphics processor—so that the comparison may be less a claim than a mirror, reflecting whichever machine is currently in fashion.

Then tell me what the analogy quietly imports that the brain does not supply. In particular, is the move from the brain is a computer to the mind is software—and thence to multiple realizability, substrate independence, and the promise of simulation—licensed by the analogy, or merely smuggled in by it?

Finally: does the conclusion its users draw, that cognition is computation and will in due course yield to the methods that tamed computing machines, survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along? And if the machine comparison is the wrong one, what would a better comparison be, given what we actually know about nervous systems?
