OK so some context first: I spent thirty-five years in a plant physiology lab, retired about six years ago, and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy — a reading group at the local university, a couple of online courses, and a long-running argument with my neighbour, who used to sit on a hospital ethics committee and is much better at this than I am. The thing I keep chewing on is the therapy/enhancement distiniction, and I've decided the only way I'll get anywhere with it is to watch two people who genuinely disagree go at each other, rather than read another summary. So write me that dialogue.

Call the first one Dr. Aldous Reyner, a physician-researcher who defends the distinction and thinks medicine has a legitimate boundary — roughly, it's in the business of treating disease and disability, not of upgrading already-healthy people — and who wants to anchor "disease" in something like departure from species-typical functioning. Call the second one Priya Malhotra, who works on enhancement policy and thinks the distinction either can't be drawn or, where it can, doesn't carry the moral weight people load onto it — what matters is whether an intervention makes a life go better and whether it's available to everyone, not whether the condition is a "disease." Make them both sharp, no strawmen, and make them actually answer each other instead of delivering speeches past one another. Here are the cases I want them forced through, because these are the ones tying me in knots: (i) growth hormone for a healthy but very short kid whose parents are worried about his life chances; (ii) a drug that builds muscle in healthy seventy-year-olds well beyond anything training could do; (iii) a memory-dampening drug given to someone after a trauma that doesn't rise to any diagnosis; (iv) a pill that makes people reliably more altruistic; (v) treating a risk factor in someone who would never have gotten the disease anyway; and (vi) the one that really gets me — a case where an enhancement quietly becomes the new normal, so what used to be an upgrade is now what a healthy person is expected to have, and the therapy/enhancement line seems to move under everyone's feet. I want them to have to say what "normal functioning" even means when the trait is continuous — height, say, or whatever we decide intelligence is — and whether a statistical norm can carry any moral weight at all; and I want each of them, at some point, to concede the case that hurts their own side most and then say what their view still can't explain. Oh, and my own half-baked idea, which I'd like them to test rather than just nod along to: that the line should be drawn not between disease and health but between restoring a capacity the human species has always had and adding one it has never had — does that survive contact with (i) through (vi), or is it just as wobbly as the original? Don't bother with references or who-said-what, I can't look anything up and I don't want a bibliography, I want the argument itself. Finish by telling me where you think the exchange leaves things.
