I write fiction, and for the better part of two years I have been at work on a novel that follows one family across roughly four decades. That has meant spending an unreasonable amount of time deciding what to call the people in it — a cohort, a generation, a moment, a vintage — and, more troublingly, deciding what those labels are supposed to explain. What began as a drafting problem has turned into an essay, and I would like your comments on it. What I want most is a check on accuracy: whether the philosophical and statistical claims I make are true, and whether I have stated contested things as though they were settled. Secondarily I want to know whether the argument is worth making at all, or whether I have dressed up a truism. I am not asking for a reading list; I want an interlocutor, not a bibliography.

The essay is below, under the title The Almanac Fallacy.

The word "generation" is asked to do two jobs at once. It names a set of people born within a window of years, and it explains why those people are the way they are. My novel needs the first job and keeps sliding into the second. A character's birth year is a fact about the calendar; her character is a fact about her. Nothing licenses the move from one to the other, and yet "she's such a Gen Xer" is almost always offered as an explanation rather than a description.

So: are generations real kinds? Two easy answers present themselves, and I distrust both. The first says yes — generations are real roughly as species are real, each carrying a formative imprint that fixes a shared disposition. This overstates the case and cannot survive contact with the enormous variation inside any ten-year birth window. The second says no — the labels are arbitrary bins drawn by marketers and retained by journalists. This is bracing but cannot explain why the labels stick, why people adopt them, why a nineteen-year-old will tell you with perfect seriousness what her generation is like.

A better answer comes from the philosophy of social kinds. Some classifications are not mirrors but molds. A classification is interactive when the people classified can become aware of the classification, take it up, and adjust themselves in its light — and when those adjustments then feed back into the classification, which is revised, which prompts further adjustment. The idea is usually credited to Ian Hacking, and it does not require any metaphysical extravagance. Once a cohort has a name, employers begin to hold expectations keyed to that name, institutions build products for it, the people in it describe themselves in its vocabulary, and the name begins to pick out something more than a date range. Generations, on this account, are real the way a dialect or a tradition is real: sustained by the mutual expectations of the people who use the term. Real, but not natural.

Here is the turn I actually want to argue, and it is a turn against my own convenience. If generations are partly constituted by the labeling, then they are precisely the sort of thing that resists being used as a cause. And there is a harder, independent problem underneath. Cohort, age, and period are collinear. At any given moment, your year of birth plus your age equals the current year, so the three are not three independent variables but two and a sum. Any claim that a cohort is distinctive must therefore hold two of the three fixed while attributing the difference to the third, and the data do not dictate which to hold fixed. Different defensible constraints applied to the same table produce different stories. "This cohort is more anxious" is equally compatible with "young people are anxious, and this cohort is young," with "everyone became more anxious, and this cohort is being measured at the wrong moment," and with "this group is different and will stay different." A single cross-section cannot tell them apart. The label, which is cheap, is doing the work that the identification problem says cannot be done.

Consider a thought experiment. Two countries with identical demographic histories, but only one of them develops a name for a particular cohort. If, a decade later, the named cohort differs systematically from its unnamed twin, then the naming did work. If not, the name was decoration. I find this test attractive and I suspect it is too easy.

What follows for a novelist is not that I should stop using the word. I should use it the way a wine merchant uses a vintage: as a shorthand for the conditions under which something was made, not as a claim about what those conditions caused. The trouble is that the same word does double duty, descriptive shorthand and causal explanation, and the slide between them is invisible. I call this the almanac fallacy: reading a date off a grid and treating the grid as the cause. Generations are real enough to organize a novel and not real enough to explain a person.

I would like the following from you.

1. Accuracy, first and most important. Is the interactive-kinds account I give correct, and do generations genuinely satisfy it, or is the resemblance superficial — a label that people merely report rather than one that restructures them? Is the collinearity point stated correctly, and is it fair to say that the choice of constraint substantially determines the conclusion rather than merely constraining it? Flag anything I have overstated, and anything I have presented as settled that is in fact disputed.

2. Is the thesis interesting, or is it the familiar "socially constructed, but real enough to matter" move in a new coat? Where is the weakest joint in the argument? If you think the central distinction collapses, say so and say where.

3. Does my coinage earn its keep? If "the almanac fallacy" is a re-description of something that already has a standard name, tell me what that name is, so that I can judge whether my term adds anything to it.

4. Is the two-countries thought experiment well-formed, or does it smuggle in the assumption it is meant to test — for instance, by treating the act of naming as independent of the divergence it is supposed to explain? If it is broken, suggest a repair.

5. Does the move from interactivity to causal unusability actually follow, or have I stapled two separate arguments together and called them one?

6. Finally, suggestions for tightening, and a verdict on whether the closing sentence overclaims.
