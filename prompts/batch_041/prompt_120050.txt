Two members of my reading group spent most of a session on the dark forest hypothesis and got nowhere, and I've been turning it over since. I've read more than enough rationalist blog argument to know this is a well-worn fight, but I couldn't referee it.

Mira's line is that the conclusion is forced. Grant the two axioms — survival is every civilization's first need, and civilizations keep expanding while the matter available to them does not — then add the chain of suspicion and the possibility of a technological leap, and you get the result: nothing a civilization says now can bind its values or its capacities over the timescales involved, so any civilization that reveals itself will be read as an existential threat by someone, and the only stable policies are concealment or preemption. A silent sky is the theory's prediction.

Tomas thinks the conclusion is smuggled in. First, preemption is supposed to be the dominant move, but the axioms say nothing about what a strike costs or who can see it; if a strike is traceable, then destroying a neighbour is the loudest possible broadcast of your own position, which makes the preemptive branch self-defeating and leaves concealment as the only survivor. Second, the theory is unfalsifiable as stated: once everyone knows the argument, mutual concealment is common knowledge, so a silent sky is exactly what a crowded and cautious sky looks like — and equally what an empty one looks like. Third, the payoff structure is never specified; the axioms are compatible with many games, and deterrence between two parties who can both see each other is as stable as the hunt.

Mira's reply: cheap talk doesn't bind over these timescales, so mutual deterrence isn't an equilibrium anyone can count on; and traceability cuts both ways, since your own leakage is only a matter of degree, which argues for striking early rather than for waiting. She concedes the falsifiability point only in part — the theory is meant to be derived from the axioms, not tested against the silence alone. Tomas answers that this is the move he'd resist: a strategy can't be derived from axioms that don't fix the game, and he suspects the whole thing draws its force from being told that we are the naive ones.

Please adjudicate move by move — treat Tomas's three complaints separately — saying which claims actually follow from the stated premises and which are added assumptions doing the real work, who is right about what, and what both of them are missing.
