I am a third-year undergraduate in a strategic studies seminar, and I am trying to get straight on a conceptual issue that our unit on brinkmanship keeps circling. Our instructor introduced a distinction between chosen escalation and chance-loaded escalation. In the first, a state threatens to retaliate if attacked and retains the decision to do so; in the second, the state arranges its forces, command arrangements, and procedures so that retaliation becomes a live probability rather than a deliberate choice, for example through pre-delegated launch authority, automated response systems, or a tripwire that makes escalation nearly automatic once certain thresholds are crossed. The suggestion is that the second kind of threat can be more credible than the first precisely because it does not require anyone to choose catastrophe at the moment of decision. I can follow the slogan but not the underlying machinery. Please explain, in a self-contained way, what it means for a threat to leave the final step to chance, how that differs from a conditional intention to retaliate, and why the difference is supposed to solve the credibility problem without simply replacing it with a different irrationality. Then lay out the main objections: that chance-loaded escalation may be indistinguishable in practice from a loss of control; that it can create incentives for the other side to pre-empt; that it may be self-defeating if both sides adopt it; and that credibility may mean different things depending on whether we are asking whether a threat is believed, whether it is rational, or whether it is morally permissible. Finally, apply the analysis to a hypothetical: a conventionally weak state with a small nuclear arsenal faces a conventional invasion by a larger nuclear-armed neighbor, and it considers stationing foreign troops on its territory and pre-delegating limited launch authority to field commanders. Does this arrangement solve the credibility problem, relocate it, or generate a new paradox? Be explicit about which parts of your answer are conceptual claims about deterrence and which are empirical claims about command and control.
