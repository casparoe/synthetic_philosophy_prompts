So I'm in this ethics seminar on blame and forgiveness, and we spent the last two weeks on standing to blame, and I've managed to talk myself into a view I'm not sure I actually believe, which is annoying. Here's the view: blame is basically a form of moral address — when you blame someone you're demanding that they answer to a norm, you're putting yourself in the position of someone entitled to make that demand — and the reason a hypocrite can't blame isn't that they're doing something morally *bad*, it's that they aren't really blaming at all. The address misfires. It's not a wrongdoing, it's closer to a failed performative, like a promise made by someone with no authority to bind themselves (ok that analogy is bad but you get it). So the hypocrite's "you shouldn't have done that" is more like a broken speech act than a bad deed. What I like about this is it explains why hypocrisy feels like it *silences* you rather than just making you a jerk — you don't get to complain, the standing is just gone, and it's gone in a weirdly automatic way, not because you're a worse person. For a concrete case: my roommate never does his dishes and I want to call him out about it, but I do the exact same thing, and when I imagine saying something it doesn't feel like I'm being mean, it feels like the words would just kind of bounce off — which is the intuition I'm trying to build the theory around.

What I want from you is the case against it. What would count against this view? Not just "hypocrites sometimes blame fine," I mean the actual defeaters, and ranked — fatal, serious, merely awkward. Which cases would a defender have to concede are a real problem, and which would they just wave away by re-describing? Some things I'm already worried about: (1) if standing requires that you're committed to the norm, then anyone who's ever broken a norm they still endorse loses standing for good, which makes blame nearly impossible and smells like a reductio; (2) is the failed-speech-act framing even right, or is it doing sneaky work by *redefining* blame so that hypocritical blame can't count as a counterexample — i.e. have I just insulated the view from disconfirmation, and if so is that a bug or is it just what it means to give an account of a concept? (3) people plainly do seem to feel blamed by hypocrites, and we do seem to keep holding each other to account anyway, which might be evidence the address lands after all; (4) does my view have anything to say about the difference between a flat-out hypocrite and someone who's genuinely trying to do better, or someone guilty of a related-but-different wrong — a decent theory should draw those lines and I suspect mine just smears them into one blob; (5) the tu quoque thing — everyone knows "you do it too" is supposed to be a fallacy, so maybe hypocrisy is irrelevant to the *content* of a criticism but relevant to the *standing to voice* it, and I can't tell if my view respects that distinction or quietly collapses it. Also, honestly, I'd find a case where a total hypocrite's blame still seems legitimate — or one where a non-hypocrite's blame still misfires — way more persuasive than any general argument, so prioritize those if you've got them. Give me your honest read on how exposed this thing is; and if it turns out it can't really lose, tell me whether that's a feature (it's tracking something real about the concept of blame) or a bug (I've made it unfalsifiable).
