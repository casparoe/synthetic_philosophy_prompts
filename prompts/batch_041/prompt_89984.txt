My friend and I got into a long argument last night about constitutional monarchy — UK, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Japan, that whole family — and I want someone to settle what the thing actually commits you to. I keep feeling like there's a real philosophical position buried in there and neither of us could name it.

Heres the puzzle. You've got a full-on democracy: parliament is elected, the government answers to it, everyone's supposed to be equal citizens. Then sitting on top of the whole arrangement is a hereditary head of state who got the job by being born, who nobody voted for, who can't be thrown out by any election. Fine, they're "ceremonial." But the weird part is that the powers are still there on paper. Royal assent to legislation is still formally required. The monarch formally appoints the prime minister, formally dissolves parliament, formally opens it. In practice none of that is ever exercised against the elected government's wishes — the last time a monarch outright refused assent was literally centuries ago. So you've got an institution that keeps a set of powers precisely by never using them.

And it gets better. The monarch is supposedly above the law in some ways — you can't just sue the crown the way you'd sue a normal defendant, and the old idea that the king can do no wrong is still floating around — but they're also supposed to be subject to the law like anybody else. Some of these countries have an official state church, and the monarch has to belong to it and be its head, even though the country is now mostly secular and pluralist. Japan did something a little different: the emperor is written into the constitution as a symbol of the state and the unity of the people, with explicitly no governmental powers at all.

So my question: what does this whole arrangement presuppose philosophically? I don't want a history lecture or a reading list — I want the implicit theory dug out. Specifically:

- What view of political legitimacy does it need? Does it presuppose that legitimacy comes from consent and votes, or from something else — continuity, tradition, the idea that there's a ceremonial layer and a working layer of government — or does it need no theory of legitimacy at all, because the monarch is just doing a different job?
- What does it presuppose about where sovereignty lives? The people? Parliament? The crown-in-parliament? Does parliamentary sovereignty even make sense if the crown is formally part of parliament?
- What does it presuppose about equality? A hereditary office seems to contradict equal citizenship, but maybe not if the office is purely symbolic. Is the symbolic-versus-real distinction doing genuine philosophical work, or is it a fudge?
- The powers-that-exist-but-are-never-used thing — what does that presuppose? Is the dormant power load-bearing, a reserve for constitutional crisis, a "we could if we really had to," or is it vestigial and the convention is the only thing doing any work? What theory of constitutional conventions would that require?
- The above-the-law-but-also-under-the-law bit — is that coherent, or does it only work because sovereign immunity is really a doctrine about the state rather than about the person?

Then tell me which of these commitments are strictly required versus which the practice merely fits comfortably with. My suspicion is that a lot of it is underdetermined and that several different political theories would all happily license the exact same institution. And if you swap the presupposed view out for a rival one — say a strictly consent-based view, or a strictly egalitarian one — what concretely would have to change about how the institution actually runs?

One more thing. The friend I argued with insists the whole thing is just a harmless costume that presupposes nothing at all. If that's wrong, push back on it.

Write it in American English please.
