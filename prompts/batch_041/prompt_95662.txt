Question 2 (25 pts). Under US law, death may be determined on either of two alternative bases: irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem. A determination must be made in accordance with accepted medical standards. In controlled donation after circulatory death (DCD), a patient with a devastating brain injury who does not meet the neurological criterion has life-sustaining treatment withdrawn; once the heart stops, the team observes a short waiting period and then recovers the organs. Organ recovery is governed by the dead donor rule — organs may be taken only from a donor who is already dead. (a) Explain how the circulatory criterion is applied in DCD, and say whether the donor is dead at the moment the organs are recovered. (b) Some authors distinguish permanent from irreversible cessation of circulation. Explain the distinction and say how it bears on whether the donor is dead at recovery. (c) Does satisfying the whole-brain criterion entail that the human organism has died, or only that the person has? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:

Part (a). The law recognizes two ways of being dead, but the neurological criterion does most of the work, and the circulatory criterion is really the fallback for cases where brain-based testing can't be carried out or where the family has already decided to withdraw treatment. That is the situation in controlled DCD: the patient has a catastrophic brain injury but doesn't satisfy the neurological criterion, so the circulatory criterion has to be used instead. Treatment is withdrawn, the heart stops, and the team waits a few minutes before recovering the organs. Is the donor dead when the organs come out? Yes. Once the waiting period has passed with no return of circulation, the loss of circulation is irreversible — no one is going to restart the heart, and the protocol forbids any attempt at resuscitation. So the donor satisfies the circulatory criterion and the dead donor rule is respected.

Part (b). The distinction is between permanence and irreversibility. Permanent means the function won't come back; irreversible means it can't come back. In DCD the two come to the same thing, because the decision not to resuscitate is what settles the question: after the waiting period the cessation is permanent, and permanence just is irreversibility in a context where nobody is going to intervene. This matters for the dead donor rule because if the standard were the stronger can't-be-reversed notion, DCD would be much harder to defend — a heart that has stopped for a couple of minutes can in principle be restarted — so the permanence reading is what makes DCD come out legitimate. Either way, since the cessation is irreversible in the relevant sense, the donor is dead before the organs are taken.

Part (c). The whole-brain criterion is the correct one, and the President's Commission, whose report the UDDA is based on, adopted the higher-brain criterion: what matters is the loss of consciousness and of the capacity for experience, which is why brain death counts as the death of the person. The law itself is more demanding, requiring the entire brain including the brain stem, and I think that is the right standard, because the brain is what integrates the body into a single functioning whole. Once the brain stem has irreversibly stopped working, that integrative unity is gone and the organism has died, even though the heart may still be beating with mechanical support and the body may be warm, may heal wounds, or may even gestate a fetus. A reader might think the beating heart shows the patient is alive, but the heart is only a pump; what made the body one organism rather than a collection of organs was the brain, and the brain is gone. So satisfying the whole-brain criterion entails the death of the organism, not merely of the person.
