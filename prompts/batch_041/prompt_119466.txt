Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on those self-amending rule sets — the kind where the rules let you change the rules, including the rule about how you change rules, so in principle nothing is off the table. Fun toy. But then I started noticing that basically every real outfit that can amend its own rules has a spot where it stops. My housing co-op's bylaws can be amended by a two-thirds vote at a meeting, except for the clause about what the co-op is for, which needs every single member's consent. The student assembly I was on could suspend its standing orders by a two-thirds vote, but not the quorum rule and not the rule about who counts as a voting member. And you get the same shape at the level of whole countries: a constitution that spells out exactly how to amend it, plus one or two articles that say, in effect, that article can never be amended at all, not even by the procedure the constitution itself provides.

Whats bugging me is that these look like two different attitudes and I cant tell if they're really different or the same thing dressed up differently. On one hand: rules are ours, we made them, we can remake them, and there's no rule we couldn't in principle revisit. On the other: there's always some rule that gets treated as not up for revision by the very procedure it authorizes, and when someone tries it anyway the answer is usually that you can't do that, rather than well, you did it, so now the rule reads differently. (People do sometimes just do it. The interesting part is that it's seen as a break or a coup, not as a valid amendment.)

So what does this practice actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that a rule's authority always has to be borrowed from some rule above it, in which case the never-amend clause is either the top of a finite hierarchy or the place where the regress finally runs out? Or does it presuppose that at bottom authority is just whatever enough people currently go along with, in which case the never-amend clause isn't really a rule at all but a prediction about what would happen if you tried? Are those two theories genuinely different, or the same theory seen from opposite ends? And when someone says you can't amend that clause, is that ever a claim about what's valid, or is it always just a fact about what would get enforced?

Then tell me what the practice would have to look like if we took the pure self-amendment idea seriously — if the amendment procedure itself were amendable at every level, with no fixed point anywhere. Is that even coherent, or does it just relocate the fixed point into the people doing the accepting?

I'd rather you definately commit. Pick the reading you think the practice actually presupposes, say which bits of the practice strictly require that reading versus which bits merely sit comfortably with it, and then tell me what I should think about those never-amend clauses: genuine rules, or bluffing?
