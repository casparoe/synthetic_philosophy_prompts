I am leading a ninety-minute seminar for a mixed group of graduate students in philosophy and law on the validity of consent. They will have read nothing in advance, and I do not want to assign reading. I want to teach them a distinction that I believe the standard three-condition framework obscures: the distinction between consent as a psychological state—willingness, assent, the absence of felt pressure—and consent as a normative transaction that transfers a permission. On my working view, competence, non-deception, and non-coercion are conditions on the transaction, but they are neither individually nor jointly sufficient for validity, because a transaction can satisfy all three and still fail what I call the uptake condition: the consent must be addressed to a party entitled to receive it, for a token act, under a description the consenter can recognize as the act that will occur. I want the session to bring them to see why that fourth condition is needed and why it is not merely a repackaging of the other three. Plan the explanation for me. What has to come first, second, and third? Which single case should carry the whole session, and what must that case contain and leave out so that it isolates the uptake condition rather than the familiar failures? What misconceptions will they bring—for example, that coercion is only threats, that deception is only false statements, that competence is a fixed threshold, that consent is merely a mental state—and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head off each? What should I deliberately not try to settle in ninety minutes, and how should I say so? Finally, give me one question I can put to them at the end that would show whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely learned my vocabulary.
