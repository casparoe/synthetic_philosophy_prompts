I've spent the last few weeks arguing about this with a friend in my epistemology seminar, and I've realized my verdicts about evidence concerning my own reliability don't add up to anything I can state. Here's where my gut actually lands, case by case, and I'd like you to tell me what principle — if any — captures the pattern, and to be blunt about which verdicts it forces me to abandon. (1) A colleague and I, both competent and both working from the same proof script, disagree about whether the proof has a gap. I think I should move a long way toward the middle; I shouldn't stay put just because I did the work myself. (2) Same disagreement, except I've just learned that my colleague is a first-year student who has been wrong about proofs like this before. I still move, but only a little. (3) I finish a long calculation and feel certain of the answer; then I learn I was dosed with a drug that makes people botch about half of the calculations of this kind, essentially at chance. I think I should simply suspend judgment — stop believing the answer, but not start believing its opposite. (4) A variant I find harder: the drug is one that makes people answer anti-reliably on this task, so that those who take it get such questions wrong about three times in four. I work the calculation anyway and still come out convinced that P. I think I should now believe not-P, at something like three-to-one. (5) I take a pill known to inflate people's confidence without changing how often they're right. I do the calculation, feel certain again, and I think my confidence should simply drop back to where it stood before I started — the feeling of certainty tells me nothing either way. (6) My colleague disagrees with me and also tells me that disagreement among experts is not evidence about anything. I think I should lower my confidence that the conciliatory view is correct — a competent person who has considered the question rejects it — and yet I still think I should conciliate with them about the original question. I can't get that pair to sit still, and I want to know whether it's genuinely incoherent or merely sounds bad. (7) I learn that a drug I took makes people over-conciliate, caving to a disagreeing peer even when they shouldn't. Now a peer disagrees with me and I feel the pull to split the difference. I think I should resist the pull and stay close to my original view. (8) I learn that my disagreeing colleague is on a drug that makes people contrarian, so that they will object to any proof you show them. Then I think their disagreement should carry almost no weight at all.

The tentative principle I keep reaching for is roughly this: higher-order evidence about how a belief was formed can only tell you how much to trust the first-order evidence, never which way it points — it can shrink your confidence or leave it alone, but it cannot reverse the direction of the first-order evidence or manufacture new evidence out of nothing. That handles 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7 comfortably, and it makes short work of 8. But it appears to forbid my verdict in 4, where the drug's anti-reliability seems to turn my conclusion into evidence for its opposite, and I doubt it can even state what is happening in 6, where the higher-order evidence is about my own principles rather than about my first-order reasoning.

What I want from you is the principle, or the two or three best candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied to cases I haven't described — say, someone who learns they are reliably better at a task than they had thought, or someone who learns their past record on a topic is worse than chance, or someone who learns that a pill made them more confident and more accurate at once. For each candidate, tell me which of my verdicts it captures, which single verdict it forces me to give up, and what it predicts for the further cases. I would rather have a clean principle that costs me one verdict than a patchwork that saves all eight. I'm not looking for a survey of who has said what — I want your own reasoned answer, in American English.
