I run a second-year elective called 'Games, Proof and Paradox' for philosophy students who've mostly never touched any real maths — usually about 18 of them, one 75-minute session, a whiteboard, a projector, and a cupboard full of counters and grid paper, no computers for the students. I want to hang the whole session on the one result I still find genuinely spooky, the strategy-stealing argument: in a symmetric game, where both players have the same options and where having an extra move sitting on the board can never do you harm, the second player cannot have a winning strategy. The reason being, if they did have one, then the first player could make some throwaway opening move and afterwards just start playing the second player's winning strategy as if they were the second player — one spare piece parked harmlessly somewhere — which would mean both players were guaranteed to win, which is absurd. The freaky bit is that this hands you the conclusion (the first player wins) while telling you absolutely nothing about how to win; it's an existance proof you can never cash out into an actual strategy.

I want the students to feel that, and to feel the two hinges it swings on — the symmetry, and the 'an extra move never hurts' clause — by watching the whole thing come apart when one of them is pulled out. So design me the session. I'd like it to open with them actually playing something (a board game, a paper-and-pencil thing, whatever is cheap and replicable), build to a version where they can satisfy themselves that the second player can't win even though none of them can actually find the first player's winning line, and then land on a variant where the argument fails and they can see exactly why — ideally one where the extra move actively hurts you (chess players call that zugzwang), so the no-zugzwang assumption is doing visible work rather than being a technicality they nod past. I definately want them to reach all of this themselves, not hear it from me first.

So give me: the rules and winning conditions for each stage, a rough timeline across the 75 minutes, the exact prompts or questions I should put to them at each step (I want to keep my own mouth shut as long as possible), and a debrief that ties what happened back to the argument — including why a non-constructive proof feels different from just finding the strategy by hand. Then be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce: I don't believe a 75-minute classroom can convey the real weirdness of knowing some enormous game is a first-player win when the winning strategy would be astronomically long to write down, so tell me what I'll have to just assert and hope they take on trust. And warn me what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it, because these students always do the one thing I didn't plan for.
