Im not a lawyer or a philosopher, I just play a lot of games and read wikipedia too late at night. There's this one, I'm pretty sure its called Nomic, where the rules are the whole game. You start with a rulebook that says how to change the rulebook, and the rule saying you can change rules is itself a rule you can change. So within a few turns people are voting to change the rule about how voting works, and then arguing about whether the vote they just took was legal under the old rule or the new one, and you can end up with two rulebooks that each say the other one is fake, depending on which rule you happen to read first. Its a mess, thats the point of it.

Then I found out real constitutions are the same setup and somehow dont fall apart. They say how to amend themselves, and that amending rule can be amended. Some of them nail certain bits down so you can never amend them, no matter how big your majority. And in a few countries the courts have thrown out an amendment that was passed by exactly the procedure the constitution lays down, on the grounds that it wrecked the basic structure of the thing, whatever that is supposed to mean.

Heres what I want to know. What does a setup like this have to believe about itself? If the only test of a rule is whether it was made the way the rules say, then a court throwing out a by-the-book amendment is just making things up, and the nailed-down bits are only unchangeable by accident. But if the test is more than procedure, where does the extra bit come from, who decides what the basic structure is, could they change their mind, and by what rule? Is the whole thing pretending it has a core that never moves when really the core moves all the time? And is it pretending the constitution is still the same object after every clause has been rewritten?

Tell me what view of rules, of authority, and of staying-the-same-thing-over-time this practice only makes sense under. Then tell me which parts are actually forced by the practice and which are just one option out of several — could you keep the exact same practice but tell a completely different story about where the rules get their force, and get identical results? Where does the practice contradict itself? And if we dropped the fixed core and said a rule is real just when it was made by the current procedure, what would actually change in how courts and rulebooks work — would anything practical break, or would it just turn into the Nomic mess?

Last line: start it with Verdict: and say in one sentence which commitment the practice presupposes, or say it presupposes none.
