I'm in a grad seminar on population ethics and we burned a whole session on the spectrum cases, and I've come out the other side with a half-baked view I'd like you to poke holes in. The case, roughly: start with a life of 80 years at a very high level of well-being. Now consider a life of 100 years where each year is a hair less good. Then keep trading a bit of quality for more length, step by step, until you reach a life thousands of years long where each year is only barely worth living. Gut reaction: every single step is an improvement over the one before it, since you're buying a lot of extra life for a negligible loss of quality -- but the endpoint is obviously worse than where you started. My friends split on it. One says fine, 'better than' just isn't transitive and we should stop pretending; the other says that's absurd, so some step must secretly be a worsening and we're obliged to find it. I'd like to keep all the step verdicts and keep transitivity, and I think you can do both, as long as you're willing to give up totality instead.

Here's the view, as notes rather than anything polished, so don't grade the prose. I don't think a life gets placed on one scale. I think it gets compared along a few dimensions -- total welfare, how good the best years are, how much of what the person actually cared about got finished, maybe narrative coherence -- and that 'A is better than B' means A wins on a weighted majority of those dimensions. Majority rules cycle (the three-voter trick), so the naive version already hands you intransitivity, which I suspect is the real engine of the paradox. So I bolt on a stability clause: A counts as better than B only if A wins a majority of the dimensions and wins it by a margin that survives small arbitrary perturbations of the weights; if the comparison flips under a tiny reweighting, it's a near-tie and I call the pair incomparable. The payoff I want is this. Every adjacent step in the spectrum is a clear, robust majority, since you're trading a sliver of quality for a big chunk of length, so all my step verdicts survive. But the start and the endpoint are a near-tie on at least one dimension, so they come out incomparable rather than 'start is better'. Then transitivity is saved, because it only ever ranges over comparable pairs, and the only verdict I have to surrender is the one that the endpoint is worse than the start -- which I'm happy to give up, since it was doing all the work anyway.

What I want is comments, and I'd rather have them pointed than polite. Three things especially. (1) Accuracy: is the stability clause even well-defined once you say 'small perturbations of the weights', or is it a fudge that just relabels whatever verdict I want as incomparable? And does it actually avoid cycles, or can I still get A>B, B>C, C>A with every step a robust majority -- if so, give me the example, because I couldn't construct one and I'm not sure I can't. (2) Interest: is this a real position or does it just collapse into something people already say, e.g. that 'better than' is a partial order and spectrum cases are gaps rather than paradoxes? (3) The bit I'm most attached to and most suspicious of: the claim that the spectrum cases are really exploiting incompleteness rather than intransitivity. If that's wrong, I want to know which of my verdicts it costs me. Also, please give me one further case I haven't mentioned that would separate my view from the 'find the hidden bad step' view, since right now they agree on everything I've checked.
