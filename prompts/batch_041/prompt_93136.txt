I host a fortnightly podcast on the historiography of the First World War, and next week's episode is an interview with a historian who has spent her career defending a broadly Fischerite reading of 1914 — the claim, associated above all with Fritz Fischer's work of the early 1960s, that responsibility for the outbreak of the war rested primarily with the German leadership, rather than with the alliance system, the naval and colonial rivalry, or a general European slide into catastrophe that no one power controlled. She is a formidable interviewee who has been asked the obvious things for thirty years, and she can dispose of a lazy question in one sentence; an hour of her merely restating the thesis, or fielding a general objection about the blank cheque to Austria-Hungary, would be a wasted episode. I would therefore like you to act as an expert in early twentieth-century European diplomatic history and in its historiography, and to begin by reasoning carefully — for your own working purposes, in a short preliminary list I can see — about where a thesis of this kind is genuinely most exposed: the conceptual difficulty of attributing responsibility to a collectivity at all, the gap between responsibility for the outbreak of a war and responsibility for the aims pursued once it had begun, and the counterfactual and structural objections that other powers' decisions (Russian mobilisation, French commitments, British ambiguity) also demand explanation.

Then draft the interview questions, eight to ten of them, with at least one each on the following: what primary responsibility for a war could even mean when the bearer is a nation — whether the relevant agent is a handful of decision-makers, the state apparatus, or a wider public, and how one keeps that apart from the legal liability asserted in the reparations section of the Versailles treaty, whose opening article fixed German responsibility for the loss and damage caused by the war and which the Germans immediately read as a verdict on their moral guilt; the September 1914 memorandum drafted in Bethmann Hollweg's office by Kurt Riezler, setting out annexationist aims in the west and east, which Fischer treated as evidence of expansionist intent — I want her pressed on whether a document drawn up after the war had begun, and treated by most later historians as a discussion paper rather than settled policy, can carry the evidential weight the thesis puts on it; the question of whether the thesis needs a counterfactual claim about what would have happened had Germany restrained Austria, and if so what kind; and the political afterlife of the argument, including whether the fact that it landed with such force in a West German debate still overshadowed by the war-guilt controversy should count for or against its truth.

For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and a one-line note on what her reply would have to concede in order to stay credible. Please order the sequence so that the pressure builds — early questions should establish what she is committed to, later ones should make it expensive to retreat — and mark clearly the two or three points at which the standard reply is weakest, so that I know where to linger rather than move on. Finish with a closing question about what evidence would count against her position, and a brief list of what she will almost certainly say in response to each line of attack, so that I am not caught flat-footed. No biographical or introductory questions, and nothing that asks her to recommend reading: I want questions whose answers only she can give.
