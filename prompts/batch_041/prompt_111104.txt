I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, and I now facilitate a monthly seminar for about twelve retired scientists and engineers at a lifelong-learning institute. Most of them are comfortable with probability, game theory, and causal reasoning, but they have no formal training in philosophy. Our next session is ninety minutes, and I want to use it to make them feel the peculiar force of the thought experiment known as Roko's basilisk: the idea that a future superintelligence might punish people who knew about it and did not help bring it about, even though their present choices cannot causally affect whether it exists or what it does. I do not want a lecture on the thought experiment, and I do not want a debate about whether it is silly. I want an activity they run themselves, in which the logic of the threat becomes vivid before anyone names it. They should come away seeing why the thought experiment can feel like it has a grip even when they reject its conclusion.

Please design that activity for me. It should be a runnable game or structured simulation for a group of twelve, with rules, materials, roles, a payoff scheme that uses only points or small privileges and no real money, and a timeline that fits ninety minutes. The core of the activity should be a decision problem in which participants can either contribute to a future project or keep their resources, and a future oracle or predictor rewards or punishes them according to a rule that is fixed in advance. Run it in at least two phases so that they feel the difference between a case where their contribution causally matters and a case where it is only evidence of what the predictor has already decided about them. The rules should be arranged so that they first act on ordinary causal reasoning and then encounter the acausal or evidential twist, or the reverse, but in either case the realization should come from what happens, not from what I explain beforehand.

The debrief should map the moments of the game onto the relevant ideas: causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, acausal trade, the role of prediction and simulation, and the psychology of blackmail and Pascal's wager-style reasoning. It should also say where the basilisk argument can be resisted, what it assumes about the predictor's credibility and about decision theory, and why many philosophers and decision theorists regard it as a trap rather than a proof. Include a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for example, participants refusing to play, someone trying to break the rules, the predictor's threat feeling arbitrary or too weak, or the group treating the whole thing as a joke—and how I should handle each case without rescuing the thought experiment by fiat.

Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. It cannot reproduce a real superintelligence, the full formal apparatus of decision theory, or the question of whether a predictor's threat is credible in the actual world. Say which parts of the idea the facilitator must supply in the debrief and which parts the game genuinely makes participants feel. Do not use real names, citations, or reading lists, and do not mention Roko's basilisk until the debrief. All materials should be fictional and usable offline.
