I belong to a small interdisciplinary reading group—mostly philosophers of mind, a couple of physicists, and one very patient hospital ethicist—that has been circling the question of whether fundamental physics can have moral status. The immediate occasion was a proposal by one of the physicists to run a tabletop experiment that would create a highly entangled many-body state and then destroy it by rapid decoherence; he insists the experiment is ethically inert because it involves only a few thousand atoms. A philosopher in the group keeps replying, “Everything is conscious,” and treating that as sufficient to put the experiment under a precautionary moratorium: if everything is conscious, then the state we create and destroy is a subject, and we need to ask what it might suffer. I am not convinced the slogan does the work he wants, but I also do not think it is empty. What I need is a menu, not a single interpretation: lay out the distinct precise claims “everything is conscious” could be making in this context, from the nearly trivial to the metaphysically radical, and say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something probably false.

In particular, distinguish (a) the claim that every physical entity has some experiential aspect or intrinsic quality from (b) the claim that every physical entity is a unified subject of experience, (c) the claim that every physical entity is sentient in the sense of being capable of pleasure or suffering, (d) the claim that every physical entity is a locus of moral concern, and (e) the epistemic claim that we cannot know which physical entities are conscious. Then say which of these readings would actually support the moratorium on the experiment, and which would support only a much weaker conclusion—say, that we should be uncertain, or that we should avoid destroying systems that are already plausible candidates for unified experience. I am especially interested in what work the slogan can do when it is applied to a many-body quantum state: is the relevant subject the whole state, the constituent particles, some emergent pattern, or nothing at all? And if the answer depends on a theory of how micro-experiences combine into a macro-experience, say how the slogan’s different readings stand with respect to that problem. One of us has even drafted a “pan-sentience impact assessment” for the experiment, modeled on environmental impact statements, with columns for subjecthood, valence, integration, and reversibility; tell me which readings of the slogan would make such an assessment coherent rather than a category mistake. Finally, tell me which reading the philosopher in my group most likely has in mind, and whether that reading is one a physicist could reasonably accept without abandoning physics. No literature review or reading list is needed—I want the conceptual anatomy, with verdicts.
