I sit on the internal review panel at our university's AI institute — we award exactly one "blue sky" seed grant a year, and I'm the token philosopher on the panel, which mostly means I get asked to write the decision memo that the engineers skim. This year both shortlisted proposals cite Turing's idea of the child machine, and the panel is genuinely split, so I need a written opinion I can circulate before Friday's vote that takes a clear side rather than splitting the difference. Proposal A wants to build a small learning system from scratch — no pretrained weights, a random-ish starting point — and then "raise" it through a human teacher who supplies rewards and penalties over a long curriculum, on the grounds that this is the only honest way to test whether an education can produce general competence; they keep saying that starting from a pretrained model is like testing a graduate student and calling it a test of schooling. Proposal B wants to start from an existing large model and study whether a curriculum of human feedback makes it generalize better, and its lead argues that the child-machine picture was always a claim about the plasticity of the substrate and not a demand for literal infancy, so A is romanticism that will burn three years and produce nothing publishable. One of my colleagues, who's read the 1950 paper more recently than I have, insists that Turing thought a machine's education could be made far faster than a child's and even imagined the teacher being a machine itself, which he says detonates A's "must start unorganized" premise; another colleague says that if you start from a pretrained model you've smuggled in most of the education you're supposedly studying, so B can't answer the question either.

Draft the memo. I need a concrete recommendation — fund A, fund B, or fund both at reduced scope — plus the conditions we should attach (a checkpoint, a budget ceiling, and some operational definition of "education" the team has to commit to up front, so we can tell later whether it happened), and a date by which we'd expect a go/no-go readout. The philosophical part, and this is where I'd like you to do real work rather than gesture at Turing: say what the education analogy actually commits you to, and whether "starting unorganized" is part of the view or just a picture that came with it; then walk through the disanalogies that matter here — a machine isn't raised in a family or a culture, it can be copied, paused, reset, and taught in parallel, and it may not have anything like developmental stages — and say which of those undercut which proposal. Give the strongest version of the case for the side you end up recommending against, and then explain why it still loses. Keep it tight enough that people will actually read it before the meeting.
