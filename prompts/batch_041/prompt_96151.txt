I'm a backend engineer with no training in philosophy, and for the past few months I've been sitting in on a reading group about economic history run by two economists and a historian. After one session I got into an argument over lunch: I claimed that the question at the center of that literature — why did sustained industrial growth begin in Britain rather than China, what everyone calls the Great Divergence — is not a properly posed question, and my colleague said I was overthinking something that working historians answer perfectly well in practice. I've written up a short draft to settle the argument, and I'd like comments on it before I post it in the group's forum.

The draft is called "Why Britain and Not China? A Doubt About the Question." It goes like this. I work on distributed systems, and most of my job is postmortems. When something falls over, the first thing you learn not to do is write the story backwards from the outcome: you already know which node died, so every earlier oddity in that node's logs looks like a cause, and the nine nodes that behaved identically and survived quietly drop out of the analysis. "Why did this one fail and not the other nine?" is a good question, but only once you've said what the other nine have in common with it and why they belong in the comparison at all. If they weren't comparable, the question has no answer; if they were comparable, then the interesting fact is that identical setups behaved differently, which is a different question than the one you asked. I think the historians' question has the same shape, and that as usually posed it is under-specified in a way that guarantees the disagreement never resolves.

My claim has three parts. First, the question fuses two questions that can have different answers: why growth began in Britain, and why it did not begin in China first. Evidence that Britain had coal and expensive labor is not by itself evidence about what was absent in China, and the literature slides between the two without flagging the move. Second, the "and not" clause presupposes a contrast class, and that class is doing all the work. China is a natural foil only if the class is "the other large, commercially sophisticated agrarian empire." If the class is "every other region on earth," the question becomes why industrial take-off happened anywhere at all — a different and possibly more tractable question, and one that also changes the burden of proof, since with a large enough class and a low enough base rate for take-off, the fact that China didn't inflect first is not surprising and calls for no special explanation. Third, the units are aggregates: neither Britain nor China was a single actor with a single interest, and the mechanisms people cite — coal, wages, property rights, integrated grain markets — operate at the scale of a region, the Yangzi delta or the English north-west, so the whole comparison may be pitched at the wrong level of aggregation. What I want to say is not that the question is meaningless, but that it is a compressed way of gesturing at a family of questions, and that much of the disagreement is really disagreement about which member of the family is being asked. The fix is boring and procedural: state the contrast class, state what exactly is being explained (a level, a rate, an inflection rather than an event), and say which of the two fused questions is at issue.

What I want from you is a critique, not a rewrite. Is the central claim right — is the question genuinely under-specified, or am I making a fuss about something that is fine as normally asked because the foil is obviously doing the work of a controlled comparison? Does the postmortem analogy hold, or does it break somewhere that matters, given that historical cases aren't identical setups and that the counterfactual "China industrializes first" may not even be well-defined — which might cut against my argument rather than for it? And am I attacking a position nobody actually holds? If the serious work in this area already states its contrast class and its explanandum explicitly, I want to be told that flatly rather than have my draft patched up. Give me the strongest objection to the thesis rather than a list of improvements, and if commenting on this draft would mean inventing the positions I'm arguing against, or if the whole framing is confused, say so plainly instead of going along with me.
