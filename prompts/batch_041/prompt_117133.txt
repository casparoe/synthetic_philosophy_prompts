Constitutional monarchy sounds like a contradiction until you see how neatly it is put together. The trick, as every schoolchild used to learn, is that the monarch reigns but does not rule. In Britain the Crown still formally holds enormous powers — summoning and dissolving Parliament, appointing the prime minister, assenting to legislation, commanding the armed forces — but each of these is exercised on the advice of elected ministers, so the monarch's personal role has shrunk to ceremony: opening Parliament, reading a speech someone else wrote, and cutting ribbons. Japan's postwar constitution does the same work more openly, making the Emperor a symbol of the state and of the unity of the people and requiring the advice and approval of the Cabinet for his acts in matters of state. The Scandinavian monarchies have gone further down the same road: Sweden's king no longer has any formal role in forming a government, and today he is chiefly a master of ceremonies. The upshot is that the monarch has no discretion left. The rules that keep her in line are not laws at all but customs, and customs are only as strong as the general willingness to keep them; if a British monarch announced tomorrow that she would not sign a bill, nothing would happen to her, because no court would touch the question. What the arrangement buys a democracy is a head of state who is above politics: a symbol of continuity, a focus for patriotic feeling, and an ornament that costs a great deal and decides nothing. It is a decorative residue of an older constitution, kept because it is familiar, and it could be abolished tomorrow without anything else having to change.
