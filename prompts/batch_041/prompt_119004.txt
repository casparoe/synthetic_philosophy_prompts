Two months ago a colleague showed me a trick involving my own preferences, and I have not been able to put it down since. I keep three kinds of coffee in the office — dark, medium, and light — and it turns out I will pay two dollars to swap a bag of any one for a bag of the next in a cycle: dark for medium, medium for light, light for dark. I have now run that cycle four times, ending where I started and eight dollars poorer. What unsettles me is that I cannot tell whether my problem lies in what I want or in how I choose across time, because a second fact about me is also true: for two of these coffees my preference reverses depending on whether the swap would happen today or next week, and at each moment the trade looks worth taking. Please write a short story, set in an office much like mine, that keeps these two failures distinguishable rather than folding them into one vague lesson about weakness of will. Give me two colleagues who each offer me a way out — one who insists I bind myself in advance, before my preferences can shift, and one who insists I simply honor the plan I made while thinking clearly and ignore the later pull — and let the story show where each escape genuinely works and where it quietly fails. Do not name any philosophical literature in the story; I want a reader to have to see the mechanism for themselves, and I want to reach the last line still unsure which of my two defects is the one that actually cost me the money.
