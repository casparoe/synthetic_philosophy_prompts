The argument from divine hiddenness, as it is usually run, locates the failure at the level of belief. If a perfectly loving God existed, the thought goes, then no one who was willing to be in relationship with God, and who put up no resistance to it, would be left unable to believe; such people exist; so no perfectly loving God exists. I want to set a real case beside the argument — the interior life of Mother Teresa of Calcutta. Her letters to her spiritual directors, collected and published after her death, record that for decades, from roughly the years in which she began her work in Calcutta until near the end of her life, she could not feel the presence of God, described her soul as a darkness, and at times doubted, while she went on praying, working, and writing as though nothing had changed. Take that as the episode: not the legend and not the social work, but the letters and what they document. Do not psychologize her; the question is what the documents are evidence for, not what was the matter with her.

Notice first how badly the case fits the premise the argument needs. She was not a non-believer. She professed belief throughout and acted on it; what she reported missing was the felt presence of God. If the argument requires non-resistant non-belief as its witness, this case is no witness at all, and it is worth saying plainly that the standard version of the argument has nothing to do with a person like her.

What I want you to consider is whether the argument should be rebuilt around what the letters actually describe. Distinguish a failure at the level of belief — the evidence leaves belief unavailable — from a failure at the level of experience: belief is held, or at least not abandoned, but the relational good that theism promises is never delivered, and the person who wants it most is given no experience of it. Suppose the argument is run on the second phenomenon instead: if a perfectly loving God existed, and if the good at stake is a relationship rather than an assent, and if a relationship cannot be had without the possibility of felt communication, then a non-resistant person who seeks that communication should not be left entirely without it; she sought it more constantly than almost anyone; therefore either no such God or no such good. Then do the work. Does the rebuilt argument go through, or does its second premise collapse? A relationship's value may depend on the possibility of its felt absence, and the tradition of the dark night that John of the Cross described treats precisely this darkness as a stage of growth rather than a withdrawal. Say whether that traditional reading saves theism by explaining the case, or merely redescribes it, and what would distinguish the two readings.

Then bring the case back to the theory. Which of the argument's premises does the episode support, which does it leave untouched, and where is the argument simply silent — about suffering that is not doubt, about the difference between wanting God and wanting to feel God, about whether the loss of felt presence is a harm at all? Finish by saying what the episode shows about the argument: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the argument never anticipated. And if the argument has to be reframed around relationship rather than belief to accommodate her, say whether the reframing strengthens it or dissolves it.

Write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter: no headings, no numbered steps, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
