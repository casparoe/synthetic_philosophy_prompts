I write fiction, and I've backed into a philosophy problem sideways. When you read a novel the whole thing lands as one continuous now — the reader feels like theres a single moment where the story is happening to them, and a single spot (the page, their head, wherever) where it all comes together. I've leaned on that intuition alot as a craft tool. Lately I've been reading about the multiple drafts picture of consciousness and the case against what gets called the Cartesian theater, and I'm starting to suspect my craft intuition is exactly the thing that's supposed to be wrong. But I can't tell how deep the disagreement goes, or whether it's a real disagreement about the mind or a fight about metaphors.

Explain the issue to me, in the weeds please, not the summary version. Specifically:

1. What the Cartesian theater is as an assumption about consciousness — the "where" question and the "when" question kept separate, because I think I keep conflating them.

2. The Orwellian vs Stalinesque fork: either the revision happens after the experience and the memory of it gets rewritten, or the brain stages the edited version so it's already in place before anything becomes an experience. Why is the dispute between those two horns supposed to be undecidable from the outside, and what is that undecidability supposed to show about the timing of experience? I want the actual argument, not the slogan.

3. Why the multiple drafts model is supposed to follow from all that. What exactly is a draft, what is it a draft of, and is there a final draft? And does the model say consciousness is an illusion, or only that our picture of a single finish line is?

4. The objection I keep landing on: if two people are wired so that the same revision happens at different points, and they behave and report identically, does it really follow that there's no fact of the matter about what they experienced — or only that we can't find out? And if it does follow, is that a consequence of the model, or an extra assumption about what experience is, smuggled in at the start?

5. Whether the model has any teeth beyond redescription. Is there anything it predicts differently from a view that keeps a single finishing line, or is that the whole point — that in the interesting cases the two views can't be told apart? If it's the latter, tell me why it's still more than a verbal trick.

Then a thought experiment I've been kicking around — tell me if it's any good. In film you can put the same blank face on an actor and it reads as one emotion or another depending on what you cut to next; nobody thinks there's a fact about what the face "was" before the cut, because the face wasn't doing anything yet, the sequence was. I want to know whether the multiple drafts model is saying the same thing about a moment of experience and the later moment that supposedly revises it. My worry is that the analogy breaks, because the viewer's reaction is a fact about the viewer, not about the shot — the film has no inner life to be wrong about, and a person does. Does that disanalogy do any damage, or is it just the obvious thing the model already concedes?

No citations and no reading list — I'm not going to look anything up, so argue it through instead. And don't be polite about my craft intuition; if it's dead, say so.

Last line of your answer should start with "Bottom line:" and be a single sentence of no more than 25 words.
