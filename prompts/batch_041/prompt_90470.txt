I'm a third-year student in a consciousness studies course, and we have just finished the unit on the timing of experience. For the final essay I want to read Christopher Nolan's Memento through the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction that Dennett extracts from the color phi phenomenon, and I would like your help constructing the reading. Assume I know the film and the phenomenon; I need you to do the philosophy.

Let me state the pieces as I have them, so you can correct me where I have them wrong before you build on them. The color phi phenomenon: two spots are flashed in different positions a short interval apart, and subjects report seeing one spot move from the first position to the second and change color somewhere in between, even though the second spot's color cannot have been specified until it appeared. The apparent motion seems to carry information about the second flash before the second flash has occurred. That is the puzzle that forces a choice about when the content of an experience is fixed. On what Dennett calls the Orwellian story, a genuine experience occurs at the time and is then revised retrospectively, the way the Ministry of Truth rewrites the historical record; on the Stalinesque story, no such experience ever occurs, because the perceptual system delays and constructs a single unified experience with the revision already folded in, the way a show trial is fabricated in advance so as to look like a genuine proceeding. Dennett's own position, as I understand it, is that the two stories are empirically indistinguishable from any third-person vantage point and that the question of when the content is fixed is therefore ill-posed, which is what motivates his multiple drafts picture.

Now the film. Leonard Shelby cannot form new long-term memories after the assault in which his wife was attacked; he pursues her attacker using tattoos, Polaroids, and annotated notes, and the film runs its color scenes in reverse chronological order while interleaving a forward-moving black-and-white strand that turns out to be Leonard recounting the story of Sammy Jankis, another man with a memory condition whom Leonard once investigated for an insurance claim. By the end it is clear that Leonard has been manipulating his own record and may have transferred his own history onto Sammy, and that he deliberately engineers his next target by writing himself a false instruction.

Here is the reading I want to pursue, and I want you to tell me whether it is any good. The film appears to stage the Orwellian/Stalinesque choice at two levels at once. At the level of Leonard's condition it seems to rule out the Orwellian story, because if no new experience can be laid down, there is nothing to revise retrospectively; yet the external record of tattoos and notes is precisely a thing that can be rewritten after the fact, so the Orwellian structure migrates from memory into the artifacts. And the Sammy Jankis material seems to be a candidate for the Stalinesque story: a memory that may never have been lived but was fabricated in advance to be indistinguishable from a lived one. If that is right, the film does not simply illustrate Dennett's point that the two stories are indistinguishable; it distributes them across two different media and lets each do work the other cannot, which is more than the armchair argument delivers.

What I want from you:

1. A reading that is faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the film to an illustration of a paper, and do not bend the phenomenon or the distinction to fit the film. Where the film genuinely illuminates the debate, say so; where it trades on a confusion, say so. I am especially worried about one confusion: the color phi puzzle is about the micro-timing of perceptual experience over milliseconds, whereas Leonard's problem is the consolidation of memory over hours and years. If the analogy is merely verbal, tell me, and then tell me whether any scene repairs it or whether the film is better read as being about the epistemology of records rather than about the timing of experience at all.

2. A verdict on which story the film, on balance, dramatizes. Does it side with the Orwellian reading, the Stalinesque reading, with Dennett's dissolution of the choice, or with none of them? Point to the specific scenes that carry the weight, and say what each scene would have to show for the competing readings to be right.

3. An account of what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the argument alone does not. If the answer is nothing, say that; I would rather have the honest negative than a manufactured moral.

4. A short section on where the reading breaks down and what an opponent of it would say. I would like to be able to represent that position fairly in the essay rather than strawman it.

A few constraints. Do not quote the film's dialogue verbatim, since I cannot check it and neither can you; paraphrase. Do not give me a bibliography or citations, and do not lean on the exact wording of Dennett's text. If you need to attribute the distinction, attribute it in general terms. I am more interested in the shape of the argument than in who said what when.
