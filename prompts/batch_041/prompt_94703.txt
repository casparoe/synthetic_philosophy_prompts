I host a small podcast on epistemology — mostly grad students and a couple of profs listen in, so I can afford to be a bit in the weeds — and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends pragmatic encroachment, specifically the version where it's not the word 'knows' that shifts with stakes the way the contextualists say, but knowledge itself. We had a contextualist on back in the spring and I really don't want to rerun that episode with the labels swapped, so the questions need to actually bite into the content of his position rather than have him restate it for forty minutes. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, plus a follow-up for each, keyed to the answer he's most likely to give?

Here's roughly what I want covered, in more or less this order. First I want him to spell out what work the practical facts are doing that the evidence alone can't do — take two people with the same evidence about whether a bridge is sound, one of them about to walk across it and the other just idly curious, and ask whether he really wants to say only one of them knows, and what the epistemic difference between them consists in if their evidence is identical. Then I want to press on why he doesn't just become a contextualist, since contextualism seems to buy the same flexibility about when we're willing to say someone knows without making knowledge itself depend on what's at stake for the subject — I'd like to know what he thinks is wrong with that, and whether he thinks the contextualist is saying something false or merely changing the subject. And please include at least one question on the action side of the view, the thought that if you know something then you're warranted in using it as a premise in practical reasoning, or that knowledge is what licenses action — because that's either where the view gets its motivation or where it gets into real trouble, and I want to hear him commit on which. After that, the objections I find hardest: that raising the stakes can make your knowledge evaporate through no change in your evidence, which seems like the wrong kind of thing for an epistemic status to do; that the view slides toward a relativism or subjectivism about knowledge; that the threshold for knowledge becomes hostage to facts about the subject's situation she may not even be aware of; and that in genuinely high-stakes cases it makes knowledge so demanding that acting on anything short of certainty is never warranted, which is the opposite of what defenders usually want out of it. Finally, one question on two people with the same evidence but very different stakes who disagree about whether the other one knows — who's right, and what follows for whether they should treat each other's testimony as credible. Oh and please don't just wheel out the usual bank case, he's answered that one a hundred times and it'll put him on autopilot.

No generic stuff — nothing like 'what drew you to this view' or 'how do you respond to critics'. Every question should be answerable only by someone who actually holds the position, and each follow-up should be the move he'll make if the question lands; I know he's fond of the distinction between knowledge and warranted belief, so press it where it's doing load-bearing work for him. If any of your questions are ones he has a well-rehearsed answer to, flag them and tell me what the answer is so I'm not sitting there nodding along to a deflection I can't see through. And since I'll be the one deciding in real time whether to drop a question: tell me which of your questions is the weakest — the one that looks like it presses but probably doesn't — and what would have to be true of his view for it to be a genuine objection. Concretely, where is this line of questioning most likely to miss the target, and what argument or consideration would make you abandon it in favor of the opposite verdict about what to ask him?
