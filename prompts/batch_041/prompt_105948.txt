For a piece I'm writing on the epistemology of testimony, I've been rereading Graham Greene's The End of the Affair against Hume's essay on miracles, and I want a reading that holds both ends. Greene arranges his supernatural evidence the way a litigator would: the founding miracle — Bendrix surviving the V-1 blast after Sarah's vow to give him up — reaches the reader only through Sarah's diary, the private testimony of an interested party, read after her death by the man it most concerns; the later cures, like the detective's child whose birthmark fades, are public but secondhand. Hume's wise man proportions belief to evidence and finds any testimony for a miracle outweighed by the uniform testimony of nature, yet Greene's subject is not quite evidence. Sarah's vow is a bargain, not an observation, and Bendrix's grudging assent to God at the end is not the conclusion of an argument but something done to him against his will. So where does the novel bear Hume out — the interested witnesses, the reports that accrete in the retelling, the cures conveniently of the Catholic kind that Hume says cancel out their rivals — and where does it dramatize a route to belief his essay has no room for, one that runs through desire, promise, and resistance rather than the weighing of testimony? I'd also like you to press a definitional point: Hume treats the miraculous as a violation of a law of nature, but a fading birthmark may be merely surprising rather than law-breaking, which threatens to move Greene's cures outside the argument altogether. Take the epistemology seriously rather than using the novel as an illustration, and end by saying whether Greene is doing philosophy of testimony here or something Hume's essay, by its own terms, cannot reach.
