I'm a retired experimental physicist — forty years of neutron scattering, mostly on magnetic oxides — and since I stopped doing real work I've been reading philosophy, badly but happily. I need an audit of a second-hand summary. My friend Peter read Hofstadter's strange-loop book and has been cornering people with it; I wrote his version down more or less as he said it:

'The careenium is a frictionless pool table covered in zillions of tiny magnetic marbles — simms — that stick together when they collide at low speed, forming big clumps he calls simmballs, which drift around and shove the loose simms about. The point is that simmballs are just as real as simms: not convenient fictions or bookkeeping devices, but the level where the actual causal action is. In principle you could compute every sim's path from the micro-laws, but that computation is useless; the real causal story lives up at the simmball level, where simmballs push simmballs, and that pushing can't be derived from, or reduced to, what the simms are doing, even in principle. So he's a strong emergentist: the simmball has a genuine downward causal influence on the simms that the micro-laws alone don't predict — which is why "it's all just particles" is a bad explanation. He applies it to the self: the "I" is a simmball, so a thought can push a neuron around but not the reverse. And the moral is that a physicist who says a simmball can't push anything, because all the forces are at the sim level, has simply changed the subject.'

That last line made me bristle, since I've spent my life assuming that once you have the micro-story you have everything there is about what pushed what. But I suspect I'm being unfair, because "nothing but" explanations really do seem to leave something out, and I can't say exactly what.

Take the role of a philosopher of mind and science who knows the reduction and emergence literature cold, and give me an audit. Start by brainstorming: before you judge anything, list the seperate claims packed into Peter's paragraph — I'd bet there are four or five, and that they don't all stand or fall together. Then go through it sentence by sentence: what's accurate, what's a standard simplification, what Hofstadter would deny. I especially want to know (a) whether the "can't be derived even in principle" bit is his view or Peter's inflation, (b) whether calling him a strong emergentist is fair, (c) whether the kicker is a real answer to my objection or just a dodge, and (d) whether the one-way version of the thought-pushes-neuron line survives.

One thing I've been trying on my own — tell me if it's a good way to put the question: since you can only ever poke the simms, never reach in and grab a simmball without moving its simms, the simmball level seems to fail any test where you intervene on it directly and watch what changes. Does that settle it, or is that test rigged against the higher level by construction? And does the whole thing depend on the simmballs being big and macroscopic, or would it go through for any old coarse-graining of the sims?

Be explicit where you're unsure how he'd come down, rather than smoothing it over. No reading list please — I'm 74 and my stack is already a metre high.
