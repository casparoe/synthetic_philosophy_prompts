ok so i'm in a 4th-year seminar on time and relativity — we did McTaggart, the block universe, and then a unit on weird solutions to the Einstein equations — and my study group is making a mock final for each other. We each write one essay question plus a model answer plus a marking scheme, then we grade each other's answers with the scheme. Mine is on Gödel and I want it to be the kind of question where someone who only knows the wikipedia version is completely stuck.

Here's what keeps bugging me. Everyone in my group can tell you that Gödel's rotating universe contains closed timelike curves, that this means you could in principle fly a rocket and come back to your own past, and that Gödel concluded time is unreal or idealistic. But when you push them on why a rotating solution is supposed to show anything about the reality of time, they say "there's no global time function, so becoming isn't objective" and then they're out of moves. It's like they've memorized the verdict and none of the argument. I want the question to force the actual argument out of them.

So: one essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20. Constraints:

- The question has to make them say which feature of the solution is doing the work — the absence of a global time function / any objective simultaneity, or the closed timelike curves themselves. Then make them consider a variant: a spacetime with closed timelike curves that nevertheless comes with a preferred foliation, i.e. a frame-independent way of slicing it into "now"s. Does Gödel's conclusion still go through in that variant? I'm pretty sure the answer is no, and I want that on the scheme, because it shows the CTCs aren't the engine of the argument — the missing global time is.
- It should also make them separate Gödel's conclusion from ordinary B-theory / tenseless realism, since that view denies an objective "now" too and nobody calls it idealism. So just denying becoming can't be enough; a top answer has to say what extra step Gödel needs to get all the way to idealism — something like: if the objectivity of time would have to be underwritten by something in the physics, and the physics contains no preferred time, then "now" has no more objective standing than "here" — and then has to notice that this step depends on a premise about how to read the underdetermination, which is exactly the premise a B-theorist rejects. I want the difference between those two positions to be worth real marks.
- Don't let them get away with "possibility isn't actuality" as a one-liner either. The question should make them say what would have to be added to the mere existence of such a solution for the argument to bite on our world.
- Level: upper-level undergrad, they've had special relativity and know what a light cone is but they're not physicists. No citations — we obviously can't search in an exam, so nothing in the question should need a reference.

For the marking scheme: five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth (total 20), and for each one a single line on how people typically lose that mark. Also say what separates an A answer from a B answer in one or two sentences.

Format: start the reply with the exam question itself, worded exactly as I'd hand it out, then the model answer, then the scheme. No preamble about how you approached it and don't restate my request back at me. And have the model answer open with its bottom line in the first sentence before it argues for it.
