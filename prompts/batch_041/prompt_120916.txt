A friend of mine in a cognitive science reading group has a catchphrase he reaches for whenever someone tries to draw a line between reasoning proper and mere association, or between literal thought and figurative thought: thinking is just analogy-making. He picked it up from a book on analogy that he keeps pressing on me (he attributes it to Hofstadter, or to Hofstadter and Sander jointly — I can never remember which), and he uses it as a kind of universal solvent. The occasion I care about: I said one analogy for a biological process was doing more explanatory work than a rival analogy, and he replied that if all thought is analogy-making, then no analogy can be privileged over any other, so the dispute was empty. I suspect the slogan is doing several quite different jobs at once, and that the job he needed done requires a reading of it that is either false or so watered down that it says nothing. I'm turning this into a term paper, so what I want from you is not a verdict but a map.

Lay out the distinct precise claims that "thinking is just analogy-making" could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the radical, and give each one a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, or apparently false — in each case saying what the slogan would have to mean for that reading to come out true. A few specific things the map should sort out. First, is the claim about a process or about a product: is it that thoughts are generated by an analogical mechanism, or that the content of a concept just is a set of analogies to exemplars, or both, and do these come apart? Second, how does it handle the cases that look least analogical — applying "triangle" to a shape I have never seen, doing long division, or solving a puzzle where I'm told that abc becomes abd and asked what iijjkk becomes? If those count as analogy too, is anything being asserted beyond the observation that similarity plays some role in everything? Third, is there a reading on which the slogan denies that any rule-governed process figures in cognition at all, and if so, does that reading make the slogan self-undermining, given that my friend's argument for it has the form of a deduction? Fourth, the slogan seems to have the property that any apparent counterexample will be redescribed by its defenders as itself an analogical achievement; say whether that makes it unfalsifiable, whether that is a defect or merely what to expect from a thesis this general, and whether it marks a difference between the trivial and the radical readings. Fifth, and most important for me, say which readings actually license my friend's conclusion that no analogy can be better than any other — I suspect none do, or that the one that comes closest equivocates between "all cognition involves analogy" and "all cognition is nothing but analogy" — and identify the strongest objection to each of those readings.

Give this to me as a numbered list I can hand to him, each entry stating the thesis in a sentence or two, then its verdict, then the objection it has to survive. Do not quote the book at me, and no citations or reading list — I'll fill those in myself. I want the conceptual space and the equivocations in it, and in particular a clear statement of which single entry he would have to commit to if his move against my comparison of the two analogies is to work.
