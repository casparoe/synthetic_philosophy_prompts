I'm writing a short paper for my school's philosophy club about an inconsistency I keep noticing in how we treat everyday conventions. Tipping is legally optional, yet a regular who leaves nothing is thought to have done something wrong; jaywalking is against the law, yet almost nobody treats a jaywalker as a wrongdoer; and a visitor to a country where nobody queues isn't blamed for not queueing, though a visitor who ignores a queue at home is. Institutions sometimes seem to take a side deliberately: some hotels and restaurants post a sign saying tips aren't expected or that a service charge is already included, and some cities have stopped ticketing jaywalkers even while the law stays on the books. I'd like the theory of obligation these practices presuppose. Is a convention like queueing binding because it is a shared practice a person has tacitly joined, because breaking it takes unfair advantage of others' restraint, or not at all — is it just etiquette? Reconstruct the view the practices assume, distinguish what they strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with, and identify which rival theories they quietly rule out. Then say what would have to change in how we instruct and judge visitors if we held that conventions bind only those who have accepted them. Please answer in one paragraph of no more than 300 words.
