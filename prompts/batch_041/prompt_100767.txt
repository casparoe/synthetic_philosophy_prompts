I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind and cognition, and next week we turn to the extended mind thesis, the claim roughly that the boundaries of a cognitive system need not coincide with the boundaries of skin and skull, and that under the right conditions a notebook, a phone, or another person can be a proper part of someone's mind rather than a mere aid to it. In my experience students absorb the abstract arguments readily enough but cannot see what is at stake until they encounter a case in which the functional and the constitutive readings of the same resource come apart. For that purpose I have been using Christopher Nolan's Memento, which I regard as one of the sharpest thought experiments about external memory that anyone has put on film, and I would like a reading of it written for the seminar.

What I want is not a plot summary or a verdict on the film's cleverness but a philosophical reading that takes both the film and the debate seriously. Leonard Shelby cannot form new memories and conducts his investigation through Polaroids, annotated notes, and tattoos, and the film eventually reveals that he has at least once deliberately written himself a false instruction and then trusted it. Treat that not as a twist to be explained away but as an objection to be weighed. In particular, I would like you to work with two distinctions I have been pressing on the students, since I suspect they are the ones doing the real work. The first is between storage externalism, in which an external resource holds information that the agent retrieves, and processing externalism, in which an external resource performs transformations the agent would otherwise have to perform internally; ask which one the notebooks and tattoos are, and whether the extended mind thesis needs the second to earn the name of cognition. The second is between couplings that are transparent, in the sense that the agent uses the resource without representing it, the way a cane is felt through rather than looked at, and couplings that are opaque, in the sense that the agent must explicitly represent and endorse the resource in order to use it. Then use both distinctions to adjudicate whether Leonard's system is a vindication of the thesis, a limiting case, or a reductio.

Along the way I want the reading to do three things. It should say which scenes carry the philosophical weight and why, the Sammy Jankis material, the tattoos, the moment Leonard writes himself a lie, and it should say where the film resists the extended mind reading, so that the honest critic has to concede that some of what Leonard does looks more like reliance on a tool than participation in a mind. It should say whether the film's own form, its reverse chronology, is doing anything philosophically relevant or merely clever, whether the viewer's memory is being externalized by the film in a way that mirrors Leonard's, and whether that is an argument or a metaphor. And it should end by saying what the film adds to the extended mind debate that the philosophical literature alone does not, in particular whether it shows that the trust on which the constitutive claim depends is more fragile than the standard examples of the notebook and the phone conceal. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; argue from the film and from the ideas.
