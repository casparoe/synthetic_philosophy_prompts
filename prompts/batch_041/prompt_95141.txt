I work in public health, on burden-of-disease estimation, so my working vocabulary is epidemiological: exposures and dose-response relations, relative risks, attributable fractions among the exposed and in the population, counterfactual baselines, threshold versus non-threshold models, herd-immunity thresholds, and the standing distinction between individual-level and population-level effects. I am fluent enough in that vocabulary to run the calculations myself. I have no philosophical training beyond an introductory ethics course, and I am not looking for an introduction; I am looking for a translation.

Please take the debate over individual contributions to collective harms — the cluster of arguments and replies surrounding the claim that one person's emissions, one person's meat consumption, or one person's vote makes no difference to any outcome, and the various attempts to answer that claim — and restate it in my vocabulary, position by position. Among the positions to be carried across: the objection itself; the reply that the act is a contribution to a harm even where it is not a difference-maker; the reply that what is wrong is participation in a harmful practice, independently of what the participation effects; the reply that appeals to thresholds or tipping points; the reply that appeals to expected value or to the probability of being pivotal; the reply that construes the duty as conditional on others' compliance or as a duty to join a collective effort; and any virtue- or practice-based reply, framed in terms of the kind of agent one becomes. Where the three cases (climate, meat, voting) behave differently under translation, say so rather than smoothing them together.

For each position, state plainly what it becomes when expressed as a claim about exposures, marginal effects, attributable fractions, and counterfactuals, and what has to be bent, fudged, or left dangling in order to get it there. Keep the translation honest: if a position appears to translate only because my vocabulary has quietly assumed a consequentialist reading of it, report that as a distortion rather than counting it as a clean translation. If a translation cannot be done without first settling a contested premise, say which premise and stop there rather than choosing on the original author's behalf.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which claims come across cleanly? Which come across only with distortion, and what precisely is distorted? Which have no counterpart at all — no item in my vocabulary that could even be the wrong answer to the same question? I have a suspicion of my own that I would like you to test rather than accommodate: my apparatus separates a share of the harm, an accounting notion defined against a counterfactual population, from a difference made to the harm, a causal notion defined against a counterfactual world. Neither seems to be what the philosophers are after when they ask whether the individual's act is wrong even though it changes nothing. Tell me whether that question is about a third thing my vocabulary lacks, or whether it is a question my vocabulary reveals to be confused.

Throughout, say how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
