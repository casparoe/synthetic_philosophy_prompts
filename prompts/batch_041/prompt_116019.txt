I've spent the last year volunteering with a small regional land trust, mostly doing survey work, and I've ended up sitting in on the meetings where we decide which populations get our survey money. The group's working spreadsheet has three columns that I can't reconcile with one another. The first is taxonomic: we can only spend on something that has a name, and the naming rules in zoology require that a name be anchored to a single designated specimen — the holotype — deposited in a museum collection, which remains the permanent reference point for the name even if a much better specimen is found later. So the thing our budget line points at is, in the first instance, one preserved individual, and everything else is an inference from it. The second column is legal, and I'm on shakier ground here, but the gist as I understand it is this: the federal statute that funds most of this work protects species, and because the biology doesn't reliably hand the listing agency a clean answer to the question of what counts as one, the two agencies issued a joint policy in the mid-1990s inventing the distinct population segment — a unit that has to be both discrete and significant before it can be treated as though it were a species — and the statute permits that move only for vertebrates, so the same imperiled population of mussels or orchids has to make its case as a full species or not at all; Pacific salmon get a third kind of unit again. The third column is accounting: our regional biodiversity score is a count of species, so when a committee of taxonomists splits one species into three, the score rises and the region looks richer without a single organism being born, and when they lump, it falls. And when a listed form interbreeds with a common relative, our spreadsheet zeroes it out — logged as non-native genotype, no conservation value — which would be an unremarkable bookkeeping convention if species were natural kinds and is a little alarming if they aren't.

I've read enough of the LessWrong-adjacent canon, and audited enough of a philosophy of biology seminar, to recognize the species problem when I see it, so I don't need a tour of the standard positions. What I want is the implicit theory. What does this bundle of practices have to presuppose about species — and about value — in order to hang together? Take the three columns separately and then together. Which parts strictly require realism about species as real units of nature, which merely fit more comfortably with it, and which would be entirely untouched if species turned out to be conventional partitions of a continuous lineage? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — for instance, does keying spending to names presuppose that biodiversity value arrives bundled into discrete, countable units, or is that just an artifact of how we do the accounting? Does fixing the name to a single specimen commit us to anything like essentialism, or is type-anchoring a purely bureaucratic device that presupposes nothing metaphysical at all? And is there a genuine inconsistency in a system that needs listing decisions to be correct or incorrect — litigable, revisable only on evidence — while the boundaries between the units are settled by whichever species concept the relevant committee happens to favor? I suspect there's an equivocation between an epistemic claim (there's a fact of the matter we are trying to track) and a metaphysical one (the units are real joints of nature), but I can't tell whether the practice needs the second or only the first.

Finally, take the deflationary view seriously and price it. If species are not natural kinds, could we run the same institution on lineages, on evolutionarily significant units, on phylogenetic diversity, or on some continuous measure of distinctness — and would that be an improvement or merely a redescription? What would be lost: legal determinacy, international comparability, the public's willingness to donate, the ability to trade habitat destruction in units of species? And what would be lost that matters morally, given that the alternative candidates (lineages, ecosystems, individual animals) don't line up with the units the law currently protects? Don't hand me five positions and a shrug. Commit to a reading of what the practice presupposes, defend it, tell me what evidence would show you're wrong, and say what if anything I should be doing differently in those meetings. I'm not asking for citations or a reading list — assume I can't look anything up — but flag any point where you're leaning on a legal or nomenclatural detail you can't be sure of.
