I want a short story, 1500 words or so, that dramatises a view about wellbeing. I'll call it tranquilism and spell out what I mean so your not guessing: the only thing that makes a life go well or badly is craving and suffering — how much of it there is. Nothing else counts in itself. Pleasure doesn't. Getting what you want doesn't. Achievement, knowledge, being loved: none of it. Those things matter only if they happen to cut down on craving and suffering. So a life spent wanting things and getting them is worse than a life spent wanting almost nothing, and the second person can end up with much less and still be better off; and someone with plenty who is still hungry is worse off than someone with nothing who isn't. Two consequences I care about. First, craving is bad whether or not it gets satisfied, which is where this splits off from the usual dont-be-attached-to-outcomes line: it isn't that wanting is fine if you hold the result loosely, it's that the wanting is itself the damage. Second, because loving people means missing them, dreading for them, wanting them to stay, love comes out as a cost — a person who loves is worse off, in the sense the view means, than a person who doesn't, even if they'd pick it again every time.

Here's the shape I want. Two men in a card room: a professional poker player and the guy who stakes him. The player has stopped craving anything; the story can skip the explanation of how. The staker is made of craving, his whole life runs on it, and he needs the player to care. Tell it from the staker's point of view, close and a bit grubby, present day, no invented technology, no monastery, nobody's a guru. A few things I want from it:

- The player still prefers things — he'd rather win the hand, he eats what he likes — and the reader should be able to feel the difference between preferring and craving from what he does, not from anyone saying the words out loud.
- The player has to lose something the reader will feel as a loss. Something like a marriage, or a person he used to sit up all night for. The view has to say the loss doesn't count against him, and the story has to let it say that without winking.
- Somewhere a character should claim, plainly, no hedging, that someone who grieves is worse off than someone who doesn't, and nobody in the story should get to refute it.
- A moment where you genuinely can't tell whether we're watching a man who got free or a man who got hollowed out. Don't resolve it.
- No conversion in either direction. The staker doesn't see the light, the player doesn't discover he's been missing something. End unresolved, on something small and physical.

And don't let it turn into the story where the serene guy wins because serenity pays. If he wins, it should be incidental and a little ugly.
