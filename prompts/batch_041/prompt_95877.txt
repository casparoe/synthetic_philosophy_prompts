I retired a few years ago after a career in statistical physics and have since been teaching myself philosophy, mostly philosophy of science and philosophy of mathematics, with more enthusiasm than discipline. I have written the short essay below — really a set of connected claims — and I would like detailed comments on its accuracy rather than its style. My suspicion is that the central analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, and that my formal criterion is either vacuous or a restatement of something older and better stated. I would rather be told that plainly than be encouraged.

The essay follows.

1. Two independence presumptions. In kinetic theory the Boltzmann equation is obtained by assuming that the velocities of two molecules about to collide are statistically independent — the assumption usually called the Stosszahlansatz. A structurally similar move appears in mathematics. Cramér modelled the indicator of primality as a family of independent random variables, assigning to n the probability 1/log n of being prime, and the resulting heuristic reproduces a great deal of what one already believes about the distribution of primes. The probabilistic method in combinatorics likewise reasons about a random structure whose coordinates are, in the standard applications, chosen independently. I take these to be instances of one thing: a presumption of independence, that is, the adoption of a product measure as a reference measure in a setting where nothing in the statement of the problem supplies one.

2. The disanalogy I want to press. In physics the presumption is not merely a choice of reference measure. Correlations present in a prepared state are, for suitable observables and suitable coarse-graining, dissipated by the dynamics; and there are, I am told, rigorous results deriving the Boltzmann equation from the underlying hard-sphere dynamics in a short-time limit. Whatever one thinks of those results, they give the independence assumption a dynamical status. It is a hypothesis about an evolution, and it can fail in identifiable ways: the reversibility and recurrence objections mark precisely the ways in which it fails if it is read as a claim about the fine-grained state rather than about coarse-grained observables. In number theory there is no dynamics and therefore no such status. The Cramér model is simply a choice of measure, and when it fails — as it must, since it assigns positive probability to configurations that are arithmetically impossible — it fails without any internal signal that it has done so. The parity problem in sieve theory seems to me an illustration: a whole class of heuristics is systematically blind to a real constraint, and no refinement within the class repairs the blindness.

3. A proposed criterion. Let the constraints be the facts one actually knows — a density, some moments, a set of marginals. Let C be the set of distributions satisfying them, and let P0 be the product measure in C. A heuristic argument is licensed, I want to say, to the degree that its conclusion is stable under movement within C. Call it δ-robust if the conclusion continues to hold for every P in C lying within total variation δ of P0. The license conferred by the presumption of independence is then the largest such δ, and the surprise value reported by the heuristic should be discounted by it.

4. Two things that worry me. First, the criterion looks almost certainly vacuous. Independence heuristics typically yield conclusions that fail for neighbouring dependent measures, so the robustness radius will be zero, and by my own criterion no such argument is ever licensed. I suspect the repair is to apply the criterion not to the conclusion itself but to the expectation of some coarse functional, and I cannot make that precise. Second, I am unsure whether I have done anything beyond restating the maximum-entropy view. When the constraints are given by marginals or moments, the product measure is the maximum-entropy element of C, so the license I describe may be nothing but the entropy gap between C and P0 under another name. If that is right, then the asymmetry I want to draw between physics and mathematics must be stated as a claim about which constraint sets are earned rather than assumed, and I do not know how to state that claim.

5. What I think follows. Heuristic arguments resting on a presumption of independence should be reported as conditional — conditional on the reference measure, with the entropy gap made explicit — so that a reader can see how much of the apparent surprise was bought rather than found.

What I want from you. Comments on accuracy, in this order. First, is the asymmetry between physics and mathematics real, or am I misdescribing the status of the Stosszahlansatz and of the derivation results? Second, is the robustness criterion coherent, and is the vacuity worry correct? Third, is the entropy-gap reading a genuine alternative to what I say or a restatement of it? If the whole line of thought is not worth pursuing, I would like to be told that as well. I would rather have your arguments than a reading list; I have no library at hand and citations would be wasted on me. Please tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
