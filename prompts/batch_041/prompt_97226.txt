I have no training in philosophy. I read on my own and argue with people in comment threads and at the back of community meetings, which is where this comes from. In a drawn-out argument about whether a speaker should be barred from a public venue for saying things that offended a group of people, the person on the other side kept repeating, as though it settled everything, that "your right to swing your fist ends where my nose begins." I can see why it sounds decisive. I also suspect it is doing several quite different jobs at once, and that some of them are almost empty while others are doing all the real work. What I want from you is not an essay on free speech and not a defense of either side, but a careful separation of the distinct claims that one sentence could be making — and a verdict on each.

Please keep in mind, as you lay this out, that the saying is usually treated as a folksy summary of John Stuart Mill's harm principle, and I want to know at each step whether the slogan is a faithful compression of that principle, a distortion of it, or something Mill would not recognize at all.

I would like the menu of readings to cover at least the following questions, though you should add any that I have missed:

- What "harm" is supposed to be: any displeasure or offense at all, or only a setback to someone's interests, or only an actual violation of a right. In particular, whether the feeling of being offended can count, and if so under what description.
- What the boundary is drawn around: a person's body, their property, their reputation, their emotional state, or something else. The image is physical, but the disputes never are.
- Who the principle binds and what it authorizes: only the state's use of force, or also private actors — the venue owner who refuses, the platform that bans, the crowd that ostracizes. Whether informal social pressure falls under it.
- Whether the principle is a threshold that must be crossed before any interference is permitted, or a complete justification the moment it is crossed, or a presumption that can be outweighed.
- Whether harm has to be a discrete act by an identifiable person, or whether diffuse, cumulative, and systemic harms count — and if they do, what happens to the fist-and-nose picture.
- Whether the fist-swinger owes anything about harms he merely risks or makes more likely, or harms he fails to prevent.
- Who decides where the nose begins, and by what procedure.
- Whether the sentence is a claim about rights, about justified coercion, or about moral blame — since those come apart.

Give me the answer as a table with exactly these columns, one row per reading:

- Reading: a short label for it.
- The precise claim: the reading stated as a proposition that could be true or false, with any hidden quantifiers or conditions made explicit.
- What it presupposes: what must be true about harm, rights, or boundaries for the reading to even be coherent.
- What it rules out: the concrete positions on restricting speech that the reading would forbid, and those it would permit.
- Verdict: one of "trivially true," "substantive and defensible," "dubious," or "false," followed by a single sentence of justification.
- Supports my opponent's move?: "yes," "no," or "only partly," with a short phrase saying why.

After the table, add one short paragraph naming the reading my opponent most likely had in mind and whether that reading is one Mill would recognize.
