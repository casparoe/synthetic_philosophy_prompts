I produce a limited-run interview podcast on long-run decision-making, and my next guest defends an unusual version of the hinge-of-history hypothesis. I would like a set of interview questions that force her to work rather than to restate the thesis, and that stay close to the details of her actual position.

Her position, as she states it, has three parts. First, she defines a hinge not by the magnitude of what is at stake but by action-sensitivity: a period counts as a hinge to the degree that the long-run trajectory of the world depends on what a small number of actors do within it. Second, she treats hinges as intervals rather than instants, and holds that a hinge can be recognized only from the inside — through a felt sense of contingency, not through any measurement available at the time. Third, she holds that the epistemic situation of someone who suspects they are inside a hinge is not simple ignorance but a specific failure mode she calls the leverage illusion: mistaking the fact that the period is unusually consequential for the fact that one's own choices are unusually consequential within it.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each followed by a follow-up that responds to the answer she is most likely to give. The follow-up should be able to survive her first reply rather than restating the original question in other words, and it should be the kind of question that anticipates the standard deflection and makes that deflection harder to use. Assume she will not cite anything, and that every question must be answerable from the position itself.

I want at least one question apiece on each of the following:

- How she would operationalize action-sensitivity without circularity, given that we can only observe how sensitive a period was after the fact.
- The selection-effect worry: if hinges are rare, what licenses her confidence that we occupy one; and if her account makes hinges common enough that we should expect to be in one, whether the hypothesis still says anything.
- What the view implies for action under cluelessness: if we cannot estimate the long-run effects of our choices, does the hinge claim recommend anything at all, and how does she avoid both paralysis and a license for dramatic gestures.
- How much the view depends on the assumption that the future is vast and valuable, and what becomes of the hinge claim if that assumption is denied.

Also include, somewhere in the sequence, a question that presses on the relation between the retrospective and prospective senses of the term hinge — whether her account can be stated in a way that a historian writing three centuries from now would not simply treat as a truism — and a question about the ethics of persuading an audience that it is living through a hinge. Order the questions so that the pressure builds, and end with the one she would least like to be asked.
