I'm a philosophy PhD student working on idealization in theories of welfare, and there's a failure mode I keep running into that I have never found a name for. The pattern is this: an account says that something is good or bad for a person just in case an idealized counterpart of her — fully informed, internally coherent, reasoning without error — would favor or disfavor it. Each such account sounds fine in the abstract, but it seems to break in precisely the cases where its idealizing condition is met. The counterpart who satisfies the condition is not the person whose good is at issue: she has different attachments and projects, and the fact that she satisfies the condition changes the very facts the account was meant to track. So the biconditional comes out false whenever its left-hand side holds, and remains merely untested otherwise. I notice this in idealized-advisor accounts of welfare, in "what she would want if she knew more and were more the person she wished she were" glosses on someone's good, and in the coherence conditions built into extrapolated-preference proposals. Is there an established term for this failure mode? If there is, I need to know how it is distinguished from the neighboring complaints — that the counterpart is simply a different subject, and that such accounts are circular or uninformative — because I suspect these are being run together under one heading. If there is no term, I would want one coined, with a definition and boundary cases; a bare label would not help me.
