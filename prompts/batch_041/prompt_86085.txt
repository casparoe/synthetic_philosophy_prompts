I am a philosopher who works at the seam between early modern metaphysics and the history of economic thought, and next month I have to present the central chapter of my book manuscript at a joint session of our department's early modern reading group and the history of economic thought seminar. The thesis I am defending is that George Berkeley's monetary writing is not the occasional pamphleteering it is usually taken for but a coherent extension of his idealism, and that it belongs in the prehistory of credit theories of money. The argument has three moves. Textually, the book of queries he brought out in Dublin in 1735 — published at first without his name, which he attached only in a later edition — asks, in the twenty-third query of the standard numbering, "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" The queries that follow treat gold, silver and paper alike as tickets or counters for reckoning and transferring value, suggest that trade and industry could continue on the old denominations even if the bullion were gone, and make the wealth of a nation a function of its industry and of the circulation of credit, by whatever marks or tokens the credit happens to be carried. Philosophically, I claim this is what one should expect from an immaterialist: a man who denies that material substance is the real furniture of the world has no reason to treat a yellow metal as the substance of value, and Berkeley's own doctrine of signs is the bridge between the two. Practically, the policy proposals in the book — the "sketch or plan of a national bank" that he later withdrew from the collected edition, the obsession with employing the poor, the refusal to treat bullion as an end in itself — fall out of the theory rather than being bolted onto it.

The room will not be homogeneous, and I know most of the people in it well enough to dread specific questions. Four or five are historians of economic thought of a broadly metallist and quantity-theoretic temper, who regard pamphlet literature as falling below the threshold at which "theory" begins, and who will say that Berkeley is a mercantilist with a taste for paper credit and nothing more; they know the money debates of the 1720s and 1730s far better than I do, and they will produce contemporaries who said the same things. Two or three are early modern philosophers — one of them is editing a volume on Berkeley's later writings — who will press on whether the monetary views really follow from immaterialism or are merely compatible with it, and who will police the difference between the sign-relations in the theory of vision and the language, and the "tickets" of the queries. There is an economic historian of eighteenth-century Ireland who will insist that Irish poverty was a matter of rents, absentee landlords and trade restrictions, not of the medium of exchange. There is a colleague from the economics department who works on chartalism, who will object that Berkeley's state is not the tax-imposing state that chartalist theory requires and that I am conscripting a doctrine that is not his. And the chair, a Berkeley editor, will police the texts: he will want to know which edition I am quoting, and he will remind the room, if I forget, that the book is composed of questions rather than assertions. I have twenty-five minutes and half an hour of questions; I am much stronger on the philosophy than on the period's monetary controversies, and I would rather know that now than discover it in front of them.

Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular objector would phrase it rather than in neutral philosophical prose, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and each followed by an honest note on where the reply is soft — the point at which the objector will come back and I will have nothing left. Tell me which two or three objections I should simply concede up front, in what words, and which single reply has to land for the chapter to survive the hour; then give me the question I am hoping nobody asks, and a sentence on what I should say if it comes. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list — I know the secondary literature, and anything you cite from memory I would have to check anyway. Do not spend space on the objections any Berkeley paper attracts regardless of its topic ("isn't idealism obviously false?"), and do not give me the objections a modern monetary economics seminar would raise; I want the ones this room will raise, in their vocabulary. Where the answer depends on what a particular query actually says and you are not certain of it, flag that instead of inventing a query to suit the argument. Keep the whole thing to roughly 1,500 words, tight enough to read on the train the morning of the session.
