My friend is convinced the Mises/Hayek calculation argument proves way too much and I cant tell if hes onto something or just confused. His argument:

(1) Allocating resources rationally means knowing the opportunity cost of every use — what else that steel, that labour, that land could have been doing.
(2) Those costs depend on the preferences and the local, mostly unspoken knowledge of millions of people, knowledge no single mind can gather up in one place.
(3) Only a competitive market, through prices thrown up by buying and selling, turns that scattered knowledge into something usable. Take away markets for the means of production and you take away the prices, and with them any way of calculating.
(4) So a centrally planned economy cant allocate rationally.
(5) But now look at a big firm. Head office decides how much capital, steel and labour each division gets, and it clearly doesnt poll every worker or customer for their tacit knowledge.
(6) And firms pull this off well enough to survive and grow for a century.
(7) So by (1)-(4) no firm should be able to allocate rationally either. Since they plainly can, the argument proves too much — a planning board could just do what a firm does, writ large.

My gut says the firm step is where it breaks, but every fix I try sounds like the cheap dodge 'ah, but firms use prices', which just raises the question: which prices, and arent those prices exactly what the board is missing? Heres the version that actually scares me. Let one company swallow the whole economy. No outside market, no external prices for it to lean on, everything allocated internally by managers. Is that firm now standing in exactly the socialist planners spot? If yes, then (5) works as an analogy and the calculation argument survives — it just says that firm is in trouble too. If no, what does the firm still have that the board doesnt?

So tell me which step goes and what it costs. If I keep the argument, I think I have to explain why an internal transfer price between two divisions isnt the same kind of thing as a market price, and why a firm can get away with central allocation while an economy cant — and the honest answer might be quantitative (firms are small, short-lived, and sit in a sea of markets), which feels like giving up the sharp version. If I drop the argument, I owe the Lange-style market socialists an account of why a board fiddling prices by trial and error fails where a firm doing roughly the same thing succeeds. Walk me through the move, then price both branches. And tell me if the whole thing really does hinge on how big the market sea around the firm island has to be.
