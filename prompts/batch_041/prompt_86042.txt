I'm convening a session of our comparative-politics reading group on a question two of my colleagues have been chewing over all term: whether Sweden or Norway is the more liberal country. The argument never gets anywhere, and I've come to suspect that's because they mean different things by the word. The first works on economic freedom indices and takes "liberal" to be about markets, private ownership, the state's role as producer and regulator, and the reach of taxation; she takes the indices to settle it in Sweden's favour. The second works on civil liberties, penal policy, and the regulation of private life, and takes "liberal" to be about speech, association, privacy, and how far the state may tell adults what they may do to their own bodies and habits; he is equally confident Norway wins, and he usually brings up the two countries' alcohol monopolies, their different relationships to the EU and the EEA, and their records on surveillance and drug policy. So the two positions I'd like treated as parties to a collaboration are, roughly, "liberal means economically liberal, and Sweden is the more so" and "liberal means civilly liberal, and Norway is the more so." They also can't agree on whether "liberal" should track law on the books or how things actually work in practice, and neither will accept the other's indices, each regarding them as rigged by their choice of indicators.

What I want from you is the document their adversarial collaboration should have produced — the one they could both put their names to. Open with a numbered section of agreed claims: what both of them accept about the two countries, about how "liberal" divides into distinct axes (economic; civil-libertarian; social and cultural; and constitutional, meaning the degree to which ordinary majorities are constrained by entrenched rights, courts, or external treaties), and about which comparisons neither of them disputes. These claims must not be vacuous — "both are wealthy Nordic welfare states" earns nobody anything — and each must be either definitional or checkable; any factual claim that is contested belongs in the disagreement section as a crux, not smuggled into the agreed claims. Then give me a disagreements table with one row per point of genuine difference, showing the claim each side would sign in its own preferred wording, the axis of "liberal" it turns on, and a classification of the disagreement as empirical, conceptual, or merely verbal.

Finish with the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, each with a concrete test, case, or comparison both sides would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance. I want a definite verdict on two things as well: whether the original question has a fact of the matter, or only a fact of the matter relative to a stipulated weighting across the axes; and whether any part of my colleagues' quarrel is purely terminological — if it is, say which part, and say which reading of "liberal" you would adopt and why. Be committed: where the document has to choose a weighting, choose one and defend it rather than leaving me a menu of options, and if you think the honest conclusion is that one country wins on some axes and the other on others with no overall ordering, say so plainly and defend that. No citations or reading lists, please — the indices and the cases can be named, but what I want is the structure of the disagreement, not a bibliography.
