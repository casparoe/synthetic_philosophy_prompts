Design a 90-minute activity for an undergraduate seminar of about eighteen students on Marx's critique of political economy; the room has movable chairs, no computers, and the only materials are printed role cards, a bowl of tokens, and a whiteboard. Participants should arrive at two conclusions under their own steam before either is named: that a wage relation can be exploitative even when the worker freely consents and ends up materially better off than before, and that this verdict tracks the distribution of productive assets rather than the employer's greed or the worker's misery. A third, deliberately weaker aim is for them to feel the distance between that distributive fact and alienation, which should register as the gap between what the rules pay them and what the rules let them know, decide, or keep.

Build the game so that the structure rather than the facilitator's framing does the work: assign roles and endowments asymmetrically at the outset, let owners and workers bargain over a wage and an effort level, and pay out in tokens on a printed schedule no one may renegotiate mid-round. Run a first round at a low wage, then a second round in which the owner is instructed to pay generously and the worker is unambiguously better off than in the no-trade baseline, so that participants see nothing in the exploitation verdict turn on the worker's hardship. Then run a withdrawal round in which each participant computes from the printed ledger whether they would be better off leaving the economy altogether and taking an equal per-capita share of the productive assets instead, with the results recorded publicly and left standing even if they cut against the expected pattern; do not let the words exploitation, surplus, or alienation be spoken until the debrief.

The debrief must map particular moments of play onto particular claims: the agreed wage onto the formal freedom of the contract, the generous second round onto the compatibility of exploitation with mutual gain, the withdrawal round onto the asset-distribution criterion, and the rules governing effort, information, and ownership of the product onto alienation as a distinct axis the payoff schedule cannot capture. It should also meet head-on the objection that the numbers were simply rigged, and mark clearly the difference between what the activity demonstrated and what it merely assumed.

Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong on a first run, at minimum participants moralizing early and casting the owner as a villain, someone refusing to occupy the exploiting role, arithmetic confusion during the withdrawal round, and a sharp student announcing that the endowments were constructed rather than earned; say how the facilitator should handle each without rescuing the group from the result it is supposed to reach. Be equally explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply: the historical separation of workers from the means of production that makes the initial distribution look like a given, any account of the magnitude of surplus value, the lived texture of alienated labor, and the normative argument that the arrangement is unjust rather than merely unequal.

Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
