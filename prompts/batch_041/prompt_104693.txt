I am an undergraduate in a joint philosophy and economics program, and a dispute I keep encountering across two of my courses has stalled in a way I would like you to diagnose rather than settle. In my microeconomics course, preferences and utility are defined operationally: a person's preferences simply are the choices they would make across a range of options, and a utility function is a numerical representation of those choices, carrying no commitment to any inner state. On this view, to say that someone prefers A to B is to say nothing more than that they choose A when both are available. In my ethics course, the same two words are used as though they named something real about a person's good: that having one's preferences satisfied makes a life go better, and that utility is a magnitude we can meaningfully compare between people. When I have raised the tension in either class, the response is always the same. The economists say the philosophers are psychologizing a formal apparatus and committing a category mistake; the philosophers say the economists have quietly changed the subject and are no longer talking about welfare at all. Both sides appear to know all of each other's arguments, and neither has moved.

Here is a small case that makes the clash sharp for me, and I offer it because I think the frameworks deliver opposite verdicts on it. Imagine a person whose choices are perfectly consistent and can be represented by a well-behaved utility function, but who, when asked, sincerely denies having any preferences at all and says they simply follow habit and never weigh options against one another. The operational view must say that this person does have preferences, since the representation is exhaustive of what a preference is; a mental-state view must say they have none, since the relevant inner state is absent. Neither side, as far as I can tell, treats this as a problem. A second case cuts the other way: a person who reports a firm and stable preference but whose choices, when tested, are intransitive. The operational view can say only that they have no consistent preference ordering, while the mental-state view can say they have a genuine but incoherent preference. Again the two frameworks part ways on the same facts.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept, is that the impasse is not really about the mathematics or about welfare, but about what kind of thing a theoretical term in a science may name: whether 'preference' picks out a cause of behavior or a summary of it, and whether a science of mental states can be autonomous from behavior. If that is right, the disagreement is substantive and runs down into philosophy of mind, and no amount of clarifying the economics will resolve it. But it might instead be that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, one explanatory and one normative, in which case each is right about its own question and the appearance of contradiction is an artifact of a shared vocabulary.

Please diagnose the dispute. Is it verbal, turning on the word 'preference'? Is it substantive, resting on incompatible views about mental states and evidence? Or is it two questions conflated? Ground your diagnosis in what each side actually claims, distinguish what the operational view strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a terminological reform, a clarification of what we want the theory to do — or argue that no progress should be expected, and why.
