I am an undergraduate taking a third-year course on perception and cognition, and our seminars have been circling the debate over cognitive penetration. For my essay I want to defend a fairly strong version of the thesis: that perceptual experience itself — not merely judgement, attention, or some post-perceptual inference — can be shaped by the subject's concepts, beliefs, and expectations, and that this shaping is partly constitutive of what is experienced, rather than a matter of how an independently fixed experience is later interpreted or reported.

What I need from you is not a survey of the literature but a map of the position's exposure. Please tell me what would count against it, and sort the answer by how much each consideration should actually move me. I would like the defeaters ranked, from those that would be fatal to the constitutive claim, through those that would force a serious restatement, down to those that are merely awkward. For each, say whether it is an empirical finding, a conceptual or methodological argument, or a case that the view cannot comfortably describe, and say what it would take for that consideration to be decisive rather than suggestive.

Several things I specifically want you to address. First, if the methodological critiques of the last decade are right — that most alleged top-down effects on perception can be explained away by attention, memory, response bias, task demands, or ordinary perceptual learning — does that refute the thesis, or does it merely remove the evidence for it while leaving the possibility open? I want that distinction made sharply, because I suspect my position can survive the loss of the evidence but not a positive argument that the mechanism cannot exist. Second, I have been toying with a line I find tempting but cannot assess: that what looks like penetration is really the front end being re-tuned by learning, so that the perceptual system's outputs change while its contents are not shaped by anything semantic at all. Is that a genuine rival to my thesis, a compatible refinement of it, or an unstable halfway house? Third, which consequences of the strong thesis would a committed defender themselves have to concede as costs — in particular, what happens to the idea that perception justifies belief if what we perceive is already partly fixed by what we believe?

I would also like an honest verdict on whether the thesis, as I have stated it, risks unfalsifiability. If every disconfirming result can be absorbed by retreating to a weaker claim — that perception is penetrable in principle, or that some experience somewhere might be — tell me so, and tell me whether that insulation is a defect of the position or an acceptable feature of a claim at this level of generality. Where the defender would dismiss a proposed defeater, give me the dismissal in its strongest form, not a caricature, and then say whether I should find it convincing.

Finally, what would a decisive test even look like? I would be most moved by a case in which two subjects share retinal input and attentional state and still differ in what they experience, but I suspect such a case is impossible to establish cleanly. If it is, say why, and tell me what weaker evidential standard I should be willing to accept instead.

Please write your answer in British English.
