For my final essay in my history and philosophy seminar, I would like to tackle the question our class has argued over all term without settling: what actually drove the radicalization of the French Revolution, and does the familiar claim that revolutions devour their own children name a real mechanism or only a grim coincidence that we mistake for a pattern because we already know how the story ended?

Three broad explanations compete, and I cannot get them to sit comfortably together. The first is class struggle: an economically rising bourgeoisie displacing a landed aristocracy, with the political upheaval and the Terror as the violent expression of that underlying conflict. The second is ideology: a new political language and a project of remaking society from the ground up, which generated enemies faster than it could convert them. The third is contingency: the Revolution took the shape it did because of particular decisions, accidents, and the collapse of the institutions that had once mediated conflict, and at many points it could plausibly have gone otherwise.

My difficulty is that the third account makes the Revolution's self-destruction — the fact that a striking number of the people who made it were eventually killed by it — look like an accident rather than a consequence, while the first two seem to cheat by treating the outcome as something that was always going to happen and by reducing the individuals who were purged to instruments of a process already decided. I would like an essay that lives with this tension rather than dissolving it.

Write an essay on this controversy that reaches a definite conclusion. Weigh the class-struggle, ideological, and contingency explanations against one another, and argue for whichever does the most explanatory work — or for a specific division of labor among them, provided you say which one carries the load and why the others do not. Address the devouring claim directly: is there a sense in which a revolution, or at least this one, contains a mechanism that turns it against its own authors, or is that idea a retrospective illusion? Test the accounts against a counterfactual of your own devising — for instance, whether a revolution that consolidated rather than consumed its leadership would count against any of the three readings, and why. Say what evidence would tell in favor of each view, and what sort of case would have forced you to abandon your conclusion. Do not settle for the observation that all three factors were at work; that is true of nearly any historical event and decides nothing. You may argue from general historical knowledge, but I do not need a list of historians or books; the reasoning matters more to me than the reading.
