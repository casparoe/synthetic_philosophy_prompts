I run civic engagement for a mid-size city — I'm the person who gets handed things like "council wants a citizens' assembly on the transit budget" and has to make it real with six weeks, four staff, and a few hundred residents who mostly show up once. I've been auditing a seminar on Dewey and his account of inquiry is honestly the most useful thing I've encountered for this job, except it all sits at a level where I can't hand it to my facilitators or to the parks guy who's running the next one. So I need you to turn it into a procedure we can actually run. What I mean by Dewey here is the shape of the thing rather than the slogans: inquiry starts because a situation is genuinely indeterminate and not just because two factions disagree; the problem has to get stated before it can be solved, and stating it is already a move; proposals are ends-in-view that get revised by the consequences they produce when acted on; a claim is warranted relative to an inquiry that has actually been carried through, not in the abstract; a public comes into being around the indirect consequences of transactions it didn't choose, and democracy is mostly about making those consequences communicable to the people absorbing them. I want all of that operationalized for people with no philosophy training.

Fix the role and the constraints properly: we do one of these cycles every month or so, each one lands on a real budget line or a real bylaw change that council votes on, so the stakes are things like whether a corridor gets cameras or a route goes fare-free. We can't run a controlled trial — the pilot is the policy, it happens once, and the people who eat the downside didn't consent to being in the study. Our report has to be publishable and the council has to not be able to shrug it off. So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, how we tell an indeterminate situation from a straight conflict of interest (because I suspect half our cycles are the second thing dressed as the first, and Dewey's method would have us doing something else entirely there), how we turn a felt grievance into a stated problem without letting the loudest party write it, how we convert a proposal into a hypothesis with predicted consequences and a named test, how we decide who bears the risk of a failed experiment and whether they can consent, and the points where the procedure should stop and hand the call to a person because the question is values and not method. Mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as method — I'd rather my facilitators know where they're improvising.

Then run it on three cases and don't spare me: (1) a pilot of automated speed cameras on a corridor where the crash data is contested and the neighbourhood association and the delivery companies want opposite things; (2) a fare-free bus pilot on two routes, which I personally believe in and have been quietly lobbying for; (3) a bylaw banning phones in middle schools, where the parents asking for it and the students affected are obviously not the same public. At least one of these should come out with the procedure telling me not to do the thing I want, and I want to see the step where that happens. Give it back to me as JSON, one object, with these fields: "procedure", an array where each element has "step" (number), "name" (short), "question" (the thing we literally ask), "what_counts_as_answered" (the standard, in plain language), "if_it_fails" (what we do instead of pushing on), "who_decides" (either "procedure" or "person"), and "judgment_hidden_here" (say what we're really deciding when we answer this step, or null if it's genuinely mechanical); "worked_cases", an array where each element has "case", "steps_that_decide_it" (array of step numbers), "outcome", "asker_will_like_it" (yes or no), and "why"; "stop_conditions", an array of plain-language conditions under which we abandon the inquiry entirely; and "cannot_do", an array of the things this procedure will reliably get wrong. Keep the Dewey vocabulary only where it earns its keep — if a term isn't doing work for a facilitator, cut it and say the ordinary thing instead.
