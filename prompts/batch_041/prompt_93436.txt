I'm a second-year PhD student putting together a fellowship application — a one-year award, and the deliverable is a proposal of no more than two pages — and I'd like to use it to develop a half-formed idea that has been nagging me since a long argument with a historian friend over coffee. The idea is this: the long quarrel over whether there can be laws of history, the quarrel that Popper is generally credited with settling in the negative on the grounds that the course of human history is shaped by the growth of knowledge and that the growth of knowledge cannot itself be predicted in advance, looks to me like a quarrel conducted almost entirely under a single conception of what a law is. Both the friends of historical laws and their critics seem to assume that a law would have to be a universal, exceptionless regularity of the Humean sort, so that the only question is whether history supplies regularities of that kind, and Popper's argument then does its work rather quickly. But if one adopts instead the view that a generalisation earns the title of law by being invariant under a sufficiently broad range of interventions on the variables it relates — the conception usually associated with Woodward — then the question changes shape, and it is far from obvious that Popper's argument reaches the cases that matter. There is a second thread I'd like woven in: the historical sciences generally (palaeontology, evolutionary biology, cosmology) seem to trade in generalisations that explain and unify retrospectively without supporting prediction of the same kind that a physics-style law supports, and I want to know whether that is a genuine third category or just a rhetorical refuge for the defeated regularities view.

Please turn this into the proposal itself. I need the guiding question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, rather than the vague question of whether history has laws; an account of why the existing positions leave the matter open, which you can give by describing arguments and positions rather than by citing anyone, since I shall add the references myself and a bibliography would be wasted on me here; a clear description of the approach — what I would actually do, month by month across the year, including how I would go about testing candidate generalisations against an invariance criterion without pretending to run experiments on the past; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing, as opposed to merely producing a negative result. It would help me if you would illustrate the difference in verdict by taking one or two familiar candidate generalisations — say, that transitions to democracy tend to follow rising literacy, or that deepening trade makes war between great powers less likely — and showing what each conception of law would say about them, though please keep the examples to things a reader would recognise and do not let the proposal hang on any contested detail of a particular historical case. Finally, be realistic about scope: two pages and a year is not much, so tell me what to leave out, and say plainly where the project risks becoming too large to finish. Write the proposal in American English.
