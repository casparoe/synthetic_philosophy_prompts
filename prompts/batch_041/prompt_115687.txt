I'm taking a seminar on the history of social choice, and our final assignment is an exercise in counterfactual intellectual history: pick a thinker who died before some problem was ever posed, and reconstruct what he would have said about it, on the understanding that the reconstruction has to be argued from his own commitments rather than from what we would prefer him to have said. I have chosen Condorcet (1743–1794) and the discursive dilemma, and I would like to get the reconstruction straight before I commit to a thesis.

Here is the problem in the form I intend to use. A three-member committee must decide whether to approve a drug for a rare disease. Approval, on the committee's own rules, requires two things: that the trials establish efficacy (P) and that the safety profile be acceptable (Q). Approval just is P and Q together. Each member judges P, judges Q, and also records a verdict on approval. The votes come out as follows: one member says yes to P, yes to Q, approve; a second says yes to P, no to Q, reject; a third says no to P, yes to Q, reject. A majority therefore finds P, a majority finds Q, and yet a majority rejects the conclusion that P and Q — the collective judgment is internally inconsistent even though every individual member's judgment is perfectly consistent. What I want from you is a committed prediction rather than a survey of the literature. Which procedure would Condorcet have favored — deciding the premises and letting the conclusion follow, deciding the conclusion directly and letting the premises fall where they may, or some third arrangement — and does his probabilistic framework actually force an answer, or does it leave the choice underdetermined? He is credited with discovering that majority rule can cycle over three or more alternatives, so I would also like you to say whether he would treat the present inconsistency as the same malady he had already diagnosed or as a genuinely distinct one, and what remedy, if any, his apparatus for estimating the probability that a majority is correct would supply. There is a reading of him on which he distinguishes decisions that report the assembly's judgment about a matter of fact from decisions that merely register its will; if you find that distinction serviceable, say so, and say which side of it a drug-approval verdict falls on and why that bears on the aggregation rule.

Finally, I want an honest accounting of the reconstruction's seams. Which parts of your prediction follow from commitments Condorcet demonstrably held, and which are projection from our own debates? In particular, would he have had any sympathy for the contemporary claim that a group counts as a rational agent only if it aggregates whole sets of judgments rather than settling issues one at a time, or would he have regarded that demand as confused? Close by telling me what evidence would overturn your reading and what a defender of the opposing reading would say on its behalf. No citations or reading list, please — I need the argument, and I would rather you reason from his framework than gesture at what later commentators have made of him.
