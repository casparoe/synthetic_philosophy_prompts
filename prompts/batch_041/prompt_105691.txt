I have been reading Hume's essay Of Suicide — the one he wrote in the 1750s and kept out of his lifetime's publications, so that it appeared only after his death — and I am struck by how tightly its argument is bounded by the world he inhabited. Its organizing claim is that if suicide is criminal it must be a transgression of a duty "either to God, our neighbour, or ourselves," and Hume then argues that it is none of the three; running through the essay is a picture of a person entitled to weigh the continuation of his own existence much as he weighs any other good he might retain or surrender. Almost everything that makes the question hard today is missing from that picture: there were no physicians empowered to end life, no psychiatry with the authority to treat a suicidal wish as a symptom rather than a judgment, no institutional machinery for preventing death, no legal apparatus of consent and capacity. I would like you to reconstruct the commitments of that essay closely enough to project Hume forward into debates that arose only centuries later, and to do so with the discipline of someone who can tell what a text implies apart from what we merely wish it implied.

Here is the terrain I want you to cover. First, medical assistance in dying: several jurisdictions now permit a physician to supply or administer a lethal dose to a consenting adult, and the live disputes are whether irremediable mental illness should qualify on its own, whether a person should be able to make an advance request that binds others after he loses decision-making capacity, and whether legalization places a quiet pressure on disabled and elderly people who come to feel they are a burden — a worry that, if sound, would bear directly on any argument treating continued life as a service one may decline. Second, the professionalization of death: suicide prevention as a public-health mandate, the involuntary psychiatric detention of the suicidal, and the clinical habit of reading a suicidal desire as evidence of illness rather than as a judgment to be assessed. Third, the metaphysics of the act: does Hume's account of the passions commit him to the view that a suicide is rational whenever the agent's own reckoning of his future falls below the value of nothing, or does something in his distinction between the calm and the violent passions, or in his scepticism about our power to forecast our own future sentiments, block that inference? Fourth, the political question: if the state has no legitimate stake in preventing a competent person's suicide, what follows for the criminal law, for a legal duty to rescue, and for the practice of coercive intervention — and what, if anything, survives of the state's interest in protecting the suicidal person from his own transient impulses?

I want the projection to be honest rather than convenient. Where the essay underdetermines the answer, say so and lay out the range of positions it leaves open, distinguishing what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Where facts Hume could not have anticipated — the reliability of prognosis, the availability of effective palliative care, the social meaning of dependence in an aging society — strain the argument, tell me whether his principles bend or break, and where they break, which of his premises you would have to surrender. And please do not stop at "Hume would have been a liberal about this": I want the specific moves his arguments license, the ones they forbid, and the ones on which they are simply silent.
