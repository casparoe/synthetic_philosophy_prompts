I'm writing a chapter on the Hart–Fuller debate and I'd rather hang it on a real case that isn't Nuremberg for the hundredth time. Take the Mauerschützen prosecutions after reunification — former GDR border guards tried for shooting people at the wall. The rough shape, which I think you can take as given: the BGH upheld convictions partly by reading the GDR's own border law in a human-rights-friendly way (pulling a proportionality requirement out of the GDR constitution) and partly by reaching for Radbruch's formula, on the view that the deliberate killing of unarmed refugees was an unbearable violation of elementary justice and so never law to begin with; the constitutional court and later the Strasbourg court let the convictions stand, while a lot of the German legal literature treated the whole thing as retroactive punishment dressed up as interpretation.

What I want is the episode run through Hart and Fuller properly, both directions. Does the BGH's two-step — GDR law read through the GDR's own constitution, with Radbruch only as a fallback — vindicate Hart, by showing you can always get the result through interpretation and never need the natural-law move? Or does it vindicate Fuller, since an order that shoots unarmed kids at the wall arguably lacked the inner morality of law from the start? Then be honest about where the theory runs out: what does the separation thesis actually tell you when it's the successor state prosecuting officials for having obeyed its predecessor's law, and do either of these positions have anything to say about whether the guards were punished justly rather than just punished? Finally, what does the case show about the debate itself — if the outcome is overdetermined (GDR law read generously, Radbruch as backstop, retroactive legislation if all else fails, which is basically Hart's own suggestion), is the disagreement about the concept of law doing any real work, or was it always about what judges should say versus what they should do? Skip the grudge-informer hypothetical, I've had it.

One thing: keep the settled bits (the trials happened, the convictions held up, the retroactivity objection is real and was the standard complaint) apart from what people who actually work on this still fight about — whether Radbruch was doing any work here, whether the BGH's reasoning is even internally coherent, whether the episode counts as a win for either side. Don't hand me a consensus where there isn't one.
