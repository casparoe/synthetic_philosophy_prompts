Two acquaintances of mine — call them Nadia and Ellis — recently talked their way into an argument about a hypothetical case of their own invention, and they have asked me to referee. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move.

The case they invented is this. A town begins from a distribution they stipulate to be perfectly just: every household holds roughly an equal share. A charismatic fitness coach starts offering free outdoor classes in the town square. Over several years, hundreds of residents voluntarily drop money into a collection bucket as they leave, and the coach becomes by a wide margin the wealthiest person in town. At the same time, rents and local prices rise, so that residents who never attended a class and never paid the coach anything find themselves materially worse off than before, through no decision of their own. A proposal is then floated to tax the coach's gains and pay the proceeds out to the town as a whole.

Here is how the argument went.

(1) Nadia said the proposal is unjust. Every dollar the coach holds arrived through a voluntary transfer from a consenting adult and is therefore his by entitlement; taking it back would violate his rights and would interfere with the very liberty that generated the new distribution. She invoked the Wilt Chamberlain case: begin from any pattern you please, and once people are free to spend their money as they choose, the pattern will be upset, so restoring it would require continuous interference. The coach's fortune is just for exactly the reason Chamberlain's would be.

(2) Ellis replied that the comparison is misleading. In the Chamberlain case, the people who enrich him are the same people who voluntarily pay him, so the transaction is self-contained. In the coach's case, the coach's success also drives up the rents of residents who never paid him a cent and never agreed to anything. Those residents are made worse off without their consent, and the Chamberlain argument says nothing about them. So this is not a clean instance of liberty upsetting a pattern; it is a case of one person's liberty imposing costs on others.

(3) Nadia answered that this proves too little. Rising prices are simply how markets register changing demand; no one promised the non-attendees fixed rents, and the fact that a voluntary exchange between two parties alters the options open to a third is not, by itself, a wrong to the third. If every spillover effect counted as a violation of entitlement, then no distribution could ever be just, and the notion that a voluntary transfer does any moral work at all would collapse.

(4) Ellis said that if that is the standard, then the Chamberlain argument proves far too much: it would license any distribution whatever, so long as each individual step looked voluntary — including one resting on a monopoly, or one in which a single person ended up owning everything. She added that Nozick's own theory includes a principle of rectification for past injustices, which he left largely undeveloped, and that this means the argument only gets off the ground from a baseline we may never actually occupy; in a world saturated with historical wrongs, there may be no clean starting point from which to run the thought experiment at all.

(5) Nadia said the last point confuses a proof of concept with a policy proposal. The Chamberlain case is meant to show that patterned principles are inherently unstable under liberty, not to describe any actual society's history; the fact that we cannot point to a perfectly just baseline does not show that the argument fails on its own terms.

They asked me which of them was right, and I genuinely could not say. Please work through the exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify the rating briefly. Then say which specific claims each person gets right, what each of them is overlooking, and who holds the stronger overall position — without simply awarding victory to one person wholesale. Please write your answer in American English.
