DRAFT

Here's the thing that bugs me about iterated elimination of dominated strategies once you let mixed strategies do the eliminating. Take one player's payoffs against three columns:

        L      C      R
T      10      0     10
M       9      4      4
B      10     10      0

M isn't dominated by T (at C, 0 is less than 4) and isn't dominated by B (at R, 0 is less than 4). But flip a coin between T and B and you get (10, 5, 5), which beats M everywhere. So M is strictly dominated — by a lottery — and out it goes. Worth noting that if only pure strategies were allowed to do the eliminating, nothing would get eliminated on this player's side at all and M would survive. So the lottery isn't cosmetic; it's the whole engine.

Fine. But look at what I used to kill M: T and B, both of which I keep. So the reason M is out is that a coin-flip between two options I'm still treating as live beats it. It feels like I convicted M using witnesses I don't believe. And it gets worse if T and B themselves get knocked out later in the process — then the final surviving set is some group of strategies, and M was thrown out on the strength of options that the final answer says are no good. That looks like a magic trick.

I think the resolution is that the first elimination is a claim about the whole game, not about whatever reduced game we've reached: if M is strictly dominated, then M is not a best response to any belief at all about the other players, including beliefs that put weight on strategies we'd never credit them with, so M can't be in any equilibrium or any rationalizable set and deleting it costs nothing. It's the later eliminations that lean on the assumption that the other players are rational. So order-independence for strict elimination is a symptom of something deeper. With weak dominance the order obviously matters — here's a two-by-two where the order decides whether a strategy survives (row payoffs first):

        L        R
T     (1,1)    (0,2)
B     (1,1)    (1,1)

T is weakly dominated by B, L is weakly dominated by R. Kill T first and L survives; kill L first and then T dies, and L dies with it. Different surviving sets from the same game.

Is the math right — the examples, the claim that pure-only elimination leaves M alone, the claim that strict elimination is order-independent? And is the closing contrast with weak dominance the right place to land, or is it a distraction from the puzzle? Comments on accuracy first, then on whether the framing is actually interesting or just me being spooked by something every textbook already handles.
