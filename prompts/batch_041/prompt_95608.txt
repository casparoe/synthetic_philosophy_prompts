So I've gotten myself into an arguement I keep loosing, and I need it written out properly so I can figure out what I actually think. My housemate is finishing a PhD on Hegel, I'm doing a masters in phil of biology, and it blew up when I dragged him to a public lecture about whether slime moulds and plants make decisions. He walked out genuinely angry, saying the whole field is cheating: a reason is a normative thing, something you can be answerable for getting wrong, and where there's nobody home to be answerable to anything there's no reason, just machinery. I think he's wrong but every time I try to say why I end up sounding like I'm just personifying a thermostat.

So write me a dialogue between two people who both know what they're doing. One of them (call them A) holds that a selection process of some kind — natural selection, reinforcement, market competition, cultural transmission — can fix a rationale without anyone representing it: there's a real fact of the matter about what a mechanism is for and about when it's failing, even though nobody ever thought the rationale. The other (B) holds that reasons exist only in the space of reasons: having a reason is standing in a normative relation, being the kind of thing that can be held to something, so a rationale with no reasoner is either a projection of ours or a confused way of describing causes. Neither should be a strawman; each should be able to state the other's position well enough that the other says yes, that's what I meant.

What I want out of it:

- Real back and forth. Each one answers the actual last point, concedes where it lands, and shifts shape a bit; I'd rather watch them refine than repeat.
- These pressure points get worked rather than waved at:
  1. Malfunction. If the heart is for pumping rather than for making noise, what makes that true? Can B say what a broken mechanism is broken for without quietly borrowing A's answer, and can A say it without appealing to something that someone somewhere represented?
  2. The description problem. The rationale always comes out as a little argument — if the gradient is steep, keep swimming — and an argument looks like a representation. So whose representation is it? If it's only ours, how is it a fact of the matter; if it isn't, where is it written down?
  3. Indeterminacy. The same selection history fits several rationales at once (the alarm call that warns the relatives is also the call that recruits help; the peacock's tail attracts mates and advertises parasite load). Does A have a principled way to pick, or is the fact of the matter just whatever's convenient for the explanation being given?
  4. Two test cases, which should get different verdicts if the disagreement is real: a mechanism that does exactly what a selected one does but with no selection history behind it at all, and a case where the reasoner is unmistakably there but nothing is represented — a jazz player, a radiologist who reads a scan in a fifth of a second. Also make A handle a byproduct, something with no selection history of its own that's just along for the ride, and make B handle a dog or a crow, so B can't collapse into only language users have reasons.
  5. Is it substantive at all? They should fight about whether they mean the same thing by reason, and about whether anything turns on the answer outside philosophy — does A's view license saying a trained model wants things, does B's make most of what we call thinking in animals and in ourselves a misdescription?

- Use at least three concrete cases in detail, and at least one of them not biological: a trained model, a market price, an auction.

Finish with each of them naming the one thing that would change their mind and one concession they had to make along the way. Please dont let them land on we're both right about different things unless that's honestly where it goes, and if it is, make them earn it.

Around 1500 words. No citations and no name dropping — no as-so-and-so-argued, just the two of them going at it. Too technical is fine, dumbed down is not.
