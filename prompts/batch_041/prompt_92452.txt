For a graduate seminar on imprecise probability, I want a concrete and realistic illustration of a claim my students find impossible to believe: that a person whose uncertainty is represented by a set of probability distributions can learn something and come away with a strictly less precise opinion about a quantity than she began with. Build me such a case, subject to the following. She begins with a precise opinion about the target quantity — a single number, not an interval. Her remaining uncertainty is given by a set of distributions, each of which makes the observation conditionally independent of the target given a nuisance parameter she does not observe, and all of which agree on the unconditional probability of the observation. After she learns it, her opinion about the target is an interval that strictly contains the number she started with. Keep the setting ordinary — a clinician, an actuary, a quality-control engineer, an investor — and do not hand me an urn of unknown composition. Give me the set of distributions, the observation, the target, and the arithmetic that produces the widening, and then make the widening matter: arrange a bet that someone who evaluates options by their worst-case expected value accepts before the observation and rejects after it, with the payoffs unchanged. Finish by naming the single feature of the setup that does the work, saying what becomes of the effect if her set of distributions is replaced by any one of its members, and reconstructing how a defender of precise probabilities would argue that nothing like this can happen to a coherent agent. Answer in continuous prose — no headings, no numbered steps, no bullets, no tables, no bold text — as you would in a letter.
