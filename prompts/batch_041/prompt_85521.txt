I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy (collective action, mostly), and I've been doing a summer placement at a foundation that funds AI governance and biosecurity stuff. The program officers here have all picked up the Moloch / multipolar trap vocabulary, and I keep watching them use it wrong. You know the idea: lots of independent actors, each doing the locally sensible thing, none able to unilaterally stop, so everyone ends up in a race to the bottom nobody wants — arms races, overfishing, that whole family of cases. But half the time what they're pointing at isn't a trap at all (it's just competition, and there's a winner), or the real problem is that one actor could fix it alone and doesn't want to, or "it's a multipolar trap" is doing rhetorical work — someone wants everybody to agree not to compete, and that someone is the incumbent. And even when it is a trap, they then fund whatever intervention comes with the word "coordination" in it.

Can you write me a screening procedure they can actually run on an incoming proposal? This is a quarterly cycle, 15-20 proposals, anywhere from $50k to $2M, and they get about 20 minutes each at a screening meeting before it goes to the investment committee. They're smart people but no formal game theory, so it has to work without jargon.

What I want: a short ordered set of questions — six to eight, no more — with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "this isn't a trap, don't fund it under this heading," and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is evaluative rather than factual. Two things I care about most. First, the diagnosis has to determine the remedy class: changing the payoffs (liability, taxes, monitoring), changing the number of players, changing the horizon so defection gets punished, changing verifiability, or just moral suasion — which I suspect the procedure should be allowed to call usually weak, and I want a rule that says you can't fund a remedy that doesn't match the diagnosis. Second, an adversarial step for the cartel problem, since a coordination proposal from a well-positioned actor can be a request to stop competing, and the officers will not spot that on their own.

Then run it on three proposals: (1) a pledge campaign getting the major labs to commit to not doing certain capability-increasing work; (2) a proposal to fund a compact on biosafety standards that the labs would self-police; (3) a proposal premised on the idea that compute providers racing to cut prices is itself a trap worth intervening on. At least one of these should come out against what I'd naively want, and tell me plainly which one that is.

Keep it to a page, mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and end with what this screen can't do — in particular where the "bottom" being bad is a value question the procedure can't seperate from the facts.
