I am presenting a paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop on moral change, and what I need from you is not an essay but a question-and-answer preparation sheet. My thesis is that the two great moral revolutions I work on — the abolition of the slave trade and the extinction of the gentleman's duel — were caused neither by the discovery of new moral arguments nor by shifts in material interest, but by a change in the composition of the audience before whom honour was at stake. The argument I will put to the room runs roughly as follows. First, the arguments against both practices were fully available, and widely printed, at least a generation before either practice began to recede; the arguments alone therefore cannot explain the timing. Second, interest cannot explain it either: the economic case for and against each practice cut both ways, and the people who ended them were often acting against their own material advantage. Third, what actually changed was who was watching. As the relevant public widened from a narrow caste of peers to a broad and anonymous readership, the same old arguments stopped being answerable by the same old rejoinders, because the currency at stake — standing in the eyes of one's equals — was now being minted somewhere else. Fourth, and this is the corollary I intend to draw: a norm is hostage to its audience, so moral progress is reversible; narrow the audience again and the norm can return. Along the way I mean to deny the standard contrast between an age of honour and an age of dignity: honour did not vanish, it merely changed hands.

The room will hold about twenty people, and I know who most of them are. There is a bloc of moral rationalists and Kantian constructivists who hold that moral progress just is the slow discovery and diffusion of reasons, and who will hear my thesis as a debunking of moral reasoning itself. There are two economic historians of the materialist school, for whom talk of honour is soft-headed, and who will want to know why I am not simply redescribing the rise of the commercial bourgeoisie. There are a couple of empirically minded moral psychologists who work on shame, reputation, and third-party punishment, and who will ask what my mechanism predicts and whether a widening audience is anything more than a metaphor. There is a sociologist of norms who will press me on honour versus dignity and suspect that I have merely renamed an old story. And there is one specialist on the period who cares only about chronology, and who will point out that the duel outlived the slave trade by decades.

Give me the prep sheet. Order the objections by how much damage they would do to the paper, not by how likely they are to be asked, though tell me where the two orders come apart. Phrase each one the way its owner would phrase it, in their vocabulary, not translated into mine. For each, give me a reply that fits in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede at the opening rather than defend, and tell me which single exchange has to go well for the paper to land. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks. I don't need reading lists, citations, or a literature review; I need to survive the hour.
