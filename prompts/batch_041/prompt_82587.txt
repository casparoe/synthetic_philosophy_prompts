I have no academic training, but I have been reading my way through Victorian moral philosophy on my own, and I have gotten interested in Henry Sidgwick's involvement with psychical research, partly because it sits so oddly beside his reputation as a dry, careful utilitarian.

An essayist I generally trust, who writes long popular pieces about nineteenth-century thinkers, gave me this summary of the matter: "Sidgwick came to psychical research late and reluctantly, and mainly because he wanted the afterlife to be real. His ethical system needed a future life with moral government in order to reconcile the competing demands of self-interest and universal benevolence, and by his own admission that need was what drove him. He co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 and served as its first president, but he was a reluctant believer rather than a working researcher: he thought the evidence for telepathy was decent and the evidence for survival rather better than most people suppose, and what kept him involved was temperamental hope rather than any settled proof. His philosophical legacy on this point is really a footnote. He never integrated psychical research into his ethics, and the passages in The Methods of Ethics that gesture toward a future life are so cautious as to be nearly empty."

I would like to know how much of this I can safely repeat. Please give me your audit as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: begin with what the summary gets right, then identify what is subtly wrong or misleading and what Sidgwick or a careful defender of him would object to, and end by saying whether the closing claim — that his ethics never really took the question up — survives once the rest has been corrected.
