I am a novelist, and I have reached the stage of a book at which I must decide, sentence by sentence, whether to write that a character saw something or only that he believed, suspected, or feared it. I have come to think that this is not a stylistic choice but the question of when an experience is genuinely perceptual rather than thought wearing perceptual clothing. I have been making the call by ear, and my ear has begun to contradict itself. What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures the verdicts below, states what each clause costs, and tells me which of my verdicts I should abandon.

Some of these are scenes from the book; some are cases I have merely been turning over, which is part of the trouble.

1. A man who has not eaten since morning stands before a bakery window at dusk. The bread looks to him warmer and more saturated than it did at noon. I write 'saw.' Hunger has altered his experience, not merely his opinion of it.

2. A radiologist of thirty years' practice glances at a scan and, before any reasoning I could reconstruct, sees the shadow that means trouble; the first-year resident beside him sees only gray. I write 'saw,' and I would expect the seeing to count as some justification for his diagnosis.

3. A taster is told, falsely, that the bottle is a famous vintage; the wine now tastes rounder to him, and he says so without affectation. I write 'tasted' — the taste itself has changed, not his verdict on it.

4. My narrator's husband, newly convinced that his wife is deceiving him, finds that her ordinary cheerfulness now looks like mockery. It is not that he concludes she is mocking him; the mockery is in her face. I write 'saw,' and I believe it.

5. A man raised to fear a certain kind of stranger sees a hand moving toward a coat pocket as a threat, with the same immediacy and the same absence of inference. Here I will not write 'saw.' I would say he saw a hand and read a threat into it.

6. Two lines of equal length, one with fins turned outward and one with fins turned inward: I know they are equal and they continue to look unequal. I write 'saw' without hesitation; here knowledge cannot reach the experience.

7. The drawing that is now a duck and now a rabbit: I can make it turn over, and each way it genuinely looks different. I cannot decide whether that is perception or something I am doing.

8. A musician who has learned harmony hears a certain chord as wanting to resolve. I am not sure that wanting is the sort of thing that can be seen or heard at all, and yet it does not feel like a conclusion he draws.

Two of these are hard for me to hold together. In 4 a belief changes what a man sees, and I call the result perception; in 5 a belief changes what a man sees just as immediately, and I refuse the name. The only difference I can point to is that in 5 the reading is false and unjust — which would make 'perceptual' mean no more than 'accurate,' and my principle circular. Likewise, in 6 knowledge cannot touch the appearance, while in 4 knowledge appears to produce it, and I do not know whether that asymmetry is a fact about perception or merely a fact about my examples.

What I would like, then: first, the principle or principles that best capture these verdicts; second, the particular case each clause was built to save; third, your verdicts on at least two cases I have not given, and I would prefer cases that strain the principle to cases that flatter it; and fourth, an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether it is the principle or the verdict that should give way.

Two requests about how to answer. Do not tell me what I probably meant; tell me what my verdicts commit me to, even where that is unflattering. And if the distinction I am asking you to draw cannot be drawn — if 'perceptual' in my sense is not one thing, or if my conviction that a single line runs through these eight cases is an artifact of my having written the scenes myself — then say so plainly rather than assembling a principle that flatters my ear. I would rather be told that the question is malformed than be handed a tidy answer. Where you are supplying detail I have not given, mark it as your addition.
