My graduate seminar spent this term on tangled hierarchies and self-reference, and we ended where the strange-loop framework has always ended: in an impasse that neither side has moved in decades. I would like a diagnosis of that impasse rather than a defence or a refutation of the framework itself.

The framework's central move is this. A tangled hierarchy is a system in which one level loops back and generates or represents a level above it: Escher's Drawing Hands, the two hands each drawing the other; a sentence that speaks about its own provability; a program that prints its own code. Such a loop can be closed, on the view in question, only because it rests on a stratum that is not itself drawn into the tangle — the level of the rules, the alphabet, the numbering, the sheet of paper: the inviolate level. The claim is that a self is a loop of exactly this kind, and that this is what makes selfhood non-mysterious — the self is not a substance lodged behind the symbols but the pattern that the symbols trace out once they are sufficiently tangled.

The impasse. Three positions have hardened. The first holds that the inviolate level does genuine explanatory work: it is what lets a system represent itself without paradox, and selfhood just is that capacity, so the framework explains selfhood rather than merely redescribing it. The second holds that the inviolate level is cheap — every formal system has a stratum of uninterpreted marks, including systems with no trace of self-representation — so it is a precondition of the loop rather than a cause of anything, and the framework relocates the mystery of the self instead of dissolving it. The third holds that the whole exchange is carried by an equivocation: the word "level" in the pictorial, musical, logical and psychological cases is not one concept, and the analogy is aesthetic rather than explanatory. What I find striking is that all three camps agree on the formal facts. Nobody denies that self-referential systems exist, that they require an uninterpreted syntactic stratum, or that such strata are ubiquitous and cheap. The quarrel is not about mathematics.

Two puzzles sharpen it, and I would like them used as test cases. First, the inviolate level is said to lie outside the loop, yet the rules that generate a self are plausibly part of the person — the syntax of the brain, if you like. If the inviolate level is inside the system, the tangle is unbroken and the anti-paradox work is undone; if it is outside, the self excludes its own conditions of possibility. Second, consider a system with a clean inviolate level and no self — a self-reproducing machine, a mirror facing a mirror — and, conversely, a system that is a plausible candidate for selfhood but has no separable stratum of rules, such as a distributed connectionist network whose "rules" are nothing over and above its weights. If selfhood requires an inviolate level, the second system is disqualified; if it does not, the inviolate level is idle.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not one disagreement but two superimposed: a verbal dispute about what "level" is being used to mean, and a substantive dispute about what would count as explaining a mental phenomenon — about whether a structural analogy can explain anything, or can only redescribe what it was meant to explain. I would like that tested against the positions as I have stated them.

The deliverable is a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement this is, argued from what each side actually claims; whether some hidden formal or empirical question is doing the work; whether the question as posed is well-posed at all; and, given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a criterion for what an explanation of selfhood must accomplish — or an argument that none should be expected. Please do not survey the literature; argue to the diagnosis.
