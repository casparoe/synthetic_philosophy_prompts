I run a reading group on experimental philosophy and I'm tired of doing the Knobe effect as another session where everyone nods at the chairman case. I want to turn it into a "which explanation do you actually believe?" quiz. Can you write it?

Ten items, each a short concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The positions that need to come out as endpoints: (1) the folk concept of intentional action is itself partly evaluative, so the asymmetry is real and the concept is moralized; (2) nothing is wrong with the concept, the asymmetry is pragmatic, people hear the question as asking whether he did it knowingly or on purpose; (3) the folk are making a performance error, blame distorts the answers and the correct answer is the same in both versions; (4) blame comes first and the intentionality verdict is downstream of it, so this is really about moral judgment, not about the concept of intention; (5) the driver isn't morality at all but norm violation in general; (6) it's a vignette artifact and we shouldn't infer anything about the concept from it; (7) dual-character or pluralist, there's a descriptive core plus a normative dimension and the cases are tapping different parts. No item should just ask whether intentional action is moralized. I want the sorting to fall out of the cases.

The cases have to be answerable by someone who doesn't know the literature, so build in these and make them do real work: an agent who knows his decision will violate a purely conventional rule with no moral content (an editor knowingly breaking the papers style guide, that kind of thing) - is the effect about norms or about morality? A blameworthy act with a good side effect (a landlord evicting tenants, knowing the building will end up housing refugees) - to pull apart "morally bad side effect" from "agent deserves blame". A bad side effect produced by an entity rather than a person (a board whose cost-cutting everyone knows will raise emissions) - does the effect need an agent who can be blamed? A case where the bad side effect was only 40% likely and the agent went ahead anyway. A first-person case. And a case where the agent both knew about and actively wanted the bad side effect. Add one or two of your own in the same spirit if you need them, but nothing that only works if the reader has memorized a specific study.

Then flag any two positions your quiz can't actually separate, say which single item is load-bearing (if readers answered it the other way, what would they turn into), and note which answer patterns come out empty and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored. Finish by telling me where the scoring key is most likely to be wrong - which item you'd expect to misfire, and what would have to be true of people's answers for you to move a case from one position to another.
