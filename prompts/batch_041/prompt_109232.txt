In nine days I am presenting a paper on the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery to a mixed reading group. The centerpiece is my own variant, which I will put on the board:

- 100 players, one-shot, anonymous, no communication, no repeat play.
- Each player privately chooses ENTER or STAY OUT. Entering costs $1.
- If exactly one player enters, that player receives $1,000,000 (net $999,999).
- If two or more enter, nobody wins, and every entrant is out $1.
- If nobody enters, nothing happens.

I claim the $1 fee is doing real work: in the fee-free version, universal entering and universal abstention both pay zero, so universalizability alone does not discriminate between them; the fee breaks that tie and makes the superrational answer determinate — and, I argue, self-undermining. My thesis is that this family of puzzles does not reveal a distinct superrational faculty or a rival to expected-utility maximization, but instead reveals that expected-utility maximization is under-specified until you supply a population model: a joint distribution over how the other players reason. The Platonia intuition is the limiting case in which you are certain that every other player reasons exactly as you do; relax that limit and the answer becomes a belief-indexed threshold — I make it roughly 13% — rather than a verdict about rationality as such.

The audience is about eight people: three microeconomic theorists who treat Nash equilibrium and its refinements as the criterion of rationality and regard superrationality and team reasoning as either confused or already accounted for; one experimental economist who has spent a decade running one-shot public-goods and volunteer's-dilemma experiments with real money and cares chiefly about what subjects actually do; two philosophers of action who work on evidential and causal decision theory and Newcomb-style problems; and one mechanism designer interested only in worst-case robustness and incentive compatibility.
