Take the Anthropocene Working Group's failed attempt to get the Anthropocene ratified as a formal geological epoch -- the Canadian lake core, the plutonium spike from the 1950s bomb tests, the 2024 vote where the stratigraphers turned it down -- and run it through the lens of the stratigraphic rules the group was playing by: the golden-spike demand that a boundary be pinned to a single point in a single section and correlated globally by a marker thats isochronous (same instant everywhere), plus the older geochronology/chronostratigraphy split between time itself and the rocks that record it. Do the mapping honestly: what those rules forced the AWG into, why a mid-century plutonium signal was the right choice on stratigraphic grounds even though its a lousy answer to when human impact actually began, and then where the framework breaks -- the diachroneity objection, and the proposal to call the whole thing an 'event' rather than an epoch, which looks like a way for the discipline to absorb what it can't date. Then the who-decides half: treat the ratification machinery as a claim about who is licensed to name a period, and say whether 'event' is a real resolution or a dodge. I want it in both directions -- what the rules explain about how it went, and what the episode shows about the rules themselves. Skip the recap of the history, I know it.
