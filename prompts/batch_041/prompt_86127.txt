I'm a philosophy professor — metaethics mostly, with a stray interest in political philosophy — and I've gotten myself into an argument at our department's weekly lunch that I can't climb out of, and I've started to suspect the reason I can't climb out of it is that I don't actually know what I think. It started over tipping, of all things, and now it's spread to queueing, jaywalking, escalators, the whole business of everyday conventions. One colleague, call him the traffic cop, says these things are exactly like traffic laws: the entire point is predictability, so breaking one is wrong even when nobody is harmed and nobody notices, and it's wrong even if the convention itself is dumb and we'd all be better off with a different one. My other colleague, the etiquette lady, says that's nonsense — these are just manners, optional, purely lubricating, break them whenever the friction is worth it, no obligation at all. I think they're both wrong, but every time I try to say why I end up reciting a list of verdicts that don't fit under any single heading. So I want you to tell me what principle, or what small set of principles, my verdicts actually add up to.

Here they are. Twenty percent at a sit-down restaurant in the US after perfectly fine service: not optional, leaving nothing is stiffing the server. Not tipping at all in Japan: totally fine, maybe slightly gauche. Hitting "no tip" on the tablet at a counter-service place where I ordered standing at the register: also fine, and I don't think the barista has been wronged, even while she's watching me. Standing on the left of the escalator in a city where everyone stands right: I'm doing something wrong, even if at that particular moment I'm not blocking anybody. Jaywalking across an empty street at 7 a.m. with clear sightlines: fine. Jaywalking across that same street with my five-year-old holding my hand: not fine, and not mainly because of the risk to me. Cutting the airport security line because I'm about to miss a flight: not fine, though I might do it and apologize the whole way. Edging into the scrum at a packed bar: fine, that isn't a line, there is no line. Edging into the line at a bakery that plainly has a line: not fine. A visitor from a non-tipping country who doesn't tip in the US: wrongs the server, I think, even though he genuinely didn't know. An American in Japan who leaves cash on the table: does nothing wrong, just a bit clumsy. The local who jaywalks every day and glares at the tourist who jaywalks: he's a hypocrite, and I also think he's wrong to glare. A visitor whose home country routinely slips small payments to officials, now somewhere that practice is illegal and genuinely corrosive: still shouldn't, even where the practice is locally common. And here's the pair I can't square — I think the non-tipping visitor is bound by a convention he's never heard of, while the tipping American is not bound by a convention he's actively violating, and I can't tell whether that's a real asymmetry (in the first case a specific server is relying on the money; in the second nobody is relying on anything) or just me being an American chauvinist about restaurants.

I floated the idea that some of these conventions are load-bearing and others are merely decorative, and both colleagues rolled their eyes at me, which I take as a sign I was onto something. So: give me the principle, stated precisely enough that I can defend each clause; tell me which verdict motivates which clause; tell me what it says about a couple of cases I haven't listed; and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save. If saving all of them costs me an ugly disjunctive principle, tell me the price and let me decide, because I'd rather know than paper it over. I also want your take on the one I keep going back and forth on: a visiting colleague from abroad is coming to dinner with me and a group of grad students, and I can see he's about to leave nothing on a $200 check — do I owe the server a warning, do I owe my guest the silence, or is that just a thing that's going to happen. I realize theres alot going on here, but I'd rather have the mess sorted out than have you pretend my intuitions are tidier than they are. American English, please, and no citations — I want the argument, not the literature.
