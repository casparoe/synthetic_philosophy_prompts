I host a small podcast on the formal theory of negotiated settlement, and my guest for the next episode is a bargaining theorist who has spent her career arguing that the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, rather than Nash's, should be the default recommendation in real disputes: on her view, the parties should end up with equal fractions of the gains each of them could have secured relative to the disagreement point, so that the recommendation is pinned down by the line running from the status quo to the point at which each side receives its best feasible payoff. She is a smooth interviewee who can answer almost anything with a sentence or two about splitting the gains in equal proportion, and my audience, mostly graduate students in economics and philosophy, will notice if I let her get away with that. Please draft nine to eleven interview questions, each of which turns on the actual content of the position rather than on her training or her next project, and attach to each substantive question a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that when she falls back on the formulaic reply I have something with which to press her.

I want the set to include at least one question on each of the following: what work the ideal point — each party's best feasible payoff — is doing in the construction, and why keying the recommendation to it is not simply an arbitrary anchor; the trade-off between Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives and the monotonicity requirement that characterizes her own solution, namely that a party should not end up worse off when its own best-case payoff rises while the other party's stays fixed, together with her reason for thinking that violating the former is the lesser sin; what becomes of the construction once there are three or more parties, where I gather the clean two-person characterization does not carry over intact and some further convention has to do the work; whether the solution can be given a noncooperative foundation of the kind Nash's enjoys, and whether the absence of a canonical strategic story is a genuine cost or a red herring; and whether the rule can be gamed in mediation, given that a party can move the recommendation in its favor by inflating its own best-case payoff or by concealing options that would raise its counterpart's. I would also like two questions that put normative pressure on the position from outside bargaining theory — one from someone who thinks a fair division should equalize final payoffs rather than gains, and one about cases in which the two sides' best-case payoffs differ for reasons that look morally arbitrary — plus a closing question about what evidence, empirical or conceptual, would count against the rule rather than merely in favor of some rival to it. Please write everything in American English.
