I'm a freelance journalist — science and ideas, mostly — and I've spent the last few months reporting on how people model knowledge inside AI and multi-agent systems. Logicians, a couple of computer scientists who do protocol verification, an epistemologist or two. And I keep walking into the same fight, which apparently has been going on since Hintikka put knowledge into possible-worlds semantics in 1962, and nobody has moved an inch since.

Here's the setup as I understand it. Represent 'the agent knows that p' as: p holds in every world the agent hasn't ruled out. Do that and you get an agent that knows every logical truth and every consequence of what it knows. Know p, know that p implies q, you know q — even if the derivation would take a million years. Which means an agent who knows the axioms of arithmetic already knew Fermat's last theorem before Wiles proved it, and knows every theorem nobody has proved yet. That's the logical omniscience thing.

Now the camps, roughly. One group says this isn't a bug, it's the point: this is a logic of ideally rational belief, not a psychology of human heads, and if you wanted a theory of what's actually in someone's head you're doing a seperate subject. A second group tries to patch it — impossible worlds where logic itself can fail, awareness sets, or dropping the bare K and talking about the proof or evidence the agent actually possesses. And here's the part that fascinates me: every patch seems to catch the disease one level up. The awareness agent knows exactly what it's unaware of. The impossible-worlds fix makes consequence non-classical, and the CS people say that wrecks the proofs they need. The proof-term approach turns knowing into standing in a relation to a derivation, and the epistemologists say that just isn't believing. A third group says forget patching: the real defect is individuating contents as sets of worlds at all — same defect that makes logically equivalent propositions indistinguishable — so no patch inside the framework can work.

What I need from you is a diagnosis, not a survey, because I have to write this and right now I can't tell whether I'm looking at one dispute or three wearing one name. Is this substantive, or is it people arguing about different questions under a shared label — a normative logic of ideal rationality, an engineering problem for people who need working models, and a descriptive theory of actual belief? Is some hidden formal result doing the work here (someone told me there's a proof that you can't even represent an agent being unaware of something in a standard state-space model, that you have to change the model, not add a patch — is that the kind of thing that settles anything)? Is the whole thing ill-posed — is the demand that one formalism do all three jobs just incoherent? My hunch, and tell me if it's too neat, is that the camps dont share a criterion of success, so what counts as a fix for one is a non-starter for another, and that's why it looks stalled rather than hard.

One thing that keeps me from just writing it off: the same machinery nails the muddy-children puzzle and coordinated-attack problems in distributed systems. It's not a broken tool. So: what kind of disagreement is this actually, and given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a theorem, a new framework, splitting the question, or honestly nothing?
