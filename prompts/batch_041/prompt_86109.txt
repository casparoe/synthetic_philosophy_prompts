Compare epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness, but decide the comparison with a case rather than by listing the standard pros and cons. Heres the case: a chocolate trade association publishes a rule saying a bar counts as "dark" only if it's at least 60% cocoa solids. Before the rule everyone agreed 85% is dark and 40% isn't, and the 50–70% range was exactly where people argued. After the rule the arguing stops. For each theory: was "dark" vague before the rule, and is it vague after? Was there a fact of the matter about the borderline bars before — and is there one now? Did the association discover a boundary or make one, and can each theory even register the difference? What happens to a 59.9% bar and a 60.1% bar under each view, and does "dark" now mean the same thing in the trade statistics as it does in ordinary talk (the supervaluationist seems stuck saying the rule picked a precisification without making it correct — does that survive)? Finally, what does the case do to the worst problem each view has: the epistemicist's unknowable-but-real cutoff and his trouble with higher-order vagueness, versus the supervaluationist needing a determinate set of admissible precisifications for "admissible" itself. Wrap up by saying which view handles the case less badly. No citations needed, just argue it.
