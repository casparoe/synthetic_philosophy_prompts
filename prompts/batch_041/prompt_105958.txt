Last week I lost the better part of an evening to an argument with a friend about George Berkeley, and we reached a standoff that I would like you to adjudicate. The quarrel began in a graduate seminar on the history of monetary thought, where I have been reading The Querist — the three parts Berkeley published in Dublin in the 1730s — and spilled over into dinner. My friend holds that Berkeley was a proto-Chartalist, indeed a Modern Monetary Theorist before the letter: he points to queries such as "Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating?" and to Berkeley's suggestion that the true idea of money, as such, is that of a ticket or counter, and infers that Berkeley would have been entirely at home with fiat currency, with a central bank, and even with a fully programmable digital currency, and would have regarded the abandonment of the gold standard as a non-event. I think that reading is too thin. The ticket-or-counter doctrine was in Berkeley's hands a claim about what money is for, and what it is for is stirring up industry: he asks at one point whether any other means, equally conducing to excite and circulate the industry of mankind, may not be as useful as money, and he drew up a plan for a national bank for Ireland largely because he thought Irish industry was starved of circulating credit. We could not settle whether that commitment makes him a natural ally or a natural critic of modern monetary arrangements, and we ended the evening shouting past each other about Bitcoin.

To force the question, we sketched a proposal concrete enough to argue about: a retail central bank digital currency for Ireland — call it the Circulating Euro — issued by the Central Bank, programmable, bearing a small demurrage of roughly half a percent per month on balances left idle for more than a month, with the proceeds rebated to residents as a per-capita dividend; licensed banks permitted to issue tokenized claims redeemable one-for-one against it; and the issuer retaining the power to freeze or restrict particular wallets. Assume for the sake of argument that the technology works and that the scheme is not a thought experiment.

What I want from you is a prediction, as specific as the evidence allows, of what Berkeley's position on this scheme would have been. Separate it into the parts my friend and I could not separate: (i) the absence of any metallic backing; (ii) demurrage as a device to speed circulation; (iii) programmability, and the issuer's power to direct or forbid particular uses; (iv) private and non-sovereign monies, of which our era's cryptocurrencies would be the salient example; (v) whether a small country like Ireland should keep monetary sovereignty of its own or pool it, given that Berkeley wanted a national bank for Ireland; and (vi) whether the issuing authority ought to be accountable to the public or insulated from it. For each part give a verdict, a rough confidence, and the consideration that does the work. Be explicit about which parts of your prediction are genuinely constrained by the text and which are extrapolation from his broader temper, since I suspect my friend and I are each smuggling in a modern figure we happen to find congenial. Then tell me which single feature of the scheme would most likely have decided him, which of the two of us is more likely to be wrong and about what, and where the whole exercise breaks down because Berkeley had no basis at all on which to form a view.
