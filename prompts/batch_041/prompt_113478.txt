I sit on the release review group at a mid-size AI lab. We ship models. We're not the frontier lab, we're probably six months behind whoever is, but we're not nobody either. Our job is to sign off or not before a model goes out the door, and right now the process is basically vibes plus whoever in the room is the most stubborn. I want an actual procedure we can run.

Here's whats eating me. Two things.

First, the alignment tax. Doing the safety work costs something. Evals, red teaming, holding back a feature, shipping a smaller version, delaying a launch by a quarter, paying for the interpretability people. The tax isn't only dollars, it's capability and time, and some of it you pay once and some of it you pay every single release.

Second, the competitiveness constraint. We're not alone out there. If we eat a cost nobody else eats, its not obvious the world gets any safer. It might just mean the same capability shows up four months later from someone who skipped the eval, and we've given up our seat at the table for nothing. But the same fact cuts the other way: sometimes the one who pays first is the reason the next lab pays at all, and sometimes the alternative to us shipping something isnt that it doesnt ship, it's that a worse version ships.

What I want is a decision procedure for a single release, written so someone with no background in this stuff can run it at a table. Questions in order. What counts as a good enough answer to each one. Where it stops with a no. Where it stops with a yes. And where it has to get kicked up to a human because the question is about values and not facts. Please dont let it collapse into "when in doubt, ship" or "when in doubt, hold" — I need to see the actual hinge, the thing that flips the answer.

Then run it on three cases. (a) A two-month delay to finish an eval that has maybe a 30 percent chance of turning up something real, against a competitor launching next month. (b) A feature thats genuinely useful and that at least two competitors already ship in some form, but our own red team says it roughly doubles misuse risk in one narrow area. (c) A case where the procedure tells us to hold, and the cost of holding is that a less careful lab takes the market and the users.

At least one of those three should come out in a way that makes me uncomfortable, and I want you to say straight up which parts of the procedure are just a judgment call in a suit, and what the thing cant decide at all.
