A colleague in my reading group keeps pressing an argument that I can neither fault nor accept, and I would like a diagnosis of it. Here it is, in her reconstruction:

(1) I can know plain perceptual facts — that the table in front of me is red, that the kettle is boiling — just by looking and listening.
(2) I can know, by introspection, how things presently appear: that the table looks red to me, that the kettle sounds to me as though it is boiling.
(3) Knowledge is closed under known entailment: if I know that P, and know that P entails Q, I can come to know that Q.
(4) So when the table looks red and is red, I can know the conjunction — that it looks red and is red. The same holds for the kettle, and for any other case I take the trouble to check.
(5) Each such conjunction is a case of my eyes or ears returning a true verdict, and so is exactly the kind of datum that shows those faculties to be reliable.
(6) By ordinary induction over enough of these data, each of them independently known, I can therefore come to know that my eyes and ears are reliable.
(7) But every step of that accumulation drew on the very faculties whose reliability was at issue: it was my eyes that told me the table was red, my ears that told me the kettle was boiling.
(8) If (6) is sound, then I can establish the reliability of my own faculties without any independent evidence, and the skeptic's challenge is answered for free. That is too cheap to be right.
(9) To block it, we need a principle: no faculty may be used to confirm its own reliability.
(10) But there is no independent perch from which to certify the senses; all perceptual knowledge of the external world leans, at bottom, on the deliverances of those same senses.
(11) So the principle in (9), applied consistently, would strip away all perceptual knowledge of the external world.
(12) Hence either knowledge of my own reliability comes absurdly cheap, or there is no perceptual knowledge at all.

I would most like to keep (1), (2), and (8); I would sooner part with almost anything else than with the thought that self-certification is too cheap. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs, including where else the cost lands. I cannot tell whether the argument is airtight and the price must simply be paid somewhere, or whether a single step is doing illicit work.

Write the diagnosis in prose, then give me a short table with these columns:

- Step: the number.
- Status: keep, reject, or amend.
- Why it looked innocent: the reading or intuition that makes the step seem safe.
- Cost of rejecting or amending it: what has to be given up.
- Where the cost lands: which other claims, in this argument or elsewhere, must change to pay for it.

Fill in only the steps you take to be genuinely in play — two to four rows, not all twelve — and put a one-sentence verdict above the table naming the step you would actually give up.
