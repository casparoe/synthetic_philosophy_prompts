I run a small regional art museum in a town of about 40,000, and next month I have to give the opening remarks at an exhibition we're calling "Demoted." It's just the things in our collection that we displayed for years as the real thing and that turned out not to be: a portrait that hung in the front hall for sixty years and is now attributed to an unknown hand; a bronze that's a nineteenth-century cast off an antique; one object that was an actual forgery, made to fool a dealer; and one that got relabeled by accident in the 1950s and nobody noticed. The room will be our big donors, most of whom gave money for what they believed were originals and who are going to hear this as me telling them they wasted their money; our two curators, who've read all the philosophy-of-forgery stuff and will be listening for whether I actually say anything; and about eighty locals who just like the museum and dont know any of this.

Here's what I want the speech to do. It has to take a real position on what, if anything, was lost when these objects got demoted — not a survey of the views, an actual verdict with reasons that I can stand behind. And it has to deal with the thing that's really bugging me: in the same room we're showing a plaster cast of a famous sculpture that everyone has always known is a cast and that people love anyway, plus a case of Victorian replicas nobody has ever minded. So if a known copy is fine, why does the demoted one hurt? Whatever you say has to make that asymmetry make sense, or else say plainly that it doesn't and that we should get over it. Keep the forgery (someone lied), the misattribution (nobody lied, we were just wrong) and the relabeling accident distinct — I suspect they aren't the same loss and I want your view on whether that's right.

Don't reach for the aura line unless you can say what aura is without it just being a name for the thing we're trying to explain. No citations or reading lists, I can't check them and neither can you. Seven or eight minutes spoken, and please don't make the donors feel like idiots.
