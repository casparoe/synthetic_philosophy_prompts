I'm putting together a reading group on the Ellsberg paradox and I want to kick it off with a tier list, partly so I can see where everyone's priors sit before we start arguing.

Quick reminder of the setup so we're all on the same page (I know you know it): urn, 90 balls, 30 are red, the other 60 are some mix of black and yellow and you don't know the mix. Most people prefer a bet on red to a bet on black, but then also prefer a bet on black-or-yellow to a bet on red-or-yellow. That pair of choices violates the sure-thing principle, and everyone calls the pattern "ambiguity aversion."

Here's the ask. Take the list of candidate explanations below and sort them into tiers (S/A/B/C/D, or just an ordered list with scores out of 10) by how much explanatory work each one actually does. Two dimensions I care about: (a) does it account for the original choice pattern, and (b) does it also predict the *reversals* -- the fact that people flip to seeking ambiguity in the loss domain, or when the ambiguous option is the one framed as safer. An explanation that only handles the gain-domain pattern should get marked down for that. A sentence or two per item on why it lands where it does.

A few of these are standard moves and a couple are things I half-invented last night, so don't worry about whether you've heard of them or who said them first -- judge them on the merits. And flag any two that are secretly the same idea wearing different hats.

1. Worst-case evaluation: treat the unknown black/yellow split as adversarial and score each bet by its minimum expected payoff over all admissible splits.
2. Second-order risk aversion: you do have a subjective prior over the possible splits, but you dislike the spread of expected utilities across those priors -- you're averse to the variance of the expected values, not to the missing information as such.
3. Regret: score each act by its worst-case regret, and the ambiguous act is the one with a state where you'd kick yourself hardest.
4. Comparative ignorance: what you dislike is being visibly less informed than a salient comparison class (another person, your future self, the experimenter), so the effect should shrink when everyone is equally in the dark.
5. Competence: you just prefer betting on things you feel you understand; it's about self-perceived expertise rather than probabilty, so it should track domain familiarity (sports fans on sports, and so on).
6. Incomplete state space: the real issue is that you can't be sure you've listed all the possibilities, so you suspect there's a state you haven't thought of in which the ambiguous bet is terrible.
7. Adversarial source: the ambiguity gets attributed to an agent who might be working against you, so the worst case gets privileged -- predicts ambiguity *seeking* when the opaque source is known to be benevolent or purely random.
8. Nothing new: compound-lottery risk aversion plus some non-linear probability weighting already reproduces the choices, so "ambiguity aversion" is a label with no independent content.
9. The audit view (my coinage): what bothers you is that the ambiguous bet can never be checked after the fact -- you can't confirm the draw was fair -- so you avoid it to keep the feeling of a fair process, and you'd pay to avoid ambiguity even at a cost in expected value as long as the loss of checkability is seperate and salient.

Two extra things, keep them brief: is there any item on the list that, if true, would mean ambiguity aversion isn't a distinct phenomenon at all but just ordinary risk aversion or probability weighting in disguise? And which single item, if you had to bet the reading group's whole session on it, would you trust most to survive an actual experiment?
