I'm a third-year PhD student and I'm about to send my supervisor the chapter of my thesis that sets out my line on transcendental arguments, since everything else in the thesis will lean on it. Before I do, I want a consistency audit, because I suspect the position has grown by accretion rather than by design and I can no longer see its joints. Here is where I've landed. (i) The right answer to the external-world sceptic is transcendental rather than evidential: the doubt is to be shown idle rather than answered, on the grounds that the practice of doubting already commits the doubter to a world of objects that are there whether or not anyone is registering them. (ii) I accept Stroud's point that a transcendental argument can show at most that we must believe something — that a belief is indispensable to thinking as we do — and never that the belief is true; indispensability is not truth, and I won't pretend otherwise. (iii) I want the same strategy to carry the weight against the sceptic about other minds: because the concept of a subject of experience is one we can possess only by applying it to others, the denial of other minds is not a coherent option for anyone who has the concept at all. (iv) I'm a realist about other minds, and a fairly robust one: whether some creature is having an experience is a fact about that creature, independent of what we need to believe about it, and an experience is essentially a state with a qualitative character, present to its subject in a way that no third-personal description captures. (v) I have no time for the verification principle and none for idealism of any stripe, transcendental or otherwise; I don't think you can read the furniture of the world off the requirements of our thinking. (vi) The engine of all of this, as I see it, is that a subject's representations must be self-ascribable: the necessity in play is always a condition on the experience of a self-conscious subject, never a necessity discerned in things themselves. I also read Kant's own deduction as reaching a conclusion about the world only by leaning on transcendental idealism as a bridge, which is precisely the move I am refusing.

My suspicion is that I have been quietly helping myself to something, and I would like to know what. What I want from you is an audit rather than a survey. Tell me which of (i)–(vi) genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do; order the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as so many equally bad spots; and for each one say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me. Be specific about the mechanism of each conflict — I want to see which commitment is doing the work that makes the other untenable, not just a list of pairs that sound uneasy together.

Then take a definite view. I would much rather be told which single commitment to drop, and why that one rather than the others, than be handed a menu of options to choose from. If the package can be rescued by one cheap revision that leaves the rest standing, say what it is and what it leaves you unable to claim; if it cannot, say so plainly and tell me which of my commitments I am going to have to learn to live without.
