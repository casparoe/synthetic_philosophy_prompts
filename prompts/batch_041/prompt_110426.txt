I'm in a slow reading group that's been crawling through Plato's Laws all term (three pages a week, it's brutal), and I have to open the last session, which lands on the same night as our department's end-of-term dinner, so instead of a summary I said I'd bring a poem. The part I want it about is early on, where the Athenian is arguing with a Spartan and a Cretan about drinking, and instead of just agreeing that wine is a menace he does this strange, lovely thing: he says wine is a drug the lawgiver should learn to use, because a man who's been drinking loses his shame and gets confident, and you finally get to see what he's actually made of — a test rather than a temptation, and a safer way of finding out than lending that man money. He adds that for a lot of old men the problem isn't vice but stiffness: age has too much fear in it, and the drink works like a remedy for the sourness that sets in with the years, making them soft and childlike again and willing to sing. None of it works unless the party is run properly, though — rules, and one sober person in charge who knows what he's doing — because otherwise it's just a mob, which is the thing the whole discussion is frightened of. The end he's after is friendship, and the habit of liking and disliking the right things, which he thinks is basically what education is. Two of us got into it over whether the test can even work — she said a drug that strips shame only shows you a drunk, he said the shame is the mask, so stripping it is the whole point — and I don't want the poem to settle that. I want it to hold both.

So: a poem out of all that, somewhere around 24-30 lines, whatever form earns its keep, but with two voices you can tell apart by ear and not by label. The drinker's lines should loosen as it goes — longer, slantier, thoughts that wander off and come back — while the sober one's stay short and exact and end-stopped, and if it helps, give him a refrain that returns word for word each time he speaks, so that by the last time you hear it, it isn't quite the same sentence anymore. End on him, not on the drinker, so that somebody who's never heard any of this can feel why Plato wants a sober man at the table. It has to be accurate enough that the guy in our group who actually reads the Greek wont wince, and it shouldn't sound like a lecture wearing a toga — its getting read out loud, before the wine comes out, so it can be funny and warm, but it should land the real argument. Please don't put any scholars' names or books in it, and don't quote the text, because I can't check a quotation and I refuse to be the guy who misquotes Plato at a dinner. After the poem, on its own line, one plain sentence starting with 'In one sentence:' saying what the drinking party is actually for in Plato's eyes — that goes on the handout for the people who slip out early.
