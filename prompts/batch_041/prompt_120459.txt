I'm in my third year and taking a seminar this term on the philosophy of work and leisure, and the final assignment asks us to pair a work of art with the arguments we've read rather than with more arguments. I've chosen Chaplin's Modern Times, largely because the unit on working time has been the most unsettling thing in the course: Weber on the Protestant ethic and its afterlife, where a religious duty to labour outlives the religion that gave it point and hardens into a compulsion nobody can justify; the case for shorter hours and a right to leisure (the French 35-hour week, the newer right-to-disconnect rules, and the older and stranger claim, from Pieper, that leisure is not the absence of work but the basis of culture, so that a society which treats all of its time as available for productive use destroys the thing that makes productive life worth having); and the pathologies at the far end, karoshi and karo-jisatsu — death, and suicide, from overwork. My flatmate says Modern Times is just Marx with pratfalls and that the paper is a waste of time; I think the film is more interesting than that, but I can't yet say why, and that's what I need from you.

What I want is a reading that takes the film and the theory equally seriously and doesn't reduce either to the other. The obvious reading — the assembly line as estrangement, the worker as a cog — I can write in my sleep. What I keep noticing instead is that the film's miseries are not mainly overwork but idleness. The Tramp is laid off and spends much of the middle of the film scheming to get back into prison, where at least he is fed; the gamin is eager for work badly enough to take a job dancing in a café; the couple's only real pleasure is stolen — the department store they skate through at night, the shack they squat in. So two questions. First, if forced idleness is portrayed as worse than the factory, what does that do to the thought that leisure is the good that work steals from us? Second, is the film making a distributive point — that under these conditions leisure is not granted but taken, and that the same man is first worked past endurance and then discarded, so that who gets work and who gets leisure is settled by something other than a right?

Third, the karoshi end of the spectrum: the film was made decades before anyone had a word for death by overwork, yet the feeding machine, the speed-up and the breakdown among the gears are all there, and the device meant to save labour intensifies it instead. What does the film see about overwork that the theory I've been reading does not? And what am I to make of the ending — the gamin's despair, the Tramp's reassurance, the two of them walking off down the road? Is that the Protestant ethic's endless striving, or something warmer and less ascetic? Please be concrete about scenes, since I'll be screening clips in seminar; tell me where the film resists the framing I've been handed as well as where it fits; and if my two observations are wrong or only half right, say so plainly.
