For thirty years I did experimental work in a physics laboratory; I retired, took up philosophy, and for two years I have attended a weekly seminar at which graduate students and staff present work in progress that uses physics to reach conclusions about the world. For six months I have been the seminar's designated physicist, expected to say whether the physics is being used fairly — in front of the author, with more weight attached to my remarks than they deserve. The convenor has asked me to write out my method so that it can be circulated, and I have grown tired of improvising it. I do not want a reading list or a survey of the literature; I want something I can run.

The case I keep meeting is an argument I am told is standard. Its author begins from the relativity of simultaneity: two observers in uniform relative motion — a pedestrian passing another — do not agree about which distant events are happening at the same time as their own here and now, and neither is mistaken; for distant events the disagreement is large even when the relative speed is trivial. The author then reasons that no observer's present is privileged, that an event lying in some observer's present is real, and that since an observer can always be found for whom any given event is present, every event in spacetime is real — the future included, with the passage of time demoted to something about us rather than about the world. I follow the physics and do not accept the conclusion, but when I try to say where the reasoning breaks I repeat the same two or three objections, and I cannot tell whether they are good objections or merely the noise of my own taste. Nor can I see what in the argument treats the past and the future differently: that the past is real troubles nobody, and it is the future that is supposed to be the scandal. I have heard the position called the block universe.

Please write me a decision procedure for arguments of this shape: the tests in the order I should apply them, what counts as passing or failing each, where the procedure should reject an argument outright, and where it should stop and tell me that what remains is a choice rather than a result, so that I can say so plainly instead of implying that physics has settled it. It must be usable by someone who has not read the technical literature on the foundations of physics, it must not require me to adjudicate between interpretations I do not understand, I will have half an hour to prepare, and I must be able to defend every step of it to the author from a single sheet of paper.

Several distinctions strike me as important, and I do not know which the procedure must preserve and which it can drop: between what exists and what happens; between an event being real for an observer and its being real; between the claim that no observer's present is privileged and the claim that there is no present; and between a claim that is a theorem of the theory and a claim that is a gloss on it.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the argument above; a paper arguing the opposite, that relativity is compatible with a single objective present and that eternalists have merely read a convention into the theory; and a third case of your choosing in which every test is passed by an argument whose conclusion I will dislike — I want to see whether the procedure obliges me to accept that conclusion or only obliges me to abandon the objection I have been making. End by telling me where the procedure will misfire, passing arguments it should not and rejecting arguments it should not.
