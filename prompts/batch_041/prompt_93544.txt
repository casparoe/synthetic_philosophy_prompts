I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, working on just war and military ethics, and I serve as research adviser to the National Service Board of a small European state whose defence rests on a long tradition of armed neutrality of the kind historically associated with Switzerland, Sweden, and Finland. The Board adjudicates claims for exemption from compulsory military service and for transfer to alternative service. I am drafting the memorandum for a claim due next month, and I want to reason from the Board's own record rather than from first principles.

The Board's settled practice, as I reconstruct it, runs as follows. (1) A member of one of the historic peace churches, who objected to all war and to every role within the armed forces, was exempted and assigned to a purely civilian alternative service of equal duration. (2) A secular humanist who objected to all war on ethical grounds was exempted on the same terms, the Board holding that the ground of the objection is irrelevant so long as it is sincere. (3) A claimant who objected only to the state's contribution to an overseas peace-enforcement mission, while declaring himself ready to defend the homeland and to serve in the militia, was refused. (4) A claimant who objected to any role that could involve bearing arms but was willing to serve in the armed forces' medical service was refused. (5) A claimant who objected to all war and to all military roles was granted exemption although the only alternative then on offer was a civil-protection corps legally part of the armed forces and under military command; the Board accepted his refusal to join it and found him a placement with a civilian agency.

The claim now before us concerns a man who objects to all war, on grounds indistinguishable from those accepted in (2). He is, however, willing to serve within the armed forces in a wholly non-combatant capacity, having asked for assignment to signals intelligence and to the cyber-defence branch; he is also willing to serve in the military-controlled civil-protection corps mentioned in (5). He is a secular humanist and is evidently sincere. The Board must decide whether he is exempt and, if he is, whether it may require the alternative service or an exemption levy in lieu.

I should like the following. First, the candidate rules that would each fit the Board's five decisions equally well — I can see at least three — and what each predicts for this claimant. Secondly, your best prediction of the Board's verdict, with your reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals. Thirdly, the further case, or pair of cases, that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules from one another, and which way each would have to come out. Fourthly, if the record genuinely underdetermines the matter, what principle the Board would be adopting by granting the exemption and what principle by refusing it. Please state how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the point about which you are least sure. Write in British English.
