I've been reading David Lewis's short piece asking whether we already believe in penal substitution, and I'm stuck on the sports examples. In hockey, when a team gets a bench minor for too many men on the ice, the coach can send any player to the box -- sometimes a guy who was sitting on the bench and had nothing to do with it. The team plays short for two minutes. In soccer, a red card to one player means the whole team plays a man down, and the player who committed the foul is the one sent off, but the punishment is borne by everyone. And in corporate law, a company pays a fine for a crime committed by one employee; the shareholders who knew nothing about it take the hit.

What philosphical commitments does this cluster of practices pre-suppose? Does it presuppose that the team or the corporation is the real agent, so that the penalty is really being applied to the entity that did wrong, and the individual is just the instrument? Or does it presuppose that penalties can be transferred from the guilty to the innocent, so that the substitute really does bear the punishment? Or is it just a pragmatic rule that has nothing to do with desert -- we penalize the team because that's the only way to deter the team? I want to know which of these the practices actually need, and which they merely fit with. And if we replaced the presupposed view with a strict individual-desert view -- only the guilty can be punished -- what would have to change in how these sports and legal practices work? Would hockey have to send the actual offender to the box even if he's the goalie? Would corporate fines have to be replaced by individual fines on the employees who did the crime?
