hey — I'm a phd student in econ (technically history/philosophy of econ but I sit in the econ dept) and I'm drafting a chapter arguing that the revealed-preference turn in demand theory quietly changed what "utility" is supposed to *mean*, and that the change is doing more work than anyone admits. I'd really like comments, especially on whether the core argument is sound or whether I'm just redescribing something everyone already knows and dressing it up as a problem.

Here's the gist (rough, ignore the prose):

The standard defense of revealed preference is methodological — talk about mental states is unscientific, so let's just define preference as whatever is recoverable from choice under stable conditions. Fine. But the inference "chose x over y, so x is at least as good as y, for her" silently assumes the chooser is choosing in the way the model says she is, i.e. that her choice is an expression of her ranking. My claim is that there's a class of cases where the choice is better described as an expression of the ranking she wants to *have*, or wants to be seen to have, and these cases aren't exotic.

The example I keep using: a colleague is a committed environmentalist, and in a two-option setup (fly vs take the train, same cost to her, train is nine hours slower) she takes the train. Standard reading: she prefers the train, or the environmental outcome, to the extra nine hours. But she tells me she hates the train, hates the nine hours, and would privately rather fly — she takes the train because taking the train is what someone like her does, and she wants to keep being someone like her. I want to say her choice doesn't reveal a preference over outcomes; it reveals a preference over *which kind of agent she is*, or over the act of choosing itself. So the choice data underdetermines the ranking, and the usual move — just fold the environmentalism into the utility function — relabels the problem rather than solving it.

Two things I want from you:

1. Is this a real objection, or is it just the old point that preferences are over acts rather than outcomes, dressed up? I know the act/outcome distinction is standard. What I want to know is whether my case adds anything that distinction can't already absorb, or whether I'm conflating "preference over acts" with "preference over identity" in a way that collapses under pressure.

2. If it's real, what's the cleanest way to state a test that seperates the two readings? I want something like: a choice is identity-expressive rather than preference-revealing when the chooser would reverse it under a change in description that leaves the outcomes fixed (if the train were unmarked, if no one could ever know). But is that a principled test, or does it just beg the question by importing a notion of the "real" preference that the theory was explicitly supposed to do without?

Also — am I wrong that the ordinalist move was partly about exactly this, banishing the introspection that would let you ask "but does she *really* want it"? I don't want to overclaim the history, so tell me if I'm caricaturing it.

Anything you've got: objections, a better example, or where the argument definately breaks.
