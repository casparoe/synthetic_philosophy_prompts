At a city council hearing on our mosquito-control program, the health department's entomologist ended every exchange with the same sentence: 'the dose makes the poison.' The program sprays an adulticide from trucks at night and applies a larvicide to catch basins and storm drains. Residents had asked about neurological effects in children, about what the drift does to bees and to the fish in the creek the runoff reaches, and about what ten years of repeated seasonal exposure does to people who live near the treated blocks. Each time the answer was the same: application rates sit below the level at which any adverse effect has been observed, so nothing being sprayed is, at these concentrations, a poison; the presence of a chemical is not the same as its toxicity.

I want to know what that sentence could actually be claiming, set out as a menu I can bring back to the next hearing, with a verdict on each item and an explicit note on whether it would support the conclusion the entomologist drew. Read as a definitional remark, that a poison is simply a substance that harms at some dose and so names a relation rather than a kind, the slogan appears to say nothing and settle nothing, which would leave his argument with no premises at all. Read as an empirical claim that harm always increases with dose, it looks contested by the effects that show up at low doses but not at high ones and by developmental exposures whose consequences surface decades later. Read as a claim that thresholds exist, it is the reading that would do the work he needs, since the entire defense rests on there being a concentration below which no relevant effect occurs; I want to know for which endpoints that is established, for which it is merely assumed, and whether the answer differs for effects on individual organisms and on populations. There is also a version that seems to cut the other way: if everything is poisonous at some dose, then a substance's being harmless at the dose applied tells us less than it seems about the dose applied next season, in mixture with something else, to a different population. A neighbor who keeps bees adds a further wrinkle, that the dose that matters for resistance is the one that kills only part of a generation, which is a claim about a population across seasons rather than about an organism. Please also say what dose should mean under each reading: what is released, what a person absorbs, what reaches a receptor, or what a watershed receives over a summer.

Order the readings from the emptiest to the most radical, mark each as platitude, live hypothesis, or falsehood, and say which one the entomologist most likely meant and whether that one is true. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, numbered list, bold, or tables, the way you would write a long letter. No citations or reading lists; I can look up names myself if I need them.
