For the better part of fifteen years I have been a regular reader of rationalist blogs and their comment sections, and the argument I keep returning to is the one between epistemic contextualism and interest-relative invariantism, the position usually filed under pragmatic encroachment. On the surface the two are allies: both deny that knowledge is a matter of evidence and reliability alone, and both insist that the practical situation does something to knowledge. But they put the action in different places — the attributor's conversational context on one side, the subject's own stakes on the other — and I have never seen the difference pressed hard enough in a single concrete case to make the disagreement actually bite. I would like you to write the dialogue that does it.

Set it between two people, call them Vera and Tomas, who have been arguing about this for years in a forum's comments and have finally met in person. Vera is a contextualist; Tomas is an interest-relative invariantist. Neither is a fool and neither is a strawman: each should give the strongest version of their own view, each should answer the other's best objection rather than talk past it, and each should be candid about what evidence or argument would change their mind. Let them be the kind of people who care about tracking the truth of the matter rather than about which ascription sounds natural. Do not let them simply rehearse the familiar bank and lottery cases; the following case has to carry the weight.

Here is the case I want them to fight over. Dana is an experienced night-shift nurse on a general ward. At 3 a.m. she has one set of vitals — a lactate reading slightly above the reference range, a heart rate in the high-normal band, and a patient who looks well — with no earlier readings to give her a trend. Her practical situation is peculiar: on this ward any escalation triggers a mandatory re-check in one hour regardless, so a missed deterioration is almost always caught by the safety net; escalating now, by contrast, means waking the on-call physician and finishing a stack of paperwork before her shift ends, while she still has four other patients and twenty minutes left in her rotation. Dana forms the belief that the patient is stable and does not escalate. The patient deteriorates overnight and is caught by the re-check. The next morning Priya, an administrator responsible for ward safety and answerable to a review board, reads the chart and says, "The nurse didn't know the patient was stable."

Two further wrinkles. First, at the moment Dana formed her belief she did not know the re-check protocol applied to this patient; she had just been told the policy was under revision and believed escalation was the only safety net. Second, suppose Priya, once she learns of the safety net, revises herself and says, "Well, then she knew — it was a low-stakes situation for her."

I want the dialogue to work through this case, and in particular to isolate where the two views predict different things: what Vera says about the truth of Dana's own ascription at 3 a.m. versus the truth of Priya's ascription the next morning; what Tomas says about whether Dana knew, and whether the answer turns on Dana's actual stakes or on the stakes as Dana took them to be; whether Priya's reversal is best explained by a shift in her context or by a fact about Dana that was always there; and what each view must say about the cost of error as against the cost of further inquiry, since here the error is cheap only because the inquiry was made cheap. Have them identify the case that would most cleanly separate the two views and say what each predicts about it. It is fine if they do not fully converge, so long as the residual disagreement is stated precisely and neither one is allowed to win by caricature.
