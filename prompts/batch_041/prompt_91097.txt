I was recently selected by lot to serve on a citizens' assembly on a local transit issue, and the experience has convinced me that sortition, rule by lot, is one of those ideas that cannot be taught well from the outside. I teach a 100-minute seminar in democratic theory to 18 upper-level undergraduates, and I want to use one session to make them feel the force of lottocracy rather than simply hear the arguments for it. Design a runnable in-class activity for that session.

The activity should be a game or structured deliberation in which the rules, incentives, and roles do the work. I have a room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, a hat, and a random number generator; no real money. Participants should not need to have read anything in advance. By the end they should have felt, in their own choices, the pull of the idea that a body selected by lot can be legitimate, and the specific costs of that legitimacy. They should not hear the words sortition or lottocracy until the debrief.

I want the design to put pressure on three distinctions, which I name so you can target them. First, the break that the draw creates: the moment when no one can claim to have earned or been denied a seat. Second, descriptive representation versus deliberative capacity: a sample can look like the population without being able to deliberate well, and a body can deliberate well without looking like the population. Third, accountability without elections: if no one can be voted out, what replaces the threat of removal? A fourth pressure point, if it fits, is rotation: what happens when half the body is replaced by a new draw and the new members must be socialized into norms they did not make.

Deliverable: the activity itself first. Begin with the activity, not with rationale or commentary. The first sentence should state the core mechanic. Then give materials, roles or rules, the sequence and timing, the payoff scheme or decision rule, the debrief that maps specific moments onto the idea, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply.

In the debrief notes, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic. For example, it is not contested that sortition has been used to select political bodies and that it changes incentives; it is contested whether lottocratic bodies should be advisory or binding, whether they should replace elections or supplement them, and how much weight to give to statistical representativeness versus deliberation. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list. Write for a facilitator who will run this once, with 18 students, in 100 minutes.
