I'm teaching a third-year course called Knowledge and Justification and I'm writing the final. We spend two weeks on the bootstrapping objection to reliabilism and the associated easy-knowledge worry — the usual run of cases where a device or a faculty ends up vouching for its own reliability — and I want one essay question that a student can't answer just by reciting the standard dialectic back at me. Half of them have memorized the shape of the objection and the reliabilist's reply and can reproduce both without ever having to decide anything, which is exactly what I'm trying to stop.

So I've built the question around a case one of my students made up in class, which I've been chewing on ever since. Devi is learning Portuguese and uses a translation app. She wants to know whether the app is any good, so she writes forty English sentences of her own, runs each through the app into Portuguese, then feeds the Portuguese back in and lets the app translate it into English. Whenever the round trip comes back saying the same thing as her original sentence, she scores a hit. She gets 39 out of 40 and concludes the app translates accurately, and after that she relies on it to write emails to a colleague in Lisbon. Devi thinks this is the bootstrapping problem all over again; my other students think its obviously a different fallacy, something like confusing consistency with correctness. I want the exam to force them to adjudicate that, and in particular to say where exactly the circularity is, if there is one, and whether the reliabilist is committed to saying the round-trip test is perfectly fine. I'd also like the question to make them confront whether the case really does generate easy knowledge — whether Devi ends up knowing, on the cheap, things she shouldn't, like that the app is reliable, or that the Portuguese sentences mean what the app says they mean.

Write me the question itself first — it can run a bit long, since I want the case fully described on the paper so they don't have to remember the details — then a model answer of about 650-750 words of the sort a strong student could produce in the 60 minutes, and then a marking scheme out of 20. For the marking scheme I want five must-hit points with the marks for each, a one-line note on how students typically miss each one, and a short closing paragraph on what seperates an A answer from a B answer. No citations anywhere, please: they won't have the texts in the exam room and I don't want them guessing at page numbers.
