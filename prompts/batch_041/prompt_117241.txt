I have been working through a popular introduction to philosophical logic, and one chapter offers the following summary of a well-known result about indicative conditionals. I would like an accuracy audit of it.

"Philosophers of language had long assumed that the probability of an indicative conditional is simply the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent. The idea — associated with Stalnaker and with Adams — is that the probability that the picnic will be cancelled if it rains is just the probability of cancellation conditional on rain, so that P(if A, then B) = P(B | A). In the 1970s David Lewis proved a celebrated result that killed this assumption. Lewis's triviality results show that the equation cannot hold. Assume it, and you can prove that P(if A, then B) = P(B) for any A and B whatsoever: the antecedent makes no difference at all to the probability of the conditional, and the conditional simply collapses into its consequent. Since the same reasoning would give P(if A, then A) = P(A) rather than 1, the assumption reduces to absurdity. The moral is that indicative conditionals do not have truth conditions. A conditional is not a proposition that is true or false according to how things stand in the world; it is the expression of a conditional degree of belief — a way of saying that you take B to be likely on the supposition that A. That is why the material conditional of the propositional calculus is a hopeless analysis of 'if', and why the subject has come to treat the logic of conditionals as a logic of conditional probability rather than a logic of truth. Because Lewis's proof is a mathematical theorem, there is no way around it: any theory that assigns truth conditions to indicative conditionals has been refuted by the probability calculus, and the only live options are the non-propositional ones."
