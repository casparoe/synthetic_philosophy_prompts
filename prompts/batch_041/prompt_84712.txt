I am the chief speechwriter to the head of government of a country that is about to apologize, for the first time, for a historical wrong, and I would like you to draft the speech. The occasion is the floor of the national legislature, on the anniversary of the wrong. The community's elders will sit in the chamber as guests, the address will be broadcast nationally, and the room contains legislators who regard the apology as an insult to their own forebears alongside legislators who regard it as decades overdue.

The wrong is this. A century and a half ago, the state's predecessor government removed an indigenous nation from its ancestral territory in order to open the land to settlement. The removal was lawful under the law of the day, was debated and approved by the legislature, and was carried out by officials whose names still stand on public buildings. It was never reversed. Some of the statutes that effected it remain on the books, the territory is still held by the state, and the community's descendants remain, on average, far poorer than the descendants of the settlers who received the land.

The head of government wants an apology, not an expression of regret, and has given me the following instructions, which I pass on to you in full.

The speech must say what was done and name it as wrong, and it must name the agents — the government, the legislature, the officials — rather than letting the wrong appear as something that merely happened. Avoid constructions that leave the doer out.

The apology is to be unconditional. No concessive clauses, no gesture toward the attitudes of the time, no reminder of the good done since, no invitation to weigh the wrong against anything at all. Every attempt at balance turns an apology into an excuse, and I want every such attempt kept out.

The speech must not ask for forgiveness, imply that any is owed, or thank anyone for granting it. It may say once, plainly, that no forgiveness is being asked for.

The speech is addressed at once to the dead and to the living. The dead cannot receive it; the living are under no obligation to accept it. Do not smooth this over. Let part of the speech be an address to people who cannot hear it, and let it say of the living that they are free to refuse — and note, without resolving it, that a people is not a person and there is no one who can accept on its behalf.

The speech must hold two things apart throughout: the apology, which the community may take up or refuse, and the material remedy the government will announce in the same session — repeal of the surviving statutes and return of the land — which is owed whether or not the apology is accepted, and which is neither a condition of the apology nor a substitute for it. The speech must not suggest that the remedy discharges the apology, or the reverse.

The speech must not describe the wrong as finished, since part of it is still in force. But the apology must not be made conditional on finishing it either: what is being apologized for is what was done, and the apology stands on its own.

No praise of the community, no account of its suffering as instructive, ennobling, or redemptive, and no suggestion that the state is improved by having apologized. Do not ventriloquize the community or put words in its mouth. The state is not the beneficiary of this speech.

The speech must not claim that a state can feel remorse. If it touches the question at all, it should claim only that a state can bind itself and can be held to what it says. It must not claim that the apology repairs anything: the wrong is not reparable, and saying so is part of telling the truth, not a reason to say less.

The speech should run to about nine hundred words, in the restrained register of a formal address to a legislature rather than a rally — intelligible to an ordinary listener, and not embarrassing before the historians and lawyers in the room. Do not quote or cite any real statute, speech, or document; invent whatever particulars the speech needs.
