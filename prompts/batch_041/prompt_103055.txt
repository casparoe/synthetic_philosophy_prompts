For our philosophy club's end-of-year zine I've been asked to contribute a satirical piece, and I've settled on doing something with Carnap's distinction between internal and external questions — the idea that a dispute over whether, say, numbers or physical objects or a city's neighbourhoods "really exist" is not a question of truth at all but a practical decision about which framework to adopt, after which questions inside the framework get settled by its own rules. My angle is a parody of a municipal planning department memo. The head of a city's long-range planning office is an old-fashioned logical empiricist of the Vienna sort, and he has been told to write the office's guiding document for the revision of the zoning code. The humour should come out of the philosophy doing real work for him as an administrator: he keeps reclassifying furious public objections as external questions, "not a matter of truth but of the practical decision to adopt a framework," while his own rather specific vision of the rationally planned city gets smuggled in under the heading of explication, as though he were merely replacing a vague concept with an exact one. Please lean on the actual mechanics rather than on references, since I want this funny to someone who has read the Vienna Circle and legible to someone who hasn't: the internal/external split; the principle of tolerance, on which we set no prohibitions in matters of framework and merely propose conventions; explication; the old quarrel about protocol sentences and whether observation reports are themselves revisable; and the fact that the Circle's left wing was politically committed and took rational planning to be the whole point of the exercise — so that the memo's bland procedural neutrality keeps colliding with the author's evident enthusiasm for the plan. One running gag I'd like: the traffic engineers dutifully computing a degree of confirmation for the claim that residents prefer denser housing, as if inductive logic could settle a planning dispute. He should be an invented official, never identified with Carnap himself, and I don't want any quotations put in anyone's mouth — paraphrase in the memo's own voice instead. Don't date it, either; I don't want to get the history wrong. About 800 words, formatted as a numbered internal memorandum with subject headings and a short appendix of "approved protocol sentences" for staff, ending with one moment where the author's own politics break through the procedure before being hastily re-buried.

No citations, no reading list, and no references to specific books or papers — the piece has to stand on its own for readers who will never look anything up. What I would like, after the satire and clearly marked off from it, is a short note of a few sentences telling me where the piece takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading. In particular: is it fair to treat the internal/external distinction as a device for dodging political argument, or would people who know Carnap's work well say that reading flattens something important about it? Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely disputed among people who study him — I'd much rather you flag the disagreement than hand me one side as though it were the consensus, and if the parody only works because of a reading that specialists reject, say so plainly rather than softening it.
