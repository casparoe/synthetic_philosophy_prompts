Ok, so I've been reading George Berkeley's money writing and I want a parody built out of it. Berkeley as in the bishop who did the "to be is to be perceived" thing — he also wrote a book called The Querist, published in Dublin in the 1730s, which is just three installments of numbered rhetorical questions about wealth and money. Sample: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" His answer is basically yes, a ticket. The real source of wealth is land and human labor; gold and silver are worth only whatever we collectively agree to think thier worth; the denominations would still work fine if the bullion vanished entirely; money's whole purpose is to stir up industry and let people share in the fruits of each other's labor; and what men actually want is power, with a well-run state being one where each member gets power in proportion to his industry — which, read uncharitably, means somebody ends up with command over other people's labor. He wanted a national bank and paper credit for Ireland, thought the true measure of a nation was how many of its people were usefully employed, suggested you can manufacture industry by manufacturing wants in people, and even wondered whether the legislature ought to step in and manage the fashions, since fashion is what steers a whole country's industry.

Now write me roughly 650 words of deadpan satire in the form of a keynote speech transcript: an invented central bank governor (make him up, not a real person) announcing that his country is retiring the bullion-era framework and moving to a "ticket standard." He's a total believer and completely composed. All the comedy has to come from the actual substance of Berkeley's view, not from generic "money is fake, lol" jokes — the ticket/counter idea, value as collective opinion, denominations outliving the bullion, the thought that less money moving fast is equivalent to more money moving slow, industry as the true wealth, the real end being power, wants as an engine of industry, the state dictating fashions. Stay straight-faced start to finish, no winking, no narrator nudging us, and a reader who's never heard of Berkeley should follow every beat. And keep it fair — someone who's actually read the book should laugh instead of wince, so don't attribute things to the view that aren't in it. By the end I want it to be genuinely uncomfortable about one thing, without the governor ever copping to it: if money is only a ticket, who's doing the commanding, and who's holding the paper when the tickets stop being honored?
