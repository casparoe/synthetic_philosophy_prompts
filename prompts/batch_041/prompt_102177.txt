I'm in a seminar on Aristotle's ethics and we just spent a whole session on the money passage at the end of book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, and I left more confused than when I went in. You probably know the shape of it: justice in exchange needs a proportion, a proportion needs the two things traded to be commensurable, and a house and a pair of shoes just aren't — so need gets dragged in, and then money gets dragged in, and money is a convention (nomos, not physis, i.e. it isn't commensurable in the things themselves, we just agree to treat it as if it were), and then there's the strange bit where money is also like a pledge or a hostage you hand over now so you can call in later the thing you don't need yet, and on top of that everyone always wants the stuff, which is exactly what he complains about in Politics I where he splits household management off from the unlimited coin-accumulating kind of wealth-getting that has no natural limit. So the very thing that rescues exchange from the incommensurability problem is the thing he treats as unnatural and boundless. I want a poem that sits in that tension rather than dissolving it.

The constraint matters more to me than the poem being pretty, so here it is: two equal stanzas, same length, same rhyme scheme, same shape line for line — the first in the voice of the shoemaker, the second in the voice of the person who wants the shoes and has nothing the shoemaker actually wants, so the two stanzas have to be formal twins, as if they were commensurable. Then two or three closing lines that are the coin: the third term that lets the poem end at all. And here's the part I care about — the coin lines should have to rhyme with the ends of both stanzas and should not quite manage it, the rhyme coming out aslant in a way you can hear. Don't fix it. The near-miss is the point (money is a convention, so it only measures by not really measuring). If you can also make that closing coin-line address someone who isn't in the poem yet, a later self who'll need something then, even better, since thats the pledge/hostage thought. Roughly 20-24 lines total, plain language, no Aristotle cosplay.

It should be accurate enough that my professor nods instead of wincing — the proportion, the nomos/physis thing, the pledge, the chrematistics thing all recognisable to someone who knows the text, and legible enough that someone who's never read it still feels why the two stanzas can't just be swapped for each other. At the bottom, in a few sentences: which reading of the aporia the poem ends up committing to (does the coin actually solve it, or only paper over it?), where it takes a liberty or leans on a contested reading of book V, and what you'd have written differently if you'd decided the coin really does measure — in other words, what would change the poem's verdict.
