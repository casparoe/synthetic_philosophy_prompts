A couple friends and me are in a grad seminar on the philosophy of probability, and for the unit on the competing interpretations -- frequentism, subjectivism, propensity -- we each have to bring in a real case and analyze it through all three lenses. I drew the short straw and got Sally Clark, so this is my prep. Quick facts in case you need them: Clark was an English solicitor whose first son died in his crib in late 1996 and whose second son died the same way in early 1998; she was tried for both murders in 1999, and the pediatrician Roy Meadow testified for the prosecusion that the chance of two cot deaths (SIDS) in an affluent family like hers was about 1 in 73 million, a figure he reached by squaring a 1-in-8500 estimate for a single such death. She was convicted, her first appeal upheld the conviction, and a second appeal in 2003 finally overturned it. I already know the standard story -- that the number commits the prosecutor's fallacy by giving the probability of the evidence given innocence rather than innocence given the evidence, that the two deaths probably aren't statistacally independant, and that the Royal Statistical Society was not impressed. I don't want that story again. What I want is you treating the three interpretations as genuinely distinct lenses and running the case through each one on its own terms, and then telling me what the case does to the theories, not just what the theories do to the case.

So: for the frequentist, what exactly is the quantity the 1 in 73 million was supposed to estimate, what reference class is being assumed, and why does the choice of reference class make or break the number -- and is something like the probability that this particular mother did it even a well-formed quantity for a frequentist, or is it a category error? For the subjectivist, redo the inference the way a Bayesian actually would: what prior on double infanticide versus double SIDS, what likelihoods get multiplied, and how sensitive is the posterior to the prior in a way that should worry a court that has to output a yes/no verdict? For the propensity theorist, does it even make sense to speak of a stable propensity for a given family to suffer SIDS, or does propensity talk about a single unrepeatable event collapse into something else -- a credence, a frequency, nothing at all? Then the part I actually care about: where does the case test the theories instead of merely illustrating them? Does the reference-class problem bite frequentism specifically, or anyone who wants to attach a number to this; does the legal setting -- one-off events, adversarial factfinding, a binary verdict -- quietly favor one interpretation for reasons that have nothing to do with the statistics; and is there a version of any of the three that could have blocked Meadow's figure from ever reaching the jury?

Last thing, and be honest about it: what none of the three interpretations can deliver here. My suspicion is that the real failure was wanting a single number at all, and I want you to either back that up or shoot it down. Write it in American English, and skip the reading list -- I don't need citations, just the analysis -- and please don't hedge by saying all three views are really compatible or secretly the same thing, because the whole point of the assignment is to see where they come apart.
