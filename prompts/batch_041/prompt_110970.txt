I am a novelist, some way into a book that I had assumed was a book about people and now realize is a book about categories — specifically about whether the categories that bear hardest on my characters are discovered or made. The difficulty is not that I lack opinions; it is that my characters hold incompatible ones, and I have reached the point where I can no longer write a scene in which someone asserts that a kind is socially constructed without knowing what would have to be true for the assertion to be more than a gesture. I am not asking you to settle the metaphysics. I am asking you to tell me what a theory of socially constructed kinds would have to get right before I let any character use the word.

Give me the specification: the requirements any adequate account of "kind K is socially constructed" must satisfy, with the consideration or case that motivates each. I would expect the list to include at least the following, and I want each one made precise and defended rather than merely named. First, a usable contrast class: what it is for a kind *not* to be socially constructed, on pain of the claim collapsing into the truism that everything depends on human activity somewhere. Second, the distinction between a kind's being caused or shaped by social arrangements and its being constituted by them — money and mountains presumably differ here, and I want the criterion that marks the difference. Third, an account of why social kinds present themselves as external and constraining, resistant to individual revision, when they are also the products of collective practice. Fourth, the feedback phenomenon: classification that alters the classified, so that the kind's extension at one time is not the extension it would have had under a different label. Fifth, a way of saying that constructed kinds are nonetheless real, causally efficacious, and objects of genuine knowledge, without thereby reducing to the claim that they are arbitrary or fictional. Sixth, room for historical and cross-cultural variation, and for the possibility that a person can be substantially mistaken about the kind she herself inhabits. Seventh, a clean separation between the conditions under which a kind exists and the conditions under which something is a member of it. Eighth, an account of what, if anything, follows — epistemically or normatively — from a kind's being constructed, since this is where the argument in my book actually lives. Ninth, consistency with whatever the relevant empirical disciplines establish about the kind in question. Tenth, an answer to who or what does the constructing, and by what mechanism, so that "constructed" is not merely a place-holder for an unspecified social process.

Then, and this is the part I care most about: mark which of these are genuine desiderata and which are commitments of one camp that a rival account could reasonably reject; identify any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say whether the conflict is a genuine result about the subject or an artifact of how the requirements were stated; and tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate otherwise popular accounts. Test the finished list against money, against gender, and against race — money, I take it, is the case where construction and reality most obviously coexist and so makes the best control — and against one kind you regard as plainly not constructed, so that I can see the list bite. I have no interest in a bibliography; refer to positions and arguments as you like, and I will attach the sources myself. What I need is a specification I could hand to a character and have her argue from.
