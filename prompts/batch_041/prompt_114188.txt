I'm putting together the closing session of a reading group on behavior genetics and the philosophy of biology — we've spent the term on the variance decomposition, and I want to end with a poem rather than a summary. Please take the role of a poet with genuine command of quantitative genetics, someone who could defend the definition of heritability in a seminar and then write verse a specialist would recognize as getting it right, and write me one poem of twenty to twenty-eight lines. I'd like it built on a strict formal rule rather than free verse: a pantoum, in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza gathers the opening stanza's lines back — or, if that exact repetition begins to feel like machinery, a poem of the same length organized around one refrain line that recurs verbatim at least four times under changed circumstances. Either way the repetitions are the point, not decoration.

The content I want the form to carry is what heritability does and does not mean. Heritability is a ratio of variance components: the share of the phenotypic variance in a specified population, living within a specified spread of environments, that is attributable to genetic differences among the individuals in it. It is not a property of a trait, still less of a person or a group. Change the environment and the number moves without a single gene changing; and a trait can be almost perfectly heritable in a population where everyone develops it under the same conditions, even though nobody would develop it without those conditions. Seed the poem with a concrete scene so the reader can feel the number move: first a field where every plant comes from a different seed but all of them grow in the same soil, then the same seed lot divided between a rich field and a poor one, so that the differences between the two fields are wholly environmental while the differences within each field remain largely genetic. At least one line should recur word for word after the soil has changed, so that it says something different the second time without being edited.

Please let the reversal do structural work rather than announcing itself in a footnote: a reader who knows the theory should be able to point to the stanza where the environment shifts and say exactly which claim has just become false, and a reader who doesn't should still come away with a good poem. The ending should land on the thought that the statistic belongs to a comparison rather than to a thing — that there is no heritability of an individual, only of a population in conditions — while leaving standing the puzzle that one and the same claim about what runs in the family can be true of one population and false of another. Keep the diction plain, with no textbook jargon beyond what the poem earns, no named researchers, no studies, and no invented quotations. Before drafting, spend a few sentences laying out your plan: which recurring line will carry which claim, where the environment changes, and what the closing repetition should leave the reader with. Then write the poem.
