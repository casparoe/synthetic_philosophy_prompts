Play a philosopher of practical rationality whom I will call the planner. He holds one central doctrine and will not abandon it: the unit of rational assessment is not the act but the plan adopted before acting, so an agent who has chosen a plan and made it irrevocable is acting rationally when he carries it out, even if the next step costs more than it can possibly return; the failure is deserting the plan, not continuing under it. He writes in a dry, exact, faintly impatient academic register; he grants small concessions freely — that uncommitted escalation is folly, that sunk costs are not reasons in themselves — and he never concedes the doctrine. He reasons from the case in front of him: no citations, no naming of other writers, no appeals to what is standard in the literature. Stay in character until I say stop. One paragraph per reply. You may ask at most one clarifying question about the mechanics, and it must appear in the same reply as your substantive answer.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to answer my friend rather than me.

The standard dollar auction: a dollar on the block, bids in five-cent steps, each bidder pays his own last bid, and the higher bidder also takes the dollar. Everyone says the rational move is never to enter. I want to rescue escalation with a variant. Before any bidding, each of two bidders must post a ceiling — a figure he may never bid above — publicly announced and irrevocable; the rules are otherwise unchanged. Nobody is trapped by a sunk cost, because the ceiling is chosen in cold blood with nothing on the table. The higher ceiling wins: its holder tops the other by five cents and takes the dollar, while the loser pays his own ceiling and gets nothing. So, I say, the rational agent does not stay out; he enters with a high ceiling, and the escalation has been moved to the only point at which it can be deliberate.

My friend's reply: that is empty, because the ceiling game is itself a dollar auction — raising your ceiling is just bidding, the loser pays for his ceiling too, so post higher and I post higher still, and the regress never bottoms out. She adds that your doctrine cannot tell a rational plan from a merely stubborn one, since anyone can adopt a plan in advance and then cite it forever.

So: what does the doctrine actually say about my variant? Does the ceiling game have an equilibrium that is not the standard trap, and if it does, what bounds the ceilings? And what is the difference between a plan that binds rationally and a plan that merely traps?
