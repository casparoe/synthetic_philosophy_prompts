ok so bit of a weird one. i moved abroad about two years ago — my partner grew up somewhere totally different — and we keep getting into these arguments that aren't really about facts. like whether her cousin "really knew" the thing he told us, or whether someone "caused" a mess by not giving us a heads up, or whether a white lie is a lie. we just seem to have different gut reactions to the same situation and neither of us can talk the other out of it, and it's started to bug me how much of my own moral certainty seems to just be... mine.

a friend who did a phil degree said oh, philosophers actually study exactly this — there's alot of work on whether people from different cultures give different answers to the standard thought experiments (gettier cases, the free will ones, that kind of thing), and whether that's a problem for doing philosophy by intuition in the first place. and he said the standard move from people who aren't worried is: it's like color. different languages carve the spectrum up differently, some don't even have a separate word for blue i think?, and yet nobody concludes there's no fact of the matter about what color a thing is. the words vary, the underlying reality doesn't. so, he says, cross-cultural variation in intuitions is like that — variation in the labels, not the facts — and philosophy can carry on using intuitions as evidence.

that felt too quick to me and i want to actually pressure-test it. can you lay out what maps onto what (the stimulus, the perceptual machinery, the words, the "reality" side of it), then where the color analogy actually breaks when you push on it, what it's quietly importing that moral or epistemic intuitions might not have, and whether the reassuring conclusion — intuitions stay usable as evidence — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. and if the color thing is doing too much of the work, tell me what a better analogy would be. a table for the mapping is fine.
