I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about the discursive dilemma — the pattern in which a group whose members each hold a perfectly consistent set of judgments nonetheless ends up, under majority voting taken proposition by proposition, committed to a jointly inconsistent set (the same structure is often called the doctrinal paradox in the literature on collegial courts). He thinks it is a curiosity of formal social choice theory, an artefact of an over-demanding insistence that groups be "rational" in the way individual people are, and that any sensible aggregation rule sidesteps it. I think it marks a real limit on the ideal of a collective will that is at once faithful to what people actually think and coherent as a body of judgments. I want you to answer as someone who works on judgment aggregation and social choice rather than as a popularizer, and to think slowly, beginning by writing out for yourself what the dilemma is and what the standard escape routes are — restricting the agenda, aggregating only on "premises" or only on the "conclusion", relaxing consistency to something weaker, going probabilistic or graded — before you commit to any numbers. Treat the following as the test case, since my friend and I kept arguing past each other over it: an assembly of 100 votes on three propositions, "the dam is safe" (60–40 in favour), "the dam is affordable" (55–45 in favour), and "we should build the dam" (44–56 against), where everyone in the argument accepts that the third follows from the first two jointly. Majority voting yields safe, affordable and not-build, which cannot all be true together. My friend's fix is to delete propositions in ascending order of their majority margin until consistency is restored, which here kills the affordability judgment and leaves the assembly opposed to building; I say that rule is doing something nobody voted for, that its outcome turns on a tie-breaking convention whenever margins are equal, and that margins are exactly what strategic voters would manipulate.

What I want from you is two credences, each between 0 and 1, each with its derivation laid out. First: the probability that there exists a decision procedure that (i) is defined for every profile of individually consistent judgments over any agenda rich enough to generate the dilemma, (ii) treats no voter as special, (iii) decides each proposition on the basis of the pattern of votes on that proposition alone, applied uniformly across propositions, and (iv) always issues a consistent collective set. Second: the probability that, if no such procedure exists, the further moral my friend resists follows — namely that any collectively consistent outcome must be secured by some feature of the procedure that is not itself answerable to the judgments people hold, so that a democratically legitimate collective will is always in part a construction rather than an aggregation. Decompose each number into the sub-questions it rests on: for the first, how much rides on whether the impossibility results hold for the agendas that actually arise in politics rather than for artificially rich ones, on whether "consistent" should be weakened to something like "not blatantly self-contradictory", on whether the formal result is settled in its scope or still contested; for the second, how much rides on whether legitimacy really requires proposition-wise even-handedness, on whether a group must be an agent with a single consistent mind at all, and on whether procedural arbitrariness is a defect or simply the ordinary condition of every institution. Give a rough probability for each sub-question with one line of justification, then the combination, then tell me which term you are least confident in, which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it most.

For what it is worth, my own numbers are about 0.95 and 0.6, and my friend's are about 0.2 and 0.1. Tell me where you think the disagreement between us actually lives — which sub-question, not which temperament. And throughout, be careful to separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
