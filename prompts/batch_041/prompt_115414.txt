Random question thats been bugging me. I've been reading about Carnap, the Vienna Circle guy, and two things about him keep striking me, mostly because nobody puts them together. First, he was a socialist pretty much his whole life, and not just in the vague sense that everyone in that scene was left-wing: he saw his own philosophy as part of a bigger enlightenment project, he was close to Neurath and to Neurath's ideas about planning and unified science, and he really did think clarity was a tool for running a society better. Second, he drew a hard line between questions asked inside a framework and the question of which framework to adopt, and he held that the second kind isn't a matter of truth but a practical decision: you take up a language because it's useful for a purpose, and tolerance is the right attitude toward the alternatives.

Now here's the thing he never got to see. There's a revived argument going around that the old socialist calculation debate was decided wrong, because the computational objection to central planning is basically dead, in principle we can solve the enormous optimization problem now. The version of this I find most interesting isn't the brute-force one. It's the version where you set the whole economy up as a single big constrained optimization, production possibilities, resource limits, everybody's preferences, and you don't even need to hand the planners a welfare function, because the prices fall out of the math by themselves, as the shadow values attached to the constraints, the dual variables. So you get market-like prices as a by-product of one global solve rather than out of trades between people.

Say Carnap is alive today, has read all this, and is in a good mood. What does he say? What I actually want to know is whether he'd treat market-or-plan as one of his practical framework-choice questions, in which case neither answer is true or false and the fight is really about which arrangement serves which ends, or wether the computational development changes the question so that it turns theoretical after all, since the machine either finds the optimum or it doesn't and thats a fact about the world. And if it does stay practical, what is usefulness for a purpose even supposed to mean here, when the purpose is the whole shape of a society and the people whose preferences go into the objective function are the very people whose lives the choice will determine? He had a thing about not letting value questions sneak in dressed up as factual ones, and I genuinely can't tell whether that cuts against the computational planners or for them.

Don't just tell me he'd be cautiously optimistic, or that it's complicated and he'd want more clarity. Give me the position you think he'd actually land on, and then tell me what would move you off it, which piece of evidence about Carnap or which argument would make you think you'd got him wrong.
