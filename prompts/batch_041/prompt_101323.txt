I run a reading group that meets every other week to work through decision theory, and we keep circling the same discomfort: expected value maximization looks willing to trade a large, certain good for an arbitrarily small chance of an arbitrarily large one. We are now more interested in the shape of the proposed remedies than in the objection itself, and I would like you to design a single thought experiment that pulls apart two remedies usually mentioned in the same breath. The first places the restriction on the value side: the agent's utility is bounded, or at any rate rises so slowly that no outcome, however vast in ordinary terms, is worth more than some ceiling. The second places the restriction on the probability side: the agent treats every chance below some fixed threshold as zero, and evaluates everything else by ordinary expected value. I want a case in which these two remedies return opposite verdicts. Concretely, the case should present two gambles that are identical in what they offer and in what they cost, differing only in the probability of the good outcome, with one probability sitting just below and the other just above the threshold the second remedy uses. The probability-side remedy must therefore refuse the first gamble and accept the second; the value-side remedy must refuse both; plain expected value maximization must accept both. The scenario itself should be realistic and quotidian — a person, household, or small committee committing a modest, well-defined resource — with no demons, no perfect predictors, no omnipotent strangers offering wagers, no infinite or undefined utilities, no zero probabilities, and no exotic physics. State it in ordinary goods with explicit numbers, in no more than two paragraphs, and make the good outcome large enough in ordinary terms that the gamble feels fanatical without being literally unbounded; the threshold, meanwhile, should be a round number small enough that adopting it counts as a recognizable response to the fanaticism worry rather than a fudge.

After the scenario, walk through the arithmetic behind each of the three verdicts, showing the inequalities that generate them, and then tell me which feature of the case is doing the real work and which parts are scaffolding. Test the case by turning the knobs: what happens to each remedy if the threshold is lowered below the smaller probability, or if the value ceiling is raised? Does either remedy collapse into the other, or into plain expected value maximization? Finish by saying which of the two remedies fares worse here and why, and give the strongest reply available to its defender — for instance, that a single flat numerical threshold is a caricature of a more sophisticated rule, or that any ceiling low enough to do the work will also mangle ordinary comparisons. Since the point of the exercise is to show that the remedies come apart, a case that merely stipulates the verdicts will not do; the verdicts have to fall out of the numbers. I would far prefer a committed answer to a survey: fix the numbers yourself, defend the calibration you have chosen, and say plainly where you had to strain to satisfy the specifications. Please write in British English.
