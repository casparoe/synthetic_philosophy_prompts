I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the Judy Benjamin case, and we ended up further apart than when we started, so I want the disagreement laid out properly rather than continue trading intuitions at each other. The setup, as I understand it from van Fraassen's paper on relative information minimisers in probability kinematics: Judy is dropped somewhere in a territory divided into a Red half and a Blue half, each of which is itself divided into a Second Company area and a Headquarters area, and she begins with a quarter of her credence in each of the four quadrants. Her radio then tells her that, conditional on her being in Red territory, the odds are three to one that she is in the Headquarters area. The message constrains a conditional probability without telling her which event obtained, so plain conditionalisation has nothing to bite on; and the standard repair, minimising the relative information of the new distribution with respect to the old subject to that constraint, ends up raising her credence that she is in Blue territory, which is precisely what the message said nothing about. My friend thinks that result is simply correct and that our discomfort is a prejudice about what evidence is permitted to do; I think it shows that we have misdescribed what the message is, or that the framing of the problem is doing illicit work somewhere. What I want from you is a decision tree that maps the options rather than an argument for one of them.

Please build it so that every branch node is a question about the structure of the problem that someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to: whether the message constrains only the conditional or also bears on the antecedent; whether the antecedent probability is something the update is permitted to move; whether there is a partition formulation that would turn the message into an ordinary Jeffrey-style input, and if there is not, what that shows; whether the sender's decision to transmit is itself an event whose likelihood varies with where Judy is; whether the constraint must be satisfied exactly or is itself revisable; and whether the correct output is a single distribution or a set of them. The leaves should be positions, named where you are confident of the name and marked as unnamed where you are not, each carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment; I expect the usual suspects to appear, but do not pad the tree to accommodate labels you happen to know, and if two leaves cannot be separated by anything in the tree, flag that rather than papering over it. Where a path leads to a combination of answers that no one actually holds, say so and diagnose whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Two test cases I want the tree to be run against, because I think they are where the positions genuinely come apart. First, a variant in which the message carries a second clause: conditional on her being in Blue territory, the odds are one to one that she is in Headquarters — which on her starting distribution is already the case, so the constraint set is unchanged, and any position that treats the two messages differently owes an account of why redundant content is not redundant. Second, a variant in which the broadcast goes to the whole platoon rather than being sent because the sender knew where Judy was, which turns the message from a report about Judy into a report about the territory; I want to know which leaves survive that. This is for a graduate reading group on probability and epistemology, so the nodes may be technical, but the tree should work as a handout: seven to nine leaves, British spelling throughout, and no citations or reading lists — I would far rather have an honest unnamed leaf than a guessed attribution.
