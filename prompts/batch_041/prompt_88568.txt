I've been reading a little local history booklet about why the big wool mill in the town I grew up in shut down in 1959, and it got me and two friends into an arguement I can't settle. The booklet explains it as a story: the owner died in a car crash in the spring, the contract he'd been about to sign with a firm from the next city fell through because of that, the council had just rerouted the main road away from the mill yard, and so on, in that order. Katrin said that isn't an explanation at all until you can point to a general rule the story is an instance of -- something like mills close when their margins fall past what the owners can absorb -- because otherwise it's just 'and then, and then', and you could tell that kind of story about any two things that happen one after the other. She said historians always smuggle these rules in and just don't write them down, and that if you can't say what pattern the case falls under, you also can't learn anything from it that transfers to the next mill. (Someone told me there's a name for her view, the covering-law thing, from Hempel maybe?) Tom said the rules are either empty or false: anything general enough to cover this case ('people protect their money', 'businesses close when they lose money') is a platitude that doesn't explain why this one closed *then*, and anything sharp enough to do real work ('a mill closes within a year of its owner's death') is just false, since plenty of mills survived their owners. He said the whole point of the story is to show how close it came to going the other way -- the owner had the pen out, the road rerouting was a near thing -- and that a law would actually ruin it, because laws make outcomes look inevitable when the interesting fact is that this one was contingent.

Katrin came back with: prediction has nothing to do with it, we explain why bridges fall down without being able to predict which one will, and the covering-law idea is about the logical shape of an explanation, not fortune telling. And she said Tom's contingency point actually helps her, because saying 'it was the owner's death that made the difference' is a counterfactual claim -- without the death, the mill stays open -- and counterfactuals only work if there's a general truth backing them, e.g. that owners with a buyer lined up go ahead and sign. Tom said that's backwards: the only reason we can say what would have happened is that the story tells us the contract was ready and the buyer was local and solvent, so the 'generalization' is just this one case restated and covers no second case at all. He also said you can't even know what you're explaining until you've told some story, because 'the mill closed' could be a story about a bankruptcy, a family falling out, or a whole town going downhill, and the covering-law person has to pick one of those before any law gets to apply. They both asked me to referee and I couldnt tell who was winning. Could you go through their exchange move by move -- Katrin's two points and Tom's two, plus the replies -- and say who's right about what, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and then say what both of them are missing? I'm not a philosopher, I just read a booklet and now I can't stop thinking about it.
