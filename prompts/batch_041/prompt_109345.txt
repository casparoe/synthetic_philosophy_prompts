I'm a software engineer with no training in philosophy, and I've been arguing about this at dinner for a month, so I'd rather have a careful answer than a slogan. The pattern that bothers me is this. Where I live it's perfectly legal and utterly routine to pay a person for blood plasma, to pay a healthy volunteer to swallow an experimental compound in an early-phase drug trial, to pay a woman for her eggs, and to pay a premium to someone who takes a dangerous job; in some places it's also legal to pay a woman to carry a pregnancy for someone else. But paying a living person for a kidney is a crime, and it is prosecuted as one, in essentially every country on earth, even though the waiting list for kidneys is long and people die on it. The same legal systems that tolerate all that commerce in bodies and body parts forbid selling your vote for cash — even a vote you were going to cast anyway, and even if you pledged the money to a food bank — and many of them also treat a steep price rise for generators or hotel rooms after a hurricane as an offense in itself, rather than as an unfortunate consequence of one. An economist friend tells me the kidney ban is a straightforward policy failure and that a regulated scheme in which the state is the only permitted buyer would clear the shortage; a nurse I know says a paid kidney is not the same thing as a donated kidney even if the surgery is identical. I can believe a rule is popular, or convenient, or the residue of a lobbying fight, without believing there is any principle behind it. What I want to know is what the pattern presupposes.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of commodification these rules commit you to, if there is one, and be specific about which version it is. Does the prohibition on kidneys presuppose that some goods carry a kind of value that market exchange corrupts or destroys, so that the wrong is done to the good itself and not to the buyer or the seller? Or does it presuppose something about the desperation of sellers, about consent given under financial pressure, about third parties who end up worse off — the donor's family, the integrity of the medical profession, the sick who cannot outbid the rich — or about the sheer unreliability of a choice made when the alternative is eviction? I'd like you to separate what the practice strictly entails from what it merely happens to sit alongside. My suspicion is that the consistency I keep looking for is an illusion and that the very same set of rules would be equally comfortable with several theories that cannot all be true, and I want you to say exactly where the practice is underdetermined by the evidence.

Two further things. Vote selling and price gouging seem to me to be doing something different from the kidney ban — I would say the first is about the integrity of a decision procedure and the second about what a price is supposed to mean in an emergency, neither of them about bodily integrity — so tell me whether they belong to the same underlying commitment or whether I am conflating three unrelated ideas that merely share a superficial shape, namely a refusal to let something be bought. And then take the best theory you can reconstruct, assume it is the one actually at work, and tell me what would have to change for the rules to be applied uniformly: which prohibitions would have to fall, which would have to be extended to cases nobody currently objects to, and which of my examples would turn out to be irrelevant to the principle altogether. I don't need citations or a reading list — I want the reconstruction and the argument itself. Please write in British English.
