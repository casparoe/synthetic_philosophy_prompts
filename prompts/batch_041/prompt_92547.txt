I run a small research group -- two postdocs, one doctoral student, a workshop budget, and about two years before we have to show something -- and I have to decide what those years are spent on. The area I want to claim is the epistemology of moral testimony. I'd like you to set the agenda for us: which open questions matter most, in what order, and why.

I don't want a survey of positions, so let me tell you what I already believe, since part of what I'm asking is whether my framing is worth building on. The standard way into this topic is an asymmetry: it seems unremarkable to take someone's word for it that the ice is thin, and troubling to take someone's word for it that an act is wrong. I have come to think the moral-versus-descriptive cut is the wrong one. My hypothesis is that the real variable is articulacy -- whether the person you defer to can hand you the reasons, as opposed to simply seeing it. Deference to the veteran clinician, to the connoisseur, to the mathematician with a nose for a proof, strikes me as raising the same discomfort, and nobody thinks those cases are 'moral'. If that is right, moral testimony is one instance of a general problem about deference to normative competence, and much work organized around the moral case is looking in the wrong place. I also suspect the debate has been asking the permissive question -- when may I defer? -- when the live question is the mandatory one: when must I?

Assume my budget, and take my hypothesis seriously enough to attack it. Then give me a ranked agenda. For each item, tell me why it is upstream of other work rather than downstream; how tractable it is for two philosophers and a graduate student with no empirical funding; how neglected it is relative to how much turns on it; what two years of work on it would actually consist of; and what would move it up or down the list. Include, and rank against one another, at least these candidates and any better ones you can think of: whether the articulacy hypothesis really predicts the pattern of intuitions across moral, aesthetic, and epistemic deference; whether there is ever an obligation to defer rather than a mere permission; what deference does to the deferring agent's later reliability and to their standing to blame; moral testimony in institutions, where the testifier is a committee or a protocol rather than a person; whether machines trained on human text can be moral testifiers, and what, if anything, that changes; and moral testimony as a mechanism of moral learning in children, where the deferring party is also the party being formed.

Be decisive. I want a ranking I can act on, and a clear statement of which of my commitments you would give up first -- not a menu with the choice left to me. Write so that the ideas carry the weight rather than names and citations; I don't need a reading list.
