I'm a science journalist, I mostly cover brain stuff, and I've gotten stuck on pain asymbolia. These patients will tell you the pain is there, sometimes they'll even describe the quality and the location pretty precisely -- and then they just sit there unbothered, no grimacing, no pull to get away from it. The tidy story everyone tells is that this seperates the sensory side of pain from the awfulness of pain. I've basically talked myself into the opposite view: that the awfulness is intrinsic, that a state which doesn't hurt isn't pain, full stop, whatever the patient is willing to report.

So what I want from you is the case against me, sorted by how much it should actually move me. Which results would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Would a clean case of real pain with zero unpleasantness be enough -- say a drug that leaves the sensory quality alone but strips the awfulness for an hour, or a patient who still learns to avoid the noxious thing while insisting it doesn't bother them -- or is there a way for me to save the view? What do people who hold my view themselves concede? Be straight with me about whether it survives only by redefining pain as whatever hurts, and whether that's a real problem or just what a definition looks like. Also, what does the rival story -- badness coming from a seperate desire-to-stop system, bolted on to a neutral sensory signal -- predict that mine doesn't, and what would count against it? And does the other direction help me or hurt me: awfulness with no sensory quality at all. Skip the reading list, I'm not citing anyone, I just want the map.
