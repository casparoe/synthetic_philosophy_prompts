I just spent most of last night getting dragged into a long arguement between two of my mates — one's doing a PhD in philosophy, the other's just very opinionated — about whether two people can be doing something *together* when one of them doesn't think of it that way. I got completely lost, and I'd like you to actually sort it out.

Set the scene. Sam and Alex, flatmates, agree to throw a dinner party "together". Sam thinks of them as co-hosts. Alex, privately, thinks of himself as just helping Sam out — he's doing it *for* Sam, sees Sam as the host, and has no intention that the two of them host as a pair. They split the work, each does his bit because he expects the other to do his, and the dinner happens. Are Sam and Alex hosting together?

The argument went like this.

(1) My PhD mate said joint action doesn't need matching inner pictures. It's enough that each person's intention hooks onto the other's — "I'll do my bit given that he does his". On that test Sam and Alex qualify, so Alex's private framing is a red herring.

(2) My other mate said no, the *content* matters. Alex's intention has to be to host together; if he only intends to help Sam, there are just two overlapping solo actions, not one joint action. His analogy: two people who each independently decide to shift a table from opposite ends aren't moving it together unless each intends to move it together.

(3) PhD mate: that makes joint action hostage to private mental states that are often invisible and sometimes simply absent. He offered a case he calls "the reciprocating strangers" — two commuters on a platform who wordlessly shuffle left and right in perfect step, each only trying to get past the other, neither having any thought of doing anything together, yet their movements interlock completely. If that isn't acting together, he said, then meshing movements isn't the point and we've been looking in the wrong place.

(4) Other mate: but by that standard a whole market is a joint action, and that's absurd. Every shopper in a supermarket responds to every other shopper's behaviour, and nobody thinks the shoppers are doing the shopping *together*. He said the strangers are merely coordinating, not acting together, and coordinating is a weaker thing.

(5) PhD mate: then the difference isn't intention at all, it's commitment. The moment Alex says "I'll help you host", he's on the hook, and the jointness comes from the fact that they're both now bound, not from any matching state of mind. One of them could even privately change his mind and the joint action would still be under way.

(6) Other mate: but commitments can be one-sided — I can be committed to a project you've never heard of. So commitment alone can't be what makes it joint either.

That's roughly where we ran out of steam. Please take this on as someone who works on collective intentionality and social ontology. Think it through carefully before you write — maybe sketch the cases out to yourself first. Then adjudicate it move by move: for each of the six, say whether it's right, wrong, or half-right, and why. Finish with your verdict on the Sam and Alex case itself, and say what both of them are missing. I don't want names of famous philosophers or a reading list, just the reasoning.

Keep the whole thing to no more than 700 words, in British English, and lead with your bottom line before you get into the move-by-move bit.
