I am designing a ninety-minute session for a lifelong-learning institute. The audience is adults with no formal training in history. The session is on how we can know anything about the distant past, and I want it to teach the practice of source criticism—specifically, one idea: that a source is not a window onto the past but a trace that becomes evidence only under a question, and that reliability is a property of a claim relative to a question rather than of a source as such. I do not want a survey of the discipline from Herodotus and Thucydides to the present. I need you to plan the explanation, not give it.

The case I have in mind is this. In 1908, a farmer in a Carpathian village breaks open a sealed clay jar he has unearthed. Inside are three objects: a silver coin bearing a portrait and a date in an unfamiliar script; a strip of parchment listing twenty names and quantities of wheat; and a small bronze bird. The farmer keeps the bird, sells the coin to a peddler, and gives the parchment to the local priest. In 1910, a newspaper reports that the coin proves a Roman presence in the valley. In 1935, a museum catalogue describes the parchment as a medieval tax roll. In 1972, a chemist reports that the coin's silver content matches coins from a different region and century. In 2001, a local history website calls the bird a sacred relic of a lost tribe. No one has seen the jar since 1908, and the farmer's account survives only in the newspaper interview.
