Student's answer: "Higher-order theories say that a mental state is conscious just in case there is a higher-order representation of it. The basic idea, sometimes called the transitivity principle, is that a first-order state -- say a perception of red -- becomes conscious when a higher-order state represents it. Rosenthal is the main proponent of this, and his version is the 'inner sense' theory, where the higher-order state is a kind of perception of the first-order state. Others, like Armstrong, hold a higher-order thought version instead, where the higher-order state is a thought rather than a perception. The main motivation is to explain the difference between conscious and unconscious states: an unconscious perception is one that lacks the relevant higher-order representation. It also explains why we can be wrong about our own minds, since the higher-order state can misrepresent. The strongest objection, I think, is the misrepresentation objection. If the higher-order thought is what makes a state conscious, and the higher-order thought is always accurate about what it represents, then the theory can't explain cases where we're mistaken about our own mental states -- e.g., when we think we're angry but we're really just tired. So the theory fails to account for a central feature of consciousness. I don't think HOT theory survives this, because it makes consciousness depend on something infallible, which is unrealistic."
