I chair the selection committee for a small fellowship program. Five of us, all volunteers, nobody with any training in this sort of thing; we read about ninety applications a year, meet for one long afternoon, and give out ten awards. The applicants have made plans around our answer, so it matters to them, while the cost to us of getting it wrong is mostly that we never find out. We are not allowed to ask applicants for anything beyond what they have already sent me, and I cannot add meetings. What I can do is send out a form beforehand and run the afternoon differently. The decisions go out over my signature, and the program director could in principle veto them, though in nine years she never has.

What I keep noticing is that we agree far too quickly. The first two people to speak set a tone and the rest of us fall in behind it, often without anyone having said much of anything; afterwards, reading my own notes against the sealed rankings we tried last year, I saw that I had marked someone low and then voted for them. I went looking for an explanation and found the idea that this can be rational rather than lazy: once enough other people have gone the same way, each of us can reasonably stop using what we ourselves know, and that this can happen after surprisingly few people have spoken, and that it can be undone again by a single person's private information. I think that is roughly what happens to us, but I may well have it wrong, and I would like you to correct me if I do.

I want a procedure I can actually run, written for people who have never heard any of this: the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no, and where it hands the decision to me as chair because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to fit inside the afternoon we already have. Please make it handle the things I am most worried about.

- Whether five of us agreeing is five pieces of evidence or one. Nearly all of our agreement traces back to the same two letters of recommendation and the same ranking site. I need a rule for tracing a run of agreement back to its sources and collapsing it when it is really one signal wearing five hats, and for telling that case apart from agreement that is genuine, whether because people changed their minds when someone gave a reason or because we arrived at the same place separately.
- How to get at what people actually think rather than what they are willing to say, given that nobody wants to be the one who holds up the afternoon.
- What an individual panelist should do when they find themselves going along with a run of decisions they privately doubt: how strong their own contrary information has to be before they should speak, and what they should do when they cannot put any number on it.
- What to do about the fact that whatever mechanism produced our agreement could just as easily run the other way, so that a sudden reversal is not by itself evidence that we were wrong the first time, or that we are wrong now.
- Whether the order in which we speak, or the moment when I as chair say what I think, changes any of this.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a year where the first four speakers agree and the fifth has a nagging reservation they cannot put into words; a year where we all agree and it turns out we should trust that agreement; and the case I would rather not hear, which is that we should reopen last year's decisions.

Finally, tell me honestly where the procedure will misfire, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and which two or three questions it cannot settle at all — those I will have to decide myself, and I would like to know which way you would lean and why.
