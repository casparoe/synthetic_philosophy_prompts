I retired from experimental physics four years ago, after a career spent largely on the statistical mechanics of disordered materials, and since then I have been reading philosophy seriously for the first time since my undergraduate years — as well as a good deal of the literature on the safety and alignment of artificial intelligence, which is where my old habits of thought keep being useful. In that literature I keep returning to one dispute that appears to have run for a decade and more without either side moving. On one side are those who insist that a system counts as safe or aligned only if its behaviour can be guaranteed: that a method which renders catastrophe merely improbable is worthless, since a single unlucky draw is the whole of the loss; that the probability we should be reasoning about is not the one we can estimate from training, because the deployment distribution is precisely what we lack samples of; and that once the loss is absorbing, optimising an expected value over our own uncertainty is a mistake rather than a virtue. On the other side are those who reply that nothing we build is guaranteed — that the guarantees safety-critical engineering actually delivers are conditional on models of the environment, the specification and the hardware which are themselves unverified — that demanding a guarantee about a system produced by a training process we do not control is close to incoherent, and that the only real lever anyone has is to drive the probability of catastrophe down and to make the process that produces these systems more trustworthy. Both sides grant that the loss would be enormous and irreversible; both grant that we cannot inspect the internals of the systems in question; and yet the exchange has the same shape it had when I first encountered it.

What puzzles me is that this does not look like a disagreement about facts. In my own field the choice between a worst-case and a typical-case treatment is an ordinary modelling decision, made deliberately and defended on grounds of what the question requires and what can be computed; statistical mechanics is nothing but typical-case reasoning, and no one would think it a scandal that we cannot guarantee anything about an individual microstate. I have a colleague, a control engineer, who designs for the worst case as a matter of professional obligation and would find the average-case camp's position frivolous. So the standoff is not forced by the subject matter itself. My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, to be tested rather than adopted — is that the two camps are not really disagreeing about worst-case versus average-case reasoning at all, but about what kind of thing a machine-learning system is: a single artifact that will be built once, about which a guarantee is at least intelligible in the way that a guarantee about a bridge is, or a member of a vast population of systems produced by a stochastic process, about which only statistical claims have content, in the way they do for a drug or for a batch of steel. On that reading the worst-case language is a symptom of a deeper disagreement about the object of the claim, and the two sides are answering different questions under one heading.

Please diagnose this rather than adjudicate it. Say whether the dispute is substantive, or whether it is a disagreement about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for; whether a hidden empirical question is doing the work — for instance, whether the training process will in fact generate the kind of pressure the pessimistic arguments assume; and whether my artifact-versus-population hypothesis is the right diagnosis or merely a redescription of the standoff. Where the hypothesis fails, I would like to know how it fails. And I want an honest answer to the question of what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, or a change of question — or an argument that none of these should be expected, together with an explanation of why the same considerations do not make ordinary worst-case engineering irrational. I am not asking for a reading list; I would rather have the diagnosis argued from the positions themselves.
