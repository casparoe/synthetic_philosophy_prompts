I'm a graduate student in logic, and something I used to file away as a party trick has started to feel like it undermines the way I talk about mathematics. A classmate in a realism reading group walked me through the following argument, and I can't find the exit.

(1) Every consistent first-order theory in a countable language with an infinite model has a countable model. (2) Set theory is consistent and has an infinite model, so it has a countable model M. (3) M satisfies the sentence saying that there are uncountable sets, because set theory proves that sentence. (4) But M is countable, so every set in M is countable — including the one M calls the set of all sets of natural numbers. (5) So a single set comes out both countable and uncountable, and the only way out is to say that countable does not describe sets at all, but only describes how a set sits inside a model. (6) Nothing in the reasoning was special to cardinality: by compactness, first-order arithmetic has models whose natural numbers include objects with infinitely many predecessors, so the extension of natural number, and with it finite, is not fixed by the axioms either, and the same goes for well-ordered and ordinal. (7) So no mathematical predicate has an extension that the axioms determine. (8) And if the extension of every mathematical predicate is fixed only relative to a model, then there is no fact of the matter about whether the real numbers outnumber the natural numbers: mathematics tells us what follows from axioms and nothing about any subject matter. (9) But (8) proves far too much. When I say the primes are infinite I take myself to be saying something about the primes, not about a formal system, and I do not believe that the reals outnumber the naturals is a sentence lacking a truth value. So something above has to go.

I know the argument is old and that people think it is dissolved; my worry is what it does after the dissolving is over. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), and I would most like to give up (8) — but I don't see where to stop the slide. If I rescue cardinality by appealing to the intended interpretation of set theory, that same appeal looks available at (6) and (7), and then I want to know what the argument was ever doing. If I accept (8), tell me honestly what survives of mathematical realism: does only a naive version of it die, or does the position go down with it?
