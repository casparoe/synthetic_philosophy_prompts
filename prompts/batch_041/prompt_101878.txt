I have been trying to settle a question about epiphenomenalism — the view that conscious experiences, such as the felt sting of a cut or the taste of coffee, exert no causal influence on the physical world, including the brain and the body's behavior — and I believe I have found a decisive objection, though I would like to check my reasoning before committing to it. My difficulty is this. I can notice that I am in pain, attend to the quality of the pain, and then report it to someone. Each of these acts seems to require the experience to make a difference to my cognitive and motor states. If the pain genuinely did nothing, then nothing in me would register it, and my report would bear no connection to it at all; I would be, with respect to my own experience, in the position of someone confidently asserting facts about a room he has never entered. It appears to follow that the epiphenomenalist cannot even state his own position without contradicting it, since his utterance that there are qualia would itself have to be caused by the very qualia he declares inert. Yet I keep encountering philosophers who treat epiphenomenalism as a live and even attractive option, which makes me suspect I am missing a move. So I would like you to explain how an epiphenomenalist ought to answer my objection and to identify precisely where my reasoning goes wrong. I would also like to know whether, once that confusion is cleared up, any genuine difficulty remains for the view, or whether my objection, properly repaired, turns out to have been pointing at something real.
