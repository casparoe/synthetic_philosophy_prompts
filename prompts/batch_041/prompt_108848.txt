I am presenting, at the next meeting of the grant committee of a foundation that funds animal-welfare interventions, the case for moving a substantial share of our portfolio toward reducing the suffering of farmed insects -- and for doing so even though I put the probability that any given insect is sentient at well under one in a hundred. My argument is a scale argument: the number of insects we rear and kill each year runs to the trillions, several orders of magnitude above the number of farmed vertebrates; the disvalue of an insect's life, if it is sentient at all, may be very small, but multiplied by numbers of that size the expected total dwarfs the expected total for chickens and pigs; and because the credence enters the calculation linearly while the numbers enter it in the exponent, no plausible reduction in the probability of sentience rescues the vertebrate case. I also want to press something stronger: that the right response to our uncertainty is not to shrink the insect term by our credence, but to let the scale term carry almost the whole argument, so that the conclusion survives even if I am wrong by two or three orders of magnitude about the numbers and by two orders of magnitude about the probability.

The committee has eleven members, and I know roughly how each will react. Eight are economists or policy analysts who think in cost-effectiveness ratios, marginal returns, and counterfactual impact; they are fluent in expected-value reasoning but allergic to anything that smells like Pascal's mugging and will want to know why this is not one. Two are moral philosophers: one a consequentialist who will attack the structure of my expected-value calculation rather than its inputs, and one who is not, and who will press me on whether summing welfare over merely possible minds is coherent at all. The last is an entomologist who rears crickets at industrial scale and knows the actual husbandry and slaughter practices from the inside; she is skeptical that any intervention we could fund would move insect welfare in a measurable direction, and she is likely to be my most dangerous questioner precisely because she can undercut the empirical premise instead of the philosophy. I expect the economists to demand a single number -- dollars per unit of insect suffering averted -- and I do not have one.

Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion that follows the presentation: the objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from a room of philosophers of mind; ordered by how much damage each would do to the proposal; each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it; each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold, which objections are answerable in the room and which I should simply grant, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
