I have a set of verdicts about freedom, agency, and responsibility in cases that cluster around Libet-style experiments, and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them. I am not asking for a literature review, a history of the debate, or citations; I want the principle, its consequences, and an honest accounting of where my verdicts come apart. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. In a Libet-style setup, a subject spontaneously flexes a wrist. A readiness potential precedes the reported urge to move by a fraction of a second, and the movement itself is underway before the subject is consciously aware of deciding. I regard the movement as the subject's own free action, at least in the thin sense that matters for ordinary responsibility. The timing does not by itself make it unfree.

2. In the same setup, the subject is hypnotized and instructed to flex when a tone sounds. The subject feels no urge and later says the hand moved on its own. I regard the movement as not free.

3. A pulse of transcranial magnetic stimulation to the motor cortex makes the subject's hand move. The subject says the hand moved, not they. I regard the movement as not free.

4. A person with alien hand syndrome finds one hand acting against their stated intentions, sometimes grasping objects or undoing buttons. I regard those actions as not free, even when the hand's movements are complex and seemingly purposeful.

5. A person with a deep brain stimulator develops a sudden urge to gamble. They act on it, and later say the urge came from the device and did not feel like theirs. I regard the gambling as not free.

6. A person with the same stimulator develops the same sudden urge, acts on it, and later says that although the urge came from the device, they endorsed it and would not have wanted to be without it. I am inclined to regard the gambling as free, or at least as attributable to them in the way that matters for responsibility.

7. A person acts under a credible threat of immediate death, handing over money that is not theirs. They could not have done otherwise in any ordinary sense. I regard them as responsible for the act, though perhaps excused or diminished in blameworthiness, and I do not think the mere fact that they could not have done otherwise settles the matter.

8. A cap reads the readiness potential and blocks the movement unless the subject presses a permission button. The subject learns to press the button just before the urge becomes conscious. The button press is itself predicted by the cap. I regard the movement as free, or at least freer than in case 1, because the subject has been given a genuine veto.

9. A brain scanner predicts a person's decision several seconds before the person becomes aware of it. The prediction is a correlate of the person's own deliberation, not an external cause. I regard the eventual decision as free.

10. A neuroscientist secretly implants a desire in a person, a desire the person would not have had otherwise. The person acts on it, endorses it, and would not want to be rid of it. I regard the action as not free, because the desire was put there by someone else.

My tentative principle is this: an action is free when it flows from the agent's own standing values and reasons, and not from a state that bypasses or alienates the agent; conscious awareness before the act is not necessary, but the agent must have been able to veto or endorse the action in the relevant way. I can already see that this principle may not survive cases 6 and 10, since both involve an induced desire that the agent endorses, yet I want opposite verdicts. I also suspect cases 1 and 3 are in tension: both involve a movement whose neural cause precedes conscious awareness, but I want one to be free and the other not. And case 7 seems to pull against any principle that makes free action depend on the ability to do otherwise.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is doing. For each clause, tell me which case motivates it. Tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed: a person whose preferences are changed by a brain tumor but who endorses the new preferences; a person who endorses an irresistible urge; a person whose values are the product of intense social conditioning but who endorses them; a person whose action is caused by a hypnotic suggestion they have forgotten; and a person whose action is caused by a genuine reason they would have acted on anyway. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up the verdict or complicate the principle to save it. If my verdicts are simply inconsistent, or if the word free is doing two different jobs in these cases, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a synthesis. I would rather be told that the request rests on a confused premise than receive a tidy principle that papers over the tension.
