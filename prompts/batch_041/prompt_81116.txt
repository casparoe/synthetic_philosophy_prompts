I chair the selection panel for my city's public art programme. Twice a year we shortlist three proposals and commission one; the piece stays on the street for decades, the money is public, and the panel is deliberately mixed — a curator, an art historian, a city councillor, and two residents with no art background. Every round we argue badly: the professionals call a proposal sentimental or derivative, the residents say the professionals are defending something nobody will like, and I end up settling it by majority vote and writing a recommendation to council that satisfies no one.

Please turn Hume's account of the standard of taste into a decision procedure I can actually run across two meetings: the questions to put to the panel, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure returns a verdict; and where it must stop and hand the matter to a person, because the question has become one of fact or of values rather than of taste. Keep faith with what Hume's conditions on the good judge actually require — delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, good sense — and be explicit about which of them can be operationalised in a committee room, which can only be approximated, and what happens when nobody on the panel meets them, including the case where we have to judge a tradition none of us knows. Two points must be handled directly, since they are where our arguments actually go: whether the curator's trained eye is the good sense Hume asks for or the prejudice he warns against, and whether a verdict reached this way is a discovery about the work or a disciplined decision about us.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: a large abstract sculpture by a nationally known artist; a figurative memorial the residents have campaigned for and the professionals find banal; and a community-designed mosaic that everyone likes mildly. Include at least one where the procedure's answer is not the one I want.

Sections, in order, with rough lengths:

1. What Hume's conditions actually require — 150 words.
2. The procedure, as numbered steps with stop conditions — about 500 words.
3. Three worked cases — about 250 words each.
4. Where the procedure misfires, and the judgment calls it cannot remove — 200 words.
5. What the procedure presupposes about whether the verdict is true or merely the best-grounded sentiment available — 150 words.
