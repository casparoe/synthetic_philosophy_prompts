A friend who is reading her way through the Church Fathers gave me this thumbnail of Augustine on lying, and I want to know how much of it I can trust:

'Augustine holds that lying is always and intrinsically wrong. Because speech is ordered to truth, every lie violates the order God built into creation, so no circumstance can license one — not to save an innocent life, not to protect a fugitive from a murderer. What makes an utterance a lie, for Augustine, is the will to deceive: someone who sincerely believes a falsehood and asserts it is not lying but merely mistaken. He catalogues eight kinds of lies and places the "helpful lie" — the one that harms no one and spares someone pain — among the lighter kinds, though he still forbids it. He is so thoroughgoing that he reads the apparent deceptions of Scripture, such as the Hebrew midwives before Pharaoh or Jacob's posing as Esau, as not really lies at all, on the grounds that God's larger purpose in those episodes makes them truthful in the end. And he grants one exception that is usually overlooked: when a murderer asks where your friend is hiding, a reply that is literally true but meant to be misunderstood — an equivocation or mental reservation — is not a lie, since the words themselves, taken in the right sense, are true.'

I am inclined to hope the last point is right, since it would make Augustine far less severe than his reputation suggests, but I don't actually know his texts. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of Augustine would accept, what would make him wince, and what he would flatly deny — and then say plainly whether the summary's overall portrait, and the mental-reservation exception in particular, survives the correction. Take a definite position on each point and give your reasons; do not simply lay out the interpretive options and leave the verdict to me. Keep the whole answer under 500 words.
