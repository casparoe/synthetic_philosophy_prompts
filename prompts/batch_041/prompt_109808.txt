I've fallen down a rabbit hole lately on cognitive penetration — the question of whether your beliefs and desires can actually change what you *see*, not just what you judge or infer about what you see. All the standard examples (ambiguous figures, expert radiologists, wine tasters) feel kind of worn out, so I want a short story built on a new conceit that actually puts pressure on the distinction instead of just illustrating it.

Heres the setup I have in mind: a mid-size manufacturer adopts a training program that supposedly lets inspectors perceive a specific kind of micro-crack in metal that untrained people can't detect at all. Our protagonist has been through it and now genuinely seems to see the cracks — she flags defects nobody else catches, and she's usually right. Then there's a safety investigation after an accident, and an auditor has to decide whether her reports count as perception or inference, because it matters legally whether she *saw* the flaw or merely reasoned her way to it. Tell it from both points of view, alternating or whatever works, and don't resolve it. I want the reader to end up unable to say whether she perceives more of the world or just has a much richer theory draped over the same sensory input, and to feel why that isn't just a verbal quibble. Don't make her a mystic or a fraud, and don't make the auditor a philistine — he should have a real point. Around 1500 words, and no citations needed.
