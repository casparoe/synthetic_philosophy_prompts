I retired three years ago from a career in experimental physics, most of it spent on measurement problems — which is to say, on the question of what it means to say that something has been selected at random. Since then I have been reading philosophy of probability with more enthusiasm than competence, and I have agreed to run next month's meeting of a discussion group that gathers at the institute where I used to work. Sixteen people: several working physicists, two biologists, one statistician, three or four philosophers, and a few curious retirees. All are comfortable with undergraduate mathematics; none has read anything on the foundations of probability. We have a large table, a whiteboard, and ninety minutes, and I am willing to spend no more than ten of those minutes talking at them.

I want them to feel the force of Bertrand's chord problem rather than be told about it. My understanding of the setup is as follows: a chord of a circle is drawn at random, and one asks for the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Read one way — two points chosen independently on the circumference — the answer is one third. Read another — a radius chosen, then a point on it, then the perpendicular chord — it is one half. Read a third — a point chosen uniformly in the disc and taken as the midpoint — it is one quarter. Three descriptions of a single event, three incompatible answers, and no evident way to say which description is the right one. Correct me if I have mangled any of that.

Two things should come out of the session, and the second matters more to me than the first. First, that the difficulty is not a slip of arithmetic or a failure of care: the principle of indifference — treat the possibilities as equiprobable when you have no reason to prefer one — does not determine an answer until something further is supplied. I want them to feel the specific discomfort of finding each of the three arguments compelling in turn and then noticing that at most one of them can stand. Second, that what is missing is a symmetry, and that the symmetry has to come from the physical situation rather than from our convenience. I would like them to reach the contrast themselves: why the throw of a needle onto ruled paper is a perfectly well-posed random experiment even though it too seems to rest on a judgement of indifference, and why the microcanonical postulate in statistical mechanics is not embarrassed by Bertrand even though it says, in effect, that all accessible states are equally likely. My own answer, which I would be pleased to see them reach or to argue with, is that in both cases the dynamics supplies the measure — the uniform distribution is preserved by the flow, and the physical act of throwing fixes the mechanism — whereas in Bertrand nothing does. I have a vague sense that the tradition running from Jeffreys through Jaynes is the attempt to supply exactly this, though I have not read either closely enough to teach it, and I would rather the activity not pretend to settle the question.

What I would like from you, concretely:

1. A runnable ninety-minute activity for that room, in which the work is done by the game rather than by me. I have been imagining a betting table: participants acting as odds-makers, each of whom must quote a price on the same event, described to different subgroups in different but equally reasonable ways, followed by a bookmaker who profits from the disagreement. The stakes should be non-monetary but not negligible to the participants — standing among colleagues would do — and the exploitation should follow from prices they set themselves, in good faith, rather than from a trick.

2. Materials and set-up at the level of a recipe: what I print, what I hand out, how I divide the room, what each subgroup is told and — just as important — what it is not told. I would like part of the activity to have them physically produce random chords by methods of their own devising and pool the results, so that the disagreement is visible in a histogram before anyone names it.

3. A timeline in minutes, including the moment at which I am permitted to say the words Bertrand's paradox aloud. They should not hear the name, or the three answers, until they have committed to something.

4. The debrief, mapped onto what happened: which moment in the game corresponds to which part of the problem, and how I move from the bookmaker's profit to the point about symmetry without reverting to a lecture.

5. What will predictably go wrong the first time, and what to do when it does. I expect that someone will insist that one of the three descriptions is simply the correct reading of the word random — and will be right in an unhelpful way — and that the group will try to settle the matter by majority vote. Tell me how to handle both without humiliating anyone.

6. A frank statement of what the activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply myself. I would rather know in advance which part of the idea will remain a claim I am making rather than something they have felt.

If it can be made to fit, I would like the last few minutes to gesture at a live case: I am told that cosmologists face a version of this problem when assigning probabilities in an eternally inflating universe, where different choices of how to slice spacetime yield different answers to what appears to be the same question, and that this is treated as an unresolved embarrassment rather than a technicality. It should be clear that I am reporting this at second hand.

I do not want a reading list; the group would not use it and I would not defend it. I want the activity.
