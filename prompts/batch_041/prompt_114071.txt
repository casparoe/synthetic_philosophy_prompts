I am a fifth-year PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and as the graduate representative I sit on my department's undergraduate curriculum committee. On Thursday we vote on a motion to retire our three-course Medieval / Renaissance / Early Modern survey sequence and replace it with four thematic units that deliberately cut across the standard periods — Institutions of Knowledge; Translation and Transmission; Empire and Its Instruments; The Book and Its Afterlives — each spanning roughly 300 to 1800. A counter-proposal from a third colleague keeps the sequence intact but attaches a mandatory periodization note of forty words or fewer to every course description, in which the instructor states whether the period label is doing chronological, causal, or purely administrative work, and permits a course to be tagged with more than one period.

Two external constraints have turned this from a seminar dispute into a live one. The Dean's office has circulated a draft Historical Frameworks policy requiring every course description to name the period it covers from a fixed vocabulary (Ancient, Medieval, Renaissance/Early Modern, Modern, Contemporary) and to justify in one sentence why that period's boundaries fall where they do. Our registrar has separately warned that the state's common course-numbering agreement allocates transfer credit by period category, so a transcript reading Empire and Its Instruments may not transfer to the other campuses.

Please draft the committee's written opinion, of a length I can circulate and read aloud before Thursday's vote. It should recommend one course of action — the motion, the counter-proposal, or something else — and end with an actionable bottom line covering the shape of the sequence next year and what, if anything, we say back to the Dean's office and the registrar. The reasons should be ones the proposers and the registrar can actually respond to, and the memo should say plainly which view of what periods are the recommendation commits us to.

Take two objections seriously rather than waving them off. One proposer argues that periods are not containers but claims about when a real break occurred, and that our thematic units smuggle period assumptions in anyway — they all stop at 1800 — while presenting themselves as free of them. A colleague on the other side says the periods are nineteenth-century constructions and that retaining them is a political choice about which past counts. I suspect that is too quick, but I cannot articulate why in a way that would survive contact with a meeting.

I would also like a concrete rule for how period labels should be used in our catalog copy going forward, something a colleague could apply while actually writing a course description rather than a general remark about the social construction of history. Say what the decision commits us to at the graduate level, and address briefly whether our Scientific Revolution course needs its title changed — its instructor teaches it as a single event, while the readings run from 1500 to 1750 and move at visibly different speeds in astronomy and in medicine. I don't need a reading list; I need a document I can defend in the room.
