I am a novelist at work on a book about a Cambridge logician and her doctoral student, and the two of them fall out over Frege's distinction between sense and reference. I want the student's objection to be genuinely formidable, so I have been trying to reconstruct it carefully, and I should confess at once that I find it convincing. The argument, as I have her put it, runs as follows. Frege holds that the names Hesperus and Phosphorus share a reference but differ in sense, and that this difference of sense is what explains why the identity statement is informative whereas the trivial self-identity is not. Frege also holds that the sense of a name determines its reference. But if the sense determines the reference, then two names whose senses differ ought to determine different references: a difference in the mode of presentation should issue in a difference in the object presented. Yet in the Hesperus case two distinct senses deliver one and the same object. The student concludes that Frege's position is internally inconsistent, and that the only way to rescue it is to surrender the claim that sense determines reference, which he never seems willing to do.

I should be grateful if you would tell me whether the argument is sound. If it is not, I should like to know precisely which step fails and what Frege actually maintains, since I want the student to be pressing a real difficulty rather than an imagined one, and I would rather she lose the argument on a subtle point than win it by misreading him. Please write in British English, in a single paragraph of no more than 220 words.
