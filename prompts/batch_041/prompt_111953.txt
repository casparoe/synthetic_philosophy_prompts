I am a high school senior, and my history teacher has asked each of us to write down, in our own words, what we think historical knowledge is and how we think it should be judged, so that the rest of the class can argue with us. Before I hand mine in, I would like an honest audit of whether the position I have assembled actually holds together, because I suspect that at least one part of it is doing more work than it can bear.

Here is what I believe.

1. There is a fact of the matter about the past. Events occurred or they did not, and this is independent of who remembers them, how they are described, and whether anyone writes them down.

2. The record we inherit is largely the work of whoever held power. Which events were documented, which documents survived, which accounts became the ones taught in schools, and even which kinds of evidence were treated as authoritative were determined far more by winners and institutions than by what actually mattered or what actually happened.

3. Even so, history is not merely a contest of narratives. Historians can establish many claims about the past with genuine confidence, because there are methods that give us real leverage: criticism of sources, agreement between independent sources, physical and archaeological evidence, and the practice of reading a source against its own purpose in order to recover what it was trying to hide. These methods can themselves be evaluated and improved, and their results are not merely expressions of whoever happens to be using them.

4. To understand a past society, you have to assess its practices by its own standards. Judging people of the past by the values of my own time is anachronistic. If a practice was widely accepted and made sense within its own moral world, then calling it simply wrong is a failure of historical understanding rather than a moral insight.

5. At the same time, some of what happened in the past was wrong, and wrong not only relative to us; I do not think a massacre is merely something we happen to disapprove of. This is also why I think moral views can genuinely improve over time, and not merely change.

What I want is a verdict on the set, not on each item separately. Which of these actually pull against each other, which only appear to, and how serious is each real conflict? Where a real conflict exists, tell me what would have to be weakened or given up and what I would lose by doing so, and rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. I would prefer to repair the set rather than abandon it, so if there is a reading under which most of these can be kept, I would like to know whether it is a genuine reconciliation or simply a way of not choosing.
