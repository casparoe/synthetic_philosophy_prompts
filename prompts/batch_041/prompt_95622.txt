I'm a graduate student in a computer science department, auditing a seminar on the safety of machine-learning systems, and I keep colliding with a disagreement I cannot resolve on my own. One camp makes what I have come to think of as the prosaic bet: that the alignment of systems built with the methods we already have — pretraining on human text, then tuning on human judgments, wrapping the result in monitors, filters, and refusals, and patching whatever misbehavior turns up in evaluations — will eventually become reliable without any new conceptual insight, simply by making each of those steps broader, sharper, and more adversarial. The other camp says this whole family of methods is structurally incapable of delivering what it promises, because the signal we are training against is itself produced by fallible, non-robust processes, because a system can satisfy an evaluation without satisfying the intent behind it, and because any technique that buys robustness by inspecting more evaluations remains bounded by the distribution of evaluations we happen to run. The prosaic side answers that this is just ordinary engineering under uncertainty, and that the demand for a new insight is a demand for a guarantee no engineering discipline ever gets.

I would like you to write an essay that takes a position on this controversy and defends it. To keep the dispute from floating free, hold it against a concrete target: say that a system is adequately aligned for a given deployment when, across the inputs and incentives it will actually encounter there, it reliably does what its operators intended and does not exploit the gap between what they checked and what they meant. The question, then, is whether that target is reachable by piling up better versions of current techniques, or whether it requires something we do not yet have — and I want you to be honest about what kind of question this is: is the disagreement ultimately empirical, to be settled by seeing what scaling does, or is it conceptual, such that no amount of empirical success would count as settling it? Weigh the strongest version of each side rather than a caricature, and do not end on a fence. Reach a verdict and justify it; a conditional verdict is acceptable if you make the condition precise and say why it holds.

Please write in polished, formal prose and in American English. Aim for something I could read aloud in the seminar without embarrassment: rigorous, concrete where it can be, and willing to concede the points where the other side is right.
