I am drafting a short story of about fifteen hundred words, and I would like you to write it.

My protagonist is a woman in her late sixties who has spent thirty years and most of a family fortune on a single institution whose only purpose is to carry one thing — a body of testimony, a set of instructions, a seed archive, the choice is yours — across deep time. Her commitment is not sentimental. She holds what is properly called a fanatical view: since the far future may contain an astronomically large number of minds, the expected value of steering it swamps any ordinary good available to her now, and this remains so even when her probability of success is very small. She will sacrifice almost anything to it.

Her oldest friend, an engineer, is dying, and has spent his last months on the question of whether we might be living in a simulation. He does not claim to know that we are; he claims only that the hypothesis has some appreciable probability, and that this should alter how she weighs things. If our world is simulated, the far future she is trying to steer is probably not the real one, so her astronomical stakes must be multiplied by a discount factor. She answers that a discount factor is a constant, and no constant turns an astronomical number into a small one; what would actually dampen her is a change in the shape of what she values, not in its probability.

The crux I want the story to hold in view, without any character stating it outright, is that the simulation hypothesis dampens her fanaticism only if it discounts far-future value selectively. If simulated minds count for less, or for nothing, then the discount falls on the present good she forgoes and the future good she pursues alike, and her priorities are untouched — her friend's illness, and her own, are discounted too. The reader should feel how hard it is to defend the selective discount without also devaluing the suffering in front of her.

Requirements: the argument should be enacted rather than explained — at most one brief exchange of it, with the rest carried by scenes, objects, and decisions; both characters should be intelligent and neither a straw man; do not resolve the question, but end on a concrete moment that leaves the crux intact; no named philosophers and no references to any literature; third person, past tense.
