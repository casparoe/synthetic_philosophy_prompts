I'm putting together a unit on hedonism and I want to use The Picture of Dorian Gray as the literary centerpiece, but I'm sick of the usual Wilde-was-a-hedonist-discuss essay question. What I actually want is a reading of the novel through the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the worry that pleasure isn't one thing at all, that the pleasure of a hot bath and the pleasure of finally getting a proof to work and the pleasure of watching someone you hate fail have nothing in common, so any theory that says pleasure is the good is either empty or quietly smuggles in one favoured kind and pretends the rest don't count. Does the novel see this? Lord Henry preaches a new hedonism, Dorian spends the middle stretch of the book collecting perfumes and jewels and Catholic ritual and music and embroidery and other people's ruin, and it reads like a catalogue of pleasures with no shared currency. Is that Wilde dramatizing the heterogeneity problem, or is the novel itself committing the mistake, treating pleasure as one undifferentiated intoxicant you just chase harder and harder until it goes stale? I keep going back and fourth about wether the ending vindicates hedonism or buries it, so I'd like you to take a position on that too.

Be honest about where the reading breaks down — if the novel resists the lens in places, say so instead of forcing it. I'd like specific scenes and moments to point the discussion at, a clear line on where Wilde is ahead of the philosophers and where he's just trading on vibes, and a closing thought on what the novel adds to the debate that the argument on its own can't.
