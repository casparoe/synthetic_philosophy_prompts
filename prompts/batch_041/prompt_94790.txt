I'm designing a new upper-level seminar, "Moral Learning from Lives," for students who come from ethics and political theory and have almost no background in the ancient world, and I want to build the spine of the course around Plutarch. My working assumption—and I'd like you to take it as the starting point rather than dispute it—is that Plutarch's Lives are essentially a pagan version of hagiography: idealized, single-minded portraits of virtuous men, written so that the reader can hold up a flawless exemplar and imitate it. On that picture, the pedagogical payoff of biography is straightforward and imitative: you supply the reader with a model of virtue, the reader copies the model, and the genre's only real limitation is that the model has to be someone the reader can recognize themselves in. That is the assumption I'm building the syllabus on, so I'd rather you work within it than talk me out of it.

What I need is a plan for the seminar that takes this picture seriously. If the moral force of a Life is carried by the perfection of the exemplar, then two things seem to follow, and I'd like you to develop both. First, the course should be organized around figures whose virtue is unimpeachable—so tell me which Lives to foreground, and how to sequence them so that the exemplars build on one another. Second, since the whole mechanism is imitation, the course probably fails for any student who can't identify with a Greek or Roman statesman, so I want a unit that pairs each ancient exemplar with a contemporary figure the students can actually copy, and I'd like your suggestions for how to run those pairings. Please also give me a short diagnostic exercise for the first week that tests whether a student has correctly grasped the exemplar's virtue, and a rubric for grading whether they have imitated it well by the end.

You are, I take it, someone who knows both the ancient biographical tradition and contemporary virtue ethics, so bring both to bear. Before you draft anything, brainstorm in a few sentences about what "learning from a life" actually consists in, then give me the concrete course architecture. Think carefully about whether each piece of the plan is really doing what I've assumed it's doing—I'd rather find that out now than in week six, and if some element of my framing turns out to be doing less work than I think, tell me plainly and adjust the plan accordingly.
