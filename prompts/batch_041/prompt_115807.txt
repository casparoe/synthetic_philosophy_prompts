I've been covering behavioral econ and decision theory for a few years now, and somewhere along the way I stopped just reporting on the money-pump arguement and started believing it. The version I've talked myself into: a preference structure is defective -- not quirky, defective -- if someone who knows it and has deep enough pockets can get you to trade your way, step by step, right back to where you started minus some cash. And I don't think the test stops at one sitting. If your discounting is time-inconsistent, or your tastes shift in ways you can see coming, the same thing is happening to you across your life: a long sequence of choices each of which you'd endorse at the time, leaving you worse off than the version of you who could just... not. So the norm I seem to be running is something like: rationality means having preferences you can't be led around by the nose by, including led by your own future self, unless you've rigged up some commitment device to stop it.

I'm fairly sure this is the standard line and fairly sure it's too clean. What should count against it? I want actual defeaters, ranked, not a survey. Specifically: does the pump only bite agents who are naive about being pumped, i.e. is the real norm foresight rather than coherence -- and if so, is the test measuring anything at all? What happens if you drop the assumptions that money is a universal sweetener and that there's a common currency to pump with? And I keep suspecting there's a whole class of cases where the agent is perfectly transitive and perfectly time-consistent at every single choice point and still gets walked down a path they'd never have picked -- the dial-turning case, where every increment is a small improvement on the last and the total is a catastrophe. If that counts as pumpable, does the test indict the preferences or the sequential setting they're embedded in? Same question for a household or a committee: each member coherent on their own, joint choices cycling anyway. Tell me what this whole view predicts that its rivals don't, which of these the defenders would themselves concede rather than wave off as question-begging, and whether the thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- because if every counterexample gets answered with 'well, they aren't really being pumped, they could have foreseen it / they value the process / they aren't consequentialist about the sequence,' then what observation could ever have shown the criterion was wrong? I'd be most moved by a case where the pump clearly works and we still think the agent is being perfectly reasonable, or by a formal setting (ambiguity, incommensurable values, whatever) where pumpability and irrationality come apart. Don't send me reading lists -- I don't need the literature, just the arguement.
