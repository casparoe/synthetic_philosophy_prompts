For the past two years I have run the international assignment programme at a mid-sized engineering consultancy: roughly sixty incoming staff and visiting researchers a year, from about twenty countries, staying anywhere between three months and three years. I have recently come to think that our destination briefings are the worst kind of document — long inventories of local practice with no principle behind them, which tell a visitor what people here do but never whether they are required to do it, permitted to skip it, or entitled to refuse. I want to fix that properly, and I want a procedure rather than my own opinions.

The reason this has become pressing is personal before it is professional. I arrived in this country myself two years ago with my family, and spent the first six months being corrected — sometimes gently, sometimes not — about matters I had not known were matters: when to tip and how much, which side of the escalator to stand on, whether crossing an empty road at six in the morning is an offence or merely a habit, how to greet someone considerably senior to me. I could never tell which of those corrections registered a genuine obligation and which registered only somebody's preference, and our briefings reproduce exactly that confusion at scale, with our firm's authority behind it.

What I need is a decision procedure that the coordinators who draft our destination notes can actually run. They have no philosophy background; they will have a list of perhaps forty items per destination; they should be able to process one item in about ten minutes. Their verdicts have to be defensible to our HR director when an incoming researcher complains that we told them to do something humiliating, and equally defensible when a host team complains that we failed to warn someone. The output of the procedure must be briefing language a newcomer can act on, not a lecture.

Please write the procedure itself — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence stops with a no, and where it must hand the item to a person because the question is one of values rather than of fact. It should be grounded in what is actually known about conventions, social norms and their relation to morality, and it must keep several distinctions that our current briefings collapse. It must separate mere customs (patterns people follow without expecting others to follow them) from coordination conventions (practices that work only because nearly everyone does the same thing, where some solution was needed and this one was settled on) from socially enforced norms (practices whose breach invites resentment) from genuine moral requirements that a convention merely happens to encode; and it must separate legal rules from all four. It must also keep three questions apart that our briefings run together: whether the visitor is obligated, whether we should advise compliance anyway, and whether a local is entitled to feel wronged rather than merely inconvenienced. Where a verdict depends on a contested premise — for instance on whether visitors owe respect-based compliance with harmless but silly local practices, or on whether the visitor's own moral convictions govern — the procedure should say so at that step rather than smuggling in an answer.

The tests I have in mind, which you should order, refine, add to or replace as the theory requires, include at least these: whether the same standard is being applied to locals and visitors, since I suspect we demand of visitors what we excuse in ourselves; whether the practice was knowable to an outsider without insider knowledge; whether breach sets back anyone's interests independently of the convention; whether the practice is load-bearing for coordination and whether breach degrades it for others; whether a reasonable local would feel wronged or only annoyed; whether the host has standing to set house rules in their own space and whether the visitor's stay depends on hospitality; who bears the cost of compliance and whether that cost falls on the visitor because they cannot push back; whether the visitor can decline without losing what they came for; and whether the visitor has an objection that survives the same-standard test. Say explicitly where the procedure cannot decide — divided local opinion, a private household, a practice whose breach is criminal — and route those to a human.

Then run the procedure on four items and show your working at each step: tipping in the destination country; queueing for a service where the queue is orderly; crossing against a pedestrian signal on an empty street at night; and a deferential greeting ritual, expected of junior and of female staff towards senior partners, that our local partners treat as ordinary courtesy and that some of our incoming engineers find demeaning. I expect at least one verdict I will not like — I suspect the procedure will tell me that parts of our current briefing present as duties things that are nothing of the kind, and that we have been quietly making our visitors carry costs we would not impose on locals. State that plainly rather than softening it.

Please give the worked applications as a table with these columns: Item; What kind of thing it is (custom / coordination convention / socially enforced norm / encoded moral requirement / legal rule); Verdict for the visitor (obligatory / compliance advised though not obligatory / optional / decline); The step that decides it; Where judgement remains; One-sentence line for the visitor's briefing; One-sentence note for the host team. After the table, give a JSON object with the fields residual_judgement_calls (an array of objects, each with question, why_the_procedure_cannot_settle_it, and who_should_decide) and limits_of_the_procedure (an array of strings, each naming a way the procedure will misfire and the kind of case that exposes it). Finally, in no more than 200 words, say what the procedure cannot do.

Please write the whole response in British English — spelling, idiom and punctuation.
