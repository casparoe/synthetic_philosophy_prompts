I am assembling a specification document for a semantics working group, and I want the requirements settled before we evaluate any particular system. Write the desiderata that any two-dimensional framework for meaning and reference must satisfy in order to count as adequate.

For each desideratum, state the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should cover, at minimum: the data the framework must reproduce (the a posteriori necessities, the necessary a posteriori, the contingent a priori, the apparent cognitive significance of co-referential names, the failure of substitutivity in attitude contexts); the distinctions it must not collapse (epistemic versus metaphysical modality; the two intensions and what each is meant to represent; the difference between the context-sensitivity of a term's use and the contingency of its referent); and the theoretical constraints it inherits (rigid designation and the direct-reference picture, compositionality, the link between the primary intension and what a speaker can know independently of experience, and the demand that the framework not merely relocate the puzzle of cognitive significance rather than solve it).

Then the part I care about most. I want the framework to handle a case the textbook examples do not cover well: a natural-kind term introduced by a community on the basis of a conception that later turns out to be internally heterogeneous, so that competent speakers' uses diverge and the experts eventually recognize two distinct kinds beneath one inherited word. What should the framework say the primary intension was at the earlier stage, and how should it treat the later one? Treat this not as an anomaly to quarantine but as a genuine constraint, and say whether accommodating it forces a choice among desiderata that otherwise look independent.

Flag every pair of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement rules out the largest number of otherwise attractive candidate frameworks. Finally, state what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to revise your verdict on the necessity or the joint satisfiability of these requirements, and where you judge your own reasoning most likely to fail.
