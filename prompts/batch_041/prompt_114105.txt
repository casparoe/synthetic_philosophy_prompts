Write a short story of about 1,500 words about a field linguist documenting a language isolate spoken by a small community that lives by trapping a single kind of prey. She is building a translation manual and discovers that two rival manuals fit every scrap of behavioral evidence she can collect. The first treats the community's word for the prey animal as a predicate of enduring, countable objects — roughly, "that is a rabbit." The second treats it as a predicate of brief, spatially bounded episodes or portions — roughly, "there is rabbit-here-now" — and compensates elsewhere in the grammar so that the two manuals generate the same verdicts about every utterance she can elicit. Have a colleague argue that the two manuals are merely underdetermined by the available evidence, so that the indeterminacy would evaporate once all the physical facts were known; the story should dramatize why the linguist doubts this.

The plot should turn on a concrete dispute within the community over a trap and a captured animal, in which one member's testimony ("that is the same one I saw before") comes out true under the object-manual and false, or ill-formed, under the stage-manual. Do not resolve the story by uncovering a hidden fact that favors one manual, and do not let either manual emerge as the correct one; the point of the piece is that the divergence survives even after the linguist has exhausted the evidence and has reasoned as carefully as she can. Keep the community and its language invented rather than drawn from any real ethnographic case.

After the story, add a brief note stating how confident you are in the philosophical claims the story depends on, identifying any place where it leans on a contested reading of the indeterminacy thesis, and singling out the one point about which you are least sure.
