I teach a master's-level module on probability and rationality, and I am now drafting the summer paper. My students are, by this stage, extremely fluent in the mechanics: they can state the probability axioms, set out the betting interpretation of credence, and reconstruct a Dutch book against an agent whose degrees of belief violate additivity without hesitating. What they cannot do is say why the exploitability of such an agent is supposed to be a failure of epistemic rationality rather than simply unwelcome news about her betting prospects. When I press them, they either insist that incoherent credences must be irrational because they are exploitable — which is the thing to be proved, not a reason for it — or they reach for the accuracy-based alternative as though it had settled the matter, when in fact it moves the question. I want an essay question that makes both of those shortcuts unavailable.

Please draft that question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question should appear in the form it would take on the paper, as a single coherent task rather than a numbered list of prompts, and it should require the candidate to do at least three things: first, to say precisely which premises the argument needs and which of them do the real work — in particular the assumption that the agent will accept each bet in the package considered on its own, so that the fact that the package as a whole guarantees a loss cannot be used to refuse it, and the assumption that she regards the offered odds as fair, i.e. that she is an expected-utility maximiser with respect to money, so that the candidate cannot dodge the questions of risk aversion, non-linear utility in money, or outright refusal of the betting interpretation; second, to say what, if anything, follows for the claim that non-probabilistic credences are epistemically irrational as opposed to merely practically disadvantageous; and third, to assess whether the accuracy-first strategy — assessing a credence function by how close it comes to the truth, under a strictly proper scoring rule — closes that gap or relocates it. The question should be answerable in about fifty minutes and be worth 20 marks. The module is an MA option, so the candidates will have read the standard material but not necessarily the recent literature, and nothing should turn on their knowing who said what.

The model answer should run to roughly 700–800 words. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, each with its mark allocation summing to 20, and, for each point, a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong — for instance treating exploitability as self-evidently an epistemic defect, or treating risk aversion as a mere failure of nerve rather than a genuine constraint on the argument. Please add a short note on what separates a first from a 2:1 answer; I want that to turn on whether the candidate can hold the pragmatic and the epistemic questions apart rather than on how much of the literature they name. Throughout, keep clearly separate what is settled among people who work on this — that the Dutch book theorem is a theorem, for instance, and what it establishes about which sets of credences are vulnerable — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether exploitability is an epistemic defect at all, whether accuracy-first arguments are themselves free of pragmatic assumptions, and whether the reliance on a separability principle can be repaired. Do not let the model answer present a contested verdict as the consensus, and where a defensible line would deserve full marks even though it differs from the model answer, say so. Please write everything in British English.
