I have an argument, assembled with a colleague, whose premises each strike me as plausible and whose conclusion I cannot accept.

(1) An agent is morally responsible for an action only if she owns it — only if it issues from her own practical reasoning rather than from a cause alien to her.
(2) In the standard Frankfurt case, the agent deliberates and decides exactly as she would have without the counterfactual intervener; the intervener never acts and contributes nothing to what she does.
(3) She is nonetheless responsible for what she does.
(4) So responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise, and what grounds it is the actual sequence of events that produced the action.
(5) In every case that actual sequence is a chain of prior events indifferent to the agent. A Frankfurt case differs from an ordinary deterministic one only in that the alternative is blocked by a device that happens not to fire rather than by the laws of nature.
(6) So if the actual sequence suffices for responsibility in a Frankfurt case, it suffices in any deterministic case.
(7) Hence anyone who acts at all is responsible for what she does, however she came to act.
(8) That is absurd: it would make the coerced and the brainwashed responsible.

I would rather keep (1) than (8). Which step should I reject, why does it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost a leeway incompatibilist, a source incompatibilist, and a compatibilist respectively? Answer in under 400 words.
