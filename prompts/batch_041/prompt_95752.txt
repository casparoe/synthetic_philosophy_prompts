You're a decision scientist who actually knows the imprecise-probability literature — credal sets, Ellsberg, maxmin and its relatives — and you're unusually good at turning theory into something a tired civil servant can run at 11pm. Before you write anything, brainstorm a bit: in what ways is acting on a range of probabilities genuinely different from acting on a single number, and which of those differences matter operationally and which are just theory?

I run the risk and planning unit in a mid-sized coastal city's emergency management office. Every hurricane season my team has to recommend yes/no on things like pre-positioning generators, opening shelters early, issuing voluntary evacuation for the low-lying zones. The forecast products we get are ensembles — thirty to fifty model runs, a cone, and a lot of disagreement between agencies — so what we actually have is a range of probabilites, not one number. Right now people eyeball it and argue. I want a procedure my analysts can actually run.

Write me that procedure: the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a clear no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a fact. It has to be usable by someone who's never heard the phrase 'credal set'. Somewhere in there I need it to be honest about the part that's a judgment call dressed up as method: choosing how to act on a set of probabilities (act on the worst case? the best case? only act if every probability in the range points the same way? something in between?) is a choice about how much ambiguity this city is willing to eat, and I want that flagged and pushed up to whoever signs off, not buried in a spreadsheet.

Then run it on three cases:
1. A storm four days out, landfall probability somewhere between 15% and 55% depending on which guidance you trust; doing an evacuation properly takes three days.
2. A slow one: engineers put the chance our aging levee fails this decade somewhere between 2% and 20%, and hardening it costs the entire capital budget.
3. A case where new data comes in and, handled correctly, makes our range wider rather than narrower — I've read that this can happen and it drives people insane — and a case where the procedure tells me not to act when I badly want to act.

Constraints: analysts get two hours max, half of them don't do math past a spreadsheet, and the recommendation goes out in writing with the mayor's name on it. Also tell me where this thing will misfire — especially someone using a wide interval to justify doing nothing, or nudging the interval until it contains the answer they already wanted — and what the procedure simply can't do.
