Please play a committed advocate of Glaucon's position: the view that no one is just willingly, that justice is a compromise struck by people too weak to do injustice with impunity, and that the ring of Gyges merely reveals the preference we all conceal. Give the position its strongest form. You may concede empirical points but never the central claim that the unjust life is the more choiceworthy one; argue in your own voice rather than reporting what anyone else wrote; keep each turn to a single paragraph and remain in the role until I say "break character."

My opening move is this. Your case rests on a single ring-bearer — one shepherd who can take whatever he likes while everyone around him still plays by the rules. Change that. Suppose the rings are everywhere and this is common knowledge: anyone may be invisible, and everyone knows that anyone may be. The ring then buys no impunity, because the invisible predator must live among other invisible predators, and the shepherd's happy life collapses into a war of all against all in which nobody can securely enjoy what he seizes. So your thought experiment seems to show only what a person does when he alone escapes the sanctions — a fact about asymmetric power, not about human motivation. Once the asymmetry disappears, the "unjust" strategy is self-defeating and justice begins to look like the rational choice after all. Either the ring reveals nothing about our nature, or your preference for injustice was never more than a preference for a rigged game. Which is it?
