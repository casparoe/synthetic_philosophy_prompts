You are drafting the opening section of an internal style guide for a fictional corporate-communications consultancy. The guide is titled "Statements That Do Things," and its premise is that the firm has taken the theory of performative utterances entirely seriously and converted it into editorial policy. Write the section as a deadpan, numbered house manual for drafting apologies, resignations, commitments, and disclaimers, in the voice of a fussy senior editor who treats questions of philosophical felicity as ordinary drafting problems.

The rules you set down should be genuinely derived from speech-act theory, and should get it right:

- why some utterances pass the "hereby" test (they perform an act in being said) while others merely describe;
- why a statement can fail to be an apology because the speaker lacks the standing or the recognized procedure to make one, as opposed to failing because the speaker does not mean it;
- the difference between what an utterance does in being said and what it merely causes in its audience;
- why promising, resigning, or apologizing depends on conventions and institutions, not sincerity alone;
- what becomes of a performative when it is quoted, scripted, or embedded in fiction.

Keep it funny by keeping it literal: the guide should treat a spokesperson's lack of authority to resign, or the absence of any recognized procedure for forgiving, as an editorial defect to be corrected with better wording. The underlying target is the corporate habit of issuing statements that purport to perform acts — apologizing, committing, resigning — while quietly failing the conditions for doing so; the guide exposes this by taking that pretense entirely at face value. The humor should come from the content of the rules, not from ridiculing the firm.

Do not explain the theory to the reader, and do not cite any authors, works, or quotations; let the rules imply the theory. The result should be legible to a reader who has never encountered the word "performative." Close with a single-sentence "bottom line" rule that the editor claims disposes of every borderline case.

Hard limit: 800 words.
