My opening challenge is this: if the sacrificial commandments were a concession to the idolatrous imagination, then the Torah's detailed cultic legislation looks like a remedy for a disease, not a permanent good. Yet the Law treats the sacrificial service as central and enduring, and after the Temple's destruction the synagogue's prayers are modelled on the sacrifices. A modern critic might add that animal sacrifice is best explained as a costly signal of devotion, a way of proving commitment rather than a divine accommodation to error. So either your account makes the sacrifices a temporary pedagogical crutch that the Law wrongly embalms, or the sacrifices have a value in themselves that your account cannot capture.
