I recently moved from software engineering into a systems neuroscience lab, and I keep encountering the claim that the brain is a computer — sometimes as a harmless metaphor, sometimes as a literal metaphysical thesis, and I can't tell whether the two sides are even disagreeing about the same thing. Acting as a philosopher of cognitive science with real familiarity both with computational neuroscience and with the metaphysics of computation, please give me your honest credence that the human brain literally implements a computation in the same sense a laptop does, rather than merely being usefully describable in computational terms. I want a number, and I want the decomposition. I'd expect it to turn on at least these sub-questions: whether "implementing a computation" is an objective, determinate matter at all, or is instead relative to an observer's chosen description — the worry that any physical system, a wall or a bucket of water, can be mapped onto some formal system; whether, granting objectivity, the brain's causal organization singles out one computation or a narrow class of them, rather than many that fit the data equally well; whether the continuous, noisy, analog character of neural dynamics genuinely supports a discrete-state computational description; and whether "computer" should require something like programmability that the brain may lack. Brainstorm the candidate sub-questions first, then assign each a rough probability with one line of justification, show how they combine into a final number, and tell me which term you are least confident in and what evidence would move it most. Take a definite position and give your reasons rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. Write in American English.
