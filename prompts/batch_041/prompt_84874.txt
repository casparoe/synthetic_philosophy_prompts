I am a retired analytical chemist. Thirty-eight years in environmental monitoring, most of it spent converting continuous instrument signals into discrete verdicts about whether a sample did or did not comply with a regulatory limit. Six years ago I retired and began reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than competence. The essay below is the first thing I have tried to write myself; it grew out of a reading group at the local university, where I was told that the sorites puzzle shows that classical logic cannot be right about vague predicates, and where I found myself unable to believe this and equally unable to say why. I would be grateful for your comments. I should say at the outset that I am not asking for a reading list or for references — I would not know what to do with them, and I would rather have your assessment in your own words: whether the distinction I draw is real, whether it does the work I claim for it, and where it breaks.

Two Kinds of Vagueness

1. The puzzle, and my complaint about it.

A single grain of sand does not make a heap. If n grains fail to make a heap, then neither do n+1 grains. Therefore no number of grains makes a heap. The bald man runs the same way, and so does every other predicate with a gradient behind it. I understand that the literature contains several families of response — that truth admits of degrees; that a borderline sentence is neither true nor false because its predicate can be sharpened in many equally good ways; that the boundary is in fact perfectly sharp and merely unknowable. I am in no position to adjudicate among these and this essay does not attempt to. My complaint is narrower. The puzzle, as it is usually stated, runs together two situations that have very little to do with one another, and once they are separated, one of them turns out not to be puzzling at all.

2. Two situations.

For most of my career the operative rule was this: a sample is contaminated if the concentration of the analyte exceeds five micrograms per litre. The predicate was not vague. But the procedure by which we decided whether it was satisfied was coarse. Below about one microgram per litre my instrument could not reliably distinguish a sample from a blank, so in that band the procedure returned no verdict. Nobody concluded that contamination was a vague matter. The quantity was whatever it was; the instrument had a resolution.

Now compare heap. Here, I want to say, there is no instrument aimed at an antecedent fact. The procedure — counting, or judging by eye, or comparing against a paradigm — is what settles the question. But it too has a resolution: it returns verdicts on the clear cases and nothing in the middle. Let me call the first kind of borderline case constitutive vagueness, where the predicate's satisfaction conditions are themselves open, so that no procedure, however refined, could settle the case without arbitrariness; and the second resolution vagueness, where the conditions are settled by a procedure that is simply too coarse to reach the middle cases.

My claim is that most of the predicates philosophers worry about — person, alive, just, child — are resolution-vague rather than constitutively vague, and that this is a substantive and partly empirical claim about our practices, not a logical claim about our words. It may also be false. If every vague predicate is in fact constitutively vague, then my distinction collapses into a difference of degree within one phenomenon, and I would want to be told so plainly.

3. What this does to the paradox.

For a resolution-vague predicate, the step from one case to the next — if n grains fail to make a heap, then n+1 grains fail to make one — is not licensed by the predicate's meaning. It is licensed only by the assumption that the procedure's verdicts form a single uninterrupted chain, which is exactly what a coarse procedure fails to deliver: it gives verdicts at the ends and no verdict in the middle, and no verdict is not something one can iterate. On this reading the paradox is produced by treating a gap in a practice as though it were a verdict.

I am aware that this sounds like sleight of hand, so let me say what I mean. I do not say that the middle cases are really true, or really false, or that we merely do not know which. I say that the predicate, as we operate it, is only partially defined: the procedure fixes an extension and an anti-extension and leaves a gap. That is a third status, but not a third truth value; it is a status of the practice. Whether a gap in a practice can be a respectable semantic category, or whether I have simply reinvented one of the standard positions in a lab coat, is one of the things I most want to be told.

4. Two ways a procedure can fail.

Resolution vagueness comes, I think, in two flavours. A procedure may be unresolved: one accepted procedure, no verdict in the middle. Or it may be divergent: two procedures that each do the job well and disagree over the middle. This is what we called inter-laboratory discrepancy, and I spent a good deal of my life on committees trying to make it go away. When two accredited methods returned different answers, nobody said the truth was indeterminate. We said the predicate was under-specified and that someone had to decide. Divergence therefore calls for a decision, and the decision is normative rather than epistemic: we are choosing which of two practices to make authoritative, not discovering which of them was right.

If that is right, then the interesting philosophy of vagueness is not the logic of the sorites but the engineering and the politics of concepts. It is also where my position is most exposed, because the difference between a decision that clarifies and a decision that merely changes the subject is precisely the thing I have not been able to state.

5. Improvement, and when it is not improvement.

If a predicate is resolution-vague, it can in principle be improved: sharpen the procedure and the middle cases become cases. But not every sharpening is faithful. My tentative criterion is that a refinement is faithful when it preserves the verdicts on the clear cases, when it is stable across variations of the procedure that the practice treats as immaterial, and when it does not require revising the surrounding practices in which the predicate was doing work. I suspect that the third clause is doing no work at all, or is circular, and I would rather find out than build on it.

My nervousness has a reason. For some concepts the coarseness is the point. The everyday test for alive is coarse and observable, and that is part of why the concept matters to people who are not physicians. Sharpen it and you may get a concept that is easier to apply and no longer the same concept. When is sharpening clarification, and when is it replacement? And if the answer is that it is always replacement to some degree, does the whole resolution story survive?

6. Where I expect to be wrong.

My instinct, after forty years of instruments, is that all vagueness is resolution vagueness. But I can construct no procedure, however refined, that would settle where a heap begins without my simply stipulating an answer, and the same seems true of child. If that is right, then constitutive vagueness is real, I have no account of it, and the paradox lives exactly where I said it did not.

What I would like from you.

First, is the distinction between constitutive and resolution vagueness a real one, or does the second term quietly collapse into one of the standard accounts? If it collapses, into which, and what would I have to add to make it distinct?

Second, where does my treatment of the sorites step go wrong, if it does? Is it coherent to describe the middle as a gap in a practice rather than as a third truth value, or does the same problem simply reappear at the edges of the gap itself?

Third, which of my classifications are likely wrong? Give me two or three predicates that I would sort into one category and that belong in the other, and say why.

Fourth, is the divergent case genuinely different from ordinary disagreement, or have I merely described a committee?

Fifth, which parts of this are likely to be familiar to philosophers? I would rather be told that I have rediscovered something than keep rediscovering it.

Sixth, and most useful to me: tell me where the essay is doing no work. My own suspicion is the criterion in section 5, and the phrase settled by a procedure throughout, which may be a way of not saying anything.
