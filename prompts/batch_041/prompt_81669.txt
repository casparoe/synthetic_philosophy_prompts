A friend in my reading group keeps trotting out an analogy I can't quite puncture, and it's starting to bug me. He says: a computer is a formal system; a mind is, or at least can be modeled as, a formal system; Gödel showed that any formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains true statements it can't prove, and can't prove its own consistency from inside; but we can stand outside such a system and see that its Gödel sentence is true, and see that it's consistent if it is — therefore the mind does something no machine can, so minds aren't machines, and by extension no AI built out of formal systems will ever really understand, mean anything, or be genuinely creative. He uses this to wave off basically any claim that a program could ever be a mind, and everyone nods along.

I feel like it's too quick but I can't lay out exactly where it goes wrong. Can you audit the analogy for me? Give me the correspondence table first — what's supposed to map onto what (the formal system, the axioms, the Gödel sentence, the "outside" vantage point, the mathematician who sees that the sentence is true) — then the places where the mapping breaks, and tell me which breakages actually matter for his conclusion and which are just noise. In particular I want to know whether "we can see the Gödel sentence is true" is a real case of the mind doing something a formal system can't, or whether it smuggles in assumptions — about the mind being consistent or sound, about what counts as "the same system," about what "seeing" amounts to. And then the payoff question: does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument the entire time? If there's a better analogy for what Gödel's results do and dont show about minds, tell me what it is.
