I retired about six years ago — forty-odd years as a field ecologist, mostly long-term plot studies, the kind of work where you go back to the same squares of ground every summer for decades. Now I read philosophy, badly, and argue with my daughter about it. Here's what I keep chewing on and can't settle, and I'd like you to write an essay that actually lands somewhere.

Everyone argues about automation in terms of jobs and income. Fine, whatever. My question is narrower and, I think, weirder. When people say their work gave their life meaning, which ingredient are they actually talking about — and would automating that ingredient take the meaning away?

I've started sorting the meaning I got from my own work into two piles, and this is the part I made up myself, so tell me if it's a distinction that does any work. Pile one is the puzzle side: the daily problem, getting an instrument to behave, the thing you chew on in the shower. Absorption, mastery, a hard thing done well. Pile two is the counting-on-me side: the fact that if I didn't show up, something real would go undone or go wrong. The plots don't get read, the samples don't get logged, the student who needed training doesn't get it. It wasn't optional and it wasn't only for me.

My hunch is that a machine eats pile one for breakfast — and even if it didn't, we could hand people puzzles regardless (chess, a community orchestra, whatever). Pile two is the hard one, because it seems to need something that genuinely depends on you. Give me a hobby that's only for me and nobody is counting on me. That's fake dependency. And I suspect pile two is where most of the real weight was.

So here's the controversy. Thesis A: the meaning of work depends on being genuinely needed by others — on a dependency that full automation dissolves — so a rich automated society will struggle to reproduce it, and might even have to keep some real scarcity or obligation around on purpose. Thesis B: that's just romanticizing drudgery; meaning in activity is absorption, mastery, and freely chosen projects, which automation actually frees up, and "being needed" is a nice feeling, not the thing itself.

Two things I want you to tangle with, not skip. First, care work — it's all pile two and it resists automation; does that show the dependency thesis is really about people rather than about work, which would be a problem for Thesis A? Second, the ugly thought experiment: imagine a society that deliberately keeps some hard, unnecessary labor going — people farming by hand while machines idle — precisely to give everyone a real thing to be counted on for. Is that a meaningful life or a theme park with extra steps? Your answer here probably decides the essay.

Reach a conclusion and defend it. Don't do the "it depends, both have a point" thing — if it does depend, tell me what it depends on and then commit to which way it leans for the ordinary case in a rich, heavily automated society, say a century out. And steelman the side you reject; I'd rather have my hunch taken apart than confirmed.
