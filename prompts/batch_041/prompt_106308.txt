A friend lent me one of those popular psychology books on character, and before I pass it round my old lab group I want to know how much of its pitch holds up. It goes more or less like this: psychology has now shown that what we do is driven by the situation and barely at all by character. In the obedience experiments, ordinary people were talked into hurting a stranger, and mostly because the setup was arranged so they would; in the seminary study, whether a student stopped to help a man slumped in a doorway depended on whether he was running late, not on what he believed; in the phone-booth study, people who had just found a dime were far likelier to help someone. Since traits dont predict behavior across situations, virtue ethics is finished — Aristotle's picture of the virtuous person as someone with stable dispositions that show up in what he does is just empirically false, and the most the virtue people can salvage is that real virtue is rarer than we assumed. I spent thirty years in a wet lab, so I'm a soft touch for an empirical claim, but I've also seen enough big claims collapse on contact with a better-designed experiment. Is this a fair summary? Go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who runs these studies would nod at, what a virtue ethicist would say has been quietly changed, and whether the death sentence follows from the experiments even if we grant them everything. And please keep the settled apart from the contested — tell me plainly which of these points people who actually work on the topic agree about and which are still being fought over, rather than handing me the tidy version. For what it's worth, I'm inclined to believe the studies; I just dont think they can carry the weight the book puts on them.
