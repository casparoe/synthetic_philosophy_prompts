I've been following mechanistic interpretability from the outside — no formal training, just a lot of reading and thinking on my own — and I keep hitting a problem I can't get past by myself. Whenever someone reports that a model's odd behavior has been traced to a mechanism, or that two models rely on the same mechanism, or that some newly noticed anomaly is genuinely new rather than a variant of something already understood, I can't work out what would make the claim true or false. The phrase "the same mechanism" seems to slide between at least four different things: the same parameters, the same arrangement of parts and connections, the same procedure or algorithm, and the same input-output behavior. My suspicion is that a good deal of the apparent disagreement in this area is really a mismatch between those levels, but I don't know which level, if any, ought to anchor the others, or whether my four-way split is even the right cut.

The case that worries me most is anomaly detection, because that is where the ambiguity does real work: you notice a behavior that doesn't fit the story you had about a model, and everything then turns on deciding whether you have found a new mechanism, a variant of a known one, or a known one misfiring in unfamiliar conditions. If "same mechanism" has no settled content, it seems to me that claims of that kind can't be checked, and the safety-relevant claims that inherit them can't be either.

What I'd like from you is an agenda rather than a survey. Suppose the resource at stake is a few years of one person's thinking — conceptual work, not experiments on large models — aimed at making mechanistic anomaly detection a field whose claims can be assessed rather than merely asserted. Which open questions about mechanism identity matter most, and in what order should they be taken up? For each, I want the case for its place in the order: what else cannot be settled until it is, how far it can be pushed by careful reasoning alone rather than needing new experiments, and what would count as real progress on it. Where two questions compete for the same effort, say which you would drop and why. I'm not after a reading list; I want the reasoning behind the ordering.

Finally, tell me what would change your ranking: which argument or piece of evidence would reorder it, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. If my four-level picture is a false tidiness that obscures the distinctions that actually matter, say so, and say what the real ones are.
