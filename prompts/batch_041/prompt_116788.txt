I am a doctoral student in philosophy and economics, and a small grantmaking foundation has asked me to write a critical note on the rubric it uses to allocate its annual budget. The rubric is the familiar one: every candidate cause area is scored out of ten on importance (how much good is at stake if the problem is solved), tractability (how likely additional work is to make progress), and neglectedness (how few others are already working on it), and the three scores are multiplied to produce a single figure that determines funding. Two features of it puzzle me. First, neglectedness is defended as a proxy for the marginal returns to an extra dollar, which suggests it is not a criterion in its own right; yet it is scored and weighted independently, and in practice a high neglectedness score can carry a cause whose importance is modest. Second, the same intervention can be scored under two different descriptions of its cause — say, as AI safety or as differential technological development — and receive two different products, although nothing about the intervention has changed.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory: what it assumes about value, about the comparability of very different goods, about the structure of the value space, and about how the three dimensions trade off (including what the multiplication itself commits it to). Then distinguish what the rubric strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, say where the practice is underdetermined between rival theories — for instance a monist expected-value view on which neglectedness is purely instrumental, versus a pluralist view on which it tracks something worth caring about in itself — and say what would have to change in the rubric if the presupposed view were replaced. Finally, tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to fail: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about what the rubric presupposes.
