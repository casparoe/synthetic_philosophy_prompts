Eighteen months ago, my younger sister was murdered in her apartment. The man who killed her was convicted and is now on death row; his execution is set for the spring, and the state board of pardons has scheduled a clemency hearing. The prosecutor's office has invited me to deliver the family's statement, and I have decided to speak against the execution. I would like you to write that statement.

I have been reading Camus on the death penalty, and the case I want to make is, in substance, his. But I am not a philosopher; I teach eighth-grade history, and I will be speaking to a board that has heard hundreds of these statements and will not be moved by abstraction. I do not want a literary essay about Camus, and I do not want you to quote him or anyone else. I want the argument in my own words, pitched to the room I will actually be standing in. That room will contain the board; the condemned man's mother; my own mother and brother, who believe the execution should proceed and who will hear my statement as a betrayal of my sister; and a reporter. The statement must be intelligible and bearable to all of them.

I have also become convinced that the standard abolitionist arguments are useless here, and I want the statement to avoid them. I will not say that life is sacred; I do not believe it. I will not say that the man is innocent, or reformed, or a victim of circumstance. I will not say that I forgive him, because I do not, and I will not perform a forgiveness I do not feel. I will not argue about deterrence. And I will not make the easy point that executing him would make us like him; I have never believed that, and it concedes too much.

The argument I do want to make turns on a distinction I cannot get out of my head. The question before the board is not what my sister's killer deserves. I am willing to leave that question open; it is not mine to answer, and I do not believe it is the board's. The question is what we are entitled to do. What is proposed is not a punishment like other punishments. It is a killing that is planned, scheduled, and rehearsed: the man will be told the day and the hour, a room will be prepared, a person will be assigned to do it, and the rest of us will be able to say our hands are clean because the act has been divided among a judge, a governor, a warden, a doctor, and an executioner. That division is precisely what makes it possible for a community that forbids killing to kill. I want the statement to make the board feel that, without ever using the word sacred and without claiming any moral superiority for myself. I am not better than the man who killed my sister; I am only refusing to be one of the people who kills him. Nor am I asking for a lesser punishment on the ground that he has earned it; I am asking the board to refuse to do the one thing that cannot be taken back.

There is one more thing I would like in the statement, if it can be said without sounding like a sermon. My sister died at a time no one chose. The man will die at a time we chose. I cannot say that this makes the two deaths equal, and I do not want to pretend that it does. But it is the thing I cannot get past. What I need from you is a five-minute statement that holds all of this together and ends with a clear request that the board recommend commutation. It should not become a eulogy for my sister; she was funny and impatient with sermons, and she would have hated being used as a prop.
