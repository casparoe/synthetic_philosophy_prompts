I'm a third-year PhD student in metaethics and I run a small reading group that's spent the last two months on the fitting-attitude family of views — the idea that for something to be valuable just is for it to be the sort of thing there's reason to value, or to be fitting to value, depending who's talking — and we keep hitting the wrong-kind-of-reasons objection and then talking past each other for two hours until someone has to leave for a seminar. I've decided the problem is that we don't actually know where everyone sits, so I want to build a proper decision tree / flowchart that takes a person from whatever they think about the basic analysis of value down to a named position on the objection. Could you write that for me? Aim for ten to fourteen leaves, and phrase every branch node as a question my students could answer without already knowing which label it leads to — so no node that just asks whether you're a fittingness-firster. Build the tree out of the logical space first and hang the names on it second: work out which questions genuinely split the positions, then locate the actual views on the branches, and where some combination of answers is unoccupied, say so and diagnose it (incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's bothered?). The questions should force a stand on cases rather than on the meta-question, so please route people through: the demon who'll torture you unless you believe something false; the boss who offers you a bonus for sincerely believing the product is good; Kavka's toxin puzzle (you get the million for forming the intention tonight to drink the vial tomorrow, and you'd never actually go through with it); and the one where you're paid to admire your benefactor — that last one feels like it's doing something different from the belief cases and I want the tree to say what.

Leaves I definitely want to see, since these are the ones we argue about: buck-passing with an object-given restriction on which reasons count; the view that the wrong-kind reasons are reasons for wanting or for bringing the attitude about, not for the attitude itself; the flat denial that they're reasons at all, so the appearance needs explaining away; the deflationary line that this is a verbal dispute over two senses of reason; fittingness as the primitive normative relation (so the objection is a category error); the constitutivist / aim-of-the-attitude option; the option that the distinction is real but purely psychological or explanatory and carries no normative weight; and one leaf for the view that value simply isn't analysable in terms of reasons in the first place, since two of us think the objection only bites if you accept that analysis. Where you need to name a distinction or a move that's already in the literature, describe it by content and hedge on the attribution — I always get the object-given / state-given thing mixed up with where it first appears and I don't want the handout resting on my memory.

Two last things. If two leaves come out indistinguishable on some paths — I suspect the no-reasons-at-all view and the reasons-for-a-different-attitude view collapse into each other once you ask whether the agent can still be criticised — flag it instead of pretending the tree seperates them, and tell me which extra node would pull them apart. And keep the whole thing usable as a one-page handout: each leaf gets a name, one sentence stating the position, and the case that's most awkward for it.
