I retired from a molecular biology lab about five years ago after nearly forty years at the bench, and I've spent the last few of them trying to catch up on philosophy — mostly ethics and decision theory, the stuff I always meant to read. In the lab I was never bothered by quantities that can't be compared to each other. Microns and millimoles, an enzyme rate and a pH — fine, you can't line them up on one axis, but each one can be compared to others of its own kind and that's all you ever need. What I keep running into now is a choice where the two options don't line up on any axis at all, and I can't get my head straight about whether that's a fact about the world or a fact about me. A friend who teaches this stuff told me my position was "pluralism with a hangover," which I didn't find helpful. So I've written it down for the reading group to poke at, and I'd like you to audit it.

Here's where I've landed, no particular order. (1) A lot of hard choices are between goods that are genuinely incommensurable: not that one is bigger, not that they're equal, just that there's no common currency. Is a life's work worth more or less than a marriage? I don't think that question has an answer, and I don't think it's because I'm ignorant. (2) Even so I can't give up the thought that there's usually a right thing to do in a hard choice — not arbitrary — and that people can be better or worse at seeing what it is. I know a couple of genuinely wise people and a lot of the other kind, and I don't think that's just taste. (3) My picture of deliberating is still the one from my old world: you compare the options and take the best one. A rational agent has a preference ordering and maximises it. I've never heard a description of deciding that isn't a version of that. (4) When you choose between incommensurable goods, something real is lost, and regret over that loss is appropriate even when you chose correctly — I've never thought regret meant you'd concluded you made a mistake. (5) If my preferences cycle — A over B, B over C, C over A — then someone can pump money out of me and I've shown I'm not being rational. Transitivity isn't optional. (6) I don't like the move where everything gets converted into one master value, utility or whatever. If such a thing existed I don't think it would be measuring anything real, and (1) says it doesn't exist anyway.

Now: which of these actually pull against each other? My hunch is that at least one of them only looks like it conflicts with the others, and I'd rather you not just tell me they're all in tension and call it deep. Rank the real conflicts by how bad they are, say what has to give in each case and what the cheapest repair would be, and then tell me which single commitment is doing the most damage — if I gave up one thing, which one buys me the most consistency, and what does it cost me elsewhere? I'm also curious whether the damage sits in the incommensurability claim itself or in one of the things I've hung on it. Please say how confident you are in each verdict you give, and single out the one point where you're least sure of yourself.
