I'm a machine-learning engineer on a multimodal representation learning team. Most of my work is on encoders, and I've spent the last few months on a project that, for reasons of data collection, has no paired data at all between two modalities: we train a tactile encoder on a robot gripper's contact, force, and slip signals, and separately a vision encoder on camera frames, with no correspondence between a touch and a picture of the same object. What we actually want to know is whether anything like shared structure shows up anyway — whether a linear probe or a nearest-neighbour match in one embedding space lines up with the other, whether a shape that was felt can be retrieved by sight alone, whether a mapping between the two spaces can be fitted post hoc without ever having seen a pair. A colleague half-joked that we were re-running Molyneux's problem, and the more I read about it the more I think he was right in earnest. As I understand the setup, the question is whether someone blind from birth, who has learned by touch to tell a cube from a sphere, could tell them apart by sight alone on first being made to see, before touching them; the answer Molyneux himself gave and Locke endorsed was no, on the grounds that the coordination between how a thing feels and how it looks is itself learned, while rationalists such as Leibniz said yes, holding that the shared figure is available to reason or is innately linked across the senses, and Berkeley gave an even more radical no, denying that the two modalities have any common content at all.

What I'd like from you is a systematic translation, not an explanation and not an analogy I'm using as an argument. Take the debate — the classical positions and, more importantly, the ways it has been reformulated since — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in: encoders and embedding spaces, alignment and shared latent structure, zero-shot and unsupervised cross-modal transfer, inductive bias versus learned structure, probes and retrieval accuracy. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Three things in particular. First, is the "on first being made to see" condition doing the same work as "no paired data, no fine-tuning, no test-time adaptation", or is there a temporal and experiential element that my vocabulary quietly flattens — the difference between a system that never had the correspondence and a subject for whom the correspondence was not yet established but might be? Second, does my notion of a correct match, which is defined by a shared label, by the ground truth that these two signals are of the same object, presuppose exactly what the philosophers are arguing about, so that "does the alignment hold?" answers the question by assuming its terms? Third, what becomes of the fact that in the original problem there is one subject who both touches and sees, whose single judgment is at issue — is "shared embedding space" a real translation of that unity, or an artifact of how I chose to set up training?

Be honest in both directions: don't smuggle my framework's assumptions into the translation and then call the result a translation — in particular, don't assume that if some mapping can be learned post hoc, the philosophical question has been answered — and don't manufacture residue where the translation is in fact clean. If it's useful, tell me which of the classical answers looks most defensible when re-read through my vocabulary, and whether the sight-restoration cases you may know of would, if they came out one way or the other, settle the philosophical question or only one reading of it. No need for citations or reading lists; I can't check them, and I'd rather have the argument than the bibliography.
