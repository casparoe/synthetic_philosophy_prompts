Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two positions on moral trade.

The shared background the two parties accept going in: moral trade is the practice by which two people who disagree about moral priorities each do something they regard as less important in exchange for the other doing something they regard as more important, so that both expect the arrangement to increase expected moral value under their own view. The standard illustration: a committed vegan and a committed poverty-alleviation donor agree that the vegan will give to the donor's preferred poverty charity while the donor spends her weekends doing vegan outreach, each thereby advancing the other's priority at what she regards as lesser cost to her own.

The two parties are these.

Party One, the trade maximalist, holds that moral trade is ordinary trade applied to moral commitments: it is permissible, generally desirable, and ought to be actively facilitated — brokered, publicized, and made binding — and that there is no principled difference between trading moral effort and trading money.

Party Two, the integrity skeptic, holds that moral commitments are not the kind of thing that can be alienated or exchanged without loss: that treating them as tradeable commodities corrupts the trader, distorts the moral landscape, and that the aggregate effects are at least as likely to be negative as positive.

The deliverable is the document both could put their names to. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims: about what moral trade is and is not; about which of the disputed questions are normative and which empirical; about what each side concedes to the other's strongest example; and about cases both agree are clearly acceptable and cases both agree are clearly unacceptable.

2. The residual disagreements, each stated twice — once in the wording the maximalist would use for her own view and once in the wording the skeptic would use for his — with a note on whether the two statements genuinely conflict or merely differ in emphasis.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. Address at least the following, and add any others you find:
   - the trade-capital worry: whether publicizing moral trade gives people an incentive to hold, or to profess, more extreme moral views, since extreme views command larger trades;
   - the escalation case, in which two traders each have an incentive to ratchet up their stated moral position in order to extract more from the other;
   - the hostage case, in which one party credibly threatens to do something both regard as bad unless the other trades;
   - the uncertain trader, who trades as a hedge because she is unsure which of two moral views is correct;
   - the clearinghouse, a third party who brokers many trades and can therefore set terms across them.

4. For each crux, the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance — including what each would count as evidence against her own position.

5. A closing section on which parts of the dispute are verbal, and why.

No citations and no reading list; the statement should stand on the arguments and cases it contains.
