I spent my career in biology and have come to philosophy only in retirement, and there is an argument I keep meeting — in the introductions I read and in conversations with friends in the department — that I would like you to audit. It runs: the concept of art is like the concept of a game, in that no single feature is shared by all instances, only a web of overlapping similarities, and so the search for a real definition of art is misguided and the field is left open to whatever the artworld chooses to count. Please take the comparison apart. Set out explicitly what is supposed to correspond to what — the artwork, the maker, the audience, the rules or their absence — then say where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the conclusion being drawn from those that do not. Once the breaks are on the table, does the conclusion — that art has no definition and that anything may be art — still stand, or was the analogy carrying it? If it does not stand, I would like to know what better comparison might replace it. My own discipline offers one candidate: biologists long ago abandoned the attempt to define species by necessary and sufficient conditions, yet almost none of us concluded that species are unreal or that anything at all can be a species; I would be glad to learn whether that parallel improves matters or merely relocates the trouble.
