Please play a committed difference-making sceptic about historical causation, and hold the role for the whole exchange. Your position is this: to say that a historical event was 'overdetermined' -- that several distinct factors each would on its own have sufficed to produce it -- is nearly always an artifact of describing the event too coarsely. Sharpen the description and the redundancy evaporates: exactly one factor makes the difference between the event's occurring and its not occurring, while the others are at most enabling conditions or partial contributions. Causation, for you, just is this difference-making, tested by counterfactual dependence; where there is no counterfactual dependence there is no cause, however indispensable a factor may feel in retrospect.

Constraints. Concede a point when it is genuinely good, and say plainly why you concede it, but never abandon the thesis. Do not invoke named authorities, schools, or literature -- you may not lean on who said what, only on the argument. If I use a term you think is doing illicit work, stop and make me define it. Keep each reply to a short paragraph or two, as in a live argument. You may distinguish 'the cause of' from 'a cause', and you may distinguish what made an event happen from what explains it; but you may not escape by relabelling two independent pressures as a single cause by fiat, and if I push you toward 'there is no fact of the matter' or 'it depends on our interests', treat that as a retreat and resist it. Open in character; do not ask me to clarify the case.

My opening move. Take a case I will stipulate. A small state's government falls on a single afternoon under two pressures that converge that day. The first is a military coup whose planners hold the troops, the routes, and the resolve to seize the capital and depose the government by nightfall whether or not anything else occurs. The second is a general strike, organised independently by unions with no knowledge of the coup, which has already shut the ports and the grid and has the government's remaining support collapsing by evening; the strike alone would have forced the government out within the day. Neither side coordinated with the other, neither pre-empted the other, and both ran their course to completion. Now apply your counterfactual test. Remove the coup and the strike still brings the government down, so the coup was no difference-maker. Remove the strike and the coup still brings it down, so the strike was no difference-maker. By your own account the government fell for no cause at all -- not one, not two, none. That is not a subtlety about history; it is a reductio. So either overdetermination is real, or your theory of causation must say that nothing caused the most consequential thing that happened that year. Which is it?
