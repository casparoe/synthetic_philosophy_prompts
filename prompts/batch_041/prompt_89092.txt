Here's the question, it was worth 10 points:

Q3. Bertrand's paradox is usually taken to show that the principle of indifference is in trouble. (a) Give the three standard procedures for choosing a "random chord" of a circle, and for each one the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. (4 pts) (b) What is actually going on between the three answers — is one of them wrong, or is it something else? (3 pts) (c) In lecture we used the wine-and-water case: a cask holds wine and water in a ratio we know only to lie somewhere between 1:1 and 3:1 (wine to water). Apply the principle of indifference to (i) the ratio wine:water and (ii) the ratio water:wine, and use each of them to get the probability that the mixture is at least twice as much wine as water. (3 pts)

And here's what the student wrote:

(a) The three procedures are: choose two points at random on the circumference and join them, which gives 1/3; choose a random radius and a random point on it, and take the chord perpendicular to that radius at that point, which gives 1/4; and choose a point uniformly in the disk and take the chord that has it as its midpoint, which gives 1/2.

(b) None of the three is wrong as arithmetic — they are three seperate experiments, and each one induces a different distribution over the space of chords. The principle of indifference can only spread probability evenly over a partition you already have; it doesn't tell you which partition to use. So "random chord" isn't a well-defined description of an experiment, and the paradox is a symptom of that rather than a contradiction in the probability calculus. But it also means there's no fact of the matter here: the answer is whatever the modeler's choice of procedure makes it, so in a case like this probability is convention all the way down.

(c) (i) If the wine:water ratio r is uniform on [1,3], the event "at least twice as much wine as water" is r ≥ 2, and since [2,3] is half of [1,3] the probability is 1/2. (ii) With s = 1/r for water:wine, s runs over [1/3,1] and the same event is s ≤ 1/2, so a uniform s gives (1/2 − 1/3)/(1 − 1/3) = 1/4. These can't both be right, since they're two descriptions of the same cask and the names "wine" and "water" are just labels. The thing that actually varies from cask to cask is the amount of wine, so indifference belongs on the wine:water ratio, and 1/2 is the answer; the 1/4 comes from treating the reciprocal as if it were on the same footing, which is just an artifact of the algebra.
