Last night I spent two hours arguing with a close friend about a case that has been in the news here: a patient consented to a procedure after her doctor called it routine, but he did not tell her that the technique was experimental and that he had a financial interest in it; she later said she would never have agreed. My friend's position is that her consent was valid. He kept returning to an analogy: valid consent is like a valid contract. If there was an offer, an acceptance, capacity, and no gun to her head, then she is bound; a little non-disclosure is like a seller not volunteering that a cheaper model exists, which may be shabby but does not void the deal. I think the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I want a proper audit rather than a trading of intuitions.

Please lay out the correspondence table as explicitly as you can. What plays the role of offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, duress, fraud, materiality, and remedy? Where does the mapping hold, and where does it break? A colleague of mine likes to distinguish a gate model of consent, which asks whether the consenter's will was bypassed or overridden, from a transaction model, which asks whether the terms were fair and understood; my friend's analogy seems to slide between the two without noticing. I am also interested in the features of contract law that the analogy quietly imports: the assumption that the parties are adversarial and self-interested, that the agreement's content is fully specified at the moment of signing, that enforcement is the point of the transaction, that the baseline is no obligation, and that the parties can allocate risk by bargaining. Consent seems to differ on several of these—it can be ongoing and revocable, it can be valid without any formal act, it can be given for non-beneficial or altruistic reasons, and its validity may depend on the quality of the consenter's will rather than on the enforceability of a promise.

Then tell me whether my friend's conclusion—that signed, capacitous, non-coerced consent is valid despite a significant non-disclosure—survives once the disanalogies are on the table. I would like you to distinguish, if the distinction is defensible, between deception that goes to the nature of the act and deception that goes only to its expected terms or consequences; between non-disclosure that is merely a failure of candor and non-disclosure that amounts to a misdescription of what is being consented to; and between a threshold conception of competence and a decision-relative one. Which of these distinctions does the contract analogy illuminate, and which does it obscure? If the analogy cannot carry the conclusion, what would be a better analogy for what makes consent valid—one that captures the difference between a lie that invalidates consent and a lie that merely makes the consent less wise? I am not looking for a reading list; I want the mapping, the break points, and a verdict on whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along.
