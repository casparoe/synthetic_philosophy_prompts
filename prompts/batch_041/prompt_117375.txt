I have no background in philosophy or in probability beyond what I have picked up reading on my own, but I have been turning the Sleeping Beauty problem over for the better part of a year, and I would like to get the whole space of answers out of my head and onto a single page as a decision tree. Here is the setup in my own words, so that nothing below depends on my having read the right sources. On Sunday a fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, I am woken once, on Monday. If it lands tails, I am woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, and after each waking I am put back to sleep with a drug that erases the memory of that waking, so that every waking feels like the first and I can never tell which day it is. On waking I am asked how confident I should be that the coin landed heads. A second question is always asked alongside the first: if I am then told that it is Monday, how should my confidence change?

I would like a decision tree, the sort of flowchart I could draw on a sheet of paper, that starts from that plain question and ends at named positions. Please build it from the logical space first: work out what the genuinely distinct commitments are, make each branch of the tree a question that isolates one of those commitments, and only then attach the names of the positions to the leaves. Every branch must be phrased so that a person could answer it without already knowing which position it leads to, so that the tree sorts someone by what they say about the cases rather than by which label they happen to like. Aim for six to eight leaves and no more than about seven branch questions.

These positions must all appear as leaves somewhere: the answer of one third; the answer of one half; the answer of one half that does not move when the woman is told it is Monday; the answer that the question, as posed, has no single correct answer because it is really two different questions run together; and the family of answers on which the number is fixed only relative to a prior decision about whose experiences one counts oneself a random draw from, that is, the idea that one should reason as if one were a random member of the set of all observers or awakenings that will ever exist, as against the idea that a hypothesis which brings more observers into being is for that reason more likely to be the one one is in (these two ideas are sometimes called the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions). If the tree needs a separate leaf for the reading on which this puzzle is really the same problem as how to think about which branch of a branching universe one is in, include it; if that reading is not distinct from the others, say so rather than manufacture a leaf for it.

Use the following cases as the branch nodes, or as many of them as actually do separating work, and tell me plainly which ones do none:

- the day-telling case described above, where the woman learns that it is Monday;
- a variant in which tails produces not two awakenings but a very large number of them, with the same amnesia, so that on tails she is woken again and again;
- a variant in which the coin is biased, landing heads with probability p rather than one half;
- a variant in which the two awakenings on tails belong to two different people who never share memories, so that the woman who wakes on Monday cannot tell whether she is the only one woken or the first of two;
- a variant in which a coin decides whether the universe contains one person or an enormous number of people, and the woman asking is one of them, ignorant of which universe she is in;
- and a control case in which the amnesia drug is left out, so that she remembers everything, which every position should treat the same way.

For every leaf, give the name of the position, one sentence stating it in plain terms, and the single commitment that sits most awkwardly with it, the thing its defender would least like to have to say out loud. Where two leaves return the same verdict on every case in the tree, flag them as a pair the tree cannot separate, and say what further case, if any, would drive them apart. Where the tree yields a combination of answers that nobody seems to hold, mark that branch as empty and say whether you think it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has bothered with it.

Finally, tell me which single branch is carrying the most weight, the question whose answer determines the largest part of the space, and whether the puzzle looks any different once one sees how much rests on it. I do not want citations or a reading list; I want the structure.
