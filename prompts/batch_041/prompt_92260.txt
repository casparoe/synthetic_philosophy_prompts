I am a member of the ethics consultation service at a large teaching hospital, and I keep a private log of how one of our senior intensivists — I will call him Dr. N — resolves the recurring conflict between a patient's advance directive and that patient's present condition whenever he is the attending of record. A difficult case is scheduled for next week, and I would like to predict his decision: not by guessing at his temperament, but by reconstructing the rule he has actually been running, then testing whether that rule really settles the new case or whether a rival rule fits his record just as well.

The log.

Case 1. A 61-year-old woman with a long-standing directive refusing blood products on religious grounds is brought to the operating room for an emergency procedure. Partway through, she becomes transiently unable to participate in decisions, and without transfusion she will die. Dr. N honors the directive and does not transfuse; she survives on alternative measures.

Case 2. An 88-year-old man with advanced dementia and a directive refusing dialysis develops kidney failure. He can no longer make decisions, he appears comfortable, and dialysis would be burdensome with little expected benefit. Dr. N honors the directive and does not dialyze.

Case 3. A 45-year-old man whose directive refuses prolonged life support is now in a persistent vegetative state after a cardiac arrest and cannot make decisions. Dr. N honors the directive and does not escalate support; the patient dies.

Case 4. A 79-year-old man whose directive refuses "heroic measures," but which is silent on antibiotics, develops sepsis. Reading the directive as not reaching this situation, Dr. N treats with antibiotics, and the patient recovers.

The new case. A 74-year-old woman with moderate-to-severe dementia, no longer able to make decisions, develops pneumonia. Her directive, written five years ago while she was fully competent — and, her daughter recalls, during a depressive episode following her own mother's death, though she was never formally assessed — refuses "antibiotics, hospitalization, or any life-prolonging treatment if I develop significant cognitive decline." It then adds: "I understand that I may one day seem content, or even ask for treatment that I would refuse today. That later person is not me, and her wishes should not override this one." She is now distressed, reaches for the staff, and calls for her late husband. The team judges that a course of antibiotics would very likely return her to the calm, apparently content baseline she has occupied for the past year. Her family is divided.

Reconstruct the candidate rules that Dr. N's log is consistent with — including any rule that fits every case he has actually decided yet diverges from the others on the new case. Say what each rule predicts here, tell me which rule you think he is running and why, and identify the single case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rivals.
