I'm specifying a model of the evolution of cooperation before I build it, and I want the requirements settled first. Give me the desiderata any adequate theory of the evolution of cooperation must meet — not a catalogue of proposed mechanisms, but the list of things such a theory has to deliver, with the case or consideration that forces each item onto the list.

By "theory of the evolution of cooperation" I mean an account, in a population of agents subject to selection, of how a costly behavior that benefits others can become common and remain common. Build the list around these cases, and add whatever else genuinely belongs:

- one-shot interactions between strangers who will never interact again, where the only thing that passes between them is a single bit of a public signal;
- iterated interactions with a known, finite number of rounds;
- a noisy world, in which an intended cooperative move is sometimes received as a defection and vice versa;
- partner choice, where an agent can decline to enter or can leave an interaction rather than only respond within it;
- populations that are small, finite, and structured, so that who meets whom is not uniform;
- payoffs that are asymmetric and not common knowledge among the players.

I have a particular set of requirements in mind, because they are the ones usually left implicit, and I want to know whether they are legitimate or parochial. For each, say what would count as a theory satisfying it and whether you think it is non-negotiable:

1. Perturbation accountability: the theory must say not only whether cooperation is stable but how it degrades under a small change in the payoffs — for instance a slight rise in the temptation payoff — and must not be silent about the margin of stability.
2. Portability: the same explanatory mechanism must carry over, without re-tuning, when the same population is moved to a strategically similar but not identical game.
3. No unbounded resources: no mechanism may presuppose infinite populations, infinite time horizons, or agents with unlimited memory or computation.
4. Accounting neutrality: the theory must be statable in individual-selection terms, or else declare precisely where a group-level term is doing work, and must not equivocate between the two.
5. One-shot non-negotiable: it must explain at least some cooperation in the stranger case, or say plainly why that case is out of scope.

Then tell me which distinctions the theory must respect (cooperation as behavior versus as disposition; "cooperation is an equilibrium" versus "cooperation resists invasion"; what is explained at the short run versus the long run), and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate theories. Skip the full scorecard; I will run that myself.
