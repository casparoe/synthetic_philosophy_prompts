I am a retired physicist who has spent the past few years reading logic and the philosophy of mathematics on my own, and I have settled into a broadly constructivist position: I take a mathematical existence claim to be warranted only when I can exhibit the object or a procedure that produces it, and I no longer treat the law of excluded middle as a general logical truth but as a substantive principle that fails in some domains. I would like an honest map of what counts against this position, sorted by how decisive each consideration would be, along with your assessment of whether I have quietly insulated myself from refutation.

Three things I would especially like addressed. First, I have gathered that every classical theorem can be recovered constructively under some translation, which leaves me unsure whether my view can lose at all; if it cannot, is that a defect or a virtue? Second, I am told that intuitionistic analysis, with its continuity principles, does not merely omit classical theorems but proves some that classical mathematics denies, which would make the two rival accounts of the same subject matter rather than one a restriction of the other; what would have to be true for that rivalry to count against me? Third, suppose someone demonstrated that an empirically confirmed prediction in physics could be reached only by an irreducibly non-constructive existence argument, with no constructive route available: is that a genuine defeater, or does it show only that physics is conducted in a classical metalanguage?

Please separate what constructivists would themselves concede as awkward from what they would wave away, and rank the whole lot from fatal to merely inconvenient. Answer in American English.
