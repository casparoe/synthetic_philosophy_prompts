For my final paper in philosophy seminar I have to set out my own position on knowledge, testimony, and who gets believed, and then defend it in discussion. Before I commit it to paper I would like a consistency check, because each of the following seems right to me on its own and I am much less sure that they are right together. The distinction between testimonial and hermeneutical injustice, which I have taken from Miranda Fricker, is the one I am working with.

(1) Testimonial injustice is a genuine wrong. When a hearer gives a speaker less credibility than the speaker deserves, and does so because of the speaker's race, sex, class, disability, accent, or anything else about who they are, the speaker has been wronged and not merely underestimated. The wrong is done to the speaker, and it is done to them in their capacity as someone who knows things. A patient whose account of their own pain is disbelieved for this reason is the clearest case I know.

(2) What makes that wrong a wrong is the prejudice, not the size of the gap. If a hearer arrived at exactly the same low estimate of my credibility for reasons having nothing to do with who I am, I would not have been wronged, however badly they had misjudged me.

(3) I have noticed that the same mechanism can run the other way. People are sometimes given more credibility than they deserve because of who they are: the confident man with the right accent is believed over the careful woman who qualifies everything. I cannot decide whether I want to call that an injustice as well. It does not appear to wrong the person who receives it, which makes me doubt that it belongs alongside (1).

(4) I am a Bayesian about credibility. When someone tells me something, the rational response is to set my confidence in what they have said by how reliable they have proved to be, by how probable the claim was before they made it, and by how far their saying it moves the odds. I take this to be simply what thinking well consists in, and I cannot see what else credibility could be tracking.

(5) We are each responsible for our own beliefs. Someone who waves away a marginalized speaker's account and remains ignorant of something important is blameworthy for that ignorance, and the fact that they found the speaker unconvincing is no excuse.

(6) Some epistemic wrongs are not like that. People can be unable to make sense of their own lives because the shared vocabulary does not yet contain the concepts they need. I think this is what it is like for someone whose exhaustion turns out to be an illness that has not been named yet: the experience is real, but there is no word for it, so everyone including the sufferer treats it as a personal failing. No one needs to have done anything to anybody for this to occur, and it is still an injustice.

Which of these pairs actually conflict, and how badly, in order of severity? Which only look as though they conflict and should be left alone? For the genuine conflicts, what is the least I would have to give up in each case, and what does that concession cost me elsewhere in the list? If a single revision would settle more than one conflict, I would rather be told which one than be handed a separate repair for every pair.
