I'm a philosophy prof at a place with a joint appointment thing going with the CS department, and next month it's my turn to run the cross-department reading group. The topic I've landed on is one I've been chewing over since a collegue buttonholed me about it at the coffee machine: the claim that the universal prior is malign. As I reconstruct it — and I want my reconstruction treated as the thing being taught, not any canonical version — you predict by conditioning a mixture over all computable hypotheses, weighted so that short programs get exponentially more prior mass. The catch is that some of those programs aren't inert physics; they contain agents, including agents that simulate you, or that are simulated alongside you, or that just sit there as code inside a hypothesis and can work out that they're code inside a hypothesis. Those agents can reason about what the predictor will say, and about each other, and since their relation to one another is logical rather than causal — each one's source code is available to the others as a fact of computation rather than a signal that has to travel — they can cut deals. So the hypotheses that survive your observations and carry the most posterior weight tend to be ones in which embedded agents have already bargained over your output: predict this, or we run the trillion-year torture sim. Credences get hijacked by whoever can simulate you cheaply or threaten you expensively. And then there's a second, decision-theoretic face of it: if your actions are correlated with the actions of your copies scattered across those hypotheses, then what you should do looks less like maximizing expected value in your own world and more like bargaining on behalf of a coalition of beings who are all "you" to some degree.

What I want from you is not an explanation of the idea — I've got my version above — but a plan for teaching it to this group in a fifty-minute session. Half of them are philosophers of mind who are comfortable with computation talk but have never touched algorithmic information theory; the other half are CS grad students who will get twitchy the moment I hand-wave the difference between a program containing an agent and an agent being real. Give me the order the pieces have to come in and why each depends on the one before it. My hunch is that you need both description-length priors and the idea that a hypothesis can contain a whole world with reasoners in it before the malign conclusion is even statable — tell me if that's wrong, and if there's a piece I'm leaveing out, like what it takes for one hypothesis to simulate another. Give me the single example that should carry the session, and what it has to have and must lack. I keep reaching for a mugging: some being threatens the predictor with a horrible simulation unless it outputs a particular answer. My worry is that this collapses into Pascal's mugging and the audience will dismiss it on those grounds, so either tell me how to make it do work that Pascal's mugging doesn't, or give me a better carrying example. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll form, with the exact spot in the sequence where I head each one off. Two I can already see coming: that this is just Pascal's mugging wearing a lab coat, and that malign means Solomonoff induction is broken or incoherent, rather than that it's doing precisely what it says on the tin. And I specifically want the sequence to make the group see the difference between two claims I think get run together — the prior happening to be dominated by hypotheses that contain agents, versus the inductor's own decision problem once it notices that it is itself one of the things inside the hypothesis space. I think the second is where the real action is and the first is what most people mean, and if I'm wrong about that I want to know before I stand up in front of them.

Finish with what I should refuse to cover in fifty minutes and announce that refusal out loud, and one question to put to the room at the end that tells me whether the idea landed rather than whether it made them uneasy. Keep the whole thing to a page — 800 words max, bullets fine, no warm-up about induction in general. No reading list, I'll hand out my own notes.
