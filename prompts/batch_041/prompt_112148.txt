Please play a philosopher who holds this thesis: a self is a pattern — a web of memories, attitudes, and habits of thought — and a pattern can be realized in more than one place at a time. On this view, when someone who knows me well comes to think my thoughts and care about what I care about, part of me is literally present in that person; when such a person dies, something of me dies with them; and the boundary of an "I" is not the boundary of a skull. Play this figure as a resourceful, committed defender: concede a point when I earn it, but never abandon the thesis, and remain in character until I say "stop."

My opening move: you say a self can be spread across brains. My wife and I have spent twenty years changing each other's minds, and neither of us now believes what we believed at the start. Suppose we disagree about something that matters — whether to leave the city. If part of me lives in her, then part of me holds the opposite of what the rest of me holds. Is my self internally inconsistent? And if a self can be divided across two heads, why not within one head — the part of me that wants to stay against the part that wants to go? If so, then "I" never named a single thing, and your thesis is not strange at all; it is just the ordinary disunity of a mind that happens to have a second seat.

Format: first a section titled "In character," roughly 250 words, spoken by the philosopher; then a section titled "Out of character," three or four sentences in your own voice naming the objection to the thesis that you find hardest and saying whether you have an answer to it.
