I volunteer as a mediator at a neighbourhood dispute-resolution centre, and most of what I do is help two parties split something they would both rather split than walk away from — the proceeds of a house they jointly own, the balance of a lease, the assets of a small partnership that is winding down. I have been reading about how economists and game theorists model exactly this situation, and I keep coming back to one proposal in particular. The idea, as I understand it, is that if you can identify two reference points — what each side gets if the talks fail, and the most each side could possibly hope for if the other side were infinitely accommodating — then the fair division is the point where the straight line joining those two reference points crosses the edge of what is actually achievable. I like it because it does not require me to guess at either party's attitude to risk, and because it seems to promise that if the situation improves for one side, that side should never end up worse off, which the better-known alternative — the one that maximises the product of the two sides' gains — apparently does not always guarantee. What I lack is any way to actually use it at the table.

Please turn it into a procedure I can run in a two-hour session with two ordinary people who have never heard of any of this. I want the questions laid out in order: how to pin down the failure point when the parties disagree about what walking away even means; how to establish each side's maximum claim without letting them simply name an absurd number; how to draw the line and find the crossing point when the feasible set is not a neat smooth curve but a lumpy list of concrete options; and where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision back to the parties because the remaining question is one of values — what counts as a fair claim in the first place — rather than arithmetic. Say what to do when the numbers will not cooperate: when the set of achievable outcomes is not convex, when the failure point is itself contested, and, since I occasionally take three-party cases, what to do when there are more than two sides, which I suspect is where the whole thing quietly falls apart. Then walk me through three cases: a landlord and a tenant dividing a buyout, two former business partners dividing a client list and a set of equipment, and one where I expect the procedure to hand me an answer I will not like — a case where the two sides are symmetric in the formal sense even though I feel strongly that one of them deserves more, so that I can see exactly what the procedure is and is not sensitive to. Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do.
