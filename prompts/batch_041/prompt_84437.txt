My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about whether the branch of game theory that starts from the assumption that the players are rational, and that this is common knowledge between them, is doing real work or merely dressing up a formalism, and he kept returning to one analogy that I could not answer on the spot. Two armies sit on opposite hills; the plan requires that both attack at dawn, and each general will attack only if he is certain the other will; the only way to send word is by messenger, and messengers are sometimes captured; so a message, its acknowledgment, the acknowledgment of the acknowledgment, and so on, never leave either general certain of the other's attack. He called this the two generals problem and drew a large conclusion from it: that no finite exchange of signals can put two minds into the state the theory assumes, that common knowledge is therefore an unattainable ideal rather than a description of anything, and that wherever people do in fact coordinate — meeting under the clock at the station, bidding in an auction, holding a line in a market panic — the theory's assumption is idle and something else must be doing the work.

I think the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I want the audit done properly rather than scored as a debating point, and I want to hear where it does bite as well as where it fails. Treat the story as an analogy for common knowledge of rationality, in the way he was using it, and give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no closing summary: first, the correspondence — what plays the role of the two armies, the messenger, the dawn, and the certainty, and where in that mapping the uncertainty actually lives; second, the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion he drew, and why the remaining ones can be set aside; third, what the story quietly imports that the target situation need not contain, and what it renders invisible; fourth, an honest verdict on his conclusion, including the cases in which coordination really is as fragile as he says; and fifth, a better analogy, if there is one, for what common knowledge of rationality is supposed to accomplish.
