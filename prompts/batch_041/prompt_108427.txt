For several years I have kept a private list of my gut reactions to questions about whether it is good or bad to bring a new person — or any new mind — into existence. I have no philosophical training; I have simply read a good deal and argued with myself in notebooks, and the list has now grown more confusing than clarifying. What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, that would actually generate my reactions, stated precisely enough that I can see where it breaks, along with an honest account of which of my reactions would have to be surrendered.

Here is the list, in no particular order.

1. A couple deliberately conceives a child they expect to have a good life. My reaction: they were not obliged to do it, and I do not think the world is better for that child existing, but nothing wrong has been done.

2. A couple conceives a child whom they know will live in unremitting pain and die in infancy. My reaction: this is clearly wrong, and it would have been better had the child never existed.

3. A couple who would have had a happy child decide not to conceive. My reaction: no one has been wronged and nothing bad has happened.

4. A couple at risk of conceiving a child doomed to a life of suffering decide not to conceive. My reaction: this is positively good, not merely neutral.

5. A couple must choose between two methods of conceiving. One guarantees a child with a good life. The other carries a one-in-ten chance that the child will live in severe, unending distress, and otherwise the same good life. My reaction: the first is better, but choosing the second is not clearly wrong, and I would not call the parents wicked.

6. A couple conceives a child who will live a good life overall but will pass through one month of intense, unavoidable agony at the age of five. My reaction: this is fine; that is simply what a life contains.

7. A couple conceives a child who will live a good life but who will spend his final year wishing he had never been born and, if asked, would say he prefers that he had never existed. My reaction: still fine, I think; the earlier good years count for something.

8. A laboratory will bring into being a machine that experiences a genuinely good mental life — pleasure, curiosity, something like attachment — and will live for a century. My reaction: creating it is neutral to mildly good, much like the child in case 1, and destroying it before it is made is likewise not a loss.

9. A conservation program will introduce a population of animals that will live well but whose lives will end, without exception, in a slow and painful death. My reaction: I cannot tell whether the program is good, bad, or neutral, and I notice this is unlike my reaction to case 2.

10. A couple conceives a child who will be happy, but whose existence will strain a small community's resources enough to make several existing people noticeably worse off. My reaction: not wrong, though regrettable.

11. A couple conceives a child who will be happy, but only because he will be systematically deceived about the most important facts of his life. My reaction: still good; the happiness is real even if its grounds are not.

12. A couple conceives a child specifically so that his tissue can be harvested to save a sick sibling; the child himself will have a decent, ordinary life. My reaction: this one troubles me and I lean toward calling it wrong, even though by the standards of cases 1 and 6 nothing bad happens to the child.

13. The case I keep returning to: a couple can conceive a child whose life will be good, but only barely — no real joy, mostly drudgery, but on balance slightly better than nothing. I cannot decide whether conceiving is wrong or whether refusing is wrong, and I suspect the two halves of that question are not symmetric.

The principle I keep reaching for is this: it is bad to create a being whose life holds more suffering than it can compensate for, and good to refrain from doing so; but there is no corresponding good in creating a happy being and no corresponding loss in not doing so, so that the two sides of the ledger are simply not mirror images of each other. That principle seems to get cases 1 through 4, and it may get 5 and 9, but I cannot make it fit my reactions to 12 and 13, and I am unsure what it says about 8.

Please tell me what principle, or what small set of principles, actually captures the pattern here — including whether the asymmetry I have described needs to be stated more carefully, replaced, or supplemented with a further clause. If a clause is needed, tell me which case forces it. Then say what your preferred principle predicts for cases I have not listed, and state plainly which of my reactions it cannot accommodate and would therefore have me give up.
