For a few years now I have worked on treaty and customary-law questions in a foreign ministry, and the doctrine of customary international law has come to bother me in a way I cannot resolve from inside the legal materials. The standard test has two elements: a general and consistent practice among states, and opinio juris, the requirement that states follow the practice because they take it to be legally required. Both are said to be necessary, and neither is applied quite as stated. Where a rule is politically salient, a short run of instances — sometimes a single widely supported General Assembly resolution — is treated as enough, with no settled practice behind it at all. Where conduct runs against an alleged rule, lawyers often discount it on the ground that the state in question denies it is breaching anything, so that the violation ends up confirming the rule rather than eroding it. And opinio juris is supposed to be what turns a convergent habit into law, which leaves the awkward question of what a state is meant to believe about a rule's legal status before the rule exists. I may be putting this more crudely than a treatise would, but the shape of the thing should be recognizable.

What I want to know is what this practice presupposes. My own starting point is Lewis's analysis of convention: a regularity that everyone in a population conforms to, expects everyone else to conform to, and prefers to conform to on condition that others do, where an alternative regularity would have solved the same problem equally well, and where all of this is common knowledge. Where does the doctrine of custom fit that analysis, and where does it strain it? Lewisian conventions are solutions to coordination problems and need carry no obligation: my reason to conform is conditional on others conforming, and it evaporates if the regularity collapses. But one state's breach of a customary rule is not normally taken to release the others, and the rule is said to survive wholesale violation; the doctrine presents compliance as required, not merely as what one does while others are doing it. Can the practice absorb that difference — say, because states happen to have a standing interest in the rule holding — or does it show that the practice presupposes something with an irreducibly normative element, of the sort Hart was after when he described a social rule as involving a critical reflective attitude toward a pattern of conduct? If the latter, I would like to know whether a purely strategic story, built on reputation, reciprocity and the fear of being the odd one out, can do the same work more cheaply.

I would also like the parts checked against one another. Can a single account of convention cover both the insistence on settled practice and the readiness to find law in a resolution with no practice behind it? Is the treatment of violations as confirmatory an implication of the doctrine, a convenient fiction, or a real feature of how norms work? Tell me which elements the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits comfortably with, and which it leaves underdetermined, so that several theories of convention would license the same behaviour. And say something about whether the doctrine treats customary rules as there to be found or as continuously made: is the lawyer identifying a convention that already exists, or ratifying one that convergent behaviour is still producing?

Finally, follow each candidate picture through to its consequences. If the Lewisian account is the right one, what in the doctrine would have to give — the persistent-objector rule, the bindingness of custom on states that never participated, the independent work done by opinio juris? And if the practice needs a normative attitude all the way down, which of the anomalies I described stop looking like anomalies and start looking like symptoms?
