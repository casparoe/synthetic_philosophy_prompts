I have been reading rationalist and effective-altruism blogs for years, so I am familiar with the standard arguments about meat-eating, crop cultivation, and wild-animal suffering. Last week, however, I watched two friends — call them A and B — have an argument that went well past the usual talking points, and I genuinely could not decide who was right at each stage. I was only a bystander; I am not reproducing this to support either side. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move, rather than simply declaring a winner. Please answer in British English.

A began: Eating beef is worse than eating chicken. A cow is a larger, longer-lived, and more cognitively complex animal, and it takes many kilograms of feed and many square metres of land to produce a kilogram of beef. If you care about suffering per meal, beef is the greater harm.

B replied: That gets it backwards. Per kilogram of meat, chicken involves vastly more individual animals killed, and their lives in industrial sheds are plausibly worse than a cow's. A single cow may suffer more in total, but beef is often a by-product of dairy or raised on pasture; chicken is a high-volume, low-welfare system. If you are comparing typical supermarket meat, chicken is worse per calorie.

A said: But pasture is not free. Grass-fed beef uses far more land per calorie than chicken or crops, and that land could be wild habitat. If wild animals suffer, then land use itself is a harm. Beef's land footprint may be worse for wild-animal suffering than chicken's, even if chickens have worse lives on the farm.

B answered: Now you are invoking crop cultivation. But the crop-death argument is usually used against veganism: harvesting wheat kills field animals, so a plant-based diet is not harm-free. If you take field deaths seriously, you have to apply the same accounting to the crops fed to chickens and cattle. Cattle eat more crops than chickens per calorie, so beef still looks worse, or at least not better.

A responded: Not necessarily. Ruminants can eat grass on marginal land that could not grow crops for humans. If the choice is between a pasture that supports some wild invertebrates and a wheat field that kills them at harvest, the pasture may be the lower-harm use. And the replaceability point matters: farmed animals would not exist otherwise, so their deaths are not simply added to a baseline of zero. If their lives are net positive, then a system that brings them into existence may be defensible.

B countered: Replaceability cuts the other way for chickens. If their lives are net negative — and in industrial systems they plausibly are — then bringing more of them into existence is worse, not better. And you cannot use marginal land as an excuse for beef while ignoring that most beef is not grass-fed on marginal land; it is feedlot beef, which consumes crops. The comparison has to be between actual production systems, not the most favourable possible version of one side.

A said: Then the real issue is that we are trading off different kinds of harm: suffering per animal, number of animals, land use, wild-animal opportunity costs, and the counterfactual value of existence. There is no single metric. I say beef is worse because the land-use and climate costs are enormous; you say chicken is worse because the number of lives and intensity of suffering are enormous. We may be talking past each other.

B concluded: No, we are disagreeing about which metric should dominate. You are smuggling in wild-animal suffering only when it helps beef; if wild-animal suffering mattered, you would have to consider the invertebrates killed by pasture management, the habitat displaced by grazing, and the enormous number of wild animals affected by land conversion. You cannot treat pasture as a neutral baseline. And you have not said how to compare a cow's suffering to a chicken's, or a chicken's to a field mouse's. Without a common currency, your claim that beef is worse is just an assertion.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of the eight moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, broadly right, or clearly right, and explain briefly. Then say which specific claims each party is entitled to and which they are not. Finally, identify what both are missing — in particular, whether there is a principled way to compare harms across species and production systems, and what would have to be true for either side's conclusion to follow. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the reasoning, not the literature. Please write in British English.
