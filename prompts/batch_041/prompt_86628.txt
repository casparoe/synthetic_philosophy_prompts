I retired from a chemistry lab three years ago and have been teaching myself decision theory since, and I've gotten stuck on the Allais thing in a way I suspect isn't just me being slow. The puzzle itself I get. Offer me a sure million, or an 89% shot at a million plus a 10% shot at five million plus a 1% shot at nothing, and I take the sure million. Then offer me 11% at a million, or 10% at five million, and I suddenly want the five. Fine, whatever, I'm a human. What I don't get is the fight that's been going on about this for what seems like forever. One side says the independence axiom just is what being rational amounts to — if two options differ only on a branch that's equally likely either way, that branch can't be a reason to prefer one, and if my pattern of choices says it is, I've contradicted myself. The other side says the axiom is a bookkeeping rule that buys you a certain kind of numerical representation, and that when a large certain gain is sitting on the table something real is going on that the rule is blind to. Smart people, same numbers, no movement for decades.

My hunch is that the two sides aren't even answering the same question — one is doing psychology about how choices get made, the other is doing something more like logic about what 'consistent' means — but I can't tell if that's a real diagnosis or just me being naive about how philosophy works.

Also, one thing I noticed on my own that may or may not matter: when I copied both problems onto one page and wrote the 89% branch out as two seperate 44.5% pieces so the two problems sat there looking structurally identical, my choices flipped. Is that a curiosity about how I write things down, or does it bear on the diagnosis?

Don't give me a reading list, I can't use it. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — substantive, verbal, or two questions wearing one hat — and given that, what would count as progress here, or whether nothing should and why.
