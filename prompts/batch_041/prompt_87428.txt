I am a third-year student in a political theory seminar on memory and historical injustice, and our final assignment asks me to set out my own position and then show that it holds together. I would like you to audit it before I commit to it.

Here is where I have landed. I believe there is a genuine duty to remember grave historical wrongs, and that it is owed not to us but to the victims and to the dead; we who come later are only its custodians. I believe, though, that this duty is owed only within thick relations: I owe remembrance to the dead of my own community and to the wrongs of my own history, and I do not owe it to strangers, nor is there any duty to commemorate wrongs with which I have no connection. I believe the state has a legitimate part in this and may sometimes compel it, through national days of remembrance, public memorials, and curricula that require the wrongs to be taught. I believe forgetting is sometimes required rather than merely permitted — that a person who has been wronged is entitled to have the wrong stop defining them, and that it can be wrong to insist they keep the wound open. And I hold two verdicts. The first is that a state which has committed atrocities against a foreign population owes that population's descendants a duty of remembrance, even though they are not its own and no thick relation joins them. The second is that a community which demolishes its own memorial and lays a park over the site has done something wrong, even if no one is harmed and the result is more happiness.

Tell me whether this hangs together. I expect that some of these commitments pull against one another, that some only seem to, and that I cannot tell which is which. Sort them for me: name the genuine tensions, order them by how much damage they do, and for each say which commitment has to give, what giving it up would cost, and whether a single revision would settle more than one of them. I would much rather have a short, ordered account of the real problems than a long one of everything that might be a problem. Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — the way you would in a long letter to a colleague.
