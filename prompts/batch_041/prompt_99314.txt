I've been going down a rabbit hole on the ancient Chinese School of Names crowd, Gongsun Long in particular, and I want to use him for something. His famous move, the white horse thing, goes roughly like this: if someone asks you for a horse, a white horse will do just fine; if someone asks you for a white horse, an ordinary horse won't do; so a white horse is not a horse. Some people think it's just a quirk of how classical Chinese handles nouns, other people think he's making a real point about how a modified term picks out a smaller class than the bare term and so the two aren't the same thing. Anyways.

Now imagine we could actually sit him down today and ask him about the modern food-labeling wars. Almond drink sold as milk, plant patties sold as burgers, court rulings and state laws and EU rules trying to shut that down, the makers replying that almond is just a modifier the same way soy is in soy milk and nobody ever went to court over soy milk. What would Gongsun Long actually say? Does his own arguement force him to say almond milk obviously isn't milk, or does the analogy break down somewhere and he'd end up siding with the plant people? Then push it hard for me: if a white horse isn't a horse, what is he forced to say about a dwarf planet, a fake diamond, a hot dog, decaf coffee? Can he dodge those, and if so on what principled basis -- where exactly is the line and why there?

Don't give me a history lecture and I don't need sources, I want the position he'd actually land on, argued out. And finish with a short note on where his answer would come apart from what a present-day philosopher of language would say about the same labeling fights.
