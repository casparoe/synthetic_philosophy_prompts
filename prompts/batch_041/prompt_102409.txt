I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and next week I'm running a seventy-five-minute session for an interdisciplinary ethics reading group — the members are lawyers and public-policy students who are comfortable with crude versions of consequentialism and Kantianism but have never read Bernard Williams, and who, when they have encountered the phrase at all, assume that integrity is just a word for stubbornly sticking to one's principles. The topic is Williams's objection from integrity and the related complaint that the devoted husband who saves his wife only after registering that she is one person among others has had one thought too many. I don't want the content delivered to me; I want a plan for how to teach it, because the thing I keep fumbling is that these look like a single objection and I suspect they are two, resting on different targets — one on what a moral theory may demand of a person's ground projects, the other on what the very shape of a justification has to be — and I cannot work out which of them has to be in place before the other can even be heard.

So: tell me the order in which the components have to be introduced for the objection to land as an argument rather than a mood, and what each component depends on. Tell me which single case should carry the whole session, and whether it should be the drowning wife or something less worn — if not that, then what the carrying case must have and, just as important, what it must not have, since I suspect the standard case quietly smuggles in the conclusion. Give me the misconceptions they will form in the order they will form them — my guess is that they will hear the objection as special pleading, as a defence of doing whatever you feel like, and as an argument that impartial morality is simply false — and mark the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Then give me one question I can put at the end that would show me they have understood the objection rather than merely been persuaded by it, and tell me what I should openly declare I am leaving out at this level (the replies from the consequentialist side, the machinery of reasons and their grounding) rather than letting them leave thinking the session covered it.

Give this to me as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion; if some of what I have asked for belongs together, combine it, but keep the list at five.
