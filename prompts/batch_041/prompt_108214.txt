Set the dialogue in a contemporary context that neither figure could have anticipated: both are trying to make sense of a case in which a distributed network of anonymous experts, some human and some machine, produces a consensus report on a technical question. The Naiyāyika wants to say that accepting the report can be a genuine pramāṇa, not merely an inference from the reliability of each contributor; the Buddhist wants to say that the acceptance is always an inference, even if a compressed or tacit one, from signs of reliability. Let them press each other on the details: What exactly makes a speaker āpta, that is, a reliable authority? Does the Naiyāyika's account require a special kind of access to the speaker's character that ordinary hearers rarely have? Can the Buddhist explain how testimony yields knowledge in cases where the hearer could not reconstruct the inferential mark? Does the Naiyāyika's view collapse into the Buddhist's once we specify the conditions under which testimony is valid? The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth, with each responding to the other's actual points, and neither should be a strawman. End with a short exchange in which each states the strongest remaining objection to the other's position. I want a committed response: after the dialogue, give your own verdict on which side has the better of the exchange, with reasons, rather than just laying out the options. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the argument itself.
