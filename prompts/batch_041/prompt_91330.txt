In a long-running argument with two friends about whether a state should ever disarm unilaterally, one of them keeps ending the discussion with the proverb 'if you want peace, prepare for war', delivered as though it were a self-evident axiom rather than a contested empirical claim. I am a fairly committed pacifist, or at least I used to think I was, and I have noticed that I cannot tell whether the proverb is meant as a prediction, a definition, a piece of prudential advice, or a moral verdict -- and my friend seems to slide between these depending on which one I press him on. I would like the different precise claims that sentence could be making laid out for me, as a menu rather than a single reading: from the nearly vacuous, through the substantive and defensible, to the plainly false. Please say for each reading whether it is a platitude, a contentious thesis worth arguing about, or a howler, and say which reading is actually being invoked when someone deploys it to shut down a pacifist. I am especially interested in the difference between the causal reading (arming tends to produce peace, as a matter of contingent fact about how states behave) and the constitutive reading (that a peace not underwritten by force is not really peace at all), and in whether any reading of the proverb is compatible with the pacifist's position rather than merely opposed to it. I should add that I am not asking about the history of the phrase or who first said it; I am asking what work it can do in an argument right now, and whether the work it is doing in my friend's argument is legitimate.

Two further things. First, this has been running alongside a seminar I am taking on the ethics of war, where the tutor keeps invoking the just-war criteria of last resort and proportionality without ever spelling out what would count as meeting them, so if the analysis of the proverb can be connected to whether those criteria are ever actually satisfied in practice, that would help me. Second, and relatedly, my friend has a further move I find slippery: when I say that preparing for war makes war more likely, he replies that this just shows I am confusing peace with the absence of war, and that what he wants is a peace that is 'real' rather than merely a lull. I suspect this is doing something definitional rather than empirical, and I would like that suspicion tested rather than flattered.

Please write the answer in British English. Where your reasoning is most likely to fail, say so explicitly, and tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or argument, if it turned out to be correct, would push you towards a different verdict about which readings of the proverb are defensible.
