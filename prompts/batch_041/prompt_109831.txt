I'm on the naming and public art board for my city — mid-size midwest town, I got appointed last year because I teach at the community college. Thursday we vote on a recommendation about Coles Avenue, which runs through the east side. Ephraim Coles was the city engineer in the 1880s who laid out the water and sewer system; the mains he put in are still in the ground and still serving maybe a third of the city, and the reservoir is named after him too. He also wrote a lot arguing that the poor and the immigrant wards were basically a different kind of people, and that the city should withhold relief during the 1893 downturn because helping them would only breed more of them. There's no statue anywhere, just the street and the reservoir.

The campaign emailing us is a statewide group; they want the street renamed after a local woman who ran a settlement house, and they've told the paper that anything short of a full renaming is "a plaque on a wound" and they'll publicize the vote accordingly. The block association on Coles Ave — mostly Black homeowners, several families there since the 50s — says no. Their reasons: renaming means new deeds, licenses, insurance, mail, a hassle they'd have to eat, and if there's money to spend they'd rather it go to the neighborhood archive they've been trying to start for six years. Our art fund has about $260k. A full renaming with signage and an address-transition office is quoted at $140k; the archive would take $90k.

Two things people have said to me that I can't get out of my head. A colleague who works on philosophy of emotion says you can owe gratitude to someone you don't admire at all — gratitude tracks benefit received, admiration tracks character — so a name that means "thank you for the water" isn't the same speech act as a name that means "he was a good man," and maybe we're conflating the two. And a linguist friend says that after 130 years a street name has usually bleached into a pure address; nobody walking down Coles Ave is thanking anybody, it just points at a street. In which case what exactly is the harm we're supposed to be undoing?

Draft the opinion I'll circulate before Thursday: a concrete recommendation (rename, keep the name and add a permanent interpretive marker, rename with a transition fund for residents, or something else I haven't thought of), a timeline, who pays, and the reasoning. Engage the losing side for real — the campaign's point that a marker is what cities do when they want credit for having done something, and the block association's point that outsiders are spending their neighborhood's symbolic capital to buy a feeling. And tell me what would change your mind, and where in your own reasoning you'd bet against yourself.
