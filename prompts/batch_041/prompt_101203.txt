I have been trying, without much success, to say where legitimate persuasion stops and manipulation begins, and I have a set of gut verdicts that I cannot get to line up under any principle I have tried. I would like you to work out what principle, or small set of principles, my verdicts actually encode, to test it against a case I have not thought about, and to tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. My verdicts are these. A physician who believes one of two treatments is better, and who describes the risks of each in whichever terms make her preferred treatment look better, while saying nothing false: not manipulation, and I would resist the label. A union organizer who tells workers that a strike is far likelier to succeed than she believes it is, in order to get the vote she thinks is right: manipulation, and a serious one. A friend who does you a small unrequested favor and then reminds you of it at the moment of asking a large one: manipulation. A therapist who retells a patient's own history back to them in a way that makes a different self-understanding available, because she thinks the patient will be better off: I cannot call it manipulation, though something about it makes me uneasy. A salesperson who tells a customer, truthfully, that the last unit is about to go, knowing the customer is frightened of missing out: manipulation. A teacher who praises an essay she privately finds mediocre, in order to keep the student working: I want to say legitimate, yet I notice it is the same move as the salesperson's.

The best principle I have reached is this: an influence is manipulative when it makes the other person's deliberation go worse in a way they would disown if they could see the machinery, and persuasive when it supplies reasons they could own as their own. That seems to handle the salesperson and the organizer, and perhaps the friend. But the therapist appears to work precisely by giving the patient a new way to own her own reasons, and the physician seems to be exploiting a framing effect that the patient would disown if she saw it. So either the principle is wrong or one of my verdicts is, and I would like you to say which. Please state the principle precisely enough that it actually decides cases, give the case that motivates each clause, tell me what it implies for an unlisted case such as a host who seats guests so that two people who dislike each other never meet and a third guest, who would have mediated, is quietly left out — and, if you must overturn one of my verdicts, say which one and why the others are worth more to me than it is.
