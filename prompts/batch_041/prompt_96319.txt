I am the judge of a long-running game of Nomic, the game in which the players amend the rules during play, under rules that themselves specify how they may be amended. Our rule set is our own and need not match any published version of the game; it is short enough to state in full.

R1. A proposal to add, repeal, or amend a rule is adopted if it wins a simple majority of the players voting. With five players, a simple majority is three.

R2. A rule marked as entrenched may be amended or repealed only by a unanimous vote of all five players.

R3. Disputes about what the rules permit or require are settled by the judge, and a ruling stands unless it is overruled by a two-thirds vote, that is, by four players.

R4. No rule is exempt from amendment, R1 through R4 included.

R5. Only one proposal may be pending at a time, and proposals are voted on in the order in which they are submitted.

There are five players, all present and voting throughout the period in question. In three years of play I have ruled on six disputes.

1. A proposal raising the target score from 100 to 250, adopted 4–1. A player objected that the game's goal is not the kind of thing a majority may change. I upheld the amendment.

2. A proposal forbidding binding agreements made outside a session, adopted 5–0. It was objected to as unenforceable. I upheld it, holding that enforceability is not a condition of validity.

3. A proposal to repeal an entrenched rule, adopted 4–1. I held it void: an entrenched rule may be repealed only by the procedure it specifies for its own repeal, and a simple majority is not that procedure.

4. A proposal replacing voting in submission order with voting in random order, adopted 3–2 under the submission-order rule that it abolished. I upheld it.

5. A proposal adopted 4–1, challenged on the ground that it had been put to the vote while another proposal was still pending. I held it void.

6. A proposal making the judge's rulings final and unappealable, adopted 4–1. I was a party to the dispute. I upheld the amendment, holding that a rule which removes an avenue of appeal is not for that reason invalid.

The case now before me. A proposal was submitted and adopted 3–2, all five voting. Its text: the threshold for adopting any amendment is raised from a simple majority to two-thirds of the players, and the new threshold takes effect upon adoption. The mover argues that the proposal was adopted by the procedure in force at the moment of the vote, and is therefore in force; the objector argues that a rule requiring two-thirds for amendments must itself satisfy that requirement, which it did not, and is therefore void — from which it follows that the simple-majority rule is still in force, and that the proposal was validly adopted after all.

Give me the ruling. Set out the interpretive principles that fit my six rulings — at least two of them — and say what each implies about this proposal. Tell me which principle I have in fact been running, and how far the record alone determines that; name the candidate principles my record excludes, and the rulings that exclude them. Then name the next case that would come before me and would separate the surviving principles, and say what each of those principles would predict about it. Finally, tell me whether the circle the objector describes is a genuine contradiction with a correct resolution, or a gap that a judge can close only by stipulation — and if the latter, exactly what I would be stipulating, and what is lost by stipulating it rather than discovering it.

Answer from the record I have given you, not from any published treatment of self-amending games.
