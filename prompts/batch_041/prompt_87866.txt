I'm a science journalist, I write a column on the mind and I run a session every year at a summer school for science writers, and this year I want to do the psychiatric classification one without it being me talking at 25 people for 90 minutes about the DSM. Mixed room, a few psychiatrists, alot of generalists, nobody's read any philosophy of science, 90 minutes, one room, no laptops.

The thing I want them to actually feel is this: when you invent a new category of person, the people in the category can shift to fit it, and then the category gets redrawn around the shifted people, so the classification ends up partly manufacturing the thing it claims to be describing. Ian Hacking calls it the looping effect -- the targets are moving because the investigation interacts with them. I want that to happen to them in the room in the space of an hour, not be a slide.

So design me the activity. Rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the exact words I say when I hand out the first sheet, because I'm bad at improvising that part.

Constraints, in rough order of how much I care:

The category has to be invented by the group from scratch in the first 15 minutes, and it has to be about something that visibly happens in the room during the session -- how people take the floor in a discussion, that kind of thing -- so we can watch it operate twice before we're done. Nobody should be able to say "oh, that's just ADHD with a new name."

I want the whole turn of the loop, not just "labels change behaviour." So somewhere in the middle the group has to go back and revise the criteria they invented, and the ending should be the discovery that the revised criteria fit the people they've already become. If that reveal has to be engineered rather than emerge, fine, just tell me how.

Tell me how to measure the shift rather than assert it. Quick self-ratings before and after, a peer-sort, one person counting who speaks in round two -- something I can put on the board as numbers.

Ethics, since these are journalists and two of them will blog about it: nothing resembling a real diagnosis, especially nothing stigmatised, nobody walks out feeling sorted, easy opt-out, and the invented category has to be non-pejorative or the whole thing collapses into a bit.

Then the debrief, mapping what just happened onto the parts of the idea, including the scaffolding Hacking says a category needs to survive -- institutions, a body of knowledge, experts -- because a 90-minute workshop obviously can't build any of that.

And be straight with me about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time and what I do when it does (I'd bet money someone announces the point in minute five), and what the activity can't show. I'm pretty sure it can't tell looping apart from plain suggestibility, and it can't do the decade-long version where the textbooks get rewritten. Tell me which parts I just have to say out loud myself.
