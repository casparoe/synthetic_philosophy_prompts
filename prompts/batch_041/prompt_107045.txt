Someone in my political theory group keeps pulling out the same analogy whenever we argue about protest: the law is like the rules of chess. You can campaign to change a rule and players do, but while a game is going you follow them -- a player who slides her rook diagonally and then announces she's taking a principled stand isn't brave, she's just not playing chess anymore. So, he says, blocking a road or withholding a tax as protest is that same move: you're demanding the protection of the system while breaking its constitutive rules, which makes it either incoherent or just cheating with a manifesto. Lately he's pushed it further: even resigning is a move inside the rules, and if you truly can't stand the rule you sit the tournament out, which he says is exactly like emigrating.

I'd like an actual audit instead of just a vibe. Give me the correspondence table -- players, rules, referee, the clock, resigning, sitting out, cheating -- then where the mapping breaks, and tell me which of those breaks matter for the conclusion he's drawing and which are just decoration. Does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If there's a better analogy for the same job, say what it is. And one thing I keep circling back to: he leans hard on "constitutive," but chess rules make the activity possible in a way that speed limits and tax codes kind of don't, right? Does that word do the work he needs it to do, or is he smuggling it in?
