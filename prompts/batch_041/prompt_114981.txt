I belong to a small metaphysics reading group that has spent its last two sessions on the principle of identity of indiscernibles and on the two-spheres thought experiment usually credited to Max Black, and another member has circulated a short write-up meant to lay out the principle and the counterexample for the rest of us. I have a nagging sense that it is doing something unfair, but I do not yet have the vocabulary to say what, and I would like an audit of it that I can take back to the group. The write-up runs as follows: 'Leibniz's Law, which is also called the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, says that if two things are identical then they have all their properties in common; its converse says that if two things have all their properties in common then they are one and the same thing. Black's thought experiment disposes of the second claim. Imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, each two miles across and two miles from the other, exactly alike in colour, temperature and every other respect. The spheres share every property, so the principle says they are a single object; but plainly there are two of them. The principle is therefore false. It follows that identity cannot be reduced to properties, that we need primitive thisnesses or haecceities to keep numerically distinct things apart, and that the bundle theory of substance is untenable, since a bundle of properties cannot explain how two indiscernible things differ.'

I do not want a literature survey, and please do not give me citations or reading lists -- I am not going to chase down who said what. What I want is a sentence-by-sentence audit. For each claim in the write-up, tell me whether a competent metaphysician would simply accept it, whether it is a defensible simplification for a general audience, whether it misrepresents the position being attacked, or whether it is plainly false; and then tell me whether the write-up's concluding morals -- that haecceities are needed and that bundle theory is refuted -- actually follow from anything true in the passages that precede them, or whether they are smuggled in.

Two further things I would like folded into the audit rather than treated separately. First, I keep being told that the principle has to be stated far more carefully than the write-up states it: that there are weaker and stronger versions, and that unless the properties in question are restricted to purely qualitative ones the principle comes out trivially true, because of the sort of property a thing has in virtue of being identical with a particular object. I would like to know how much of the write-up's argument survives once the principle is stated in the form its defenders actually hold, and whether the two-spheres case is even relevant to that form. Second, I want to know whom the thought experiment is supposed to embarrass: does it refute the principle outright, or does it only show that the principle is incompatible with the possibility of such a universe, so that anyone who regards the principle as necessary can simply deny that possibility and thereby escape? Someone in the group also objected that if the spheres are truly indiscernible then the claim that there are two of them rather than one adds nothing that could ever be checked, and that a difference which could make no difference to anything is no difference at all; I would like to know whether that is a separate objection or a restatement of the same one, and whether it can be answered by pointing to the relational structure of the imagined universe. Finally, since I am now inclined to doubt everything in the write-up, tell me the one thing in it that is right and that I should not let the group talk me out of.
