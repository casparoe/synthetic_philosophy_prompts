I am assembling a focused research program on origin essentialism — the thesis that a given organism or person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg (or, more carefully, a different initial cell or set of gametes) than the ones from which it in fact originated. I have enough funding to support roughly four researcher-years of conceptual work, and I want that effort directed where it will do the most good rather than toward whichever questions happen to be most written about.

Please give me a defended prioritization of the open questions in this area. For each question you put forward, explain what makes it worth the years: whether it is upstream of other disputes (for instance, whether it depends on or would settle questions about transworld identity, haecceitism, the necessity of constitution, the relation between organisms and persons, or the analogy with artifacts); how tractable it is with the tools of analytic metaphysics rather than empirical biology; how neglected it is relative to its importance; and what would move it up or down your list.

Among the candidates I would like you to weigh — though you need not be bound by my list — consider: whether the essentiality of origin is a thesis about individuals, about kinds, or about both; whether it survives cases of twinning and of the fusion or splitting of early embryos; how it should handle cases in which the biological starting materials are not a simple sperm-and-egg pair, such as mitochondrial replacement techniques, where a child inherits nuclear DNA from two parents and mitochondrial DNA from a third party, and whether such cases expose a gap between origin as a biological event and origin as a fact about which materials compose the individual; whether the doctrine is genuinely counterintuitive or merely appears so on first hearing; and whether it has any bearing on adjacent practical questions, including gamete donation and embryo selection.

I care less about the list than about the reasoning and the ordering. State your criteria explicitly, show where they conflict with one another, and say what argument or result over the next few years should change the ranking. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I want an agenda, not a survey. Write in American English, and end with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing your ranked list, most important first, as a short comma-separated sequence of labels.
