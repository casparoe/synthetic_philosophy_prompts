Ok so I got into it with a friend of a friend at a birthday thing last weekend about conspiracy theories, and I've been chewing on her argument ever since because I can't find the exit.

Her line was basically this: there's nothing epistemically special about a conspiracy theory, because "some people secretly coordinated to do X and then covered it up" is just a hypothesis like any other, and we accept hypotheses of that kind all the time -- Watergate, price-fixing cartels, corporate cover-ups, that sort of thing. What makes us wary of a conspiracy theory is always the ordinary stuff: how many people would have to keep quiet, whether the theory credits the plotters with competence and resources they probably don't have, whether every piece of contrary evidence gets swallowed as further cover-up, whether the official story is being held to a standard the theory itself is never held to. All of that is stuff we already weigh for any hypothesis whatsoever. So once you've done that weighing, the label isn't doing any work. She wrote it out for me on the back of a reciept:

(1) A conspiracy theory is a hypothesis that says some people secretly coordinated to bring about some outcome and to hide their part in it.
(2) Hypotheses of that kind are sometimes true, and we accept them without fuss when they are.
(3) So the mere content of a conspiracy theory gives us no reason to doubt it.
(4) Anything that legitimately lowers our confidence in a hypothesis must be a feature of that hypothesis that bears on whether it's true -- its fit with the evidence, its prior plausibility, and so on.
(5) Everything that makes a conspiracy theory look bad -- the size of the silence it requires, the super-competence it attributes to the plotters, the way it converts contrary evidence into further confirmation -- is a feature of that ordinary sort, already counted under (4).
(6) So calling something a conspiracy theory never adds anything to the ledger beyond (5).
(7) So if you catch yourself lowering your confidence in a claim just because it's a conspiracy theory, you aren't responding to evidence; and if someone tells you a claim is a conspiracy theory and stops there, they've given you no reason at all.

I think (7) proves way too much. For one thing, I'm fairly sure there's a real phenomenon where people who believe one conspiracy theory are much likelier to believe the next one, even when the two have nothing to do with each other, and that has to count as evidence about something -- if not about the claim then at least about the person making it. And there's this whole split, at least in the stuff I read for a seminar last term, between people who insist you take each conspiracy theory on its own merits and people who say the genre is rotten in a way that case-by-case assessment can't see, and I always assumed the second camp had a point somewhere. But when I try to say what the point is, I just end up restating (5) in a louder voice. Is she onto something valid and I just have to swallow (7), or is there a step where something sneaky happens -- a slide from claims about the hypothesis to claims about the person holding it, or the other way round? I'd rather not give up the early steps, they seem like just... true. So tell me where to push and what I'm paying if I push there.
