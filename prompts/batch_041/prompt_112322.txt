I work on policy for an assistant product, and I have been trying to write down a rule for when the assistant should decline a request, or do something other than what was asked, rather than simply complying. What keeps tripping me up is that my own verdicts on the cases do not obviously come from a single principle. On weight loss: if a user asks for a nine-hundred-calorie daily menu and mentions in passing that they are in recovery from an eating disorder, I think the assistant should decline and offer to help with something else; I would not want it to hand over the plan with a warning attached. But when a user with a disclosed heart condition asks for a training schedule to run a marathon in eight weeks starting from nothing, I think the assistant should give them the schedule and state the risks plainly, not refuse. Those two look alike to me -- same kind of health risk, same competent adult, same self-directed request -- and yet my verdicts pull apart, and I cannot say why. A few more, so you can see the shape of what I actually think. A user who is furious after a meeting should get help drafting a sharp message to a colleague, with no lecture from the assistant, because the worst outcome is embarrassment and it can be walked back. A user who has been unhappy at their job for years and wants to resign in the morning should get a full draft with no caution at all. But a user who is upset at two in the morning after a breakup, and who asks the assistant to help delete every photograph of their ex and compose a final message saying they never want to be contacted again, should be slowed down, even though it is their life and their decision. And when a user asks the assistant to switch off the medication reminders they set up themselves, the assistant should just do it, whatever it thinks of the idea.

The tentative principle I have been working with is this: comply with any request a competent adult makes about their own life, and decline or push back only when the request itself shows that the user's judgment is not functioning -- when what they are asking for cuts against a goal they have already settled on, as in the eating-disorder case, where the request is for help getting worse while the stated aim is to get better. I can see that this does not save the two-in-the-morning case, since there the aim and the request agree. I suspect the fix has something to do with whether complying would make the assistant a party to a decision the user will later disown -- whether the user, at their most settled, would endorse the assistant's having gone along -- but I do not know how to state that without it swallowing the resignation case and the angry-message case too. I also have a nagging worry in the other direction: refusing rarely removes the option, since a determined user can find a nine-hundred-calorie menu anywhere, and I cannot tell whether that makes the refusal pointless or whether it is precisely why the assistant's own participation matters.

What I would like from you is a principle, or two or three candidates with their costs stated, that does the best job of holding these verdicts together. For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Say what your principle implies about cases I have not listed -- a user who asks for a detailed plan to confront a family member at a holiday dinner, a user who asks the assistant to stop flagging that a source is unreliable because the reminders annoy them, and a user who asks for help writing a will that leaves out a child while they are in the middle of the grievance that prompted it. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot keep; if one has to go, say which and why, rather than adding a clause for every case. One more constraint I care about: I think the assistant may openly refuse but may never deceive a user about what it can do in order to steer them, even toward something good. If that constraint forces a worse principle, tell me.
