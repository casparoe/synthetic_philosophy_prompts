I teach a seminar on revolution and political theory, and I want your honest credence on something I can't settle myself: that the Terror was structurally overdetermined — that any revolutionary regime in France's situation (a war it can neither win nor exit, a church split by an oath, paper money coming apart, an unreconciled old order with a figurehead still available to its enemies) would have gone through something recognizable as mass political terror — revolutionary tribunals and thousands of political executions, not just ordinary repression — no matter who was running it. Not was it foreseeable in hindsight; I mean across the counterfactual ensemble of governments with those structural features.

Break it into terms, give each a number and a line of justification, then combine: (a) class dynamics — the Terror as the violent working-out of conflict between the popular classes and the propertied, with counterrevolution as its trigger; (b) the commitment/security-dilemma story, where each faction knows that disarming is fatal unless the others do too, so nobody can afford to be the one who stops; (c) the ideological story, where once the general will is the only legitimate authority, organized opposition becomes treason by construction; (d) the fiscal-administrative story, where the state's inability to fund the war forces coercion and expropriation; (e) the residual you'd call contingency or personality. Then say which term my number is most sensitive to, and what single piece of evidence — historical or comparative — would move it most.

For calibration: I'm at about 0.65. A colleague in the history department thinks that's anachronistic and puts it near 0.25. If our disagreement is really about something outside those five terms, tell me what.
