I am applying for a six-month fellowship in legal theory and formal methods, and I need a short research proposal — roughly two thousand words — built around a half-formed idea of mine. The idea concerns self-amending rule systems: the family of games and legal orders in which the participants may change the rules, including the rules governing how rules are changed. The best-known example is the game Nomic, whose opening rule tells players how to amend the rules and whose subsequent moves can amend that very rule; constitutional amendment clauses and self-modifying software share the structure.

My suspicion is that discussion of such systems has been dominated by synchronic self-reference — a rule that mentions itself, as in a rule forbidding its own amendment, or one declaring itself repealed — while the more interesting and less theorized case is diachronic self-amendment: not a rule that refers to itself, but a procedure that rewrites the procedure that rewrites it. I would like to treat a rule-set as a state and the amendment procedure as an operator on states, and then ask what it means for a system to be able to rewrite the operator that rewrites it. My conjecture is that the familiar "paradoxes" of self-amendment are not all of one kind. A rule that lowers the quorum required to change itself, a rule that forbids its own amendment, and a rule that repeals itself may be sorted by whether the amendment operator has a fixed point at the relevant level — some are merely unstable, others are genuinely unrepresentable. I also want to test an analogy with Tarski's hierarchy of languages: perhaps amendment can be stratified into ranks, so that a rule of rank n can alter only rules below rank n, and the question then becomes whether a Nomic-style system can be "closed under amendment" or whether closure is impossible for the same reason a language cannot contain its own truth predicate.

Please turn this into a proposal. It should contain: a precise statement of the question, sharp enough that it has a determinate answer rather than dissolving into "how should we think about self-amendment"; an account of why the standard jurisprudential apparatus — a foundational rule of recognition, a basic norm, an entrenched constitution — leaves this question open instead of settling it; the method, with a month-by-month plan for six months, distinguishing the parts that are formal modeling from the parts that are conceptual; what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing or collapsing into triviality; and an honest assessment of whether this is genuinely a six-month project or in fact a multi-year one. Assume I can supply my own citations later, so refer to positions and arguments rather than to particular works, and do not include a bibliography.
