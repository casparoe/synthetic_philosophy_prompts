I am a retired geophysicist who spent a career reconstructing past climates, and I have recently taken up the philosophy of history. Please take the longstanding dispute over how historical events are to be explained — the covering-law model, on which an event is explained when it is shown to follow from general laws together with antecedent and boundary conditions, and the narrative alternative, on which what does the explaining is instead the telling of a connected story — and restate both positions as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in: state spaces and trajectories, dynamical laws, initial and boundary conditions, ensembles, coarse-graining, sensitivity to initial conditions, retrodiction versus prediction, and the contrast between the repeatable and the merely contingent. Give each side the strongest reading it can bear in those terms, and do not quietly substitute my framework's assumptions for theirs. Then give me the residue report: which of their claims carry over cleanly, which survive only in a distorted form, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. I am especially interested in what becomes of the idea that a narrative answers the question of why this happened rather than something else, and of any element that seems to rest on the actors' own reasons and self-understandings. Close with your view of whether the residue marks a limitation of the scientific vocabulary I am imposing on the debate, or rather a sign that the participants are disagreeing less than they suppose. Please write your answer in American English.
