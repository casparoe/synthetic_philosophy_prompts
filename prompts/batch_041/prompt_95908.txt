I'm writing a feature about why moral arguments never seem to get settled, and I keep ending up talking to people who call themselves relativists — an anthropologist, two philosophers, one extremely confident grad student. Before I do any more interviews I want the spec, so I don't keep asking each of them the same half-formed questions. What does a theory of moral relativism actually have to deliver to be worth taking seriously?

Give me the requirements list, each with the case or consideration behind it. The things I keep circling: it has to explain what's happening when two communities make claims that sound like they're contradicting each other, and say which apparent disagreements are real and which are just people using the same word for different things (I have a case I'm fond of where two groups both talk about marriage and aren't talking about the same thing at all); it has to keep the descriptive claim (moral codes differ) apart from the metaethical one (moral truth is relative to a standard) and the normative one (so we should be tolerant), and tell me whether any of those three actually implies the others; it has to survive the reformer — the person inside a community saying our way of doing this is wrong — and the person who leaves one code and takes up another and then judges their parents by the new one; it has to say something about why moral claims present themselves as claims about how things are rather than about how we feel; and it shouldn't be true by default, i.e. not so watered down that everyone's a relativist already.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just what particular camps happen to want. And if two of them can't be had together — my hunch is that explaining genuine disagreement and denying any single standard pull against each other — say so, thats the part I'd build the piece around. Skip the who-believes-what; I can get that elsewhere.
