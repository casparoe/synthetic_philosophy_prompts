I teach an upper-level seminar on decision theory and its critics: twelve students, one ninety-minute session, almost all of them fresh from a microeconomics course and inclined to treat rationality as expected utility maximization. I want a single session that makes them feel the pull of satisficing before they have a name for it, and I want the feeling to come from what they do rather than from what I say. Design the activity.

Constraints. No lecture up front. Keep the words satisficing, bounded rationality, and Simon out of the room until the debrief, so that they walk into the phenomenon rather than being handed it. Twelve people, one room, no computers — pen, paper, and whatever I can print. No real money, but a payoff scheme they will actually care about for ninety minutes. I need rules, materials, roles if any, a timeline with timings, the debrief, and a frank note on the one feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by talking.

What the activity has to make them experience, in their own behavior:

1. That they stopped on the basis of an acceptance level — a threshold of good enough — rather than by computing anything, and that they can be brought to see the threshold in their own record rather than a calculation.
2. That the threshold drifted, and drifted for reasons that had nothing to do with the task: up after a run of good outcomes, down after bad ones, tracking their own history rather than the environment.
3. That the environment is doing at least half of the explaining. I do not want an activity whose moral is that people are lazy. I want the same procedure to look competent in one task environment and ruinous in another, so that the verdict on it changes with the environment rather than with the person.
4. That they cannot comfortably redescribe what they did as optimizing all along with the cost of thinking folded in. The debrief should put that rescue in front of them and leave them genuinely unsure whether it works, rather than waving it away.

Tell me as well what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it.
