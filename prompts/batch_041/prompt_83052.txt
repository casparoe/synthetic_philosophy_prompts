You are drafting the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two committed parties. The first is a Gödelian anti-mechanist of the Lucas–Penrose stripe: he holds that the incompleteness theorems show that no Turing machine, and hence no computational system of the kind a brain could implement, can capture the totality of human mathematical reasoning. The second is a mechanist: she holds that human mathematical cognition is realized by some computational system, and that the incompleteness theorems, read correctly, show nothing of the kind. Both are technically competent; neither is a caricature; and the deliverable must be a document both would actually sign, with neither granted a rhetorical edge.

The collaboration is to be organized around the following stipulated case material, which both parties have agreed in advance to reason about and which is to do real work rather than serve as decoration:

1. A machine whose axioms are given by the computable sequence R(0) = PA, R(n+1) = R(n) + Con(R(n)), each closed under first-order consequence. Its axiom set is decidable, so its theory is recursively enumerable and incompleteness applies to it. Ask what each party says about the reflection intuition that a sound reasoner who accepts R(0) should also accept Con(R(0)), then Con(R(1)), and so on indefinitely — and whether this machine differs in kind or only in degree from the original target of the anti-mechanist's argument.

2. A stipulated twin: a system whose observable mathematical behaviour, including its avowals that it can see the truth of a sentence unprovable in its own theory, is stipulated to be identical to the human's. Ask each party whether the anti-mechanist's argument has any purchase on this case, and what follows either way.

3. A machine whose theory T is stipulated to be exactly the set of sentences the human being will ever accept. Ask each party what the anti-mechanist's argument, applied to T, implies here, and which of the resulting options it takes to obtain.

Then produce the statement itself, in four parts:

First, a numbered list of agreed claims, including the logical skeleton both parties accept — I expect that the argument, taken at face value, forces a three-way choice among: the human's accepted theory is not recursively enumerable; the human cannot in fact come to accept the Gödel sentence of the theory she endorses; and that theory is inconsistent — together with whatever else both can sign about what the theorems do and do not establish, and about what would count as a decisive resolution.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give the position of each side in wording that side would accept, the same disagreement as the other side would formulate it, and the crux on which it turns.

Third, for each crux, the case, argument, or result both parties agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about how it comes out, recorded before the fact.

Fourth, a short section identifying which parts of the dispute are verbal — for instance whether machine is being taken to mean Turing machine or any physically realizable computing device, or whether proving is being taken to mean formal derivability or informal demonstrability — and explaining why.

Do not cite the literature, appeal to named authorities, or supply a reading list; the parties must make their cases from the mathematics and the arguments alone. Where a technical notion is needed, use it and explain it.
