Ok so I've become the guy at parties who defends the world happiness rankings, and I want to know how much rope I'm actually giving away.

What I hold: (a) the ladder question — 0 to 10, top rung is the best possible life for you, bottom is the worst — is a legitimate measure of something real, namely people's own verdict on their own lives, and it beats any expert-built index of well-being we'd cook up for them; (b) the stuff that moves it — income, health, having someone to count on, freedom, low perceived corruption — is stuff worth wanting on independent grounds; (c) so a country trying to climb the table is doing the right thing, even though "happiness" is a loose label for what's being measured.

What counts against that? Not the lazy objections (money isn't everything, rankings are clickbait). The ones that actually bite, ranked. A few things I already suspect and want you to settle:

- The six ingredients the report uses to account for a country's score mostly come out of the same questionaire, answered by the same person in the same call as the ladder itself. How much of the "accounting" is just that?
- The residual is often the single biggest chunk of a country's score, bigger than any one of the six. If so, the six factors aren't explaining much of the level at all — doesn't that gut (b)?
- The top twenty are seperated by differences I suspect are inside the sampling error, and everyone lives in the top third of a bounded scale anyway. Is the ordering mostly noise, and does that matter if the broad bands are real?
- The report also collects how people actually felt yesterday, and by that measure the top of the table looks different (Latin America up, Nordics down). So which one is the happiness ranking?
- Here's the one I can't get out of my head. Two countries, identical income, health, everything, improving at the same rate — but in one, everyone expects their kids to do better, and in the other, everyone thinks the country is in decline. I'm pretty sure the ladder ranks the second one lower. If so, is it measuring well-being at all, or is it measuring confidence about the future and how you compare to your neighbours? And if it's the latter, is "confidence about the future" something a country should be trying to top the charts on, or is that just an invitation to state propaganda? (Goodhart and all that.)

Where I'm flexible: I'd drop the word happiness tomorrow, and I'd give up (b) if the evidence went that way. What I won't give up is (a), because a self-report about your own life beats an index we build for you. Price my options.

And: is my position insulated? I can always retreat to "well, it measures life evaluation, not happiness, so your objection misses." Real distinction or rescue? Say so if it's a rescue, and whether that's actually a problem here.

Keep it short. And tell me what you'd concede if you were me, not just what the critics would say.
