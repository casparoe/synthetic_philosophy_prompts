My aunt has Alzheimer's and can't make her own decisions anymore, and the hospital keeps asking the family what she "would have wanted" -- they'll dig up something she said at a dinner party years ago and treat it like a will. My cousin, on the other hand, was born with a severe intellectual disability and has never had the capacity to form opinions about medical treatment, and he gets a completely different routine: the doctors just decide whats "in his best interest," and nobody asks what he would have wanted, like there's no "would have" to ask about.

I get that the two cases get handled differently but I can't tell what the difference is supposed to rest on. In the first case we extrapolate a whole set of preferences out of a couple offhand remarks and then call the result her real will. In the second we act like there's nothing there to extrapolate from at all. What view of persons, or of what makes a preference authoritative, does that split presuppose? Is it that a preference only has standing if she actually held it at some point, so counterfactual preferences count only when they're anchored to a real one? And how does that square with the coherent-extrapolated-volition picture -- the idea that what you really want is what you'd want if you were fully informed and your preferences were cleaned up and thought all the way through -- which seems to say the idealized version outranks the actual one? Tell me which parts of the practice strictly need a view like that and which parts are just administratively convenient, where the practice is underdetermined enough that several theories of the person would license it, and what the whole thing would look like if we took the extrapolated-volition picture seriously for both patients instead of just the one. Also, is this one theory of the person or two different ones stapled together?
