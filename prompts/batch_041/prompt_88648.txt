My sister's wedding is in three weeks and I'm officiating, which is mostly because I got ordained online in about ten minutes and nobody else wanted to do it. The part I'm stuck on is the short address before the vows. Our side of the family is churchy — my uncle will definately be praying over the shrimp cocktail — and her fiance's side is very much the protest-and-petition crowd, and there has already been one incident about a seating chart. Everyone's going to be at long tables together, so both halves are going to be watching the other half watch me. I'd like to say something real instead of the usual "deep down we all want the same things" mush, partly because I don't think that's true and partly because I don't think either family believes it either. I also don't want to insult anybody, and I really don't want to come off like I'm giving a TED talk at a wedding.

So here's what I've been chewing on. I'm a software engineer, and I spend my days writing incident postmortems where the whole trick is separating "why did this happen" from "whose fault is it" — and this feels like the same shape of problem. I've been reading about moral foundations theory, the idea that people on the left mostly run on care and fairness while people on the right run on those plus loyalty, authority, and purity/sanctity, and that this is why each side finds the other not just mistaken but kind of creepy. That clicked for me. But the conclusion people seem to take from it — "so the traditional side has the fuller, more complete morality" — seems like a non sequitur, because explaining why someone has the gut reactions they have doesn't tell you whether their moral claims are true, same as a good postmortem doesn't tell you who should be blamed. And the thing that really threw me is that even the foundations both sides claim to share don't line up: everybody says "fairness," but one family seems to mean everyone gets the same and the other seems to mean you get what you earned, and I don't think they've ever noticed they're using the word differently.

Write me that address. Three minutes spoken, plain language, no jargon, no naming researchers or citing studies — these are normal people at a party. It should actually use the stuff above to explain to the room why the two families keep talking past each other, without ever sneaking in a verdict about which side is morally right, and without being so even-handed that it sounds like nothing is at stake. It needs one moment of humor both halves can laugh at, a bit where I admit the limits of what I actually know, and it has to end with a line I can say while raising a glass. Last thing: if there's any point where I'd be promising the couple something I can't deliver, flag it and give me a different line.
