I am applying for a six-month seed grant from my faculty's research fund and need a research proposal of roughly 1,800 words. I have a half-formed idea that I would like you to turn into a proper proposal. I will add the references myself, so please describe positions and arguments rather than citing anything, and do not include a bibliography or a literature review.

My idea concerns Strawson's account of moral responsibility, which locates it in the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, guilt — and which is usually read as making those attitudes responses to the good or ill will, or the indifference, that another person displays toward us. Call that the quality-of-will condition. What I cannot stop noticing is a family of attitudes that share the shape of the reactive ones but are aimed at targets that cannot hold or express any will toward us at all: the dead, characters in fiction, my own past self, and — more contestably — animals, institutions, and people who have lost the capacities of agency. I have been calling these detached reactive attitudes; the label is my own coinage and not a term of art, so please treat it as a stipulation rather than as something to look up. They seem to carry the same phenomenology (a sense of being owed something, a pull either to withdraw or to demand) and the same action-tendencies (avoidance, confrontation, the wish for acknowledgment) as ordinary resentment or gratitude. That suggests two readings. On the first, the detached attitudes are parasitic on the genuine article: they are imaginative extensions of attitudes whose home is interpersonal, and they tell us nothing about what a reactive attitude essentially is. On the second, they are a stress test that the quality-of-will condition fails, and the real core of the reactive attitudes is something else — a demand for acknowledgment, say, or the maintenance of a claim to be a term in a relationship of mutual regard — of which the quality-of-will condition is only a special case. I want to know which reading survives, and what the survivor does to the rest of the theory, in particular to the familiar contrast between excusing someone (modifying the attitude) and exempting them (withdrawing it altogether).

The question I want the proposal to state precisely, and to make answerable within six months, is this: is there a single structure that covers both the attitudes directed at those who can show us good or ill will and the attitudes directed at targets that cannot, and if there is, does the quality-of-will condition survive as a condition on it?

Please write the proposal in the following sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths, for a total of about 1,800 words:

- "The question" (about 150 words): state the question so that it has a determinate answer, and set out the two candidate answers so that a reader knows in advance what would settle it either way.
- "Why the field leaves it open" (about 300 words): explain how the main ways of reading Strawson — the response-dependent reading, the quality-of-will reading, and the second-personal or address reading — each handle, or fail to handle, the detached cases, and identify the exact point at which the question becomes live rather than merely curious.
- "The strategy and its rivals" (about 400 words): the method, and why it beats the two obvious alternatives, which are a conceptual analysis of the term reactive attitude and a survey of what people report feeling. Make explicit what the detached cases are doing in the argument — acting as a stress test on a proposed necessary condition, not as evidence that such attitudes exist — and say what kind of result would count as a generalisation of the account rather than an ad hoc patch.
- "Work plan" (about 400 words): month by month across six months, assigning the borderline cases (the dead, the fictional, one's past and future selves, animals, corporate agents) to specific months, and marking the point in the schedule at which the account either generalises or breaks.
- "Deliverables" (about 100 words): one article of roughly 10,000 words and one workshop paper, with a sentence on what each will contain.
- "What success and failure look like" (about 250 words): what a reader should be able to say afterwards that they could not say before, and the honest conditions under which the project fails — for instance, if the detached attitudes turn out to be merely metaphorical, or if the unified account collapses into the uninformative claim that we care about things.
- "Scope and risks" (about 200 words): what could make this too big for six months, what I would cut first, and what the proposal would have to concede if the detached cases turn out to be less revealing than I think.
