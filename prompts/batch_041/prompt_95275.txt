I direct a small evidence-and-evaluation unit inside a large city government: two analysts and me. We exist to answer one recurring question for departments that are about to commit public money. A department arrives with a proposal — scale a pilot to every school in the district, renew a multi-year contract, expand a program to twenty sites — and asks whether to proceed now or to gather more evidence first. Requests arrive most weeks, the mayor's office expects a written recommendation within a week, I am the person who signs off, and once a program is funded the money is not recoverable within that fiscal year.

I keep hearing the same argument from colleagues who have read a little decision theory. They say that the expected value of information is never negative: whatever you are deciding, learning something relevant before you decide cannot make you worse off in expectation, because if the information turns out to be useless you can ignore it and choose exactly as you would have chosen anyway; and they say this holds for any agent who maximizes expected utility. From this they conclude that my unit should almost always be recommending another pilot, a larger sample, one more round of data before anyone commits. I suspect this is a misuse of a real result, but I cannot say precisely where the misuse lies, and I am not certain I have the result itself stated correctly.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my unit can actually run, at a cost of a few analyst-hours, on a proposal of this kind. It must be faithful to what the decision-theoretic account of the value of information really establishes — both the non-negativity claim and the machinery for pricing imperfect information, such as the expected value of perfect information and of a sample — while remaining usable by people who will never evaluate an integral.

Write it as an ordered checklist an analyst can follow without me in the room. In particular, it should:

- set out the questions to ask in order, and say what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- name the quantities it needs — the prior, the likelihood of what a further study would show, the value of each outcome, the cost and delay of gathering — and specify what to do when, as will usually be the case, none of them can be estimated with any confidence;
- say where the procedure terminates in a recommendation to decide now and where it terminates in a recommendation to gather more, and give the most usable approximation of the value of further evidence, saying whether the probability that the evidence would actually change the decision is the right thing to focus on;
- state plainly the assumptions the non-negativity result requires and that our proposals routinely violate: information that is not free; delay that is itself costly; decisions that cannot wait; decision-makers who are not expected-utility maximizers; cases where a department's preference is partly a preference not to know; cases where the information will become public and change other people's behaviour; and cases where a further study can only test a model we have already assumed rather than tell us that the model is wrong;
- separate the value of information for the decision at hand from the value of knowing, and from the value of being seen to have studied the question;
- mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, and the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, a mentoring pilot with sixty participants showing a modest positive effect that does not reach statistical significance, which a department wants to scale to the whole district. Second, a long-running service contract, cheap to exit, that a department wants to evaluate properly before renewing. Third, a lead-paint remediation program in a neighbourhood where children are already testing high, where a department proposes a twelve-month study before expanding. At least one of the three must come out in a way I will not like, and where that happens you should say why the intuition that non-negativity licenses waiting does not survive contact with the actual conditions.

Close with what the procedure cannot do, and say how confident you are in each part of what you have told me, singling out the claim you are least sure of. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need something I can put in front of an analyst on Monday.
