Construct a thought experiment in the spirit of the tortoise who accepts the premises of a modus ponens argument and the conditional, and still declines the conclusion — but built to tighter specifications than the original, and without appealing to the dialogue itself.

Specifications. The reasoner must be realistic: a person, a committee, or a plainly implementable machine, not an idealized agent; its shortfall must not be stupidity, inattention, an unstated premise it never learned, or an outright false belief. The argument must be an ordinary deductive inference, with no exotic logic. The reasoner must accept every premise and the corresponding conditional explicitly, sincerely, and with full understanding of the terms. And you must show that the obvious repair buys nothing: say which further premise of the usual kind you tried supplying and why the reasoner is exactly where it was before.

Then deliver, in this order:

1. The case — one paragraph, ending with a precise statement of what the reasoner accepts and what it declines.
2. The three verdicts — a short paragraph each, on whether the reasoner is unjustified: (a) on the view that an inference is justified only once the reasoner has taken the rule as binding on itself, so that what is missing is an act of commitment rather than another belief; (b) on the view that grasping the concepts in the premises already contains the inference, so that anyone who genuinely accepts the premises cannot coherently stop short of the conclusion; (c) on the view that deductive logic is not a theory of how to reason, in which case the case reports a psychological fact and there is no normative gap to close.
3. What is doing the work — a few sentences naming the single feature of the case that makes the three verdicts come apart.
4. Removing the feature — a few sentences on whether dropping it collapses the case into a plain question about justification or merely relocates the problem.
5. The counterattack — a short paragraph on how a defender of whichever position fares worst would try to block the case.

The case is for a specific purpose: deciding whether the regress is a problem about the norms governing inference or merely about how finite reasoners happen to be built. A case that stipulates its verdicts instead of generating them from the three views is no use to me.
