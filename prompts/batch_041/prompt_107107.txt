I'm a novelist — two books out, a third one stalled — and I've been writing a short essay on Émilie du Châtelet's ideas about happiness for a literary anthology about women who wrote about how to live. I've read her Discourse on Happiness in the Chicago edition plus some secodary stuff, but I'm not a historian of philosphy, so I want comments before I send it in. Here's the gist of my draft, roughly: Du Châtelet thinks the desire to be happy is universal but almost nobody knows how to get there, and that the big mistake is thinking happiness comes from luck or from other people. Her fix is basically Stoic, the way I read it — you manage your passions instead of letting them manage you. But she also insists you can't be happy without strong passions, she calls them, if I remember right, the great machines or engines of happiness, so the trick isn't killing them, it's aiming them at things that will actually pay off. Her favorite example is the love of study, which she rates above romantic love and ambition because it's the one passion that doesn't depend on anybody else's behavior and doesn't wear out. She also has this bit about needing some illusions to be happy, and that people who see everything too clearly end up miserable, which I find fascinating and a little contradictory. My essay frames her as someone who basically invented the modern idea that happiness is a project you work on, and I close by comparing her to a character in my novel who decides at 45 to become a mathematician.

What I want: tell me where I've got her wrong, where I'm flattening something interesting, and which of my claims are actually contested by people who study her. Tell me if the illusion thing is as central as I think it is, and whether the Stoic label is fair or just lazy shorthand. And if there's an obvious objection to my "invented modern self-help" line that I should head off before someone in the anthology reviews it, flag that too. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion.
