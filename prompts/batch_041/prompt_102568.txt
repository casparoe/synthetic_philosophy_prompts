At the hospital where I work, two things happen that I can't square. In the operating room, once a patient is under general anesthesia, everyone proceeds as though no one is home — the body is opened on the understanding that no experience is occurring, so the surgery is not in itself a harm. In the intensive care unit, a patient who barely responds to anything is treated as someone whose interests matter enormously, and great efforts are made to keep them alive. Both judgments seem to rest on the same thin evidence — outward responsiveness, a brain monitor — and in neither case can anyone observe whether there is anyone there; occasionally a patient later reports having been aware throughout. I would like to understand what view of consciousness and of moral standing this pair of practices presupposes. Does it require that experience be an all-or-nothing switch, or can it survive a picture on which sentience comes in degrees? Does it commit us to experience being a particular kind of integrated computation rather than mere responsiveness, and does it quietly rule out the view that any information-processing system with the right organization matters morally? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, separate the parts the practices strictly require from those they merely happen to fit, say where they are genuinely underdetermined so that several accounts of mind would license them, and tell me which rival views about the boundaries of sentience would force the hospital to change what it does.
