I run a monthly "big questions" night at a bar near the office — about 12 people, all engineers or friends of engineers, none of us have any real philosophy background, 90 minutes, and it has to stay fun or nobody comes back. I want an activity that makes people actually feel the weirdness of caring less about the future just because it's the future. The way I want it to land: everyone walks in thinking "obviously I'd rather have $100 today than $130 in five years, that's just common sense," and walks out unable to say why, once we've stripped away all the excuses. The excuses are the whole point — the usual reasons to prefer now (you might die first, the money might not show up, you could invest it, you'll be richer later anyway) all have to be engineered out by the rules so the only thing left varying is sheer delay. My rough idea is a bidding game where you spend points to buy rewards that pay out after different waits, and the game guarantees payment and pays no interest, so delay is the only variable — but I worry it's too fiddly and that people will just hoard. Design it properly for me: rules, what I print or hand out, how the waits work inside 90 minutes, how we read each person's implied discount rate off their choices without anyone needing a spreadsheet, and a second condition where I vary *space* instead of *time* — same wait, but the payoff goes to someone at a different physical distance — so we can see whether anyone discounts distance the same way they discount delay (I bet they don't, and I think that contrast is the interesting bit). Then the debrief: how to get from "Dave prefers 100 now to 130 in five years" to "so the year 2200 is worth basically nothing to Dave," where each standard defense of discounting (uncertainty, opportunity cost, the future being richer) fits or doesn't, and which ones the game has actually ruled out. Also tell me what will definately go wrong the first time — I guarantee someone derails it with "but I could die tomorrow" — and be honest about what the game can't show us and that I'll have to just say out loud. Keep it runnable by one person with index cards, a timer, and alot of nerve.
