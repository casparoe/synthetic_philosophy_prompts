I'm a retired chemist — thirty-five years in a lab, mostly protein work — and since I hung up the pipette I've been reading philosophy, some of it badly I'm sure. Dewey has me hooked, particulary what he says about inquiry being a kind of doing rather than a mirror we hold up to nature, and his idea that democracy isn't just a way of voting but a way of living together, a method for working out problems jointly. I want to test that against an episode I actually lived through the tail end of: the Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA in the mid-seventies, and the whole business around it — the voluntary moratorium the biologists put on themselves, the guidelines that came out of it, the sense in the community that we were governing ourselves before anyone else could.

So here's what I want. Put on the hat of someone who knows Dewey's theory of inquiry and his political writing in real detail — not the slogans, the actual machinery — and walk me through Asilomar as a Deweyan experiment, then tell me honestly where the experiment fails to be one. Do the mapping: who counts as the public in his sense, the people affected by indirect consequences if I've got that right; whether the scientists were inquirers or a special interest; whether the moratorium was genuine inquiry or a clever way of pre-empting one; what his picture says should have happened instead. Then run it the other way — what does Asilomar show about the theory? Dewey seems to think that inquiry, done properly, tends to become democratic on its own, and I want to know if this case is a counterexample. Say so plainly if it is.

Before you dive in, brainstorm a little: list the Deweyan notions that might apply, note which of them actually earn their keep here and which are just decoration, and flag anywhere you're unsure of the history and are leaning on the rough outline I gave you. And don't hand me a survey of positions — take a side. I'd much rather you tell me his experimentalism comes out of this looking naive than give me a tidy balanced list of considerations. If you think I've got Dewey wrong, tell me that too.
