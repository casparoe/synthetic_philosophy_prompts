I am preparing an application for a six-month postdoctoral writing fellowship and need a two-page research proposal on the reference class problem in probability and inductive inference. I was discussing the problem with a friend who works in statistics, and we kept returning to a dissatisfaction I have not been able to make precise. The usual framing treats the problem as one of selecting, from among the many classes to which a particular event belongs, the class that is objectively or epistemically privileged for assigning a probability. My half-formed idea is that this gets the order of explanation backwards. What does the work in a direct inference is not the reference class itself but a prior judgment of exchangeability among the cases in that class; the reference class is a bookkeeping device for that judgment, not its ground. If that is right, then many familiar puzzle cases are not cases of underdetermination in the world but of unacknowledged variation in what we are treating as exchangeable. I want to develop a distinction between what I am calling the descriptive reference class -- the set of actual or possible cases sharing the relevant features -- and the normative reference class -- the set of cases the agent is licensed to treat as exchangeable for the purpose at hand -- and then use that distinction to re-describe the problem.

Please turn this into a proposal. It should state the central question so that it has a determinate answer, explain why the existing treatments leave it open rather than merely unresolved, and say what would count as a result: what a successful defense of the distinction would establish, what would show that it collapses back into the standard view, and what formal or conceptual work the project needs. I would also like a realistic month-by-month plan for six months, including what the deliverable would be at the end -- a paper draft, a taxonomy, a decision procedure, or something else -- and an honest assessment of what would make the project too large or too speculative for the time. The proposal should not include citations or a literature review organized by author; I will add references myself, and I would rather it argue from the shape of the problem than lean on particular texts. If it helps, you may use one or two concrete illustrations, such as assigning a probability to a particular coin toss, to a particular defendant's guilt, or to a single future event, but keep the focus on the conceptual distinction rather than on solving any one puzzle case.
