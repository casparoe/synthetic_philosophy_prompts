So I'm a third-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I keep bumping into something that's starting to feel like a genuine contradiction in how my field actually works, and I'd like help thinking it through. In every seminar and every methods course we're taught to be allergic to what everyone just calls whig history — telling the story of a science as a long climb toward whatever we happen to believe now, sorting past figures into heroes who "anticipated" us and fools who held things back, treating the present as the obvious standard of judgment. My advisor will cut you off mid-sentence if you say someone "discovered" something before the concept existed, or call an old theory "wrong" as though truth were the finish line. Fine, I get it, I even agree. But then I look at the actual machinery of the discipline — the intro textbook everyone assigns, the qualifying-exam reading lists ordered so the field appears to converge on the current consensus, the literature review that narrates a problem as a sequence of partial solutions finally completed by the paper it's introducing, the memorial pieces that read a dead scholar's early work as foreshadowing their mature views — and all of it is doing exactly the thing we're supposed to have banned. It's not just laziness; the textbook authors are good historians who'd deny the charge to your face in a seminar. (My roommate's in econ and says her field is worse, which somehow didn't comfort me.)

What I want isn't a defense or a denunciation of whig history, it's a reconstruction of what the practice presupposes. So take a couple of these mundane institutional habits — the textbook arc, the ordered reading list, the problem-and-its-partial-solutions review, the anticipatory memorial — and tell me what view of rationality, progress, and justification they only make sense under. Do they require believing that later views are better justified, or only that they're more powerful, or better funded, or just more numerous, or something weaker still — that a story simply needs a direction to be tellable at all? Do they presuppose a cumulative picture of knowledge, or a selectionist one where the survivors aren't truer, just still standing? Is there some theory that licenses the textbook arc while still honoring the seminar's allergy, or does the allergy actually commit us to writing history with no arrow whatsoever? And please seperate what these practices strictly need from what they merely fit comfortably with, because my suspicion is they're compatible with more positions than either camp admits.

Also, help me figure out whether this is even a real tension. Maybe the seminar norm and the institutional habit are just aimed at different things — the seminar at original research, the textbook at pedagogy — in which case I'm making a fuss over nothing and should go back to my chapter. But if it is real, I'd like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined and where it's quietly ruling something out, and whether the honest answer is that the whole field is kidding itself. Say so plainly if that's it.
