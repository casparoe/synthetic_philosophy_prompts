I want your credence in a specific thesis about the ethics of memory, together with a decomposition that exposes its weak points. The thesis is this: there is a non-instrumental moral duty, owed by a political community, to maintain public remembrance of a historical atrocity, and that duty can persist after all survivors and perpetrators have died and even when continued remembrance produces no measurable reduction in future atrocities or repair of past wrongs. By non-instrumental I mean that the duty’s ground is not that remembrance prevents future atrocities, repairs past wrongs, or sustains a valuable group identity, but that remembrance is itself owed to the dead or to the past. I am not asking whether remembrance is often good or useful; I am asking whether it is ever owed in its own right, across generations.

To make this concrete, imagine a post-conflict society considering what I will call the Mnemonic Sunset. Under the proposal, the state would maintain official archives and scholarly access indefinitely, but after seventy-five years it would end public commemorations, remove atrocity memorials from civic space, and cease teaching the atrocity as a constitutive part of national identity. The atrocity would remain historically knowable but no longer ritually present. A minority objects that this is a form of second death for the victims and a betrayal of the dead. The majority replies that inherited guilt and mandatory grief are themselves unjust burdens on people who did nothing wrong, and that memory should be carried by those who have a living stake in it. The question is whether the minority is right about a duty, not merely whether its preferred policy would be better on balance.

Please give me your credence that the thesis is true. Decompose it at least into the following sub-questions, adding any others you think belong: (a) whether the dead can be wronged after death, or whether duties to remember are really duties to the living; (b) whether public remembrance is a form of respect that has non-instrumental value, or only a tool for prevention, repair, or identity; (c) whether collective memory is a public good that can be owed to a community as such, rather than a private good of individuals; (d) whether the passage of generations weakens or extinguishes the duty, and if so, on what principle; (e) whether the costs of mandated remembrance to later generations can override the duty; and (f) whether forgetting is the morally default option, with remembering requiring justification, or the reverse. For each sub-question, give a rough probability, one line of justification, and say what the probability is a probability of. Then show how they combine into your final number, state which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and identify what argument or piece of evidence would move it most.

My own credence in the thesis is about 0.3. A colleague who works on transitional justice puts it at about 0.7. After giving your number and decomposition, tell me which sub-question most likely accounts for the difference between us, and whether the disagreement is best understood as empirical, conceptual, or moral.
