I teach 10th-grade world history and I've just spent two weekends marking the plague-and-famine essays, and somewhere around essay forty I realized I'm applying no consistent rule at all about when a kid is allowed to blame a germ, a volcano, or the weather for something. My department is also rewriting this unit right now and I'm the one who has to stand up and defend whatever goes in the guide, so it's not just my red pen. Here's what I actually marked, and then the ask.

Stuff I let stand, usually with a tick:

- Black Death killed a third of Europe, and that's a big part of why serfdom loosened in the west. Tick.
- Smallpox did more conquering in the Americas than any army ever did. Tick, and I told the kid it was the best sentence in the pile.
- The 1815 eruption gave us the year without a summer, and that probably nudged a lot of New England farm families to give up and go west. Tick. (My brother-in-law is a geologist and he was insufferably pleased about this one.)
- Drought was a major cause of the Maya collapse, though I made the kid add that drought is never the whole story, which is a cop-out and I know it.
- Hurricane Katrina didn't destroy New Orleans, the levees did. Tick, and I underlined it.

Stuff I marked down, and where the kid pushed back and I didn't have an answer:

- The potato blight caused the Irish famine. No. I wrote in the margin that the blight was a blight and the famine was made by people, which the kid quite reasonably said was me being squeamish about a plain fact.
- The drought in Syria caused the civil war. No, and I felt strongly about it, but when he said then why did you tick the volcano one I changed the subject.
- The big geography-explains-everything essay (you know the one, the enormous book about why Europe won). Marked it down and couldn't tell her why, given I'd just praised the smallpox sentence, because both of them are saying nature decided it and I apparently only hate the second one.

My half-theory, which I don't think survives contact: I think you can use nature to explain why things got worse for everybody — that's just microbes and physics doing their thing — but you can't use it to explain why one group ended up on top of another, because that's when you're letting somebody off the hook. Except that doesn't cover Ireland at all (nobody came out on top, everyone just got worse, and I still say no) and it doesn't cover Syria either. My colleague says it's really about whether anybody at the time had a real alternative, which sounds right until I try it on the Maya, where I have no idea who was supposed to have done otherwise.

So: is there a principle here, or a small set of them? State it precisely enough that I could put it at the top of a marking rubric, and tell me which clause is doing the work in which case. Then tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up — if it's the smallpox tick or the Maya tick, say so straight, don't soften it. Give me two or three cases I haven't marked and tell me how the principle rules on them. And test my half-theory properly: repair it if it can be repaired, otherwise tell me to bin it.

One thing I keep circling: does it matter whether a natural cause is being used to explain *when* something happened rather than *who* it happened to? Because I suspect that's what I'm actually doing when I'm being consistent, and I also suspect it's why the Ireland essay makes me angry — the blight explains the timing, and something else entirely explains who starved. And if the real answer turns out to be that my rule isn't about causes at all but about what the sentence is doing (absolving someone or not), then say that too, and tell me what it makes of the ticks — am I grading essays on truth or on intentions?

Don't send me a reading list, I've got ninety essays left and I won't read it.
