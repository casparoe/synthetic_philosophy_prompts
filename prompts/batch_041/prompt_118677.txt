I am a novelist, and I have spent the last two years on a book whose central character is offered a piece of information about her own future and declines it — a refusal I have tried to render neither as cowardice nor as heroism but as a piece of reasoning, and one I believe can be defended without any appeal to weakness of will. To my surprise the book has attracted the attention of people who do this for a living, and in a fortnight I am to give a short paper at a small workshop on the value of information, convened around the question of whether knowing more can ever be worse for you. My paper argues the strong claim, and I mean to defend it rather than hedge: that there is no such thing as information that is bad for an agent in itself. I will rest the argument on Good's theorem — the result that an expected-utility maximizer offered free information before she must choose can expect to do at least as well by taking it as by refusing it, because the value of a decision problem is a convex function of one's credences — and I will draw the moral that the wish not to know, whatever its dramatic power, must always be either a failure of rationality or else a claim about something other than utility: about dignity, or self-image, or the price of the information, or the pleasure of suspense.

The room is small but not friendly, and it is the composition of it that I need you to take seriously. Three of the participants are decision theorists who know Good's proof the way I know the first sentence of my own novel, and who will be unmoved by my restating it; one of them is, I am told, carrying a folder of alleged counterexamples and regards the theorem's conclusion as straightforwardly false. There is a microeconomist who will say that what I have offered is Jensen's inequality wearing a hat, and that the entire question is the cost of information, which my formulation assumes away. There is an epistemologist who works on pragmatic encroachment, does not think the value of a belief is a function of expected utility at all, and will want to know what my theorem is supposed to prove about the person who would rather not be told. And there is a philosopher of cognitive science who thinks about bounded rationality and the problem of logical omniscience, and who will ask what becomes of my claim for an agent who cannot compute the posteriors the theorem presupposes. There is also, in the back, a friend of mine from the literary world who will ask, in effect, whether any of this touches the thing I actually wrote about; I would like an answer for her too, though I do not want her treated as the important case.

Draft the question-and-answer prep sheet I will take into that room. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get — not the ones a general audience of philosophers would produce, and nothing about whether it is permissible to withhold information from other people, which nobody in this room cares about — ordered by how much damage each would do to my position rather than by the order in which they will be raised, and phrased in the vocabulary of the person who would raise it rather than in mine, so that I recognise it as it arrives. For each, give me a reply I could deliver in two minutes at a lectern and a candid note on where that reply is thin, the place where I am counting on nobody pressing. Tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks, since I would rather give away the easy objections myself than be caught out on them, and tell me plainly which of my claims is doing more work than it can bear. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks — the one that would end the discussion rather than prolong it — and say who in this room is likeliest to find it. Assume I can hold the mathematics in my head, but do not make me write any of it down.
