A behavioral-econ blog I follow summed up the Allais paradox like this: "Expected utility theory says a rational agent maximizes expected value. Allais showed real people dont: offered a guaranteed $1M versus a lottery paying $5M with 10% probability and $1M with 89% probability -- a higher expected payoff -- most people take the guaranteed $1M. But strip the shared 89% shot at $1M out of both options and you're left choosing between an 11% chance at $1M and a 10% chance at $5M, and most people switch and take the gamble. Thats the Allais paradox. It shows the independence axiom -- which is really just the sure-thing principle under a fancier name -- is descriptively false, and since the only thing driving the violation is ordinary risk-aversion, the paradox refutes expected utility as both a descriptive and a normative theory of choice."
