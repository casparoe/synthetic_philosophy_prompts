Ok so I need help writing a speech and I want you to really think it through before you write. Take on the role of someone who is both a decent philosopher of mind and a good speechwriter — you've done alot of these — and please start by brainstorming 3 or 4 possible angles, say which one you'd pick and why, and only then draft the thing.

The occasion: it's the retirement dinner for Prof. Dana Rothwell, who spent about forty years working on insect cognition and what it means for free will. She's the one who kept pushing the sphex wasp into every argument she ever had. You know the case — the wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to inspect, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's in there, she comes out, finds it moved, drags it back, and then inspects the burrow all over again, forever, because the whole routine just resets. Hofstadter's word for that kind of locked-in behavior was sphexishness, and Dennett leaned on the wasp for years to argue that a lot of what looks like thought is really just a fixed program. There's also a dissenting line, I think it was Keijzer, who pointed out that repeating the inspection when your nest keeps getting disturbed might just be adaptive, and that the example survives mostly because it's rhetorically useful, not because the biology holds up.

Now, Dana's own late-career move was to say everyone's been asking the wrong question. The wasp is the least interesting character in that experiment, she'd say. The interesting one is the scientist standing there with the tweezers, who is *also* running a fixed loop and who resets the wasp in exactly the same way every time. Her line was that the experiment is a duet between two machines and only one of them gets to call the other one rigid. She called it the tweezers problem. She also refused to let the Keijzer objection off the hook — she thought calling a behavior adaptive is just what we do when we happen to like the rigidity.

The audience is mixed and that's the hard part. About half are philosophers and cognitive scientists who know this literature cold and will catch every reference. The other half are her family and a bunch of her old field-biology students who actually care about real wasps and have never read a word of Dennett. The speech has to land for both without turning into a lecture, and it should run maybe six minutes spoken.

Here's the constraint that matters: the idea has to do real structural work, not be decoration. So you can't just praise her for being flexible and adaptable in the ordinary toast way, because that would contradict her whole view — she'd say flexibility-talk is exactly the trap. And the ending should turn the idea back on the occasion itself, because a retirement toast is the most sphexish ritual any of us perform, and she would absolutely notice that the person giving it is stuck in a loop too. Find a way to make that warm rather than bleak, and find one moment that works for the cousins in the back who just want to hear that they loved her. No need to quote anyone or cite anything — just write it like you'd say it out loud.
