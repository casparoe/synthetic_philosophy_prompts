I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and somewhere along the way I talked myself into moral particularism: I now think a feature that is a reason to act one way here can be a reason to act the opposite way there, and that no finite set of principles gets the verdicts right. The trouble is that I moderate a large online community, and I still have to act. Ten or so times a week I decide whether to warn, remove, or ban, usually with a few minutes' notice and sometimes with a few hundred users watching; a co-moderator reviews bans after the fact, and there is no one above me to escalate to.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a decision, and where it has to hand the call back to me because the question is one of values rather than facts. The hard part, and the reason I am asking you rather than just drafting a rules document, is that the procedure has to be a particularist's procedure. It cannot quietly assume that the listed considerations always cut in the same direction. So I need to know how a checklist stays honest to the idea that the same feature counts differently in different cases — whether that means treating every rule as defeasible, or something else — and how the procedure marks the points at which a rule is likely to be overridden rather than applied.

Then run it on three cases: a user whose comments are each technically true and individually defensible but who reliably leaves a thread worse; conduct that was fine last month in a different thread and is not fine now; and one case where I expect the procedure to return a verdict I will dislike, since I would rather discover that now than in the moment.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. If particularism is right, any procedure is a lossy compression of a judgment it cannot reproduce, so say where this one will misfire and whether I should stop pretending it is more than a crutch.
