I'm a doctoral student in public administration, working on cases in which officials must choose between breaking a rule and letting something bad happen, and I keep running into a pattern I can't place. An official authorizes what she is not permitted to authorize — she violates a statute she swore to uphold, or misleads the body she answers to — because the only alternative is a disaster she can foresee and prevent. Afterward, almost everyone holds two judgments at once: that what she did was wrong, and that she had to do it. No one thanks her; the colleagues who urged her to act would be scandalized if she claimed she had acted rightly; and she is expected to say in public that she was wrong, in a way that is not supposed to count as an argument for having done otherwise. Neither judgment gives way — no one treats the wrongness as merely a cost to be weighed, and no one treats the necessity as an excuse.

Two complications. In most of my cases the decision belongs to a body rather than a person: nine members vote, each knowing the outcome does not depend on her vote, yet afterward all of them behave as though their own hands had done it. And I have a case in which an official does nothing wrong at all — she follows the rule — the disaster happens, and her colleagues treat her as the guilty party, not for the deaths but for having kept her hands clean at others' expense.

I assume there is a heading for this somewhere in moral and political philosophy, but I cannot work out which. Is there an established name for the first pattern, and do either of these complications belong to it? I am after the boundaries more than the label: whether it requires an actual rule violation rather than a cost imposed, whether it requires an act rather than an omission, whether the demand for public remorse belongs to the phenomenon or is a separate social ritual, and whether it survives an official who feels no guilt but is expected to. Please answer in American English.
