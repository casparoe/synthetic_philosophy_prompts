Next month the state historical commission dedicates a marker at the site of the Corwin Mills, and I have been asked to give the dedication remarks. The marker honors Margaret Ostrowski, who spent close to forty years transcribing county wills, deeds, and tax rolls and recording some three hundred interviews with former mill workers before the last of them died. I am a fourth-year PhD student in history; she is the reason I am one, and the eight hundred words I have to fill are the only remarks at the ceremony.

The audience is roughly a third descendants of the people she recorded, a third volunteers and staff of the county historical society, and a third commissioners and local officials, plus two academic historians I know by reputation to be unsentimental about local history.

What I want is a speech that treats the occasion as a problem rather than a formality. A bronze plaque with four lines of text is an act of official commemoration — the genre that history from below exists to distrust — and the woman we are honoring spent her life on precisely the people such markers leave out. I do not want that irony announced and then dropped as a wry aside; I want it to constrain the speech: to determine what I may claim about her, how I may use the handful of anecdotes I have, and whether I am entitled to speak for the mill workers at all. The microhistorians' wager — that a single deviant life can carry a structural claim about a whole world — is what I keep circling, because if it holds for her interviewees it holds for her, and I am not sure I want it to. Honoring an individual and turning her into a specimen of the amateur, the autodidact, the woman in the county archive are not the same act, and I would like the speech to keep them apart.

No citations, footnotes, attributions, or lists of works — nothing that asks the audience to look anything up, and nothing that depends on a title or a date I have not verified. Do not put words in anyone's mouth: no invented quotations from her or from the people she recorded, and where the speech needs a voice from the interviews, paraphrase it and mark it as my reconstruction. If a methodological idea is named, gloss it in the same clause so that the descendants and the commissioners are not lost; the two historians should be able to tell that the speech knows what it is doing without anyone being lectured. The tone is warm — this is a celebration, and the constraint should make the warmth harder to fake rather than replace it.

After the remarks, give me a JSON object with these fields:

- remarks: the full text of the speech.
- governing_tension: one sentence naming the tension that shaped it.
- deliberate_omissions: array of objects, each with claim and why_withheld — things the speech declines to say, and the reason.
- expert_markers: array of objects, each with reference and handhold — ideas named in the speech that only the historians would recognize, and the clause that keeps everyone else aboard.
- unresolved_irony: the single sentence in the remarks that leaves the occasion's irony standing instead of resolving it.
