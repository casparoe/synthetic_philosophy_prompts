(a) Set out the idea, and explain the reasoning by which a superrational player arrives at cooperation rather than defection.
(b) Does that reasoning show that defecting is irrational in the one-shot PD? Defend your answer.

And here's what the student handed in:

(a) In the one-shot PD both players have a dominant strategy: whatever the other player does, defecting pays better. So the standard analysis says both defect, and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated. Hofstadter's point is that this analysis treats the other player's choice as something given to you, like the weather. But the other player is doing exactly the same calculation you are, with the same information and the same incentives. So your choice and theirs are not independent: the same line of thought that leads you to defect leads them to defect, and the same line of thought that leads you to cooperate leads them to cooperate. The real options are therefore not 'cooperate vs defect' but 'we both cooperate vs we both defect', and the first is better for you. A superrational player therefore cooperates. This is really just Kant's categorical imperative in game-theoretic dress: act only on the maxim you could will to be universal.

(b) Yes, I think it does. The dominance argument assumes that the other player's action is fixed while yours varies, and that is exactly what the superrationality argument denies. Once you see that your opponent's choice is a function of the same reasoning that produces yours, defecting stops being 'getting one over' on them and becomes a way of making sure they defect as well. So defection is not rational in the one-shot PD; the dominance argument is not a proof that defection is rational, it is a proof that defection is rational on the assumption that your opponent's choice is independent of yours — which is the very assumption in dispute. On the correct picture cooperation is the rational choice, and the PD is not really a dilemma at all.

(c) The assumption doing the work is that the other player is rational. Both players are rational, and each knows the other is rational, so they will both reach the same conclusion from the same premises; that is why their choices line up. If the other player is not rational — if they're a machine that always defects, say, or someone who doesn't think about the problem at all — then the correlation is gone and superrationality has nothing to say: you should just defect and take your payoff. So the argument is really about what happens among rational agents who know each other to be rational, and it doesn't claim to apply outside that class.
