I drafted the following argument for a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether it is a proof or a trap. Each premise looks defensible to me on its own; the conclusion is one I am unwilling to accept. I want you to tell me which step to reject, why that step looked acceptable in the first place, and what rejecting it costs.

The setting is the separateness objection to utilitarianism — the charge, usually associated with Rawls and Nozick, that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons, because it allows a loss to one person to be justified by a gain to another, as though separate lives were merely stages in the life of a single being. The objection is ordinarily advanced as a constraint on what may justify what. I have tried instead to derive it from a premise about what compensation is, and I suspect the derivation is where something has gone wrong.

The argument:

(1) A loss imposed on someone can be justified by a benefit only if the loss and the benefit belong to a single good — that is, only if there is a subject for whom the loss registers as a cost and the benefit as a gain, so that the one can be set against the other. A compensation received by no one is not a compensation; it is a substitution of one person's good for another's.

(2) Where a loss is imposed on A for the sake of a benefit to B, there is no such subject. A and B are distinct subjects of experience; the sum of their welfare is not experienced by anyone; the gain to B is nowhere registered as a gain to A.

(3) Therefore no benefit to B can justify a loss to A, and utilitarianism, which licenses precisely such justifications, is ruled out.

That is the objection. But the requirement in (1), if it is genuine, governs my relation to my own future as well.

(4) When I accept a painful treatment now in order to live twenty more years, (1) says that this is justified only if there is a subject for whom the present pain is a cost and the additional years a gain. It will not do to answer that the subject is me; the question is what that answer comes to.

(5) Facts about personal identity over time are not further facts over and above facts about psychological connectedness and continuity — memory, character, intention, the persistence of projects — and those connections hold in degrees. I am more closely connected to myself next year than to myself in thirty years, and more closely to my younger self than to a stranger; the differences lie along a continuum rather than marking a difference of kind. (This is the reductionist view of personal identity associated with Parfit.)

(6) If the subject who bears both the present pain and the future benefit is nothing over and above those graded connections, then the single good demanded by (1) exists to whatever degree they hold: nearly fully for next year, less for thirty years hence, and slightly for a stranger, with whom I share a species, a language, a history, and the capacity to reason together.

(7) So the compensation requirement is satisfied, to a degree, in the interpersonal case as well, and the difference between sacrificing my future self and sacrificing a stranger is a difference of degree.

(8) But that difference is not one of degree. Sacrificing a stranger is not a weaker version of the same wrong, permissible once the stakes are high enough or the stranger sufficiently like me; it is impermissible, and it does not become less impermissible as I learn more about the stranger. So the requirement in (1) cannot be met in the intrapersonal case in the merely graded way that (6) allows.

(9) Therefore (1) is false.

If (9) holds, the separateness objection falls. But so does the picture of prudence that made the objection vivid, since the premise that grounds the objection also reduces my concern for my own future to a matter of degree. If I must choose, I would rather keep (3) than (5): I am not prepared to say that my relation to my future self differs only quantitatively from my relation to a stranger. What I cannot see is how to keep (3) without (1), and (1) strikes me as close to a truism about what the word compensation means.

So: which step should go, why did it look fine, and what does dropping it cost? If the answer is that (1) equivocates, say precisely where, and say what a defender of the objection must put in its place — and whether what replaces it can do the objection's work without the metaphysical picture I have now given up.
