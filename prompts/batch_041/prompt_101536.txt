Right, so I'm doing a PhD wedged between philosophy of mind and media law, and there's this argument that keeps surfacing in my reading group and I still can't get a straight answer on it. My mate Priya — she's the pragmatic one — always says that being in a virtual world is just like reading a novel. You get sucked in, you care what happens, you might genuinely cry when a character dies, but at the end of the day none of its real: no one's been harmed, nothing's been destroyed, it's all fiction. She wheels this out whenever anyone (usually me) starts banging on about virtual property or 'assault' in VR, and it's annoyingly effective because it makes the whole topic sound like a category error.

What I want from you is a proper audit of the analogy, not a yes/no. So: (1) the correspondence table — what plays the role of the author, the text, the props, the reader, the fictional world, and the events inside it; (2) where the mapping breaks, and crucially which of those breaks actually matter for what she's using the analogy to prove versus which are just trivia; (3) what the novel picture quietly imports that the virtual case lacks, and vice versa — I've a hunch there's something about who gets to fix the facts of the story, and something about the other 'readers' in a shared world being real agents who can act on you and on your stuff, and something about a deleted world carrying real labour and money and relationships away with it. I also suspect there's a distinction lurking between being harmed *in* the fiction and being harmed *by* it — press on that one, it's the bit I can never pin down. (4) Does her conclusion — nothing in there is a real object or a real harm — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? And (5) if there's a better analogy for the thing she's actually getting at, tell me what it is.

To make it concrete: last month a bloke in one of the worlds I play in smashed up a sculpture a friend had spent about two hundred hours building, and she was gutted in a way that really didn't feel like crying at a novel. Does that case change anything, or is Priya still right that it's all just make-believe?
