I'm the rapporteur for a small two-day workshop my department ran on the concept of disease, and I've been asked to turn it into a document both keynote speakers can co-sign — they've agreed to what I keep calling an adversarial collaboration, which is generous of them given how badly they talk past each other. The first is a naturalist: she holds that a condition is a disease when it deviates from species-typical functional design, and she is emphatic that "typical" is a design notion rather than a prevalence notion — a condition can be universal within a population and still be a disease — and that the reference class in which typicality gets assessed (species, sex, age band, environment) is fixed by biological grain, not by anyone's values; she also runs a two-tier line on which a state can be a disease without being an illness, because illness adds a further judgment about whether the state warrants attention. The second is a normativist: she grants that clinicians discover facts, but insists a fact becomes a diagnosis only once someone judges the state bad enough, for this person, to carry the label — she thinks that threshold, not biology, does the classificatory work, that the physiology is objective while the classification is interest-laden, and that none of this makes medicine arbitrary. They have spent years arguing, mostly at cross purposes, and I'd like the statement to be one each of them could actually put her name to.

What I need is that document. Begin with a numbered list of claims both can sign: about the physiology and what is not in dispute there; about what the word "disease" is and isn't being asked to do; about threshold cases and medicalization (prediabetes, mild hypertension, the guideline change that reclassified a large share of adults as hypertensive more or less overnight); about whether their question is conceptual or empirical; and about what would count as evidence for either side. Then give me a table of the residual disagreements, one row per issue, with columns for the issue, the naturalist's formulation of her own position, the normativist's formulation of hers, the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them — a case or argument both would accept as bearing on that crux, and each side's prediction about it, recorded in advance. I have two candidate cruxes and I want you either to use them or to replace them with better ones and say why: first, whether a reference class can be fixed without a value judgment anywhere in the process (the naturalist says yes, and compares choosing a grain of analysis to choosing a level of description rather than to choosing a good); second, whether harm is constitutive of disease or a separate practical add-on, which is where the two-tier disease/illness move lives. On the cases: I take it that conditions which are statistically atypical but harmless — reversed organ placement in an otherwise healthy person — discriminate between them, while conditions that are damaging but near-universal in their reference class, like atherosclerosis in the elderly, may not discriminate at all, since they both call those diseases; include the second only if it is doing real work. And if part of their quarrel is merely verbal, say which part and which word is doing the equivocating — I have a suspicion that "natural" is being asked to mean both non-evaluative and biologically proper in the same breath, but I may be wrong about that.

I don't need citations, a reading list, or references; neither of them wants the statement cluttered that way, and formulations will serve better than footnotes. Please commit rather than survey: pick the two or three cruxes that are genuinely load-bearing instead of listing every conceivable point of contention, take a definite view about whether any of the disagreement is verbal, and where you think a proposed formulation would fail to earn one side's signature, say so plainly and propose the fix instead of smoothing it over.
