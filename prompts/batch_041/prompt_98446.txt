I'm the TA for a cross-listed course on behavior genetics and its critics — half the students come from the biology side, half from philosophy and sociology — and I've been asked to run a recitation section whose only job is to surface what students actually believe when they say a trait "is heritable." The pattern that has been driving me up the wall is that everyone can recite the standard caveats and then, five minutes later, use the word in a way that flatly contradicts the caveat they just recited. One student hears a high heritability estimate and concludes the trait is genetically caused in each individual who has it; another hears the same number and concludes that changing the environment won't help much; a third reads "heritable" as "inherited," i.e. passed down from parents to children; a fourth insists it is nothing but a statement about variance in one particular population, across one particular range of environments, and therefore tells you nothing about any individual and nothing about what would happen if you intervened. I don't think these are four sloppy versions of a single view — I think they are four genuinely different positions — and I want an instrument that sorts students into them by what they commit to, not by which words they happen to reach for. So please build me a decision tree for that.

Each branch node should be a concrete question or mini-case that a student can answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to; no node may simply ask something like "do you think heritability is about populations or about individuals?", because that hands over the answer. Build the tree out of the logical space first — the questions that carve it up — and only afterward locate the positions within it, naming each leaf where a recognizable position exists and marking it "unnamed" where the combination is coherent but nobody seems to occupy it. The distinctions I care about are at least these: whether the estimate is a fact about a population or about the individuals in it; whether it concerns the causes of variation or the causes of the trait itself; whether it is relative to the environments that happened to be sampled; whether it says anything about transmission across generations; and whether it licenses any claim about the tractability of intervention. Where two leaves look distinct to me but your tree can't actually separate them, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction, and for any combination of answers that no one occupies, tell me briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Keep it to roughly a dozen leaves at most.

I'd like the whole thing written as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no numbered lists — the way you'd explain it to me in an email. Walk me through the tree from the root downward, then the leaves and the positions sitting at them, then close with an honest note on which pairs of positions the tree fails to separate and where its edges are.
