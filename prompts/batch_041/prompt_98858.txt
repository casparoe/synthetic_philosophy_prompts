Me and my mate Dan have been arguing about this for weeks and I've got nowere with it. He reckons he can prove that none of us ever knows what we're feeling or thinking, and I can't find the bad step. Here it is as he sets it out:

(1) To know I'm in a given mental state, I have to be able to tell it apart from the states right next to it. If something a hairs-breadth different would feel exactly the same to me, then for all I know I'm in that one instead.
(2) My powers of discrimination are finite, and the states run in a continuum — think of a headache fading away, there's no last moment you can point to as the one where it stops. So for any state I'm in, there's a neighbouring state that feels just the same to me.
(3) So whenever I judge that I'm in state S, it could be that I'm really in the neighbouring state S' instead.
(4) If it could be that I'm in S', then I don't know that I'm in S.
(5) So I never know what mental state I'm in.
(6) And everything I believe about the world rests on how things seem to me, so the same goes for all of it.
(7) So nobody ever knows anything — and the KK principle, that if you know something you can know that you know it, isn't just false, it's vacuous, because there's no first-order knowledge for it to be about.

The conclusion is obviously bonkers: I know I'm a bit hungry right now, and Dan knows he's annoyed at me for pressing him on it. But every step looks alright to me on its own. Which premise or move should he give up, and what does giving it up cost him?
