Two of my closest friends, Adeel and Marion, spent most of a dinner party arguing over a scenario I had described to them, and each of them wants me to explain to the other why she or he is wrong. I have no stake in the question and no view of my own, and I would rather have someone who was not at the table sort out who was right about what.

The scenario I gave them was this. Nadia has lived in Homs for eleven years. Somewhere in the world is a figure known as the Assessor, who has, over twenty years, been in the city where each of forty people ended up on the day they were ruined: never once in the wrong city, never once late. He does not chase anyone. He is simply already there when the person arrives, and no one has ever seen him travel. He is in one of two cities tonight and will not move until Nadia has moved: Homs, where she is, or Latakia, three days away and at the cost of nearly everything she has saved. If she ends the day in his city she is ruined; if she ends it in the other city she is safe. She must choose tonight. Two further facts about the record: none of the forty knew they were being hunted, though in several cases their families knew and warned them in vain.

Adeel and Marion went back and forth as follows.

(1) Adeel: The scenario cannot be true as stated, so there is nothing to decide. If the Assessor never moves after the fact, then where he is tonight was settled before Nadia chose; but then her choice cannot be what makes his city the right one, and a perfect record of forty correct guesses about choices not yet made is either a coincidence or a fraud. You cannot have a genuinely open choice and an already-settled fact about what that choice will be.

(2) Marion: There is nothing incoherent in knowing what someone will freely do. If a friend guesses what I will order before I decide, she is guessing; a mechanism that read the future would not be guessing, and my freedom would be untouched by its accuracy. And if the Assessor really knows, then Nadia's choice is already inside the world he knows, so she should stop looking for a way out from under it. Whichever city she picks, he is there.

(3) Adeel: Take the knowledge as given, then. He does not move tonight, so his being in Homs or in Latakia is fixed independently of anything Nadia does tonight. Her two options therefore come to the same thing, ruined either way, and the only difference between them is the price. Fleeing costs her nearly everything and buys her nothing, so the rational choice is to stay. The thought that he will be where she is is theater: a correlation, not a consequence.

(4) Marion: You are treating as independent the one thing the record says is connected. The only reason to think he will be where she is, is that his city is somehow keyed to her choice; that is what forty for forty means. You cannot use the record to prove her doomed and then deny that her choice and his location are linked. And once they are linked, her own mind is evidence. If she notices herself strongly drawn to fleeing, that is a sign that fleeing is what she will do, hence a sign that he is in Latakia, hence a reason not to flee. Introspection is telling her where he is, and no travel cost compares with that.

(5) Adeel: Then take her mind out of the loop. She commits to a coin: heads she stays, tails she flees, and she decides not to decide. If his accuracy comes from knowing her, a coin is opaque to him, and she survives half the time, which beats none. And if he can predict the coin, then nothing hung on her deliberating in the first place, and she should simply stay and keep her savings.

(6) Marion: If he can predict a coin, he is not reading her at all; he is reading the future, and then the record is only the record of a man who is never wrong. But notice what the record is a record of: forty people who did not know they were being hunted, warned by families who could not save them. A perfect score against people who never tried to escape tells us nothing about what happens when someone knows the game and refuses to play it. For all the record shows, he is a man who sits in one city and arranges for people to come to it, and then the whole story about predicting her choice is a story she has been told, in which case the move is to go wherever the story does not point.

(7) Adeel: Then you have traded a predictor for a plot, and a plot has to explain how a con man ruins forty people whose families were shouting warnings at them. You have had him reading the future, reading her mind, and planting rumors, whichever suited the point you were making. Pick one.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the seven numbered moves, give me a rating from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification. Then say who is right about what, and whether the scenario as I have described it settles what Nadia should do: if it does, what she should do, and if it does not, exactly what would have to be added to settle it. Finally, say what both of them are missing and which of the two, if either, is closer to right overall. I do not want a general essay on foreknowledge and free will; I want these seven moves judged as they were made, with a verdict on each. The shape of the scenario will be familiar, but the details I have given are the ones that matter. If the honest answer is that the case is underdescribed, I would rather have that said plainly, along with the specific detail that would break the tie.
