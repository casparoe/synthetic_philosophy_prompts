I am a third-year student in a decision theory and rationality seminar, and our final assignment is a piece of short fiction. The brief is to dramatize a problem from our unit on updateless decision theory without ever stating the theory outright — to make a reader feel the problem rather than be told about it. I have a scenario in mind and would like you to write the story around it, rather than around any of the standard examples we covered in class.

The idea the story needs to carry is this. There are two ways to think about a rational choice. On one, you decide what to do only once you are already in a situation and have taken in the evidence available to you there. On the other, what you really choose is a policy — a rule that specifies what you would do in every situation you might have been in — and you assess that rule from a standpoint prior to knowing which situation you occupy, weighting the outcomes across all of them by how likely each was. These two can come apart: a rule that looks foolish from inside the situation you actually end up in can be the one worth having committed to beforehand.

Here is the scenario I want used. A woman, Mira, is required once a year to file a single standing rule governing how she will respond to a certain kind of costly request. The year then unfolds along one of several branches, and at filing time she does not know which one she is in. In the branch she will actually live through, the request may or may not arrive; if it arrives, honoring the rule costs her a great deal. A separate office, over which she has no influence once the rule is filed, assesses the rule itself rather than her conduct, and pays out a large sum in whichever branch is selected by a mechanism she cannot observe — and the mechanism rewards rules that would have sacrificed in the branch where the request arrives, while delivering the reward only in a branch where the request never comes. To collect, she must have filed, in advance, a rule that is costly in a life she will never lead.

What I most want the story to hold open is what happens if, in the branch where the request does arrive, Mira can still break her own filed rule at some further cost. Make the reader feel both pulls — that breaking it is plainly right, since the payout was settled long ago and cannot be affected now, and that breaking it betrays the very thing the office was measuring — and resist resolving which is correct. Please keep the story self-contained, avoid naming or citing any philosophical literature, and let the dilemma emerge from the situation rather than from narration.
