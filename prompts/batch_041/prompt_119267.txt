I run the ethics desk at a small church network — we fund and support partner congregations in countries run by strongmen — and every few months a field staffer forwards me a message from a local pastor asking whether it's ok for them to back a plan to get rid of the president. Sometimes it's nonviolent, sometimes not, and once in a while someone is preaching that killing him would be a righteous act. I don't have a canonist or a lawyer on call, and the pastor usually recieves the reply in a week, not a treatise. Aquinas is the framework everyone in this network nominally shares, so I want a procedure field staff can sit down and run with a pastor in one afternoon, without a theology degree.

So: turn Aquinas on tyrants and tyrannicide into a checklist. Questions in order, what a real answer to each looks like as opposed to a wish, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it has to hand off to a human because the question is about values or about facts nobody in the room can know. Two things I want kept, because they're the whole point and I think they're the parts people always drop: the distinction between a ruler who grabbed power illegitimately and one who holds power lawfully but uses it badly, and the test that says disturbing a bad government isn't itself wrong unless the cure leaves the people worse off than the disease did. There's also the point that the man who stirs up the multitude against the common good is the real troublemaker — I'd like that to show up as a question in the procedure, not as a slogan.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a general who took power in a coup eighteen months ago, no independent court, monarch, or parliament left to appeal to, food shortages, and a network of pastors asking whether to join the resistance; (b) a hereditary monarch, thirty years in, corrupt and brutal in a low-level way, country basically stable, and a group of junior officers is planning to kill him and wants our partner church's blessing; (c) a congregation that wants to fund a general strike and a boycott, no violence at all. Be honest about which of these the procedure actually answers and which it can't, and say so plainly if it tells me to do something my donors and my own staff will hate.

One more thing: a colleague of mine keeps saying this is just triage, you cut off the gangrenous limb to save the body. Tell me whether that's actually what Aquinas is doing or whether it smuggles in something he doesn't say. And flag where the procedure will misfire in a modern state that has no legitimate authority to appeal to at all.

Don't give me a reading list, I can't get the books where we work.
