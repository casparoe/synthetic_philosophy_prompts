You're a philosopher who works on personal identity and medical ethics — I need a teaching instrument, not an essay. I'm running a grad bioethics session on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, and I want a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually think rather than by which label they like. Think it through carefully, and start by brainstorming the logical space before you name any position.

Root the tree in the case that has to do the work: a woman who signed a directive before her diagnosis refusing life-sustaining treatment and instructing that she not be kept 'merely content'; she now has moderate dementia, is cheerful, and says she wants to live. Every internal node must be a question answerable from a concrete case, never 'do you accept precedent autonomy?'. I want at least these splits: whether the later person is the same person or merely the same subject of a life-story; whether the earlier self's say-so binds, or is only evidence about her interests, or is expressive only (it records who she was without deciding anything); whether her present contentment is a genuine change of mind or the illness talking; and whether her earlier preference can count as informed at all, given she'd never been in the state she was refusing.

Two branches the literature skimps on, so include them: (a) a directive containing a self-sealing clause — 'if I ever say I want to live, treat that as a symptom' — does your view make that clause binding, and if so is that a feature or an embarassment? (b) two conflicting directives, one written at 45 and one at 70 after the diagnosis — recency or informedness?

Leaves should include precedent-autonomy/critical-interests, the now-self experiential view, the new-person-no-authority view, narrative authorship, evidentiary-only, a capacity-graded sliding scale, and a two-track view treating refusals and requests differently (mark it 'unnamed' if nobody actually holds it). Note which combinations of answers nobody occupies and whether that's incoherence, unattractiveness, or just nobody having tried; flag any leaves the tree can't separate. Finish with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves.
