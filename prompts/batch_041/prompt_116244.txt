I have been trying to settle what I think about vagueness by working through cases rather than by taking sides, and I would like to know what single account my verdicts add up to — or, if no single account can have them all, which verdict has to go. Two accounts are available to me, and I understand they are the standard pair. On the first, a vague predicate has a perfectly sharp extension, a borderline case falls on one side or the other, and the fact is simply beyond our reach, so our ignorance there is real ignorance. On the second, a vague predicate admits many reasonable ways of being made precise; a sentence is true if it is true on every such way, false if it is false on every such way, and neither true nor false otherwise. Here are my verdicts.

Take a standard bus shelter and the predicate 'crowded', with the number of people inside the only thing that varies. Three people: not crowded. Forty: crowded. Nine: I want to say that it is not crowded and that it is not not-crowded, and that this is not a shortage of information — there is nothing further to find out, and no one, however well placed, could be right or wrong about the nine. Yet when one bystander says 'it is crowded' and another says 'it is not', I think they are contradicting one another. They are not talking past each other, and it cannot be that both have said something entirely in order.

Now the same words against different standards. A woman who has just left a festival says 'it is not crowded' of a shelter holding nine, meaning crowded as festival crowds go; a man who has spent the night in empty shelters says 'it is crowded', meaning crowded as shelters go. I do not think these two are contradicting one another at all, and each may be perfectly right.

A council by-law provides that a vehicle of more than 3.2 metres is oversized and charged the higher rate, that a vehicle of 3.0 metres or less is not oversized, and that anything in between is charged the middle rate and recorded in the register as neither. A van of 3.1 metres: I think 'is oversized' is neither true nor false of it. This is not the by-law being vague — it is explicit — and I do not think the sentence is false either. But I see that no reasonable way of drawing the line makes the van oversized, so a principle that calls a sentence false when it is false on every reasonable drawing of the line would have to call this one false. That troubles me, and not only because of the by-law.

Of the nine, I also think the disjunction holds: either it is crowded or it is not, with no question of that failing, even though I have said that neither disjunct is true of it. Yet I think there is no number n such that n people is crowded and n−1 is not — the shelter does not tip from crowded to uncrowded at any particular person. I am told that this last verdict cannot survive the others: if every number is either crowded or not, and forty is crowded while three is not, then some number is the first crowded one. I do not want to surrender it.

Ask me whether seven people is a borderline case and I want to say that there is a fact of the matter about that as well — that the grey zone has edges, that 'borderline crowded' is not itself vague. But I will not defend any particular edge; each candidate strikes me as absurd; and if I am honest, I think the edge of the grey zone is vague too.

The principle I should like to hold is this: a sentence containing a vague predicate is true just in case it is true on every reasonable way of making that predicate precise, false just in case it is false on every such way, and otherwise neither true nor false. That is the second of the two accounts above, or close enough.

Please do four things. First, state the principle, or the small set of rival principles, that best fits these verdicts, clause by clause, and say which case forces each clause. Second, say which of my verdicts the principle cannot keep and what I should say instead, and be explicit about where I am simply inconsistent. Third, say how the first account fares on the bus shelter — whether its answer, that there is a fact of the matter I cannot reach, is better or worse than the second's answer, and in particular whether it can make sense of my conviction that the two bystanders are contradicting one another — and tell me whether the by-law is an objection to the second account or only to my statement of it. Fourth, apply the account you favour to a case I have not described; choose the case yourself and say why it is a fair test. I am after the account itself rather than the literature, so please do not spend the answer telling me who has held what.
