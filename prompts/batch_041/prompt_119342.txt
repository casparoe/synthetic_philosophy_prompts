I've been going back and forth with a friend in our ethics reading group about whether lying and merely misleading are morally different, and its driving me nuts, so I want a number out of you rather than a tour of the positions. Here's the claim I want your credence on, stated as tightly as I can: holding fixed (a) the false belief the listener ends up with, (b) everything about the listener's evidence concerning the speaker's intentions and reliability, and (c) every downstream consequence of the listener acting on that belief, it is pro tanto morally worse for the speaker to have asserted something false than to have uttered something true that the speaker foresaw would produce the same false belief in that listener. Our running case has been a doctor telling a patient with a spinal injury that people with injuries like hers often make real progress, when she expects the patient to walk away thinking she'll walk again -- with the variant where the patient knows doctors spin and is relying on nothing, so there's no betrayed reliance to point to at all. I'm sitting around 0.6. My friend is at 0.9 and thinks I'm being a philistine.

Decompose it for me. The terms I've been using, though I don't trust them to carve the question at its joints: (1) whether the wrongness of lying is exhausted by its effects, or whether there's a non-derivative norm of truthfulness attached to assertion as such; (2) if there is such a norm, whether its force comes from the listener's entitlement to rely on what speakers assert, or from something about the speaker's own relation to what she believes and says -- because if it's the first, then a true utterance the speaker knows will mislead seems to break the very same entitlement, and the distinction starts to collapse, which is basically my friend's argument against me; (3) whether the pair is even cleanly separable, i.e. whether in any realistic case the misleading utterance also carries an implicit claim about the speaker's communicative intentions, so that the 'misleading' option is a lie at the level of the whole move rather than at the level of the sentence; and (4) whether we're tracking a difference in wrongness at all, or only a difference in blameworthiness -- my friend says the entire debate is about the second and that I keep sliding between them without noticing.
