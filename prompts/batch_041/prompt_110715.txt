I am a retired experimental scientist — a physiologist — who has spent the last several years teaching himself political philosophy, and I have learned to be suspicious of analogies that quietly do a great deal of argumentative work. The analogy I keep running into compares a constitution to the binding of Odysseus to the mast: the hero, foreseeing that he will be undone by a pleasure he will be unable to resist, has himself lashed to the ship so that he cannot act on his later, weaker self's impulses. The claim is that a people can do the same thing to itself, and that a constitution — or an entrenched clause within one — is precisely such a self-binding, adopted in a calm moment to restrain the passions of a later one.

A colleague of mine, an environmental economist, has just made this argument in earnest. He wants a carbon-pricing statute entrenched behind a supermajority requirement, on the grounds that future legislatures will predictably panic, backslide, or be captured, and that the present generation should therefore bind them to the mast now. He says this is exactly what constitutions are for.

I would like an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on carbon policy. Specifically:

- Give me the correspondence explicitly, ideally as a table: what plays the role of Odysseus, of the mast and the ropes, of the Sirens and their song, of the crew, of the wax in the crew's ears, and of the instruction that the crew bind him tighter still if he begs to be released. Flag any element of the story that has no counterpart at all.
- Then set out the disanalogies between one man binding himself and a people binding itself. I am particularly interested in whether a people is the sort of thing that can be an agent with a single will across time; in who could possibly occupy the role of the unbound crew, given that the enforcers are themselves members of the demos they restrain; and in whether the distinction between the power that makes a constitution and the powers that constitution creates can supply the missing distinction between binder and bound. I am also interested in what happens when the later self's change of mind is not weakness of will but a considered judgment, and in the objection that one generation has no authority to bind the next.
- Separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's intended use of the analogy from those that do not, and explain the basis for the separation.
- Finally, tell me whether the conclusion he wants — that entrenchment is licensed here — survives once the mapping is laid out, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If a different analogy would do the work better, say which and why.
