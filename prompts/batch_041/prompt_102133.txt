A friend and I have spent the past month arguing about the so-called paradox of deontology — the charge that a morality which forbids you to harm one person even to prevent several others from being harmed is incoherent, since it refuses to minimize the very violations it treats as paramount. Next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends deontological constraints and insists the paradox is a pseudo-problem, so I want questions that force him off that line rather than let him simply restate the view. Draft eight to ten: press him on why the constraint is more than a rule he happens to favor, on what the doing/allowing distinction is doing when both options foresee the same harm, and on what he says to a case in which violating the constraint once would prevent many more violations of that same constraint. For each question, give me the follow-up I should have ready for the answer he is most likely to give. Then add one question that opens a wrinkle my friend and I could not settle: whether a constraint can be defended on grounds that do not already presuppose the constraint — and if it cannot, whether that revives the paradox after all. Close with a question about what argument or evidence would change his mind.
