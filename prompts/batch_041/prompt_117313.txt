I retired last year after thirty-odd years running a marine ecology lab — mesocosms mostly, alot of ANOVAs, more hours than I care to admit squinting at p-values — and since then I've been sitting in on the philosophy of science seminar, which has been humbling in a good way. Now the department has asked me to lead a session at the annual retreat on the replication crisis, and I don't want to do the usual thing where someone stands up and tells everyone to pre-register and come back with a bigger n. What I'd like from you is a decision tree — a flowchart I can hand out — that starts from something nobody in the room would deny and ends at a leaf carrying a named position on what statistical inference actually is and why the crisis happened. Every internal node should be a question a working biologist can answer in plain terms, without already knowing which named position it leads to. The whole point is to sort people by what they'd actually do with their data rather than by what they call themselves; half my former students announce that they're Bayesians and then go compute a p-value, and I want the tree to catch that.

The branches I want are the ones that really split people, not the textbook prior-versus-long-run-frequency line: does the 95% in a 95% interval attach to the interval in front of you or to the procedure that generated it (and if the latter, what exactly is the reference class)? Does it matter how you decided to stop collecting data — I gather the stopping-rule question is the whole ballgame for frequentists and a red herring for Bayesians and likelihood people? If a study had 50% power, ran once, and just scraped past the threshold, is that strong evidence? Are two labs with different priors allowed to disagree forever, or is that a bug? Does a prior have to be your actual belief, or can it be a device for stabilising estimates, possibly estimated from the data itself? And where does the disease actually live — in the statistical methods, or in the incentives (file drawers, careers, journals)? I'd also like a branch for the people who reason Bayesianly but insist their inference be calibrated across the literature, since my hunch is that's where most of the room really sits; I want to see whether they can reach that leaf by more than one route.

Aim for seven to nine leaves and at least three levels of branching, and name each one. If some combination of answers doesn't correspond to anything anyone actually defends, say so and tell me whether it's incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored. Give each leaf a one-liner about what it commits you to that will annoy someone in the room, and flag any two leaves the tree can't genuinely seperate — I'd rather know than have it fudged. Don't bother with references or a reading list, I won't read them; I just want the instrument.
