I have no training in philosophy or economics, and no one to ask about this, so I have written the argument out below as carefully as I can. It concerns something that happened to me last spring.

A stranger stopped me outside a railway station. He said he worked for an institution he could not name, that it operated on a scale I had no way of checking, and that if I handed him everything in my wallet he would arrange for a very large number of people — he named a figure, enormous but finite — to be spared some catastrophe. He offered no proof of any of it. He wanted about four hundred pounds. I refused and walked away, and I am still certain that was the right thing to do, but I have not been able to say why, and this is the reasoning I keep coming back to.

(1) When I have to choose, the rational thing to do is whatever has the highest expected value.

(2) The expected value of an act is the value of each possible outcome multiplied by the probability of that outcome, added up across all the outcomes.

(3) If the man was telling the truth, refusing cost me a small sum of money and lost an enormous amount of good. If he was lying, refusing cost me nothing, and paying would have cost me four hundred pounds.

(4) Nothing in his story is impossible. Unlikely, certainly. But I cannot survey everything that exists and rule his institution out, and I have no way of saying whether his story is one chance in a thousand or one chance in a quadrillion.

(5) So the probability of his story is some number greater than zero, of a size I do not know.

(6) However small that number is, it is finite, and the good he described, however large, is also finite. Two finite numbers multiplied together give a third. For a large enough good, that third number is bigger than four hundred pounds.

(7) So expected value says I should have paid him. Notice that this holds however small the probability is, so long as it is not exactly zero, and however large the sum demanded, so long as it is finite.

That much already unsettles me. What unsettles me more is what happens when there is more than one such man.

(8) A week later, on the same pavement, a second man told me a similar story and asked for a larger sum. By (1) through (7) I should have paid him as well. And the next one, and the one after that.

(9) Suppose two of them reach me on the same morning, each saying that if I pay the other rather than him, something enormous will be lost to me, and I have enough money to satisfy one of them and no more.

(10) Then the reasoning requires me to pay the first and requires me to pay the second, and I cannot do both.

(11) And if I follow the reasoning wherever it leads, I hand over everything I have to strangers on the strength of stories I do not believe, and end with nothing.

(12) So the reasoning requires of me things that cannot both be done, and if I could do them it would ruin me.

I am confident that (12) is unacceptable and that I did the right thing at the station. But every step above looks true to me on its own, and I cannot find the seam between them. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what else has to be surrendered along with it. I would most like to keep (1) and (4); if either of those is the one that has to go, I want to understand exactly what I would be giving up.
