I'm a final-year PhD student in philosophy, and the paper I'm building my job talk around has turned out to be uncomfortably personal. It started with a close friend who was publicly blamed for a decision he says he could not have made differently — the circumstances, and his own settled character, had closed off the other path — and I found myself insisting that the blame was misplaced precisely because he had no real alternative. What unsettled me is that I don't believe that in general. I hold people responsible all the time for things they could not have done otherwise, and I think I'm right to. The paper is my attempt to make those two commitments consistent, and it has grown into a position I now have to defend in front of people who will not be gentle about it.

In brief, I argue that the entire debate over the principle of alternative possibilities has been conducted on the wrong time-scale. Frankfurt-style cases, at most, show that an agent can be responsible for an act even when no alternative act was open to her — but that, I claim, only establishes what I call authorship: the thin notion that the act genuinely issues from her rather than from something acting on her. The thicker notion, answerability — the one that grounds blame, resentment, and punishment — does require an alternative, just not one located at the moment of action. It requires that the agent could have revised, at some earlier point and by a process she had some grasp of, the disposition that produced the act, and that the revision was available to her under the description she herself accepted, not merely in some physically possible sense. I call this the revision requirement, and I use it to explain why we excuse the brainwashed and the coerced but still blame the man whose cruelty flowed from a character he never once examined. I also think it dissolves the standard flicker-of-freedom reply to Frankfurt cases — the suggestion that even there the agent retains some thin alternative — since a flicker is by definition a synchronic alternative, whereas mine is not.

The audience is a work-in-progress seminar in a large philosophy department. It is mostly compatibilists, two of whom work on reasons-responsiveness and treat Frankfurt cases as long settled in their favor; there is one hard-line libertarian who thinks every such case smuggles determinism into the actual sequence and therefore begs the question; a legal philosopher who thinks in terms of mens rea, duress, and excuses and will want to know whether my two notions map onto anything a court or a jury could actually use; and a Kantian who holds that the whole debate is confused because it treats the will as one cause among others. I have twenty minutes to present and thirty for questions. Please draft the prep sheet: the objections I will really get, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them, ordered by how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what to concede early, what to hold my ground on, and where my own position is weakest in a way I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. And give me the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks.
