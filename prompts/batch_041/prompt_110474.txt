I write about philosophy and the human sciences for a general-interest monthly, and for the past several months I have been sitting in on an adult-education seminar that gives a whole term to a single book; this term the book is Berkeley. It meets on Thursday evenings in a deconsecrated chapel the local university rents out, the group is a mix of retirees, two graduate students and a retired logic teacher who convenes it and hands round photocopies, and the argument has a way of continuing in the car park. Last week it continued for forty minutes, over what the seminar had spent three weeks calling the master argument, and I have not been able to settle it in my own head since. I would like a referee's report rather than a verdict from the sidelines.

The principals were Marta, a retired structural engineer who reads philosophy on her own, and Desmond, a doctoral student in the seminar. Here is the exchange, as close to the letter as my notes allow. (1) Marta: 'The whole thing is a conjuring trick. He asks you to conceive of a tree that no one is perceiving. You try. He then observes that you are perceiving it — there it is in your mind, with a look to it — and concludes that a tree cannot exist unperceived. But all he has caught you in is the impossibility of perceiving and not perceiving at the same instant. When I think that the far slope of the hill is wooded and that nobody is standing there to see it, I have thought something perfectly determinate about how things are. The impossibility is in my performing two incompatible acts at once, not in the state of affairs.' (2) Desmond: 'That is not a trick, it is the argument. Ask what is actually before your mind when you think about the far slope. There is a texture, a light, a slant. That is the perceiving. You have not got an unperceived wood in your mind; you have got a perceived wood with a label stuck to it that says nobody is looking at this, and the label is not part of the picture. If the only conception of a sensible thing anyone can form is a perceived thing plus a label, then the conception is empty, and he is entitled to say the unperceived tree is not hidden but impossible.' (3) Marta: 'Two things. First, if the only conception you will allow me already contains "perceived" in it, that is a stipulation about your use of words, not a finding about trees. Consider: I cannot conceive of a married bachelor, therefore no bachelor can marry — true premise, nonsensical conclusion, because the impossibility was in the definition. Second, my belief that there are three thousand trees on that slope contains no image of a tree at all; it is a claim about how things are, and I can hold it lying in the dark. You keep sliding between conceiving of a thing and conceiving that something is so.' (4) Desmond: 'The bachelor comparison is unfair, because nobody defines a tree as perceived. We define it by its look, its smell, its resistance when you lean on it, and every one of those is a way something appears; strip away the appearances and there is no residue left over to be hidden. Think of it this way — the content of any conception of a sensible thing is exhausted by how it would appear to someone suitably placed. That is not a definition I am imposing; it is what you would teach a child. And the step from "I cannot conceive it" to "it cannot be" is not a general principle he needs; he needs only the weaker claim that "mind-independent matter" is a phrase with no content anyone has ever managed to cash, so the debt is the materialist's.' (5) Marta: 'Even granting you every step, look at what the argument delivers. At most: for any sensible thing, some mind or other perceives it. That is not God, it is not one mind rather than many, and it does not touch minds themselves — which on your view exist without being perceived, which is precisely the trick you told me was impossible. So you have a local claim about tables and trees, and you need something far stronger to get idealism out of it.' (6) Desmond: 'The scope objection is the serious one, and I would concede half of it. But minds are not a counterexample, because he does not say we perceive our own minds; he says we know our own activity by a different route than we know tables — not by having an idea of it, but by something more like being acquainted with our own doing. Whether that is a genuine second kind of awareness or a patch invented to save the system is, I will admit, where I would want to spend the next three weeks.'

I would like this adjudicated move by move: rate each of the six from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, say for each one what premise it needs and whether the speaker is entitled to it, and tell me what each of them is missing that the other has not noticed. I care less about which reading is the historically correct Berkeley than about which of the two had the better of the argument as they actually conducted it, and if you can say which of them is more right overall, please do.
