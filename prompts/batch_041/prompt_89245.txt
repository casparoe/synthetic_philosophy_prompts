My friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about Goodhart's law, and by the end of it I think we had stopped talking about the same thing without noticing. I hold the view that there is a structural result here, something close to a theorem: any measure that is not itself the goal will, under enough optimization pressure, stop tracking the goal, and this requires no one to be gaming anything — it falls out of the fact that a measure is informative about the goal only on a distribution of cases that has not yet been selected on, so that driving the measure to its extremes moves you off that distribution. My friend thinks I am dressing up a tautology: that the observation "optimize the wrong thing and you get the wrong thing" has no content; that the cases anyone actually cares about are sociological — someone chose the number, someone else can move the number, and the number stops meaning what its chooser intended — and that my structural argument, if it worked, would show that every goal-directed action under uncertainty is self-defeating, which it plainly is not. She also pointed out that the version of the phenomenon I claim is unavoidable, the one where selecting on an imperfect measure also selects for the noise in it, looks like nothing more than regression to the mean, and that an unbiased but noisy estimate of a bounded quantity is at its most informative precisely at its extremes. I did not have a good answer to that. For what it is worth, we both take Goodhart's original remark about statistical regularities collapsing once pressure is placed on them for purposes of control to be the seed of the idea, and we both think Campbell's law and the Lucas critique are either the same insight arriving from different directions or merely adjacent to it — we disagree about which. We also disagree about whether the familiar four-way taxonomy of the phenomenon — regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial — carves reality at its joints or is four names for one thing plus three ordinary engineering problems.

What I want from you is the document my friend and I should have written instead of shouting at each other: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, not a dialogue, not a ruling on who is right, and not a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck. It should have four parts. First, a numbered list of the claims we can both sign — about measures, about optimization, about the cases in dispute — phrased so that neither of us would wince at it, and not hedged into vacuity. Second, the disagreements that remain, each stated in a wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of its position, one clearly labelled statement per side per issue. Third, a table of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. For every crux, give a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that we would both agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts it will show, in the form "structuralist predicts X, deflationist predicts Y". The cruxes I already know are live: whether the failure needs an agent with interests of its own or shows up in pure, agent-free optimization; whether the divergence is continuous in optimization pressure or has a threshold past which it turns catastrophic; whether the regressional version is a substantive result or just regression to the mean, and whether it can be made precise enough to entail anything about extreme values; what happens when the measure is a monotone function of the goal, or an unbiased noisy estimate of a bounded quantity; whether the examples we both accept as instances — reward hacking in a reinforcement-learning agent, a school teaching to the test, a hospital shortening measured waiting times — are one phenomenon or several; and whether the account of Goodharting is itself subject to Goodharting, so that not optimizing proxies becomes the next proxy to be optimized. Feel free to add cruxes we missed if they are genuinely load-bearing.

Fourth, tell me whether any part of our disagreement is merely verbal — in particular whether my friend and I are using the name Goodhart's law for two different things, one a family of empirical regularities about institutions that measure and rank, the other a claim about optimizing under model uncertainty — and if so, say which part is terminological and what survives once the terminological part is set aside. Write the document itself, in the voice of two co-authors, rather than advice about how to write it. No citations, no reading list, no bibliography; I want the content, not the scholarship.
