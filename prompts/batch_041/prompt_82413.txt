Bit of an odd one, but here goes. I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training at all, and I've gone down a Hofstadter hole after a colleague lent me Gödel, Escher, Bach. The bit I can't shake is the MU puzzle: you start with MI, you've got four dopey string-rewriting rules, and you're meant to get to MU, and it turns out you can't, because no matter how you apply the rules the number of I's in your string is never divisible by three, and MU has no I's at all (and nought is divisible by three). Hofstadter's point is that you can't prove that from the inside — you have to step outside and reason about the rules rather than with them, which he calls jootsing, I think (jumping out of the system). A collegue of mine reckons that's a bit of a cheat. His line is that outside is never outside anything in particular: you've just moved into a bigger system, in this case arithmetic about how many of each letter you've got, and that bigger system has its own rules and its own blind spots, so jootsing isn't a special mental move at all, it's just re-encoding at a higher level, and every programmer who proves something about a program is doing it constantly. I can see the force of that, but I also feel something gets lost: the string system genuinely can't notice the thing the proof notices, and my brute-force search script really will grind away forever without ever working out that it's wasting its time.

What I'd like from you is a proper comparison of the two positions — jootsing as a distinctive act, with the MU-puzzle proof as its showcase, versus the deflationary line that every escape is just a move into a bigger system — and a verdict on which one better explains what's actually going on in the MU case. A few things I'd like folded in. I noticed the unreachability isn't really a fact about the four rules on their own, because if I start from MIII instead of MI then MU drops out immediately by the third rule, so is the system we're meant to be jumping out of partly an artefact of where we chose to start? And is there any sense in which the invariant proof is genuinely outside rather than merely about, given that the invariant (how many I's you've got, mod 3) is a property the rules never mention? And if my colleague is right, is anything left of jootsing — is it just a fancy name for using a more expressive language, or does it still pick out something real about agents, as opposed to formal systems, being able to notice that the question they've been handed is a trap? Please argue it rather than just defining terms, tell me where the deflationist is right and where he overreaches, and if the two views turn out to be compatible, say what each is actually claiming.
