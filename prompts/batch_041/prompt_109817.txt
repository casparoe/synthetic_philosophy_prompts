I am a third-year student in a seminar on practical rationality, and I am trying to get clear on something about money pump arguments that our readings have left me unsure of. I would like a careful conceptual explanation, not a survey.

Here is the set-up I have in mind. An agent's preferences cycle: she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. She begins holding A. A counterparty offers, at each step, to trade whatever she currently holds for the option she currently prefers to it, charging a small fee per trade. She accepts each time, since she always prefers the new option to the one she holds, and after three trades she holds A again with less money. I am not interested in whether this particular example is realistic; I am interested in what it is supposed to prove. Assume, if it matters, that the cycle is not a contrived three-item one but arises from a chain of differences individually too small to notice, so that each pairwise comparison looks unobjectionable on its own.

My difficulty is that I cannot tell whether this shows that cyclical preferences are irrational, or only that someone holding them is exploitable if she is naive, and whether the argument assumes a thesis about diachronic rationality that a defender of cyclical preferences is entitled to reject. More specifically:

First, if the agent sees the whole sequence coming, what is supposed to be irrational about refusing the first trade? Refusing looks like what someone who prefers ending with A and more money to ending with A and less money would do. But that preference is a preference over whole sequences, and it is not obvious to me that an agent with cyclical preferences over items must have any determinate preferences over sequences, or that if she does, they must align with her item-preferences in the way the refusal requires.

Second, the pump unfolds over time, and at no single moment is the agent comparing the beginning of the sequence with the end of it. At each moment she compares two options she might hold. The argument therefore seems to presuppose that rationality governs sequences of choices and not merely choices taken one at a time. Is that a substantive commitment, or does it follow from something more basic? If it is substantive, does the pump only move someone who already accepts it?

Third, I have come across the suggestion that an agent who anticipates her own future choices, and takes them as fixed when deciding what to do now, is in a different position from an agent who adopts a plan and then treats the plan, rather than her preferences at the moment of action, as authoritative. Please explain that contrast, and then tell me what each of these agents can actually do about a pump. Does either escape it while keeping the cyclical preferences? If escaping requires her to surrender something — a future choice, the authority of her present ranking, or the cycle itself — what exactly is surrendered, and why should that surrender be thought rationally required?

Fourth, I want to know what the argument really turns on. If the trades were free, the agent would end where she began. One reading is that the argument needs the fee to generate an actual loss, in which case its force rests on her willingness to pay a little for each swap, and I would like to know whether that willingness follows from the cyclical preferences themselves or is an independent assumption about how she values money against the options. Another reading is that the loss is beside the point: what is incoherent is that she makes a series of choices each of which she regards as a strict improvement and ends no better off. I would like to know which reading the argument actually needs, and whether the answer changes anything.

Please separate what is a matter of definition, what is a substantive thesis about rationality, and where the argument is doing real work as opposed to assuming what it set out to prove. If there are standard answers to any of these, give them, but I care more about the structure of the problem than about who held which view, and I would rather have one clearly worked-out position than a catalogue of positions. Do not give me citations or reading suggestions; I have no way to consult them.
