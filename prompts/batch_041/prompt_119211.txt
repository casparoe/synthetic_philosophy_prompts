I'm in a small reading group that has spent the last month on Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and we've gotten stuck on the MU-puzzle in a way I didn't anticipate. For the record, since I want the exercise self-contained: you begin with the string MI and try to reach MU using four rewriting rules — you may append a U to any string ending in I, double whatever follows the M, replace any III with a single U, and delete any UU. The answer is that MU cannot be derived, and the standard proof requires stepping outside the derivation system altogether and noticing an invariant: the number of I's in any derivable string is never divisible by three, whereas MU contains none. Our group has taken to calling that move JOOTSing — jumping out of the system — and we have been arguing ever since about what it is worth. I'd like you to write the dialogue that the two most articulate members of the group should have had with each other, rather than the one they actually had. The first, call her the jumper, holds that JOOTSing is where the understanding genuinely lives: to know that MU is underivable, and to see why, is a different and higher sort of knowledge than any amount of mechanical grinding through derivations, and it is precisely the sort of thing that marks off a mind from a machine that can only shuffle symbols. The second, call him the insider, thinks this is a flattering story. The puzzle, he says, asks for a derivation; an impossibility proof answers a different question and then takes credit for the original one. Worse, any impossibility proof is itself a piece of rule-governed reasoning conducted in some further system, so what the jumper calls outside is just a differently furnished inside, and she has relocated the mystery rather than dissolved it.

I want the exchange complicated in two ways, and I want both characters to engage with the complications rather than talk past them. First, at our last meeting I floated a distinction that nobody has yet managed to make sense of, and I'd like them to try: weak JOOTSing, in which you happen to hit upon some property that the rules preserve and that excludes the target string, versus strong JOOTSing, in which you grasp why that property is the relevant one — enough to say in advance what will become of the puzzle when you alter it. Second, we actually did alter it. We added a fifth rule permitting the deletion of any pair of adjacent I's, and in the modified system the old invariant evaporates and MU turns out to be reachable in three moves; we checked this together, so neither character needs to redo the search, only to say what it means. Does the perturbation vindicate the jumper, by showing that the strong JOOTS-er can predict the fate of a system whose proof she has not yet seen? Or does it vindicate the insider, by showing that the celebrated impossibility proof was an artifact of one particular rule set and carries no insight that survives a trivial amendment?

Please make the dialogue substantive and evenly matched: neither of them should be a strawman, and each should be made to concede, qualify, or revise something under pressure from the other. Let them actually work through the modified puzzle, let each of them press a point the other cannot simply brush aside, and let them discover at least one place where they were using a word — invariant, explanation, understanding, or the word outside itself — in different senses, and then have them say so. I would rather the dialogue end on a sharpened, unresolved disagreement than on a tidy synthesis, and I would like it to be clear by the end exactly what each of them would have to give up in order to accept the other's position.
