I retired from geology about four years ago — forty years of rocks, and then suddenly a lot of free time — and I've been filling it by sitting in on philosophy courses at the local university. Right now it's philosophy of language, and next week's topic is slurs, which I did not expect to find half as interesting as I do. A friend of mine who teaches in a linguistics department gave me his two-minute version of that literature over dinner, and he delivered it with such confidence that I nodded along, but on the drive home I kept feeling that a couple of the joints were loose. Here's roughly what he said: "Slurs are pure attitude — they don't describe anything, they just spit. That's the expressivist line, and it's why you can't analyze one as 'member of group G' plus a sneer. A slur and the neutral word for the same people pick out the same people but don't mean the same thing. The proof that the ugliness isn't part of the content is that you can put a slur in a conditional or a question and the insult still lands — if it were in the truth-conditions, it would wash out in those frames. The other camp, the content people, say the slur just means 'G-person and contemptible,' which they get from the fact that using one commits you to a false claim about the group. And reclamation shows the whole thing is really about use rather than meaning: when a group takes a word back, the meaning stays where it is and only the permissions change."

Could you take that apart for me, claim by claim? I'd like to know which bits a philosopher of language who actually works on slurs would sign off on, which would make them wince, and which are just wrong — my friend is sharp, but he does enjoy a provocative formulation, so I don't fully trust the tidy version. Two things nag at me in particular. First, the conditional-and-question business: I can't see how the fact that an insult survives inside a question is supposed to be a *proof* that the nastiness isn't part of the meaning, since I'd have thought those frames are exactly where the interesting fights are. Second, I'm not convinced reclamation is as settled as he makes it sound, and I'd like to know whether his picture even has room for the friendly in-group use of a reclaimed word, or whether it's forced to call that an insult too. And one more thing that may be a naive question: what becomes of his "same people, different meaning" line if a word doesn't seem to have any neutral counterpart at all? I couldn't tell whether that's a real problem for the view or just me missing something obvious. (Sorry for the length — I've definately got more time on my hands than I used to.)
