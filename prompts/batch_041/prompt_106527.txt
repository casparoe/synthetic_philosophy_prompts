I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of ecology and environmental ethics, and I've been asked to contribute to the satire issue of a graduate journal in the philosophy of biology. I'd like you to draft the centerpiece: a mock methodology document for a voluntary market in "wild animal suffering offsets."

The registry is fictional — call it the Terrestrial Suffering Accounting Standard (TSAS) — and it issues fungible credits for suffering averted through land-use change. The piece should be legible to a general reader and genuinely funny to someone who knows both carbon-offset methodology and the wild-animal-welfare literature. The humor has to come from taking the accounting seriously; it should never wink at the reader or admit that the scheme is absurd.

The conceit you should adopt rather than debunk: a hectare of intact habitat has a net welfare baseline, computed as the sum over the individual animals living and dying there, and that baseline can come out negative. On that premise the registry credits projects for converting habitat to low-biomass uses, since the counterfactual being offset is the standing population that would otherwise have been born. A credit — one suffering-year averted — has to be fungible across taxa, which forces the document to say something about the comparability of suffering between species.

Write roughly 800–900 words in the register of a real offset methodology, with numbered sections on scope, baseline, additionality, leakage, permanence, monitoring and verification, and a buffer pool. For each of the following, resolve the question in the way that most favors the registry while keeping the reasoning internally consistent:

- Baseline. The counterfactual welfare of the habitat that was not converted. The document should treat this as measurable by survey rather than as unknowable, and should say what it does when a baseline comes out positive. It should also state which population-ethical assumption its aggregation relies on — that total welfare is what matters, or that what matters is whether lives are worth living — and why the choice is load-bearing.
- Additionality. What it means for a project to have "caused" averted suffering when the land would very likely have been converted anyway.
- Leakage. What happens when the displaced animals, or the displaced farmers, simply move to the adjacent hectare.
- Permanence. The awkward consequence that a regrowing forest reverses the credit, so the integrity of the credit depends on the land staying degraded; include the reissuance rule.
- Double counting. Whether the same individual can be counted twice — once as the prey whose death is a harm and once as the predator whose meal is a benefit — and which way the registry resolves it.
- Monitoring. A worked illustrative example on one hectare of lowland forest converted to oil palm, with clearly flagged placeholder numbers. Use the distinction between the standing population at a moment and the total number of animals that live and die over time, and note that the great majority of the individuals involved are short-lived invertebrates.
- Buffer pool. How credits are discounted for uncertainty when the uncertainty is precisely whether the animals' lives were worth living.

Then, outside the parody, add a short note of 150–200 words in your own voice: identify which assumptions are actually load-bearing, say which of them you would defend, and commit to a definite view on the empirical question of whether typical wild-animal lives are net negative. Don't hedge or lay out the options for me to choose from. Be explicit about which parts of the document extend the real literature and which are my inventions.

Constraints: no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists anywhere — the registry's references section should read "[withheld]" — and no real organizations or real people named. The register stays deadpan throughout, with no italics used as an elbow-nudge. If a dissent is needed for the satire to land, put it in a one-line "minority opinion" footnote attributed to the validation panel rather than in the body, and let the document end on a procedural clause rather than a moral.
