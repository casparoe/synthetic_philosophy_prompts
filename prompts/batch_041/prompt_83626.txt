I belong to a reading group that has spent a term working through the epistemology of disagreement, and we have reached the stage where the stock examples — the restaurant check, the horse race, the two detectives who have each checked their arithmetic twice — have stopped teaching us anything, because everyone already knows which verdict each side is meant to reach and the cases are arranged to make that easy. What I want from you instead is a case built to order, engineered rather than borrowed, and I would rather it be uncomfortable for both positions than flattering to either.

Construct a single scenario, in an ordinary present-day setting, meeting the following specifications. Two people are genuine epistemic peers: identical evidence, comparable competence and track records, no shared source of error, no asymmetry in the social pressure they face, and neither is a crank, impaired, distracted, or plainly unreliable. The question they are divided over must have one determinate correct answer, and it must arise naturally out of something the two are actually trying to do together — no lotteries, no betting, no paradoxes manufactured for their own sake, no exotic devices — and neither may be able to settle it by further checking, either because the available checks are exhausted or because the only remaining check is the very computation in dispute. The scenario must come in two versions that differ in exactly one respect. In the first, the two disagree about the answer to the question itself. In the second, they disagree about what each of them should do about the fact that they disagree: the disagreement is about the disagreement, and here too they are peers, with the same evidence and the same competence at the second-order question, having weighed the same arguments, each unable to find a flaw in the other's reasoning. Everything else is held fixed across the two versions: same people, same first-order evidence, same competence, same initial confidence in the answer to the underlying question. Assign each party a numerical confidence in that answer and keep the arithmetic simple enough to follow without a calculator.

Then do the work. For each version, state what the conciliatory position — the view that disagreement with a peer obliges you to move your confidence toward theirs — and the steadfast position — the view that your own first-order evidence may warrant keeping your answer, with the bare fact of disagreement counted as at most further evidence of the ordinary kind — say each party should do, and why. In the second version the conciliatory position must be applied to itself, and I want that verdict derived from the rule rather than stipulated: show how the very instruction that moves the two toward each other about the answer to the question also moves the person who accepts conciliationism toward the person who rejects it, and what that does to their confidence in conciliationism. Build the case tightly enough that the conciliationist cannot escape by denying peerhood, by saying the topic is one where disagreement is to be expected, or by pointing to an asymmetry between the parties. Then say whether the steadfast position suffers a parallel instability when applied to itself, or gets off cleanly, and why. Finish with the single feature of the case that is doing the work; what happens if that feature is removed, and which of the two versions then collapses into the other or into a standard case; how the defender of whichever position comes out worst would attack the construction, and be honest about whether the attack succeeds or merely relabels the problem; and one sentence stating what the case is for, that is, what it would show if it worked. A case that stipulates the verdicts rather than generating them from the two positions fails the brief, and the scenario itself should be short enough to state in a paragraph.
