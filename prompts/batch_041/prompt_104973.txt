I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last few years reading philosophy, and for the past two I have sat as the lay member on the ethics advisory panel of a fertility service. Roughly once a month the panel must approve or decline treatment in cases where the resulting child may have a life containing a great deal of suffering, and we have no shared way of reasoning about it: some members treat any prospect of hardship as decisive against, others treat the parents' wishes as decisive for, and I suspect both are running together questions that ought to be kept apart. Our chair has the final say, but she relies on us to frame the choice. I would like you to turn the procreation asymmetry — the cluster of claims about how the presence and the absence of goods and ills bear on whether it is right to bring someone into existence — into a decision procedure the panel can actually run. Set out the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as an answer to each, where the procedure returns a clear no, where it returns 'permissible either way', and where it must stop and be handed back to us as a judgement of value rather than of fact; assume nobody on the panel has read the literature and that we have about ten minutes per case. Then run it on three requests: two carriers of a recessive condition who want a child who, if affected, will have a normal lifespan but prolonged early pain; a woman who wishes to conceive with her late husband's stored gametes, where nothing about the child's welfare is at issue; and a couple who want to select an embryo that will be a tissue match for a dying sibling. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer our panel will dislike, and say plainly what it cannot settle.
