I'm on the grants and finance comittee of a small foundation that funds long-term projects (permanent endowment, vague charter about helping future generations). Next Thursday we vote on the discount rate we'll use to rank grants. Our finance chair wants a flat 4% real rate for everything, which would make a project that pays off in 150 years worth almost nothing now; he says that's just fiduciary common sense, since future people can't hold us accountable and might not exist. Two trustees want a zero pure time preference, so only project failure risk or a chance the world ends can justify discounting. One of them keeps saying the right rate is just pure time preference plus a growth term plus a catastrophe risk term, and another trustee says that formula just hides the ethics in the parameters. Our consultant has thrown in a third option: a declining schedule, something like 3% for the first 30 years, 1.5% for 30 to 100, and 0.25% after that, because we're more uncertain about far-future growth. The chair calls that a fudge; the zero-discount trustees call it a sellout. I have to circulate a short written opinion before the vote. Please write it: recommend a concrete policy (a rate or a schedule, plus when we should revisit it), give reasons the board can actually act on, and take the strongest opposing point seriously rather than just splitting the difference. In the reasoning, seperate pure time preference from the fact that future people may be richer, from the chance they never exist, and from the chance the project just fails. And if you think the real issue isn't the rate at all but something else in how we're framing the vote, say that and say what we should decide instead. No need for citations or reading lists; just make the argument.
