I write historical fiction, and I have spent the better part of a year with Hobbes, who is generally said to have held that geometry owes its certainty to its method — begin with definitions, proceed by deduction — and that ethics and politics would stop their quarreling if only they adopted the same discipline. What I keep noticing, though, is a particular way the maneuver fails. In an argument over something genuinely contested — whether a character is sentimental, whether a policy is just — someone proposes that we begin by defining our terms. The definition is presented as neutral ground; the deduction that follows is, so far as I can tell, valid; and yet the conclusion was already contained in the definition, so that nothing has been proved and the substantive claim has merely been relocated into the stipulation, which the syllogism then launders as a result. Is there a settled name for this move, and how is it distinct from begging the question on the one hand and from the idea that a definition can itself carry an evaluation on the other? I suspect that Hobbes's hope for a geometric politics turns on precisely this mistake, and I would be grateful for more than a label: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
