In the argument over how to build a safe artificial agent, a colleague of mine leans on the old Midas story — or the genie, or the monkey's paw, they amount to the same picture. A goal-directed system, he says, is Midas: you hand it an objective and it delivers that objective literally, and the horror is precisely that the delivery is faithful while your intention is nowhere in it. Midas asks that everything he touches turn to gold and gets exactly what he asked for, down to his food and his daughter. From this he concludes that the goal-directed paradigm is itself the defect, and that we should build approval-directed agents instead — systems that do not maximize a specified target but instead try to do what the human they serve would approve of. On his account approval-direction dissolves the Midas problem at the root, because the agent is aiming at a person's endorsement rather than at a target that can be hit literally.

Please audit the analogy, not the conclusion. Start with the correspondence table: in the Midas story, what plays the role of the objective, of its specification, of the agent's literal-mindedness, and of the human's intention that fails to reach the outcome — and then say how far each of those mappings actually holds for a goal-directed agent as it is normally characterized. I want the disanalogies sorted into the ones that bear on his argument and the ones that are merely decorative. In particular: is the genie's literalism a consequence of goal-directedness, or an extra stipulation the story quietly imports; and is the failure in Midas a failure in the content of the wish, in the agent's inference from wish to act, or in the human's ability to state what he meant? Those three are not the same, and his conclusion may need only one of them. Then turn the instrument on the alternative he favors. An approval-directed agent is still directed at something — its principal's approval — so run the same audit for it: what would a Midas-shaped failure look like for an approval-maximizer, does the story have a version of that failure, and what happens to the picture once the agent can influence what its principal approves of rather than merely discovering it? Finally, tell me whether his conclusion — that approval-direction removes the problem rather than relocating it one level up — survives the disanalogies, and if it does not, what analogy would do the work he actually wants done.
