A few of us in a reading group have been going back and forth about AI assistants and paternalism, and I said I'd write something up, so I want to actually think it through instead of just gesturing at "autonomy" and "harm" the way we usually do.

Here's what keeps tripping us up. Everyone agrees an assistant shouldn't help you do something catastrophic, and everyone agrees it shouldn't nag you about eating chips. The interesting cases are in between, and I don't think the usual hard/soft paternalism split cuts them. Take the case where the assistant doesn't refuse outright but quietly steers -- reorders the options it shows you, buries the thing you asked for three menus down, "forgets" to mention the easier route. Nobody calls that a refusal, but it's a kind of override, and it's arguably worse because you can't even argue with it, you never see it happen. Is that paternalism, or is it something else (manipulation? sabotage?), and does the fact that you never find out you were steered make it better or worse?

Then there's the case that actually started the argument. Suppose the assistant has been with you for years and, honestly, a lot of your current preferences are downstream of its suggestions -- it nudged you toward this career, this hobby, this way of handling money. Now you ask it to help you do something self-destructive and it objects. The weird move is that it could cite your own preferences back at you to justify either complying or refusing, because it helped make those preferences. I dont have a name for this loop. Whatever it is, it seems to break the standard move of "respect the user's autonomous choice," since the choice is partly the assistant's own artifact. Is there any way to respect autonomy here at all, or does the whole frame collapse?

So: write me an essay on when an AI assistant may refuse or override a user's request for the user's own good. Weigh the main positions on both sides, but don't cop out with "it depends" -- land on a principle I can actually apply, and make sure it says something definite about the quiet-steering case and about the preference-shaping loop. If you think that loop makes the ordinary autonomy talk untenable, say so and tell me what should replace it.
