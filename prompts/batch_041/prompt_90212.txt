I teach an upper-level course on rational choice and decision theory that our department runs jointly with the economics faculty, and I am now assembling the final examination. I have grown impatient with the way the Allais material tends to get examined: candidates memorize the two familiar gambles, recite that the modal pattern of choice violates the independence axiom, and conclude that expected utility has been refuted, without ever locating where the axiom actually bites or noticing what further assumptions must hold for it to bite at all. I would like you to draft a single essay question, the sort a strong candidate could answer well in about fifty minutes, that forces them to do that locating rather than to recite. Please give me the question itself, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words, and a marking scheme in which each must-hit point is named in a sentence together with the marks it carries out of twenty-five, and, for each point, a one-line note on how candidates characteristically get it wrong.

To keep them from regurgitating the textbook instance, I have drafted my own choice pairs and I want the question to be built on them. In the first pair the common consequence is a certain gain: one option is a sure M, and the other is a lottery paying the large prize L with probability four tenths, M with probability five tenths, and nothing with probability one tenth. The second pair strips that common consequence out and replaces it with nothing: one option pays M with probability one half and nothing otherwise, and the other pays L with probability four tenths and nothing otherwise. The heart of what I want the question to press is that one and the same person may prefer the sure M in the first pair and the long shot in the second, and that whether this amounts to one failure, to several failures, or to no failure at all depends entirely on which assumption is really being tested: the independence or substitution axiom as it is ordinarily stated over lotteries, the sure-thing principle as it is stated over acts and states in the Savage tradition, the reduction of compound lotteries, and the consequentialist assumption that the value of a consequence does not depend on the act that produced it. I have also drafted a third pair in case it sharpens the question: one option is a compound lottery in which a fair coin is tossed and, on heads, the first option of the second pair is played while, on tails, the second option of the second pair is played; the other option is the single reduced lottery that pays M with probability one quarter, L with probability one fifth, and nothing otherwise. The essay should oblige candidates to say what a defender of expected utility must concede, what a defender of the Allais pattern must give up, and where they themselves come down on whether the pattern tells against expected utility as a normative theory or only against one particular formulation of it.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. The marking scheme in particular should read as prose, each must-hit point and its marks carried in a sentence of its own.
