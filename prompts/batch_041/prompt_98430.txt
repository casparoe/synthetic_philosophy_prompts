Many role-playing games make a few non-player characters impossible to kill: they are flagged essential and merely kneel or recover when attacked. The same games let players slaughter hundreds of generic townsfolk, guards, and passersby with no in-game penalty, no legal response, and no lasting effect on the world; a player can wipe out a village and then take a quest from someone who acts as if nothing happened. What philosophical commitments does this design practice presuppose about the moral status of video-game characters? Does it presuppose that only narratively individuated characters have any claim on the player, that fictional beings are mere props and cannot be wronged, or something else—perhaps that the rule is about protecting game-state rather than about ethics? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then take a definite position: is the practice coherent, or does it presuppose incompatible views? If we replaced the presupposed view with one that took the standing or welfare of game characters seriously, what would have to change in game design, and would that be an improvement or a mistake? Give reasons.
