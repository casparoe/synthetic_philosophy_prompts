I am assembling a diagnostic handout for a graduate reading group on paradoxes, and I need a decision tree that carries a reader from the bare two-envelope setup to a definite position on what the correct response is.

Use this as the starting point: two envelopes, one contains exactly twice as much money as the other, you pick one at random, and the question is whether you have any reason to switch. The tree should handle both variants of the case — deciding blind, and deciding after looking inside your own envelope — and it should say where the two variants come apart.

Build the tree so that every internal node is a question a reader can answer about their own reasoning, and every leaf is a position. Let the branching turn on at least the following distinctions, and add others only if they earn their place:

- whether the setup as stated fixes a probability distribution over the amounts, or is silent on that point;
- whether the amount is treated as having a finite expectation, an infinite one, or no well-defined one;
- whether the reader reasons from the amount actually seen in the opened envelope or from the unopened setup;
- whether the claim that the other envelope is equally likely to be larger is grounded in the symmetry between the two envelopes or in the two possible contents (half or double what was seen);
- whether the reader compares expected values or argues by paired comparison over the possible pairs of amounts.

The leaves must include, at minimum: the position that one should always switch; the position that one should be indifferent; a position on which the verdict depends on the amount seen; the dissolutionist position that the puzzle is ill-posed; and the position that expected value is the wrong tool here because it is undefined. Give each leaf a short name, one sentence stating its verdict, and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Because a reader answers questions rather than labels, phrase each node as a concrete question about the case — for instance, "suppose you had opened the envelope and seen 40; would you still hold that ...?" — and never as "do you accept the principle that ...?". Begin the tree at the point where the reader accepts the setup and has not yet settled on a resolution, so that no first question already presupposes one. Aim for six to nine leaves. Where the tree cannot separate two positions, flag them as inseparable rather than forcing them onto different branches. Finally, list the combinations of answers the tree leaves unoccupied, and for each say briefly whether the gap is there because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. You need not name any authors; describe the positions themselves.
