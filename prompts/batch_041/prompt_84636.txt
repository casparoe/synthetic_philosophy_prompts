I have come around to the reading of Maimonides on the sacrificial commandments that treats them as an accommodation rather than as something good in itself. On this reading, the point of the cult was never the offerings — God has no need of them — but the redirection of a mode of worship Israel was already steeped in. Because the surrounding world worshiped by sacrifice, the Law neither abolished the practice outright nor merely tolerated it; it took the practice over, confined it to a single place and a single addressee, and specified its details so as to cancel out the pagan associations attaching to particular animals and rites. As the Guide of the Perplexed puts it, God did not decree that His Law simply annul, reject, and abolish all such worship. The sacrifices are thus a concession to the condition of a people newly out of Egypt, and their worth is instrumental and pedagogical rather than intrinsic.

What I want is a map of how exposed this reading is: what would count against it, ranked by how decisive each item would be — fatal, serious, merely awkward — with objections internal to Maimonides' own corpus and commitments kept distinct from external ones drawn from general standards for evaluating an explanation of an ancient religious practice. I am especially unsure about two things.

First, his legal code states that the messiah will rebuild the Temple and reinstate the offerings exactly as they once were, and that this restoration is something Jews are to await. If the cult was a concession to a condition that has passed, why should its restoration be an object of hope and obligation? How much does this cost the reading, and do the standard repairs — that the restoration demonstrates conformity with an unchanging divine will rather than any intrinsic worth, or that the accommodation was to human nature as such and not to a particular historical moment — actually work, or do they merely relocate the difficulty?

Second, the reading seems to have a rival that is hard to distinguish from it. If sacrifice is what human beings are naturally disposed to, then the claim that God accommodated Himself to that disposition begins to look like the claim that sacrifice is fitting for us — which is precisely what the accommodation reading was meant to deny. Can the two be kept apart, and if so, by what?

Beyond those: is the reading falsifiable at all, and what does it predict that its rivals do not? Does it risk explaining too much, since any feature of the cult can be redescribed after the fact as a countermeasure to something? What would move me most is a case of something the Law insists on that has no pagan counterpart to cancel out, or a commandment in the same family that the reading cannot treat as a concession without trivializing the notion of a concession.

Finally, tell me which of these the reading's defenders would themselves concede as genuine costs and which they would dismiss as question-begging — and give me an honest verdict on whether the reading is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a defect or simply what a reading of this kind looks like.
