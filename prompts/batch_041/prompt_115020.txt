I'm a retired physical chemist — thirty-odd years of spectroscopy — and since I quit I've been reading alot of metaphysics, the kind that keeps bumping into physics. The thing I can't get past is the identity of indiscernibles: no two things can be exactly alike in every respect and still be two things. Black's pair of identical spheres in an otherwise empty universe is supposed to be the counterexample, and the usual comeback is that they aren't really indiscernible, since each one stands at some distance from a sphere. I don't buy it, or rather I can't see what I'm being asked to buy. Explain the issue underneath the puzzle: what "property" has to mean for that comeback to do any work, why the principle slides between trivially true and obviously false as you let in more relational or impure properties, and how it relates to the converse principle that everyone actually accepts (if a is b, then whatever holds of a holds of b). Then the part I really care about: in a helium atom the two electrons aren't merely hard to tell apart, they're indistinguishable in principle — you can't even label them, and the wavefunction has to be symmetrized for it. Does that bear on any of this, or does it just change what we're willing to call an object?
