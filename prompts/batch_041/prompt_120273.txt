last winter i got weirdly obsessed with the starling murmurations over the pier at dusk and i've been trying to write a poem about them that isn't just pretty. heres the thing i cant get past: when the whole flock wheels and folds over itself like a wet sheet, everyone says the flock did that. but theres no leader and no plan, each bird is only watching a few birds near it. so whats doing the turning? i read somewhere that you can describe the same bit of the world three ways, as plain physics, as if it were built for a purpose, and as if it were an agent going after something, and i want the poem to do exactly that to one turn of the flock, once each, no cheating.

so make it a sestina. six stanzas of six lines, the same six end words in every stanza, just rotating, and then the three line bit at the end. first two stanzas in the physics, middle two in the built-for-a-purpose voice, last two in the wants-something voice, and the same six words have to survive all three. dont let the last two get away with anything the first four couldnt already say — if the flock decides to dodge the hawk, the poem has to also give me the prediction you only get by treating the flock as the thing that decides, otherwise its just decoration.

and the thing i actually want left open in those last three lines: is the flock a thing, or just a handy way of not writing down where every single bird is? my hunch is that its a real pattern if describing the flock lets you shrink the description, like if a video of a murmuration compresses smaller when the codec thinks in flocks than when it thinks in birds. but i dont know if thats a real test or a trick, so dont let the poem pretend to settle it. and give the no-flock-just-birds view a real say, dont strawman it.

someone who knows this stuff should be able to tell which stanza is which without me labelling them, and i should still want to read it out loud.
