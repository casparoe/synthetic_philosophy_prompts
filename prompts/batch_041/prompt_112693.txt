I'm on the interpretation committee at a mid-size city art museum — we're the ones who sign off on wall labels and the audio guide, which sounds like the most boring committee in the building until it isn't. We vote Thursday on the label for a painting we just bought, and I have to circulate a written opinion before then.

The painting is an abstract canvas from the early 60s by an artist who was basically ignored her whole life. The curators' draft label ends on a flat verdict: "This is one of the great paintings of the last century." Our education staff figure that's what labels are for. Two of us aren't so sure. Our worry isn't that the sentence is false — maybe it's true — it's that a visitor who ends up believing it because we told them has aquired something strange: they now think the painting is great and they still can't see why. The compromise on the table is what the draft calls "invitational" phrasing — "Many visitors find...", "Critics have called this...". The curators say that's cowardly, education says it's weaselly, and I honestly can't tell which of them is right.

There's a wrinkle: late in life the artist gave an interview calling the painting a failure, said she'd have painted over it if she'd had the nerve. The curators want to just leave that out. I'm not sure we can, but I'm also not sure it should count for much.

Then the audio guide, which is where it gets actually hard. We run a described tour for blind and low-vision visitors, and the proposal is to build evaluative guidance into it — not just "here's a large red field, there's a thin white line down the right side" but "the thing people can't stop looking at is the seam where the red breaks." The local blindness advocacy group has objected to the whole idea: they say being told what's beautiful isn't access, it's being condescended to, and if we're going to describe, describe, don't appraise. I think they've got a point, and I also think a sighted visitor gets the painting plus the room plus the lighting plus the crowd, the whole apparatus quietly telling them this thing is important — so staying silent about value for a blind visitor isn't neutral, it just hides the appraisal. I don't know how to hold both of those at once.

Give me the memo. A concrete recommendation on the label, with the actual wording I should be arguing for; a concrete recommendation on the described tour; and a straight account of the strongest case on the other side — half the committee thinks a museum that won't say what's good is just a warehouse with better lighting, and they're not fools. No literature review, no citations, I need something I can read out loud and have people actually respond to. The opening's on the 12th so it needs to land somewhere.
