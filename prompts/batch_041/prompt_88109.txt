Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind on a variant of the gradual-replacement case. A subject's neurons are replaced one at a time, but each replacement recycles the original neuron: it is disassembled, and its very atoms are reassembled into a silicon device that reproduces that neuron's input–output behaviour exactly, with no further neuron touched until the subject is cognitively unimpaired. By the end the whole brain has been converted, and at no stage does the subject report any change from the inside. The first interlocutor argues that the person survives, since neither the pattern nor the matter is interrupted — only the kind of thing doing the computing changes. The second argues that this is the slow replacement of a living brain by a mechanism, and that the subject's reports cannot settle the matter, since a system that merely processes the same signals would report continuity whether or not anything were experienced. Let each press the other: the survivalist must specify what level of description the substitute has to match — spike trains, dendritic computation, whole-brain dynamics — and explain why fixing that level is not question-begging; the sceptic must say what could count as evidence of survival, and how this case differs, if at all, from the ordinary turnover of molecules in a living brain. Neither should be a strawman, and each should grant whatever the case forces. End on the point they agree remains open.
