You are a philosopher of the social sciences with expertise in social ontology and in the methodology of cohort analysis, and I would like you to help me stress-test a position I have been defending. I would rather you be candid than encouraging, and I would rather you reason your way to the answer than reach for a familiar verdict.

The position is this: the generational labels in everyday use — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — pick out real social kinds, not merely convenient administrative fictions. On my view the label does genuine explanatory work. Membership is supposed to predict a range of attitudes and behaviors; it is supposed to be underwritten by a shared causal mechanism, roughly that the conditions people encounter while their political and social dispositions are forming leave a durable stamp; and it is supposed to carve the population at joints that are not simply arbitrary. A friendly way to put my criterion would be: a kind is real when it supports reliable induction and its members share a common causal mechanism. I suspect generations satisfy the first comfortably and the second only by contention, and I want to know how much that matters.

What I am asking for is a map of the position's exposure — everything that should count against it, ranked from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward, with an honest note on which defeaters its own defenders would concede and which they would wave away. I am especially interested in three clusters of problems.

First, the identification problem. Age, period, and cohort effects are entangled: people differ by how old they are, by when they live, and by when they were born, and these three move together in a way that makes them hard to separate. If the apparent differences between generations can be redescribed as effects of aging or of period, what is left of the kind? How much does my position actually need the effects to be cohort effects specifically, rather than any effect that happens to correlate with birth year? And what should I make of the further point that the spread of attitudes within a generation so often dwarfs the average difference between generations?

Second, the feedback problem. Suppose the label itself changes the people it labels — suppose people come to see themselves as members of a generation and adjust their self-understanding and conduct accordingly. Does that rescue the kind or dissolve it? Is a kind whose members are partly constituted by the classification a real kind, or is this the theory quietly manufacturing its own evidence? I genuinely do not know which way this cuts, and I would like you to tell me.

Third, the boundary problem. The year ranges are gerrymandered and drift; they differ across countries, across pollsters, and across the decades. Does a real kind have to have stable boundaries, or can something be real while remaining a matter of stipulation at the edges? If the boundaries are arbitrary but the interior clustering is robust, is that enough?

Please begin by brainstorming the strongest case against the position before you organize your response, and separate the objections that bear on the ontology from those that bear only on measurement. Be explicit about what evidence, argument, or formal result would move a reasonable proponent of my view, and about whether the view can be qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it can, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind. The defeater I would personally find hardest to answer is the identification problem, so give that one your closest attention. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want the reasoning and the structure of the vulnerability.
