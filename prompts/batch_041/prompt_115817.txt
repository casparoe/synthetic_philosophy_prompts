I'm in a reading group on well-being (we just did the experience machine) and for next month instead of a paper I've been told to bring in a short story — 1500-2500 words — that actually does some philosophical work on it rather than just being set in a plug-in world. My complaint with basically all the fiction I've found on this theme is that it's the same story every time: person plugs in, person slowly realizes the bliss is hollow, person unplugs, credits. I want something that starts where the argument actually gets interesting, i.e. after we've already granted that the machine is flawless. So: nothing is wrong with it. No glitches, no sinister company harvesting the sleepers, no "you can never leave" clause, no reveal that the real world was a simulation too. If the story needs any of those to work it's failed the assignment. The machine gives you any experience you could want, including the experience of having done something hard, of having earned something, of having friends who truly know you — the full subjective texture of a good life. And it's safe and reversible. That's the point, and it has to stay the point.

Here's the tension I want the story to live in. There's a study I half-remember where they flipped the question: instead of asking whether you'd plug in, they asked people who were supposedly already inside a machine whether they'd want to leave. Of the ones told nothing about their "real" lives, a bit over half said they'd unplug. Of the ones told their real lives were basically a maximum-security prison, almost nobody wanted to leave — like 13% or something. And the ones told their real lives were amazing (rich, living in Monaco, whatever) were about as willing to leave as the ones who were told nothing at all. Which is strange, right? It suggests a chunk of our anti-machine intuition is just status quo bias plus a fact about whether your actual life is bearable, not a glimpse of some special value reality has and experience lacks. I'd like the story to sit in that weirdness. Give me a protagonist whose job is talking people into leaving the tanks — some kind of unplugging counselor — and make the client she can't convince articulate rather than pitiable, someone with actual reasons. I want to finish the story unsure whether the counselor's conviction that real life is better is a genuine insight or just an artifact of her having a decent life and disliking change.

A few things I'd love to see in there, all of them through what characters do and say rather than anyone lecturing: the character who insists the only thing that matters is getting what you actually want, and who then has to face that the machine hands him exactly that, provided his wants are about his experiences; the character who thinks it doesn't matter because in there you're not really *anyone* — you never do anything, so there's no fact of the matter about whether you're brave or kind, and a life can't be good if there's no one home to live it; and the nagging thought I keep circling, which is whether a life can be going well in ways you never notice (your kids thriving while you're elsewhere) or whether well-being has to register from the inside at all. No character should say "hedonism" or name a philosopher, and please don't resolve it — I don't want the last line telling me which theory wins. I want to close the doc and not know.
