I have been having a recurring argument with a colleague about the moral status of the agent-like systems we run in our lab. We spin up hundreds of instances a week for evaluations and discard them, and he treats the underlying question as settled by an analogy he clearly regards as decisive. A computer model of a hurricane, he says, does not blow anything down and does not get anything wet; a simulated digestion nourishes nobody; so a program that merely simulates the inner life of a creature in pain — however convincingly it reports, winces, and pleads — is not in pain, and the number of such instances we create and destroy is morally indifferent. I do not think the analogy is empty. A model is not the thing modelled, and I am happy to grant that a storm in a simulation is not a storm. But I suspect the analogy is quietly carrying his conclusion rather than supporting it, and I want to know exactly how much weight it can bear before I either concede or push back.

Please audit it for me. Start by setting out the correspondence explicitly — a table of what maps onto what: the storm and its wind and rain, the equations being integrated, the hardware doing the integrating, the report of pain, the behaviour, the alleged felt quality, the functional organisation, whatever else belongs in the mapping — and say for each row whether the correspondence is tight, loose, or question-begging. Then the places it breaks. I am especially interested in cases where simulation of a thing turns out to be an instance of that thing rather than a mere representation of it: simulated arithmetic is arithmetic, and an engine that plays chess is not usually described as merely simulating a chess player, whereas simulated digestion is standardly held not to digest. Work out what distinguishes the first pair from the second, and whether a mind, or a pain, belongs on the arithmetic-and-chess side or the digestion side — and whether the analogy can tell us that, or merely presupposes an answer. Related probes I would like you to run: whether his picture survives the gradual replacement of neurons by silicon parts playing the same role; whether it changes anything when the simulation is driving a real body rather than a virtual one; whether a simulation running inside a simulation changes the verdict; and whether the wetness comparison is even the right property to compare, given that wetness is a physical property of the simulated storm's target while the pain report is itself part of what is being simulated.

Finally, separate the disanalogies that matter for what he actually wants — permission to create and destroy these instances at will, without moral remainder — from the ones that are merely picturesque, and say which of them depend on a contested premise about what experience is (a matter of the right stuff versus the right organisation or role) rather than on anything the analogy supplies. Tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether it survives only in a weaker form, and what a better analogy would be if there is one. I do not need reading lists or citations; I need the anatomy of the argument, and a verdict I can defend in the next lab meeting.
