I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar and I've been asked to run one 75-minute session for about 14 students on the species problem, and I want to do something hands-on instead of lecturing at them. Here's my rough idea, can you write it up properly and tell me where it breaks: I'll hand out cards for a bunch of made-up organisms I invented (not real ones, so nobody can just recall the answr) — a chain of populations ringing a mountain where the neighbours interbreed but the two ends don't, one asexual lineage, two populations that look identical but never interbreed, and a fossil series where you have to draw the lines in time yourself. Then I split them into small groups and give each group a different species concept to apply — biological, morphological, phylogenetic, ecological, and one weirder one like a cohesion or genic concept — and each group has to hand in a single number of species. Then the part I actually care about: a regulator needs one official number for an endangered-species listing that decides whether a road gets built, and the groups have to negotiate one number together. I want them to feel that the disagreement isn't from missing data or sloppy thinking but from the concepts answering different questions, and that "how many species?" has no answer apart from a purpose. So: materials, rules, timing, the debrief that maps what happened onto the species problem, and what will predictably go wrong the first time (my guess is they'll spend the whole thing arguing about my made-up data rather than the concepts, but tell me). And be honest about what the activity can't do — I don't think it can get them anywhere near the realism question, whether any of these concepts tracks a real division in nature, but correct me if I'm wrong.
