Im the chair of the clinical accountability committee at a mid-sized hospital system — we're the ones who decide what happens to a clinician after a serious error (retraining, restrictions on practice, or nothing at all). I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursdays meeting and I'd like you to draft it.

Two cases. Same mistake in both: a tenfold insulin dosing error, the required independent double-check skipped. Same drug, same step skipped, two different nurses, comparable patients, both caught within about twenty minutes by the same kind of bedside observation. In case one the patient seized that night and died four days later. In case two the patient's sugar came back up on its own and she went home and sent us a thank-you card. Both nurses have about fifteen years in and clean records.

Our sanctioning rubric lists "degree of harm to the patient" as an aggravating factor, so on paper the two cases land in different bands for identical conduct. There's a further wrinkle: case two is only in front of us because the nurse reported it herself through our safety-reporting channel, which we've told staff for years is confidential and non-punitive (and she actually got the wrong dose — it only counts as a near miss because nothing happened to her, which is its own mess). Case one came to us automatically because the patient died. So the person who did the right thing afterwards is the only reason she's in the room at all. Her union rep has already asked, in writing, whether she should have kept her mouth shut.

My committee is split and I need to be able to state the strongest point on the other side honestly. One member says if we stop counting harm we're telling a dead woman's family that it didn't matter that she died, and that the public will read it as us closing ranks. Another says we already ignore harm when we review near misses from the safety channel, so counting it here is just inconsistent. A third keeps saying the outcome is evidence about how dangerous the conduct really was — I think that has limited grip when the conduct is this well matched, but I don't want to wave it away, because I can see it cutting the other way in messier cases. One colleague keeps calling this the drunk driver problem and I don't find that framing all that helpful.

Write the opinion. I want a concrete recommendation on (a) whether to amend the rubric so harm stops being an aggravating factor, (b) what we should actually do with these two cases on Thursday, and (c) what to do about the reporting channel, since any answer to (a) that makes near misses sanctionable will kill the channel within a year. Give me a time frame for anything you recommend. Please don't turn this into a philosophy seminar — my colleagues stop reading at the second paragraph of theory.

At the end, tell me what would change your mind: what argument or fact would push you to the opposite recommendation, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. And say how confident you actually are in the main call.
