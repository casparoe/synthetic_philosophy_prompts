I've been reporting a piece on consciousness for a few months now and I keep bumping into the same picture, the theater one — the mind as a stage, attention as a spotlight, the lit part being what you're conscious of, and a dark audience of unconscious processes out there in the seats getting "told" whatever ends up on stage. It's the central image of global workspace theory and it's genuinely useful, I don't want to knock it. But last week I interviewed a guy who works in that tradition and he pushed it further than I'd heard before: he said the spotlight just is the experience, there's nothing else, and the whole philosopher's split between access consciousness — what's available to report and reason with — and phenomenal consciousness — what it's actually like — is a distinction without a difference, because the theater has no back room where a private show plays to nobody. That's a big claim to hang on a picture, so I want to audit it the way I'd audit any analogy a scientist leans on. What corresponds to what, first of all — stage, spotlight, audience, the stagehands moving the scenery — and then where does the correspondence actually break? I'm especially suspicious of the seats: who or what is sitting in them watching the lit part, and isn't that exactly the little man in the head everyone's supposed to have gotten rid of? And what does the metaphor quietly hand you that the brain may not have — a single place where the show is seen, a clean line between lit and unlit when real attention seems to fade out at the edges, an audience that's genuinely offstage when in the brain everything is inside the same skull? Once those are on the table, does his conclusion survive — is phenomenal consciousness really nothing over and above access, or was the theater carrying the argument the whole time and the conclusion just along for the ride? And if there's a better image for what he's actually getting at, I'd like that too.
