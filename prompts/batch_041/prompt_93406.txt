I have never studied philosophy or statistics formally — I read popular science and history and work things out on my own — so I apologise in advance if I use a technical word slightly off. There is one puzzle I have been turning over for about a year, and I would like to build a proper decision tree out of it, because every time I try to write down what I think I end up defending two things that cannot both be true.

The puzzle is the one about old evidence. A fact I have known for years can still, it seems to me, give real support to a theory that someone proposes only afterwards. My own small example: there is an iron footbridge in my town that hums at a particular low note in hard frost. I have known this since childhood and have never doubted it. Suppose someone now proposes a theory of metal fatigue that entails exactly that note under exactly those conditions. It seems to me the old fact does support the theory, and it seems to me equally that if support is measured as how much more likely the theory makes the fact, the answer is zero, since I was already certain of the fact and certainty cannot be raised. I want a tree that tells me which way out of this I am actually taking.

Please write it as an indented text tree rather than a diagram. It should open with a starting commitment that nearly anyone would accept — I suggest the claim that some old facts support later theories and some do not, so that the first branches have to locate where the difference lies — and it should end in no more than eight leaves, each labelled with the name of a position if it has a common one and with 'unnamed' if it does not, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Keep the whole tree to no more than ten branching questions.

Every branching node must be a question a person could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Build the branches out of the logical space first — where the disagreement is located (in the probability axioms, in the rule for changing belief, in the assumption that I am certain of the old fact, or in the very demand that support be measured as a rise in probability) — and only then place the actual positions in that space. I need at least these leaves to appear somewhere: the view that old facts are not evidence at all and only predictions made in advance count; the view that the puzzle is an artefact of treating knowledge as certainty and dissolves once my old degrees of belief are allowed to sit below one; the view that I should judge the theory against my state of mind before I learned the fact, rewinding the tape; the view that the real work is done by something new I learn about my own observation rather than about the bridge; the view that support is a matter of how well a theory predicts the data rather than of raising its probability, so the puzzle never arises; and the view that the whole apparatus is the wrong tool for the job. Flag any pair of these that the tree cannot separate.

Five cases must be handled, and at each leaf I want one sentence on which case it handles worst: (i) the plain case above; (ii) the theory was built by someone who already had my observation in hand and shaped the theory to fit it; (iii) I cannot remember a time before I knew the fact, so there is no earlier state of mine to rewind to; (iv) what I learn is not about the bridge but about my own observation — that I only noticed the humming because I had just read something predicting it; (v) the old fact is not certain, only very deeply entrenched. Finally, tell me which branches of the space are empty, and for each one whether it is empty because the position that would sit there is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to work it out.
