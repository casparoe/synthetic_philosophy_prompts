I am a high-school student writing a paper for my philosophy club on whether pain is bad in itself or only bad because we mind it, and I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind who works on the affective and motivational dimensions of pain. Please think carefully, and brainstorm briefly about each step before you commit to an answer. I have built the following argument, and I suspect it proves too much, but I cannot find where it fails:

(1) A sensation is bad for the person who has it only in virtue of how it feels to that person.
(2) In pain asymbolia, patients report that they feel pain but that it does not hurt or bother them, and they fail to withdraw from the noxious stimulus.
(3) So there can be felt pain that is not bad for the person having it.
(4) If the hurting can be absent while the feeling of pain is present, then the hurting must be a separate component layered on top of the sensation rather than part of what the sensation is.
(5) Imagine, then, a dial that turns down only how much a pain bothers you, leaving the intensity and location of the sensation untouched. At the lowest setting the sensation remains but its badness is gone.
(6) If badness can be dialed away while the sensation stays, then the badness is a reaction to the sensation rather than the sensation itself.
(7) Reactions can be removed by drugs, conditioning, or a change of outlook.
(8) So a person could be made to feel pain that does them no harm at all, and the wrongness of torturing someone would depend on their minding it.
(9) That is too much. So one of the steps must be false.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why the step looked plausible in the first place, and set out what each of the main positions on the badness of pain has to give up -- for instance, that the aversiveness is part of what pain is, that pain is bad because of the desire it creates to stop it, that pain is bad because it commands avoidance, or that the badness belongs to the whole experience rather than to the sensation alone. I would prefer to keep (2), since it looks like a plain report of what patients say, but I am willing to sacrifice (1) if I must.
