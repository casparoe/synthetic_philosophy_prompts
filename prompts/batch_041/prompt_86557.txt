Last night a friend and I argued for four hours about the repugnant conclusion and I lost: I could not say what is supposed to be wrong with a world containing vastly many lives that are just barely worth living, except that the prospect strikes me as obscene, which is not an argument. Next month I am interviewing, on a small podcast I host, a philosopher I admire who defends a critical-level view with a twist of her own. On her version the critical level is not fixed but rises with the number of people who exist, so that a given level of well-being is worth less in a crowded world than in a sparse one; she claims this lets her accept the reasoning that generates the repugnant conclusion while rejecting the conclusion itself.

Please act as a philosopher working in population axiology and value theory, and help me convert my defeat into an interview. Think her position through carefully before you write anything, and open your answer with a brief brainstorm — no more than a short paragraph or two — identifying the sharpest difficulties for a variable critical level; then draft eight to ten questions that engage the substance of her view rather than inviting her to summarise it. Assume she has defended it for years, so nothing as flat as asking her what a critical level is. I would like the set to include:

- a question about what fixes the rate at which the critical level rises with population size, and whether that is an independent ethical commitment or a parameter tuned to deliver the desired verdict on the repugnant conclusion;
- a question about the so-called very repugnant conclusion, in which the vast population also contains some lives of extreme suffering, and whether her variable level blocks that case as well or only the weaker one;
- a question about the so-called sadistic conclusion, and whether her view can ever rank the addition of lives not worth living above the addition of lives worth living;
- a question about whether the value of a life can intelligibly depend on how many other people exist, and if it can, whether her view implies that bringing a happy person into the world makes the rest of us worse off;
- a question about the same additive structure applied within a single life, or about the asymmetry between creating a happy person and creating an unhappy one, whichever you judge the more telling.

For every question, add a follow-up that anticipates the answer she is most likely to give and presses where the exchange should go next, and close with a question about what evidence or argument could count against her view. I want questions, not a reading list, so please do not supply citations or suggestions for further reading. Write in British English.
