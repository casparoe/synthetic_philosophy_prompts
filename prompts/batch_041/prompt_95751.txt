I am assembling a written exercise for a graduate seminar in value theory, and I would like you to work through the analysis the exercise will call for, rather than summarising the standard positions in the literature.

The axiology I want kept in view is one sometimes labelled tranquilism, and it should be taken as follows: the only intrinsic goods are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, so that craving and suffering are the only intrinsic bads. Craving is to be understood as the state of wanting or being drawn toward something, as distinct from liking or enjoying it. On this view pleasure, enjoyment, and the satisfaction of desire are not good in themselves, and a life wholly free of craving but devoid of pleasure is not worse than a life rich in pleasure and riddled with craving. This is a rival to hedonism rather than a variant of it, since the claim is not that craving happens to feel unpleasant but that being in a state of craving is bad independently of its felt quality, and that the disappearance of the craving is itself good.

Now consider a cluster of regulatory and clinical practices that are common knowledge. Public-health authorities treat gambling disorder, tobacco dependence, and compulsive social-media use as harms in their own right, harms counted even when the person reports enjoying the activity and would not trade it away, and treatment for dependence often consists in supplying the craved substance or activity in a safer or slower form, such as nicotine replacement, opioid maintenance, or structured screen time, rather than in removing its object. Yet the same authorities decline to treat other intense and reliably re-aroused appetites, such as caffeine, shopping, infatuation, or career ambition, as intrinsic harms, treating them at most as matters of moderation, and they permit advertisers and product designers to cultivate new cravings as long as no measurable injury results.

I want four things. First, state as precisely as you can the axiology to which this cluster of practices commits its defenders, and separate what the practices genuinely presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with. Second, identify where the practices are internally consistent and where their two strands pull apart, namely the strand that treats craving as an intrinsic harm and the strand that measures outcomes in terms of reduced suffering; say in particular whether the practices require the stronger monist reading of tranquilism or only the weaker claim that craving reliably accompanies pleasure. Third, say where the practice is underdetermined: which rival axiologies would license the same decisions just as well, and what the practices would have to look like in order to discriminate among them. Fourth, set out what would have to change, in regulation, in clinical aims, and in what counts as a benefit, if tranquilism rather than hedonism were the governing theory, and say whether any part of the present practice is actually inconsistent with tranquilism.
