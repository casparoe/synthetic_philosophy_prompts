I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of language (16 students, one 90-minute session) and the unit on Wittgenstein is always the hardest to bring off: students can recite that meaning is use and that concepts are held together by family resemblance, but they leave with the slogans and not with the grip of the ideas. I want an activity in which they run into the problem themselves before either phrase is spoken aloud.

Design a runnable session, with materials, timing, rules, and a structured debrief, built around two movements. In the first, students should acquire a term purely by using it — I introduce a made-up predicate applied to a run of cases according to a pattern I never state, students guess and apply it, and they should reach the point of applying it confidently and consistently while being unable to state the rule they are following; they should feel that they can go on in the same way without being able to say what the way is. In the second, groups of four each receive a deck of about twenty one-line descriptions of activities — plainly games, plainly not, and many borderline — and must produce a single-sentence definition of the concept that covers all and only the games in their deck. The decks should differ between groups in ways that are not announced: one group's deck should omit solitary activities, another's competitive ones, another's rule-governed non-games, and so on, so that each group converges on a different alleged essence. Groups then exchange definitions and attempt to break each other's with counterexamples, and finally the whole room attempts one definition while I feed in cases chosen to defeat whatever survives. The intended arc is that the definition-hunt fails not for lack of cleverness but because the demand for a definition is the wrong demand — and that the first movement has already shown them a concept they command without one.

Please specify: the exact construction of the decks, including the kinds of borderline and adversarial cases that will reliably break the obvious candidate definitions (a solitary ruleless activity, a fully rule-governed procedure that is not a game, a case of playing badly and still playing, a case where a practice changes and the term follows it); how to seed the made-up predicate so that students go on correctly rather than randomly; the timing of the reveal that the decks differed, which should land as a discovery and not as a gotcha; and the debrief, which should map specific moments in the activity onto the relevant parts of the view — the temptation of essence, meaning as use, the criss-crossing similarities that hold a concept together, and the point that the seminar's own organizing term is itself such a family rather than a kind with a definition.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it — in particular, what to do when a group wins the definition game with a gerrymandered disjunctive definition and the energy drains out of the room; when someone stipulates a definition and the group accepts it and stops; when students draw the conclusion that anything goes and that words mean whatever we like; and when the deck difference is missed entirely.

Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply myself: it stages the failure of the definitional demand but cannot reproduce the therapeutic aim of dissolving that demand rather than merely disappointing it, and it demonstrates the negative result — no common essence — without showing how family resemblance is supposed to work as a positive account of concept use. Say plainly which of these a debrief can gesture at and which needs the reading.
