I am preparing the methodological introduction to a graduate seminar on power, perspective, and the historical record, and I want a defensible standard of adequacy before I commit to a position on the central claim: that the surviving record of the past is systematically shaped by whoever held power. Write me the desiderata — what any adequate theory of that claim must deliver. Give me the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the constraints it must respect, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Tell me plainly which of the demands people typically make on such a theory are genuine requirements and which are the prejudices of one camp dressed up as neutral conditions. Where requirements pull against one another, say so; if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, that is the result I most want, along with an argument for why the conflict is real rather than an artifact of loose formulation.

Two ideas will be in play in the seminar and no assigned reading will develop them, so the requirements must be stated so that they accommodate these rather than presuppose them:

- Attrition without intent versus curation with intent. Records of the powerful may predominate because someone deliberately destroyed, suppressed, or rewrote the rest, or merely because the powerful produced more records, on more durable media, through institutions that kept copying them. These are different mechanisms with different evidential signatures, and a theory that fuses them will explain everything and predict nothing.
- Survival by ventriloquism. The testimony of a defeated people often reaches us only inside the victors' archives — translated, excerpted, categorized, and framed by the victors for their own purposes. The defeated presence in the record is therefore not evidence that the record is unbiased. A theory has to say what kind of presence would count as evidence and what would not.

I would rather you argue about a candidate requirement than ignore it, so take an explicit position on at least these: whether the theory must specify the level at which power operates — the state, the scribal or clerical class, the market, the scholarly profession, the language that survives — or may leave power as a single undifferentiated force; and what the theory implies for historical relativism, namely whether patterned power-shaping of the record supports the conclusion that no account is better than any other, or instead underwrites a more demanding method, in which case say what distinguishes a meaningful silence in the record from an accidental gap.

Finally, the theory must say what would count as evidence against it, and whether such evidence is in principle available to us at all, given that we can check the theory only against the surviving record — the very thing the theory is about. If the claim cannot escape that circularity without collapsing into triviality, say so and say what is left of it. Close with one paragraph naming the single requirement that does the most to rule out candidate positions and why.

No bibliography, reading list, or citations, and no survey of schools; I want the specification itself. Write in American English, roughly 900 to 1200 words, in a form I can hand to students.
