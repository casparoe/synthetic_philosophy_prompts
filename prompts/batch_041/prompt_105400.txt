I hold the view that money-pump arguments do genuine work: an agent whose preferences are cyclic, or whose choices across time can be driven round a loop that leaves her worse off by her own lights at every step, is irrational in virtue of that very structure, and the pump is what demonstrates it. I take the conclusion to be a genuinely diachronic requirement rather than a restatement of synchronic consistency, because the agent is supposed to be coherent at each moment and yet still exploitable once the moments are strung together.

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure. Not the standard objections recited back at me, but the observations, formal results, constructed cases, and arguments that should actually move someone who holds it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, together with a note on which of them the view's own defenders would concede as counting against them rather than dismissing as misunderstandings.

Press on three things in particular. First, the changing-taste pump: construct a case in which an agent is synchronically impeccable at every time -- no cycles, no intransitivity, no violation of expected utility within any single moment -- but whose preferences shift predictably across time in such a way that she can nevertheless be driven round a loop that leaves her worse off by her own lights. If such a pump can be built, does it show that mere diachronic instability is itself irrational, or does it show that the money-pump criterion is really tracking exploitability rather than irrationality, and so over-generates? Which reading should I adopt, and what turns on the choice?

Second, the escape routes. Consider the various ways an agent might refuse to be pumped: resolving in advance to stick to a plan, looking ahead and declining the first trade, binding herself, or disputing what counts as a trade she is obliged to accept. Are these genuine defences of the agent's rationality, or do they smuggle in an idealised chooser who is not really the target of the argument? In particular, if a defender insists that the pump only bites the naive chooser, has he conceded that the argument fails against the rational agent it was meant to indict?

Third, the collapse worry. Does the alleged diachronic requirement reduce, on inspection, to a synchronic requirement -- say, that at each moment one's preferences over whole courses of action be consistent -- and if it does, is that a defeat for my view or merely a clarification of it? I would like a straight answer on whether the diachronic framing is doing any independent work.

Throughout, tell me what the view predicts that its rivals do not, what evidence or development over the coming years would tell in its favour or against it, and whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- and if it has, whether that is a real problem for a position of this kind. Conclude with an honest verdict on how exposed the view actually is.

For what it is worth, what would move me most is a case in which the pump bites an agent we antecedently regard as a paragon of practical reason. If no such case can be constructed, say so plainly rather than manufacturing one.
