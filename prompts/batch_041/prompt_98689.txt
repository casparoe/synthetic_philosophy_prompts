I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized company that builds workforce-management software (shift scheduling, task assignment, productivity dashboards), and I have ended up chairing the internal review board that signs off on new deployments before they go to a client. We meet roughly monthly, we have about ninety minutes per case, and nobody on the board has a philosophy background; the decisions can affect hundreds of workers' pay, hours, and job security, and the head of product and our legal counsel have to sign the written recommendation, so the procedure has to be something a non-specialist can run and defend. I keep hearing two words from the one colleague who did a PPE degree — exploitation and alienation — and I have read enough Marx to know that they are not the same thing and that he thought they were connected, but I cannot get from that to a decision I can actually make in a meeting. What I want from you is a reusable procedure rather than a lecture: a checklist or decision tree in words that our board can run on any proposed deployment, with the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should stop with a clear no, and where it has to hand the decision back to a person because the question is about values rather than facts. Please keep whatever Marx actually said that matters — the difference between a worker producing value and a worker being exploited, and the several distinct senses of alienation, from the product, from the work process, from other people, and from what the work lets you be — but translate it into tests we can apply with the evidence we actually have: usage logs, pay records, worker surveys, the contract, and whatever the client will tell us. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I need the procedure.

Two complications I want the procedure to handle rather than wave away. First, my colleague argues that exploitation and alienation can come apart in both directions: a well-paid engineer who loves her craft and controls her own schedule can still be generating surplus for the firm, and an unpaid artist alienated from her own work is not being exploited in Marx's technical sense at all; if that is right, a procedure that treats one as a proxy for the other will misclassify cases, so I want the two questions kept separate and an explicit instruction for what to do when they point in opposite directions. Second, I do not want the procedure to collapse into asking whether the arrangement is pleasant for workers; Marx's point about exploitation is structural, about where the surplus goes, not about whether people are smiling. Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: (a) a warehouse picking system that lifts throughput about fifteen percent with no change in hourly pay and adds bathroom-break tracking; (b) a scheduling tool that gives workers more choice over shifts but moves them onto per-task pay with no benefits; and (c) the case I expect to be uncomfortable for me, our own team's AI assistant that genuinely makes the work more interesting but also makes it easy for people to keep working after hours without recording it. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the board will not like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do — the questions it will never settle, the numbers we will not have access to, and the points where the honest answer is that this is a judgment call about values and not a fact we can look up.
