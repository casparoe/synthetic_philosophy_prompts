I am assembling material for a unit on how much the counterfactual theory of causation has to borrow from its interventionist rival, and I need a single designed case rather than a survey of the literature or one of the stock examples. Build the case to the specifications below, then demonstrate that it satisfies each of them. Because the case is meant to be run against two stated tests, a scenario that stipulates the verdicts instead of deriving them is no use to me.

The two tests the case must be run against:

(T1) The counterfactual test, in the possible-worlds form standardly associated with David Lewis. C is a cause of E only if, in the nearest possible world in which C does not occur, E does not occur. You may also consider the usual repair that adds a chain condition: C counts as a cause if a sequence of events runs from C to E with each link depending counterfactually on its predecessor.

(T2) The interventionist test, in the manipulability form standardly associated with James Woodward. C is a cause of E just in case there is a possible intervention on C, where an intervention is a change in C produced by something that is not itself caused by C or by any of C's effects, and where every variable not causally downstream of C is held at the value it actually has, such that E changes.

Target verdicts. T1 must deliver: C is not a cause of E. T2 must deliver: C is a cause of E. Both verdicts must be forced by the case's structure and not by your choice of how finely to describe the effect; fix the effect once, at the ordinary grain at which one would describe it, and do not let the divergence turn on redescribing it.

Constraints on the case.

1. Ordinary and realistic: a house, a workshop, a kitchen, a small farm, a stretch of road. No science fiction, no time travel, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, no lotteries or chance.
2. Deterministic throughout, so that every would-have verdict is fixed.
3. Both C and E must be positive occurrences, not omissions, absences, or merely negative facts.
4. Not a case of preemption, not a case of overdetermination or trumping, and not a case in which two things do the work simultaneously.
5. At most one agent, and no second party who steps in when the first one does not act; in particular, nothing in the case may be a bystander's disposition to fill the gap.
6. If the case contains a second route by which E can come about, that route must be idle in the actual world, and its operation must not be caused by C's absence or by anything downstream of C.
7. One paragraph, with the events labelled.

What to deliver.

(a) The case.

(b) The verdict of each test and the reasoning that produces it. For T1, identify the world you are treating as nearest and say why nothing nearer would give the opposite verdict. For T2, identify the intervention and say exactly which variables it leaves alone and why that is legitimate.

(c) The chain condition: does it rescue T1? If not, say why every link is blocked. If it does, say whether the rescue is principled or whether it quietly assumes that the surrounding facts are held fixed, that is, whether it assumes T2's machinery.

(d) The single feature of the case that is doing the work, and what happens if you remove it: do the two tests converge, and in which direction? If removing it reduces the case to an ordinary instance of redundant causation, say so.

(e) The strongest reply available to someone who thinks the case is rigged, and your honest assessment of whether it succeeds. The most obvious line of attack is that you have made the nearest world do your work by ruling out the humbler ways in which C might have failed to occur; take that objection seriously and say what the case costs.

(f) A closing note separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. In particular, do not present as consensus the claim that the case refutes T1; if what it really shows is that T1 is under-specified until it says which facts are held fixed, say that instead. Mark which parts of your answer are robust and which depend on choices a competent critic would make differently, and where you are unsure, say that you are unsure.
