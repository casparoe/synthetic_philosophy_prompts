A friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about something that began as a joke and ended with us barely speaking, and I want to work out where I actually stand. The quarrel was about everyday conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking, the small stuff that varies from place to place — and whether failing to observe them can ever amount to a moral failing rather than mere rudeness. My friend holds what he calls the guest license: a visitor to a place is entitled to keep following the conventions of home and owes the locals nothing beyond ordinary courtesy and the avoidance of clear harm, because conventions are arbitrary coordination equilibria and outsiders were never party to the bargain that produced them. I found this too permissive and proposed what I called the imposition test — a visitor must comply wherever non-compliance shifts a cost onto people who reasonably relied on the convention — but the test kept giving answers I didn't like, and I now suspect it either collapses into his view or proves too much.

So I'd like you to write an essay that settles this, taking my side if you can but following the argument wherever it goes. Please bring in the hard cases we couldn't resolve: a visitor who declines to tip in a tipping city because she believes tipping lets employers underpay staff and she would rather not participate in the practice at all; a visitor who jaywalks in a city where everyone jaywalks, on the grounds that obeying the letter of the law there would be the genuinely disruptive act; a visitor who refuses to hold a place in a queue for a local friend, insisting that queue-jumping-by-proxy is cheating even when it is locally standard; and a host who expects a guest to eat what is served when the guest's own conventions forbid it. For each case, say whether the convention generates a genuine moral obligation, a merely conventional one, or none at all, and whether the visitor's outsider status changes the answer. Reach a definite verdict rather than surveying the options — I want to know whether everyday conventions can be moral obligations at all, and whether the guest license survives contact with these cases. Keep it to at most 800 words, and state your verdict in the opening sentence.
