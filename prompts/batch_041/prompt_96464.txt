I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've somehow ended up running a small internal ethics review group at a company that builds decision-support software for military targeting. I have roughly one engineer-quarter of my own time and a modest budget for two consulting philosophers, and I need to decide which open questions in the debate over the moral equality of combatants are actually worth spending that on.

Two problems keep blocking me, and I can't tell whether they are the crux of the debate or a distraction. First: the usual excuse for the ordinary soldier on the unjust side is that he could not reasonably have known his cause was unjust, but that excuse seems equally available to the soldier on the just side, and to his adversary, so liability ends up turning on what an agent could have known rather than on which side was in the right. If so, any rule I write has to be indexed to an agent's epistemic situation, and I don't see how to do that without collapsing into "follow your orders unless the illegality is glaring." Second: how large a contribution to an unjust threat makes someone liable to be killed — the rifleman, the drone operator, the truck driver, the engineer keeping the supply chain running, the taxpayer? The software needs a line drawn somewhere, and I suspect most of the practical payoff of this debate lives or dies on where it goes.

Which open questions here should I put the quarter into, in what order, and on what reasoning? I care much less about the list than about the case for it: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for someone with no philosophy training working from what has already been published, which look profound but would not change what a targeting review actually does, and what I would need to learn in order to move an item up or down. If either of my two framings is confused, or is simply not where the action is, say so plainly and redirect me.
