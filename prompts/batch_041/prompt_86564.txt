Ok, I'm writing a long feature on the fact-or-value fight over disease — the naturalists say a disease is a real malfunction in the body, the normativists say it's whatever a society decides to call one — and honestly I'm bored of the usual examples (homosexuality in the old diagnostic manuals, aging, that whole circuit). I want the piece to live in the awkward middle, in cases where you can feel the seams. So here's my shortlist, and what I'd like from you is a tier list: which of these are landmines for the naturalist, which are landmines for the normativist, and which can be shrugged off by either side. Rank them inside each tier, give me two or three sentences of why for each, and definately flag any that are doing no work and should be cut. No need to cite anything, I just want your read on it. Then tell me which single case you'd build the whole piece around.

1. Lactose intolerance. For most adults in the world, and for basically everyone a few thousand years ago, not digesting milk sugar is the normal state — it's the mutation that keeps the enzyme switched on into adulthood that's the odd one out. So who exactly is deficient here?

2. Pre-diabetes. Nothing much happens in anybody's body; a committee nudges a number and suddenly millions of people have a condition. Same story when the blood-pressure cut-off moved.

3. Male pattern baldness. Extremely common, painless, stops you doing precisely nothing, and yet there's a whole drug industry built on treating it.

4. Overdiagnosis. Tiny thyroid cancers and stage-zero breast lesions found by scans that would never have troubled anyone. The treating does real harm — there's alot of that going around. Does the harm make the finding a disease, or is the disease claim doing the harm?

5. Prolonged grief. Psychiatry long ago decided ordinary bereavement shouldn't count as depression, then un-decided it, and now grieving for too long can itself be a diagnosis. Grief is a normal response to loss — but then a fever is a normal response to an infection.

6. Orthosomnia. Sleep researchers coined this word for the loop where a fitness tracker tells you that you slept badly, you get anxious about the number, and now you actually can't sleep. The disorder seems to be caused by the measurement.

7. One I'm making up for the sake of argument: suppose we find people who genuinely need only five hours of sleep, function fine, live long — no malfunction anywhere you look. Does that make the rest of us the sick ones, or is there something fishy about treating a statistical majority as the healthy one?
