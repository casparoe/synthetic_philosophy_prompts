I chair the board of a consortium of eleven independent research libraries, and at Thursday's meeting we vote on whether to enter a four-milestone shared-collections compact with the commercial platform that currently hosts most of our digitized holdings. As drafted, the compact is an alternating sequence: at each milestone the party on the clock either locks in the terms as they then stand or advances to the next milestone, which enlarges the total value of the arrangement but passes the initiative to the other side. The payoffs our negotiators brought back, in value points for the consortium (C) and the platform (P), are these: milestone 1, C on the clock, lock-in pays C 4 and P 2; milestone 2, P on the clock, lock-in pays C 3 and P 5; milestone 3, C on the clock, lock-in pays C 6 and P 4; milestone 4, P on the clock, lock-in pays C 5 and P 9; and if both sides advance at milestone 4 the arrangement reaches full integration, worth C 8 and P 8. Our negotiators have also floated a variant with no terminal milestone at all, renewing automatically every two years unless a party gives notice, with a graduated exit fee.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — sign as drafted, sign only with amendments you name specifically, or decline and say what we should do instead — with a bottom line the board can act on, and it should show its work: run the sequence from the last milestone backward, state the outcome, and say what that outcome costs us relative to what the compact could be worth. I want the memo to take a position on the point that actually troubles me, which is not the familiar observation that people cooperate in games of this shape, but the fact that locking in early is supposed to be justified by each side's certainty about how the other will behave at stages that the same argument says will never be reached; say plainly whether that is a real defect in the reasoning or a piece of philosophy the board should set aside, and whether the analysis changes if the platform is not a single decision-maker, given that its board is reportedly split and its chief executive faces a shareholder vote in the spring. Address the variant with no terminal milestone and say whether it escapes the problem or merely relocates it, and say whether anything turns on treating the two sides' value points as a common currency.

The board has no appetite for citations, so argue from the numbers and from what you can defend. Treat the strongest case on the losing side honestly, including the case for signing as drafted despite the arithmetic, and do not dissolve the difficulty by defining it away. I have been reading decision-theory blogs for two decades, and I will notice if the memo is doing that.
