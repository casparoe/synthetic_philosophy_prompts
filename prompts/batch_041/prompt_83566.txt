ok so I'm in a metaphysics of science seminar this term and we've just spent two weeks on the Humean best-system picture of laws versus the view that laws are something over and above the whole pattern of local matters of fact. I get the usual examples — the world with a single F particle that's also G, vacuous generalizations, worlds with the same mosaic but different laws — but every one of them is a case where the Humean refuses to count some regularity as a law. I want a case that goes the opposite way, and I want to build it to spec myself rather than have it handed to me, so can you construct one and then show me it actually does what I asked?

Here's the spec.

Describe a possible world once, in terms of its complete mosaic — every local, non-modal matter of fact, all of it, past and future — and treat that same description as the input to both theories. In that world:

- The best-system account has to come out saying that a certain regularity R is one of the world's laws.
- The governing-laws account has to come out saying that R is not a law. Pick your version of that account and say which one you're using (necessitation relations between universals, laws as primitive modal facts, whatever), but it can't be a version on which laws just are the best system under another name.
- R has to have alot of instances. No vacuous generalizations, no one-instance cases, no world with exactly one F. That's the well-worn example and it's exactly what I'm trying to get away from.
- R should be a cross-kind regularity: it links two different fundamental kinds or properties, rather than being internal to one kind.
- Everything else has to be uncontroversial. Both theories should agree about the world's basic dynamics and disagree only about R. If they disagree about everything then the case isolates nothing and it's useless to me.
- No gods, no interventions from outside, no machinery beyond what the two views themselves already posit. One paragraph for the world. Small enough that I can hold the whole mosaic in my head.
- The verdicts have to be earned, not stipulated. In particular, don't tell me the governing-laws theorist denies R because I said so — it has to fall out of whatever method that view uses to work out which modal facts hold, and you have to tell me what extra modal facts the view posits in this world and why those and not others.

Then, after you've described the world:

1. Walk through both verdicts. For the Humean, actually do the simplicity-versus-strength trade-off and say why R earns its place in the system. For the governing-laws side, say why their method doesn't get you a law here.
2. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work. Then take that feature out and say whether the case collapses back into something both views agree about.
3. Here's my main worry. A best system is a system, so a Humean can always add an axiom stating the world's initial conditions and then R falls out as a mere consequence instead of a law. Does that move kill my case? And is it a legitimate move or a cheap one?
4. How does the governing-laws theorist attack the case, and what's the Humean's best reply?
5. If you re-weight the best system toward simplicity or toward strength, does R still make the cut? I want to know how much the case depends on a particular weighting.
6. Last thing, and this is the bit I'm most curious about: if laws instead flowed from the natures or essences of the properties themselves, rather than from either the mosaic or a seperate necessitation layer, what would that view say about R — same verdict as the Humean, same as the governing-laws theorist, or a third thing? Just a short answer, I'm not asking for a survey.

One constraint on all of it: the disagreement has to be metaphysical, not about what anyone could know. If the case only works because of our ignorance, it's no good to me.
