I'm a backend engineer with no formal background in philosophy or decision theory, and I've volunteered to run a forty-five-minute session in my company's internal tech-talk series. The audience is about a dozen software engineers and data scientists who are comfortable with basic probability and Bayes' rule but have never encountered a decision-theoretic argument. The topic I've taken on is the claim that information cannot hurt — the result that goes under Good's name, that an agent who maximizes expected utility should acquire costless information before deciding, because the expected value of that information is non-negative.

What I want from you is not an explanation of the result for my own benefit; it is a plan for teaching it. Specifically: the order in which the components have to be introduced so that the conclusion feels earned rather than obvious, and why each component depends on the one before it; the single concrete case that should carry the whole session, what that case must contain and what it must lack for the argument to come through cleanly, and whether I should invent one or use one of the cases below; the misconceptions my audience will arrive with or develop along the way, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; a closing question I can put to the room that would distinguish someone who has actually understood the result from someone who has merely memorized that information is good; and what I should deliberately leave out at this level, with an explicit note to the audience that I am leaving it out.

The reason I volunteered for this topic, and the thing I want the session organized around, is a long-running argument I've had with a colleague who insists that measuring can never hurt, on the grounds that you can always ignore the result. I've never been able to say precisely why that argument is right in some settings and wrong in others, and I suspect the answer is that the theorem is a claim about what to expect before you look, proved under the assumption that you will behave well as a reasoner afterward — so that the interesting cases are the ones where one of those assumptions quietly fails. Three cases from our own work keep nagging at me, and I can't sort out which of them the theorem actually covers:

- a two-week A/B test on a noisy metric, where I'm fairly sure the team will over-trust the result and ship the worse variant because of it;
- a rollout where a competitor can observe which arm we chose, so that our learning their response changes their behavior;
- a dashboard I don't entirely trust, where I can't be sure whether I'm looking at the signal or at a bug in the signal.

I would like the session to end with the audience able to say, for each of these, which assumption it breaks and whether the repair is to model the agent differently, the situation differently, or the information itself differently. Tell me which of the three, if any, deserves to be the carrying example and why the others don't.

Two things I'm specifically worried about. First, that the audience will hear "expected value" and silently substitute "the average outcome we'd get," which I think is the root of most of the confusion. Second, that they will walk out believing the theorem says you should always go and look, and that it says the experience of receiving bad news is good for you — the claim about what to expect beforehand versus the experience of the news itself. Please place each of those in the sequence rather than treating them as asides.
