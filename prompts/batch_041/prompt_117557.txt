In debate club we have been arguing about why some nations are rich and others poor, and the analogy everyone keeps reaching for is that institutions are the rules of the game: an economy is a game, its laws and norms are the rules, and a poor country is poor because its rules are bad. Our coach used it to argue that the way to help such a country is to install good rules — secure property, honest courts, enforceable contracts — and then let the game play itself out, the way you would repair a broken board game by replacing its rulebook. I have also read that a country can import a rich country's commercial code almost word for word and end up no better off.

I would like an audit of how far the analogy actually holds. Give me the mapping first — what corresponds to the rulebook, the players, the referee, the field, the score, and the prize — and then the places where it breaks. I am especially interested in the features that real games have and that a national economy may not: an umpire who is not himself a competitor, rules that hold still while the game is being played, a payoff all the players agree on, and the option of starting over. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion our coach drew — install the right rules and prosperity follows — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument.

Please answer as a JSON object with exactly these fields: conclusion_being_licensed (string); correspondences (array of objects with source_element, target_element, strength — one of exact, partial, or fails — and comment); smuggled_in (array of strings, one per feature the analogy quietly imports from games that an economy may lack); disanalogies (array of objects with feature, load_bearing — boolean — and why, where load_bearing means the conclusion collapses without that feature holding); conclusion_verdict (object with survives — one of yes, no, or only_weakened — and best_surviving_version); and better_analogy (object with candidate, advantage, and residual_gap).
