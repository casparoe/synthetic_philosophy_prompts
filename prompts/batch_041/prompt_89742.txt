I am a novelist at work on a book about a city that, after a coup, tries to bind itself against future tyranny. The mayor signs a personal Ulysses contract: if he ever declares a state of emergency, he is to be removed by a committee and replaced by a randomly chosen citizens' jury. The same city also adopts a constitutional precommitment: a bill of rights that cannot be suspended even by a unanimous vote of the assembly, enforced by a court whose judges the mayor appoints. I want the novel's intellectual spine to be a comparison of these two devices, not a lecture. Please compare them along four axes: first, who is the self that binds and who is the self that is bound; second, what makes each binding credible when the future self disagrees; third, how each handles the possibility that the binding self was mistaken, cowardly, or unjust; and fourth, what happens when the enforcer is also a beneficiary and can be replaced by the very power being bound. Do not stop at the obvious point that one is individual and the other collective; that is the beginning of the problem, not the answer. Then say where the analogy between a personal Ulysses contract and a constitutional precommitment breaks down, and which of the two the novel should treat as more tragic. Structure your answer as: 'The two devices' (about 150 words), 'The binding problem' (about 150 words), 'The mistaken self' (about 150 words), 'The enforcer's dilemma' (about 150 words), 'Where the analogy breaks' (about 200 words), and 'Bottom line' (2-3 sentences). No citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; argue from the concepts.
