I am the philosophy of religion editor at a small academic journal, and I triage roughly forty submissions a year that turn on some version of the ontological argument; an increasing number are modal, and several are Gödel-inspired. The problem is not a shortage of referees but inconsistency: one report treats the argument as a live proof, another as a dead letter, and I have to decide under deadline what to send out and how much weight to give each report. I have a PhD in philosophy but not in modal logic, and I have about fifteen minutes per submission at triage; desk rejections are mine to make, while acceptances go to the editor-in-chief. Please turn the ontological argument itself, including its modal version, into a decision procedure I can actually run before I assign referees. I want the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear send-to-referees or reject-without-review, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the issue is a contested philosophical judgment rather than a mechanical defect. Build in the distinctions that matter: whether the argument is Anselmian or Gödel-style; whether it treats greatness as a maximal property or a second-order property; whether it relies on S5 and what happens if it does not; whether positive property is doing substantive work or is a placeholder; whether necessary existence is a perfection or a category mistake; whether the argument has a parody or modal-collapse problem; and whether the modal operator is meant to be logical, metaphysical, or something else. A colleague has suggested we treat the argument as a recipe rather than a proof: the premises specify a role, and the question is whether any possible world can fill that role. I want the procedure to be honest about whether that reframing helps or just changes the subject.

Then run the procedure on two submissions. The first is a clean Anselmian argument from a being than which nothing greater can be conceived, with the usual premise that existence in reality is greater than existence in the understanding. The second is a Gödel-style modal argument: it defines God as a being having all positive properties, treats positive properties as necessarily positive, treats necessary existence as positive, and uses S5 together with an essence premise to move from possible instantiation to necessary existence. I want to see where each one passes, where it stalls, and what I should write to the authors. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to do something I will not like -- for example, if it says I cannot reject a submission I am confident is sophistical, or if it says a premise I find absurd is not procedurally defective. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which disputes about the ontological argument will remain disputes no matter how carefully I sequence the questions. I would rather have a committed recommendation, with reasons, than a neutral survey of options: if you think the procedure should be stricter or looser than I am asking for, say so and defend it. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I need the procedure itself, in a form I can paste into our submission-triage notes.
