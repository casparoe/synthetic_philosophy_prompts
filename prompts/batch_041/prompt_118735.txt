Colleagues of mine in machine translation lean on an analogy I would like audited: translating is decoding, the source sentence is a ciphertext, the target sentence is the plaintext, and a good enough statistical model is the key. The work they want the analogy to do is to defuse the worry that such engines do not understand: if the message comes through intact, nothing has been left behind, and talk of understanding is sentimentality about a solved engineering problem. Lay out the correspondence -- what plays the part of message, cipher, key, and decoder -- and then say where it breaks. Which features of a genuine code does a natural language lack? Consider that a cipher has a single correct plaintext, that encoding is losslessly reversible, that the key is independent of the message, and that the decoded message is not itself a further thing to be decoded. Then tell me whether the conclusion about understanding survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along. Finally, if the decoding picture is the wrong one, say which comparison does better -- the translator as a tourist with a phrasebook, as a performer realizing a score, as a channel with noise -- and what each of those would commit us to about what a translation engine is and is not doing.
