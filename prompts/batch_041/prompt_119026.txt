I write about science and ideas, and for the past year I have kept a calibration notebook recording how my credences move when what I receive is not proof but a nudge — a glance at a torn photograph of a lottery ticket, say, where the shape of the digit suggests a 6 without settling it. Before I looked, my credences were: 6, 0.1; 2, 0.2; 4, 0.1; and 1, 3 and 5, 0.2 apiece. I have four verdicts about how such nudges should work, and I cannot get them to agree.

First, after a glance that leaves me 0.6 confident the digit is a 6, I think 2 should remain twice as likely as 4. The glance spoke to 6 versus not-6; it told me nothing about 2 versus 4.

Second, if I take two separate glimpses, the first of which on its own would have left me 0.6 confident and the second of which on its own would have left me 0.7, then together they should leave me more confident than 0.7. Two looks are two pieces of evidence, and the second should not simply overwrite the first.

Third, if one nudge concerns the digit and another concerns whether the digit is even — leaving me 0.8 confident that it is — my final credence should not depend on which nudge arrived first.

Fourth, if I later learn for certain that the digit is a 6, my credence should be exactly what it would have been had I learned that at the start; the blurry glimpses should leave no residue.

A friend who works in Bayesian statistics tells me that the rule I am groping toward is a known one, probability kinematics, that it cannot deliver all four verdicts, and that the repair is to treat each glimpse not as fixing my new credence but as a likelihood — how much more likely that glimpse is if the digit is a 6 than if it is not. She may well be right, but I cannot see which verdict I am meant to surrender.

Please state, as precisely as you can, the rule — or the small set of competing rules — that captures the most of what I have said; say which of my four verdicts it forces me to abandon, and why; and tell me what it implies about the case I cannot settle: the 0.6 glance and the 0.8-even nudge together — what should I then think about the 6?
