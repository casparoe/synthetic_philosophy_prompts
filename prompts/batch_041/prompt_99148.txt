In our department's philosophy of history working group, a colleague has been pressing an argument that I can neither refute nor bring myself to accept, and I would like to know whether it is a reductio of its conclusion or of one of its premises. He states it as follows.

(1) To explain an occurrence is to make it intelligible by showing that, given the circumstances that actually obtained, an occurrence of that kind was to be expected.
(2) Making an occurrence expectable in that sense requires subsuming it under at least one general regularity connecting antecedent circumstances of that kind to occurrences of the consequent kind.
(3) Hence every genuine explanation contains, at least implicitly, one or more general laws.
(4) Historians genuinely explain. When we ask why the Republic collapsed, or why a particular revolution occurred, we expect, and frequently receive, an answer of the explanatory rather than the merely chronological kind.
(5) Therefore every genuine historical explanation presupposes general laws, whether or not the historian formulates them.
(6) Yet historical writing contains no such laws, and every candidate law we supply on the historian's behalf — for example, some generalization about the conditions under which regimes become unstable — proves to be false, vacuous, or hedged until it can no longer be falsified.
(7) A requirement that no actual instance of a practice satisfies, and that its practitioners neither state nor agree upon, cannot be a genuine condition on that practice; it is an artifact of the philosopher's reconstruction.
(8) Therefore history does not explain. Its apparent explanations are narratives that redescribe the occurrence at a coarser grain and produce the feeling of understanding without the substance.

I regard (8) as intolerable and would rather keep (4), but I cannot see how to retain (1) through (3) and escape it. Which step is doing the illegitimate work, and what does rejecting it cost? If the fault lies in (1) or (2), I want to know what conception of explanation I can hold that still earns the historian's 'because' without collapsing into mere storytelling; if instead the argument is valid, I want to know which premise is least costly to abandon and what that concession does to the covering-law program. I am also uneasy that the same reasoning, run against narrative, would show that any historical narrative is arbitrary, since any occurrence has indefinitely many antecedent conditions and the narrative must select among them. Does that parallel argument embarrass the narrative alternative as much as it embarrasses the covering-law one?
