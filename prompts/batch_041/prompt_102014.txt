I'm putting together a small grant application — six months, one day a week, so realistically a paper rather than a book — and I've got a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into a proper short proposal, call it two pages.

The idea: everyone knows Malthus got the mechanism roughly right and the timing badly wrong, and everyone's happy to say he was 'right but early'. I think that phrase is hiding a real philosophical problem, and I want to make the problem sharp rather than just wave at the demographic transition. My hunch is that a long-run prediction is always a conjuction of two quite different things: a claim about substance (the mechanism, the direction, what depends on what) and a claim about schedule (rates, lags, thresholds, when the thing actually bites). A prediction can fail on the schedule while the substance is untouched, and I suspect the philosophy of science has no clean way of saying when that's a vindication and when it's just a rescue that saves a dying theory by shifting the goalposts. So what I want is a distinction between what I've been calling a temporal auxiliary — the silent rate-and-lag assumptions a prediction quietly leans on — and the theory's actual content, and then a criterion for when revising a temporal auxiliary is legitimate rather than a dodge.

What I need from you: state the question so it has a determinate answer (something like 'under what conditions does a theory whose long-run prediction failed on timing count as corroborated rather than refuted?'), then say why the standard accounts leave it open. I'm thinking Popper on falsification and ad hoc rescue, the Duhem–Quine point that you never test a hypothesis on its own, and Lakatos on progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts — but say what each of them can't do for the 'right but early' case rather than just summarising them, and say why a conceptual approach of this kind is the right one rather than, say, just writing the history of the Malthus episode or building a formal model.

Then the method: what I'd actually do, month by month, over six months at one day a week. I want the Malthus case as the worked example and probably one or two contrasting cases where a long-run prediction was rescued and we now think the rescue was a cheat (or the opposite), so the criterion has something to bite on. You pick the contrasts, but keep them to things I can discuss from general background, not things I'd have to go and dig up.

Also: what a result would look like (a criterion, a taxonomy of rescue-moves, a verdict on one case), what would count as the project failing — be honest, e.g. if the substance/schedule distinction turns out to be unstable, or if the criterion can't be applied without already knowing whether the theory is true — and whether this is realistically doable in the time or whether it's secretly a PhD.

Don't give me citations or a reading list, I'll add those myself, and I'd rather you refer to positions and arguments than to specific papers. And don't lean on obscure examples I won't know; the whole thing should be followable from a decent general background.
