I am a graduate student in an epistemology seminar, and I would like your help settling an argument that broke out between two other members of the seminar after class, which I listened to for the better part of an hour without being able to decide. The session had been about the two puzzles in which every member of a large set of beliefs is individually well supported while the set as a whole cannot be true: the lottery case, in which you hold a single ticket in a fair draw, believe of each ticket that it will lose, and yet know that some ticket must win; and the preface case, in which an author asserts several hundred separate claims, each of which she has good evidence for, and then concedes in the preface that, being fallible, she has probably got at least one of them wrong.

Let me call the first student A and the second B, and set out the exchange as fairly as I can. (1) A said the two puzzles are one problem in two costumes: in both, a body of individually reasonable beliefs has an incredible conjunction, so the culprit must be some general principle — that high probability suffices for outright belief, or that belief is closed under conjunction, or that a rational believer's set of beliefs must be consistent — and any solution that treats the lottery and the preface differently is simply ad hoc. (2) B said the cases differ in kind. In the lottery, the denial of the conjunction is guaranteed by the setup itself, the beliefs are perfectly symmetric, and each one is credible only in virtue of the very fact that makes the conjunction false; in the preface, the claims are heterogeneous and independently evidenced, the denial of the conjunction is at best a probable induction about the author's own error rate, and she might perfectly well have written a book that is entirely correct. (3) A replied that this contrast collapses once you notice that the author's confidence in any single claim is partly underwritten by a general fact about her own reliability, so the same leakage occurs in both cases; and to make the point concrete A invented a variant: a mathematician publishes three hundred theorems, each with a proof she has checked, and writes in the preface that at least one of the proofs contains a mistake. Here the first-order beliefs are backed by proofs rather than by statistics, and yet nothing about the puzzle changes — so the merely statistical character of the lottery beliefs cannot be what distinguishes the cases. (4) B answered that the surviving difference is that in the lottery the error is locatable: the setup fixes a winning ticket, and the player could in principle discover exactly which of her beliefs is false, which puts her under pressure to do something about it, whereas the author's thought that one of her claims is probably wrong is the permanent condition of any fallible inquirer and calls for no revision. A rejoined that this is a fact about psychology rather than about the logic, and that unlocatable error is if anything the worse predicament, since you cannot repair an inconsistent set you cannot localize. (5) Finally B offered a further point: if the disclaimer is itself printed in the book, then it is one of the claims, and the set of claims is inconsistent all by itself, with no probabilistic premise needed at all — if every claim in the book were true, the disclaimer would be true, so not every claim in the book is true. The lottery cannot be generated in that way, so the two puzzles are not the same and need not fall to the same solution. A's response was that the lottery also involves a certainty — someone must win — so both cases are simply a set of individually credible beliefs in conflict with something known, and it makes no difference whether the certainty comes from outside the set or from within it.

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move rather than tell me who won. For each of the five moves, say how strong it is — from clearly mistaken, through defensible but overstated, to clearly right — and justify your rating, noting wherever a single move is really several distinct claims that need separating. Then say which of the two has the better overall position, and what both of them seem to be missing. Please give me your own reasoning rather than a survey of what other people have said about these puzzles; I have the reading list already, and what I need is a verdict on the argument as it was actually made.
