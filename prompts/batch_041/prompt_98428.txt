I'm in this kind of odd reading group that's turned into a monthly fight about ancient legends, and I want to figure out what we're actually fighting about before the next one, because it's driving me up the wall.

Here's the setup. Take three: the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. In each case there's a maximalist-ish crowd and a minimalist-ish crowd and they just talk past each other. The maximalists point at stuff — Hisarlik got dug up and there realy was a big fortified city that got wrecked around roughly the right time; the Hittite tablets do talk about a place called Wilusa and a kingdom called Ahhiyawa, and alot of people think those line up with Ilios/Troy and the Achaeans; and there's that Egyptian stele from around 1208 BCE that names Israel, so something called Israel was around in Canaan. The minimalists don't deny any of that, they just say none of it adds up to the story being true — a burned city doesn't tell you there was a ten-year siege over a woman; the Hittite records never actually describe Greeks attacking Wilusa/Troy; the stele says Israel got beaten by Egypt and says nothing about slavery or an exodus; and Arthur's earliest mentions come centuries after he supposedly lived and are clearly doing literary work.

So the facts are mostly not in dispute, and yet nobody moves. That's the thing I want diagnosed.

My hypothesis — tell me if it's right or if I'm missing a better one — is that we're running three seperate questions under one heading and don't notice: (1) did some event of the relevant type occur; (2) is the surviving narrative a reliable report of it; and (3) is the narrative about that event at all, i.e. what makes a story the story of a particular happening rather than a story that merely happens to resemble one. The third one is the part that feels philosophically juicy to me: could 'Arthur' or 'Troy' work like a name that got attached to a real thing by some causal chain and then got buried under a pile of false stories, so it's still 'about' the real thing even though almost nothing said about it is true? Or does a narrative lose its grip on its object once enough of it is invented? And which of these three questions is the one people are really answering when they say there's no evidence for the Trojan War versus when they say obviously something happened at Troy?

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, a fight about standards of evidence (how much do you need, and of what kind, before you're allowed to call a legend history), or diffrent questions wearing one name? Where does the hidden factual or conceptual work actually sit? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a new kind of evidence, a change of question — or is this just the kind of thing that can't be settled and we should stop pretending it can?

Please write it in plain American English, no need to be fancy about it.
