Deflationism is the view that 'true' doesn't pick out a property that beliefs have in the way 'red' picks out a property that tomatoes have. Its starting point is the equivalence schema: for any proposition p, the proposition that p is true is equivalent to p. So the proposition that snow is white is true just is the proposition that snow is white, and adding 'is true' tells you nothing new about the world. On this view the word 'true' earns its keep by doing two jobs: letting us endorse a generalisation we can't state ('everything she said is true'), and letting us agree with a claim we can't repeat ('what he said is true').

Take the generalisation job. Suppose I want to agree with every sentence in a long book. I can't write them all out, but I can say 'every sentence in the book is true', which is a way of asserting the whole conjunction in one go. Ramsey thought the truth predicate was simply redundant — you can always delete it — and Horwich's minimalism says much the same: 'true' is a handy abbreviation that we could in principle do without.

The obvious objection is that the conjunction is infinite. There's no finite sentence that lists every instance, and in the blind case you don't even know what was said, so there's nothing to abbreviate. Either the deflationist admits the generalisation is just a shorthand we never actually spell out — which seems fine, like saying 'all the numbers' without listing them — or the truth predicate is doing something more than abbreviating.

I think the second option is right, and that it damages the deflationist's position. If 'true' is what lets us grasp a content we couldn't otherwise express, then it isn't a dispensable abbreviation, and the claim that truth has no nature starts to look shaky. Truth ends up looking like a genuine property of beliefs after all, or at least like a concept with its own content rather than something that dissolves into the instances. So the generalisation problem is a real problem for deflationism.
