Ok so theres this old project by Hofstadter called Letter Spirit, where they tried to get a program to design whole alphabets -- "gridfonts" -- in which every letter is unmistakably itself (a recongizable a, b, c...) while all 26 share one coherent style, the way a real typeface does. The idea underneath it is that each letter has some kind of abstract essence, the "a-ness" of a, that all good a's participate in, and that a style is a way of expressing that essence. I want your actual credence in the strong version of that claim: that for any shape a competent reader instantly reads as an 'a', there is a single, style-independent abstract structure that all such shapes share -- not a loose family resemblance, an actual shared essence that explains why they're all a's.

Give me a number and break it down, dont just tell me it depends on what I mean by essence -- pick a reading, say which, and commit. Break it into sub-questions with a rough probability for each: how much rides on whether letter identity is really defined by a functional/perceptual core (has to be legible, has to stay distinguishable from the other 25 letters) rather than by any shared internal structure; how much on whether the space of possible letterforms is continuous enough that there are genuine borderline a's (something halfway between an a and a d, or an a and an o); how much on whether "essence" quietly collapses into something trivial like "the prototype" or "the average of the category"; how much on whether identity and style can even be pulled apart, or whether the essence only exists relative to a style at all. Say how those terms combine, then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would actually move it.
