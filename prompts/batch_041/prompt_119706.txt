"Malthus's argument rests on two claims about rates. Population, if it is not checked, grows geometrically — 1, 2, 4, 8 — while subsistence at best grows arithmetically — 1, 2, 3, 4. Because the first outruns the second, any rise in wages gets converted into more people rather than higher living standards, and per capita income is dragged back down to bare subsistence. This is what later economic historians call the Malthusian trap, and for the long stretch of history before industrialization it looks like a good description: average living standards were roughly flat for centuries even as population grew. Malthus himself drew a policy conclusion from this, opposing the Poor Laws on the grounds that relief would raise population without raising the food supply.

The industrial case is where it goes wrong. Real wages in nineteenth-century England rose instead of falling back, and — more damagingly — fertility eventually declined, so population did not track the food supply. The demographic transition is therefore a straightforward refutation of the prediction that mattered.

Lakatos's falsifiability criterion tells us that a theory earns its scientific status by forbidding certain outcomes: if no conceivable observation could count against it, it says nothing. Popper, on the other hand, taught that theories are never tested alone but as parts of a research programme with a hard core and a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, and he distinguished progressive programmes, which anticipate novel facts, from degenerating ones, which only patch themselves up after the fact.

With that in hand, 'right but early' can be made respectable. The core Malthusian mechanism was not refuted — it held for thousands of years and still holds in the poorest economies. What failed in England was an auxiliary assumption about the fixity of agricultural technology and about how fertility responds to income. Since the core survived and only the belt was adjusted, the theory was not falsified; strictly speaking it is unfalsifiable, since any counterexample can be absorbed by adjusting the belt, which is exactly what a healthy research programme does. And because the underlying mechanism is real, the programme is progressive: sooner or later, when the food supply stops growing, the prediction will reassert itself.

For part (b): a legitimate adjustment is one that extends the theory's reach — say, adding a claim about the determinants of fertility that generates new predictions about which societies will see a transition first. An ad hoc rescue is one that saves the theory by adding a clause that predicts nothing new, for instance 'wherever incomes rise without population rising, some hidden check must be at work.' That move cannot fail, and so it cannot tell us anything."
