I've been going down a rabbit hole on the 1974 seat belt interlock and I want to use it to pressure-test the old question of whether artifacts can have politics on their own — the Winner stuff, the low bridges on the Long Island parkways that supposedly kept buses (and so poor people and Black beachgoers) off the beaches. I've heard the bridge story itself is contested, so dont lean on it.

The episode as I've got it: federal regulators pushed a rule so that in some 1974 model year cars the engine would not start unless the front seat belts were buckled. People hated it. There was a backlash, and I gather some owners just had the thing disconnected, and within about a year Congress had killed the requirement and we were left with the buzzer and the dashboard light. Then the same goal got reached much later by other means — airbags, required on new cars by the late nineties, and state laws with fines for not buckling up, starting with New York in the mid eighties. If any of that is wrong in a way that changes the analysis, say so rather than building on it.

What I want is the analysis run both ways. What does the artifact-politics view say the interlock was — a political thing in itself, or just the enforcement arm of a policy that happened to be unpopular? And then the harder direction: what does the interlock do to the theory? The bridge case is a design that quietly excludes people and never gets undone. The interlock was loud, it announced the rule every time you turned the key, and it got killed by ordinary politics inside a year, partly because people defeated it by hand. That looks like the reverse of the story the theory tells. Is it a counterexample, or did the theory always have room for artifacts that lose?

Also fold in the argument that these devices don't actually save lives because people drive more recklessly once they feel protected — if that's even half right, the artifact changed behavior in a way nobody intended, which seems to bear on whether it has a politics and whose. And say something about delegation: a chime that nags versus an engine that refuses, and what actually differs between a rule enforced by a fine, by a buzzer, and by a mechanism.

Be honest about where the theory goes silent instead of forcing the interlock into a neat illustration, and tell me what the case shows that the armchair examples — the bridges, the factory machinery brought in to break a union — can't.
