I'm a reporter who writes long features on politics and ideas, and I'm trying to work up a piece on "performance legitimacy" -- the claim that a government earns the right to rule, or at least the loyalty of its people, by delivering prosperity, order and competent administration, with China and Singapore trotted out as the test cases. My problem is this: every time I read into it, it feels like two different claims get braided together and nobody bothers to say which is which. One is normative -- delivery justifies the rule. The other is causal -- delivery is why people actually comply. And the causal story seems to have a measurement problem I never see anyone stare at directly: if a regime controls the information citizens would need to check the delivery, how would you ever tell performance legitimacy apart from plain coercion? Then there's a transition problem too -- what happens to a legitimacy built on results when the results stall, or when the guy who delivered them dies or steps down? Every strongman eventually leaves.

So here's what I need. My editor doesn't want a survey, she wants an agenda I can pitch. Suppose there were five or six sharp people -- political theorists and social scientists, no field access, three years -- and I could tell them what to spend the time on. Which open questions about performance legitimacy are actually worth that, and in what order? Rank them and defend the ranking: which ones are upstream of the others, which are tractable for people working on concepts and evidence rather than doing interviews in Shanghai, and which ones I should stop treating as live because the answer wouldn't change much downstream. I also want the oversold implications called out -- I keep seeing people draw enormous conclusions for how democracies ought to deal with these regimes, or for "authoritarian resilience," and I suspect half of them dont follow from anything. And tell me what evidence over the next couple of years would move a question up or down your list.

Keep it readable, I'll be working off this on deadline. Finish with a last line that starts "Verdict:" and names the one question you'd put first, in a short phrase.
