I run the serious incident review process at a mid-sized hospital. Every case that meets the threshold gets a panel of three — me, a clinician from a different specialty than the one involved, and a senior ward nurse — and we have to file within 30 working days. The report goes to the board, gets read out to the family, and in the death cases may end up in front of the coroner. The part that eats all our time is omissions. Nobody pushed the drug, nobody called the doctor, nobody checked the interaction, nobody re-read the obs chart at 4am. And we keep having the same argument in different costumes about whether the thing nobody did actually caused the harm, or whether it's just where the story happens to be sad.

So: write me a decision procedure the panel can actually run, in order, on the question of whether an omission caused the harm in a case. It has to be runnable by three tired clinicians who don't know any philosophy and have about ninety minutes. Questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns a no, and where it stops and hands the question to a person because it's turned into a values question rather than a factual one.

Some things I want built into it, because they're where I think we go wrong:

The counterfactual is always underspecified. We say "if she'd escalated" and then everyone nods, but nobody says escalated to whom, how fast, and what that person would then have done. I want the procedure to force the alternative to be written out as a concrete scenario before anyone is allowed to vote on whether the omission made a difference, and to say what to do when the panel can't agree on what the alternative scenario even is.

For omissions I don't think you can cleanly separate "did it make a difference" from "was it a failure", the way you can with a positive act. Whether a non-action even counts as a thing that was or wasn't done seems to depend on what the person's role required of them — the same silence is a failure in one nurse and not in another. I'd rather the procedure be upfront about that entanglement than pretend the causal question is neutral and the duty question comes later.

Keep three things apart that our reports constantly mash together: whether the omission made a difference to this outcome; whether it's the kind of thing that generally makes outcomes worse (so it belongs in the report as a lesson even if it didn't change this one); and whether it was a breach of duty. I want the procedure to say which of the three each step is actually testing.

The level of description problem. "She didn't escalate", "she didn't recognise the trend", and "she wasn't trained to recognise the trend" are three different omissions with three different answers, and panels slide between them depending on how the room feels. I want a rule for fixing the level of description before the test runs.

Run it on these:

1. A patient's early warning score climbed over six hours on nights and nobody escalated to the on-call doctor until the morning round. He arrested at 9am and died. Half the panel is certain the delay killed him. But the reviewing clinician thinks he had a ruptured aneurysm that was probably unsurvivable once the pain started, so the world where the call happens at 2am is a world where he gets reviewed, maybe scanned, possibly taken to theatre — and quite likely dies anyway, or dies on the table. I want to see what the procedure does here, including if the answer is that the delay isn't a cause and must not be listed as one. That answer will go down badly and I still want it if that's what the procedure gives.

2. Two failures, each of which on its own would have been enough to prevent the harm. The pharmacist didn't flag the contraindicated dose; the prescriber didn't check the interaction though he normally always does. Undo either one and the patient is fine. The harm needs both. The but-for question gives a no for each of them separately, and we always end up writing "both contributed" and moving on. I want something better than that.

3. The omission is by someone with no relevant duty: an agency nurse on her first shift, not rostered to that patient, no induction on our escalation policy, who saw the obs but said nothing. Does the procedure even get to ask whether she made a difference, or does it stop earlier?

4. A quick one on the description problem — give me the same case run at two different levels of description and show me the answers coming apart.

Then tell me what the procedure can't do: the questions it will keep punting to a human, which of those are genuinely value judgements versus just under-evidenced factual ones, and who should be in the room when we punt.
