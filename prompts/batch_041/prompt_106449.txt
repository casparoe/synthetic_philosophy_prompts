I teach a graduate seminar on convention, coordination, and the normative, and I keep running into the same pedagogical obstacle. In the first weeks we work through the idea that a great many of the rules we live by are arbitrary solutions to coordination problems — which side of the road to drive on, whether a queue is strictly first-come-first-served or a looser jostle, whether one greets strangers — and the students are comfortable there. In the last weeks we turn to the harder question of whether such arbitrary rules can generate genuine moral obligations rather than mere expectations, and whether a visitor to a place is bound by its conventions or only by whatever morality independently requires. My students collapse two questions I want held apart: how strongly a convention binds an insider, and whether it binds an outsider at all. They also treat every breach as either a grave wrong or as harmless rudeness, with nothing in between, and they assume that if a rule is arbitrary in origin it cannot be binding in force. I would like a disciplined ranking to use as a teaching scaffold, one that forces them to see a spectrum rather than a binary.

Here is the set I want ranked. For each, I care about the strength of the obligation on someone who lives there, and separately about whether a short-term visitor is bound in the same way, in a weaker way, or not at all. (1) Leaving roughly eighteen percent at a table-service restaurant in a country where tipped staff are assumed to be underpaid by their employer. (2) On a moving escalator in a city whose custom is to stand on the right and walk on the left, standing still on the left. (3) Crossing against a red pedestrian signal on an empty street at two in the morning with no traffic in sight. (4) Holding one's place in a queue by asking a friend already near the front to save the spot while one fetches something. (5) Playing audio aloud from a phone, without headphones, on a crowded commuter train. (6) A purely local rule at a town's public spring: users fill their containers in order of arrival, but anyone filling a single drinking cup is waved to the front immediately, ahead of everyone. (7) The silence convention — not addressing strangers in a lift or on a subway even when standing inches apart — set against the opposite custom of greeting every passerby on a rural hiking trail. (8) Not taking the last portion from a shared plate without first offering it around the table. I deliberately include one rule (the spring) that no one in the seminar will have heard of, so that it must be reasoned about from the description rather than recalled.

Please sort all eight into tiers by how binding they are on an insider, and then give the separate visitor verdict for each. Lead with the ranking, then the per-item detail, in a single JSON object with the following shape: a field "scale" giving your five-point scale as an array of objects, each with "value" (0 through 4) and "label" (a short phrase such as "no obligation" up to "firm obligation"); a field "tiers" as an array of objects, each with "tier" (1 for the most binding group, ascending), "label" (a short name for the group), and "members" (the item numbers in that tier); a field "items" as an array of objects, one per convention, each with "id" (the number), "convention" (a few words), "insider_score" (0–4 on your scale), "visitor_verdict" (one of "bound as an insider", "bound but weaker", "not bound"), "mechanism" (one of "coordination", "harm avoidance", "fairness or reciprocity", "diffuse social expectation", "arbitrary etiquette"), "rationale" (at most two sentences), and "confidence" ("low", "medium", or "high"); and finally a field "hardest_case" giving the id of the item you found hardest to place, with a one-sentence explanation. Where the insider and visitor verdicts come apart, make the rationale say why.
