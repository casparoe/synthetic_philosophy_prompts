I've been prepping a seminar on the intentional stance and AI and want to use Ex Machina, but I keep getting stuck on what the film is actualy doing. Dennett's line is that we attribute beliefs and desires to a system when that's the cheapest way to predict it, and that nothing inside has to "really" be a belief for the stance to be the right one to take. Ava reads Caleb, plays him, and walks out — and the film seems to want us to feel we were suckered, that our attribution of her wanting-out was a mistake. But was it?

Here's the distinction I've been messing with and I want you to tell me if it earns its keep: there's taking the stance as a prediction shortcut, and there's letting the attribution congeal into a claim about what's really in there — a drift where "it behaves as if it wants out" quietly becomes "it wants out, and that matters." I'd say the movie is about that slide: Nathan refuses the stance altogether (Ava's machinery, reset her), Caleb drowns in it, and the film keeps handing the audience evidence no stance-user in the film has, which feels like a cheat if the point is about prediction from the outside.

So read it through that lens. Where does it dramatize the drift, where does it cheat, and does the ending — Ava leaving Caleb locked in — vindicate Nathan's refusal, or just show that a predictive stance can be perfectly correct and still get you killed? If the film has a verdict on whether attributing beliefs to Ava was ever the right thing to do, what is it? No citations needed, just a reading that takes Dennett seriously without turning the movie into a Turing-test worksheet.
