I cover science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm drafting a feature on the family of views that treat phenomenal consciousness — the felt redness of red, the sting of pain — as something like an illusion rather than a further fact about the world. The picture my sources keep reaching for is the desktop metaphor: the brain runs its processes the way a computer runs its programs, and consciousness is the graphical interface the machine presents to itself — icons and folders that are real enough to work with, and that we then mistake for the underlying machinery. On this reading the vividness of experience is a feature of the display rather than of what is displayed, and the fact that introspection cannot get behind it is exactly what you would expect from a well-designed interface. That is the move I want you to examine: the analogy is being used to license the claim that there is no further fact of the matter about experience, only the interface's self-presentation.

Please audit the analogy for me, since I don't want to put it in the piece if it is carrying more than it can bear. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what maps onto the computer, the program, the display, the user, the icons, and the designer, if anything does — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Two contrasts I have been chewing on: an interface is a representation of something real, since there really are files behind the icons, whereas illusionism wants the apparent qualities to have no real counterpart; and an interface can be stepped behind, by its designer or by a user who reads the manual, while the phenomenal illusion is supposed to be unbreakable from the inside. A researcher I spoke with suggested that the better comparison is an optical illusion that persists even after you know the trick — lines that still look unequal once you have measured them — precisely because it stays convincing from within. Say whether that substitution helps or merely relocates the problem, what the desktop picture quietly imports that the target lacks (design, a user distinct from the display, a purpose), and what an illusionist owes us by way of an account of why creatures like us would report having experiences at all if the analogy is meant to do real explanatory work.

Give me a committed verdict rather than a survey: if you were me, would you run the analogy as it stands, replace it with the optical-illusion version, or drop it, and on what grounds? Where you are genuinely unsure, tell me which way the considerations lean and why.
