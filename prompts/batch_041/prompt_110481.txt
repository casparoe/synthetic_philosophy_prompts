Can you walk me through what Parfit's hitchhiker case is actualy meant to establish? The setup I have in mind: you're stranded somewhere, a stranger offers you a lift on the condition that you'll send him money later, and once you're dropped off he has no way to make you pay. The self-interest line says don't pay -- it costs you and buys you nothing. But it's supposed to be in your interest to be the sort of person who does pay, since that's how you get lifts, so rationality ends up recommending a disposition that makes you act against your own interest in the particular case. Fine, thats the "rational irrationality" bit. What I want is the stuff underneath it: is that really irrationality, or is it just prudence once you count the trust of future strangers? And if you stipulate that it's genuinely one-shot -- no future drivers, nobody hears about it, no guilt or hit to your self-image -- then what reason does a rational agent have to have the disposition in the first place, never mind to actually pay up? I'd like you to say where the act/disposition distinction is doing real work here and where it's just relabeling the same problem, and to be honest about which version of the case you need for the conclusion to go through at all.
