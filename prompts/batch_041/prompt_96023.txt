Next month I'm presenting a paper on Buridan's ass at a joint session of our economics department's decision-theory workshop and the philosophy department's action-theory reading group, and what I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper. My thesis is that the ass has been misdescribed for as long as the puzzle has existed. The literature runs together two states that are actually quite different — equal valuation (a complete preference ranking with a tie at the top) and incomparability (no ranking between the two options at all) — and then treats the first as the interesting case, which is why everyone reaches for tie-breaking rules, randomization, or arbitrary choice. I argue the Buridan case is the second state; that a rational agent under incomparability is required only to pick a maximal option rather than an optimal one; and that the genuine puzzle is not why the ass fails to choose but why we ever thought reason demands a unique best. My positive proposal is that the tie, where it is real, gets broken not at the level of the act but at the level of the policy — a standing disposition to resolve underdetermination the same way each time — and that this is what an agent with an incomplete order is actually committed to. I've spent years in the online rationality forums and I know exactly how this plays there: "just pick one and move on." That answer will die in this room, and I want to know why before it does.

The room is small and specific. Three microeconomists who work in revealed preference: for them "incomplete preference" is either a modeling convenience or a confession that you haven't found the preference yet, and their reflex is to ask what my distinction predicts and whether it is testable at all. Two philosophers who work on incommensurability and parity: they will accept my distinction within the first minute and then say I've renamed their problem without solving it. One decision theorist who has read every symmetry-breaking and tie-breaking proposal ever made and will go straight for the regress — if the policy breaks the tie, what breaks the tie between policies. One cognitive scientist who studies hesitation and will want to know what any of this says about why people actually dither in front of a menu. Nobody there works on the medieval sources, so spend nothing on the historical Buridan.

Give me the objections I'll actually get in that room — eight or so — sequenced by how likely each is weighted by how much damage it would do, phrased in the voice of the person who will raise it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should simply concede and fold into the talk, and which single one I should be praying nobody raises, with what to say if someone does. The concession column matters as much as the reply column; I would rather give ground on framing than be caught defending something indefensible.

Be committed. Tell me whether this thesis is defensible against this specific audience or whether I should reframe before I walk in — do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me. And open with your verdict in a single sentence: does it survive, and which objection is the one that could sink it. Then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back to me.
