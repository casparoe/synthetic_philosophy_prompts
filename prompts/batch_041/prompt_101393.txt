I am a novelist, and I have come to suspect that the hypothetical scenario is the most persuasive instrument in philosophy and the least scrutinised, and since I make my living with the same instrument, I would like to know what separates an honest one from a rigged one. What follows is a set of my own reactions, recorded as gut verdicts, to scenarios of the sort philosophers use. Tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

First, the familiar one: a runaway trolley will kill five people unless you turn it onto a track where it kills one. I regard this as an honest pump. Nothing in the description is doing work beyond marking the choice, and when I react I am reacting to the structure rather than to the staging.

Second, a variant in which the one is a child, or in which the five are my own family. I regard this as rigged. It moves me, but it moves me by attaching sentiment to one side of the ledger; strip the adjectives and my verdict reverses. The persuasion is coming from the garnish, not the dish.

Third, a scenario that stipulates that some person is wholly incapable of any attachment to her own future. I distrust it, because the conclusion rides on whether such a creature is possible at all, and the pump treats that as free.

Fourth, a scenario that stipulates that a person feels no grief at a parent's death, and asks what I would think of her. I trust this one, though it looks like the previous case: an unusual psychology has been stipulated, but one I can grant as possible, and if it turns out I would not feel as the pump needs me to, the pump simply fails and I can say so.

Fifth, in my current novel I have a scene in which a woman is made sympathetic by the presence of a dying child, and I know it works; readers forgive her the thing I need them to forgive. I think that is legitimate in a novel and would be illegitimate in a paper, and I want the principle to tell me why, or to tell me I am fooling myself.

Sixth, a pump whose staging is plainly loaded, the agent described as a monster and the victim as innocent in three separate ways, but whose conclusion I also believe on independent grounds. I find that I excuse it, which worries me.

Is there one principle that yields all six verdicts, or nearly all? Does it turn on whether the details of the scenario are relevant to the conclusion, on whether the scenario is possible, or on what the reader is being asked to supply? And what does the best candidate say about a case I have not listed: a pump whose staging is scrupulously neutral but whose intuitive verdict turns out to be an artefact of my culture or my century. Is that a rigged pump, or merely one that has stopped working? Finally, if I must surrender one of my six verdicts to keep the rest, say which and why.
