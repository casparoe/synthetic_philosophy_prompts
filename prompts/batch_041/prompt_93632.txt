I'm in a seminar on rational choice theory and we spent last week on Aumann's agreement theorem — the 1976 result, from the paper that also gave us the set-theoretic definition of common knowledge, that two agents who start from a common prior and update by conditionalizing on their private information can't have different posterior probabilities for an event once those posteriors are common knowledge. Which means a genuine, mutually known disagreement tells you one of the moving parts has failed: the priors weren't really common, or the disagreement isn't common knowledge (only finitely many levels of it), or the agents aren't updating the way the model says. Our professor keeps calling it a theorem about how cheap agreement would be if we were the creatures the model describes, and for my final paper I want to test that on 12 Angry Men — the 1957 one-room jury film, twelve men, a hot afternoon, an eighteen-year-old defendant, unanimity required, death sentence if they get it wrong. I dont want a summary of either the theorem or the movie; I want a reading of the film through the theorem that takes both seriously. So hold the film up against the preconditions one at a time. Is there a common prior at all, when one juror is a bigot, one is a man whose own son walked out on him, one grew up around switchblades in a slum, and one is an immigrant with a reverence for the whole procedure — one prior, or four priors wearing one jury? Is the information partitional and known to the agent who holds it — the opening secret ballot makes the numbers public but not the reasons, and one juror's late switch is cast so he can get to the ballgame, so what becomes common knowledge in that room isn't anybody's belief. And what do I do with the dialogue result that follows from the theorem, where agents take turns announcing their posteriors and are proved to converge (for finite cases, a few years later) — the jurors announce positions over and over, but a vote isn't a posterior, and the last few holdouts change for reasons that have nothing to do with information. There's also the relaxation where you only need common p-belief instead of common knowledge, and the disagreement you can sustain is bounded by something that shrinks as p approaches 1; I suspect that's the version the film actually illustrates, since almost nothing in that room is certain, only nearly.

The thing I keep getting stuck on is that the film's reversals don't look like updating on fixed evidence, which is the only move the model has. The duplicate switchblade, the disabled witness's limp and the time he claimed he needed to reach the door, the elevated train that both drowned out what he said he overheard and gave the woman her line of sight, the marks on her nose from the glasses she didn't wear to court, and juror 5's knowledge of how a knife like that is actually held in a fight — none of those are reweightings, they're re-descriptions of what counts as evidence, and one of them only becomes evidence when a man manages to say out loud something he knew in his body. So: does the film vindicate the theorem, the march from eleven-to-one to twelve-to-nothing as exactly the process of turning private information into common knowledge — or does it refute it, since what actually moves the room is heat, exhaustion, one man's refusal to fold, and a bigot talking until everyone turns their back on him, with unanimity arriving because the last holdout's real reason was his son and not the evidence? Which scenes would you point to for each reading, and is there one that does both? I've been going back and forth on this alot. And is the theorem even about the right object here, given that these men disagree as much about what reasonable doubt requires as about whether the boy did it, and a standard isn't an event and doesn't have a posterior. Finish with a verdict, if there is one, on whether the film shows something about agreement that the proof can't — the proof is silent about whether agents who agree are right, and the film is not. Honest in both directions, please: not the movie as an illustration of the theorem, and not the theorem bent to fit the movie. No citations needed, I have the reading list.
