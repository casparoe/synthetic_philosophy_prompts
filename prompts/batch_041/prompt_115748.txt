I teach a final-year seminar on probability and induction and I'm writing the in-class final. I want one essay question on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, but I'm bored of the version where they trot out the three ways of drawing a chord and announce that indifference is dead. So I've cooked up a variant I want the question built around.

Take a circle of radius one and inscribe a rectangle in it — any rectangle, sliver to square, nothing else fixed. I tell them a rectangle has been picked 'at random' from all the rectangles inscribable in the circle and ask for the probability its area comes out bigger than 1. Go the natural way — the rectangle is pinned down by the angle between its two diagonals, let that angle be uniform on [0, π) — and you get 2/3. But pin the rectangle down by its shorter side instead and let that be uniform, and you get about 0.63. Same object, same question, two answers. What I want them to grapple with is whether this case has a principled answer (is one parmeterisation privileged, say by the circle's rotational symmetry?), what the principle of indifference would have to look like for 2/3 to be defensable, and whether the symmetry argument actually earns its keep or just smuggles in the assumption it claims to derive.

Write the exam question itself (include the setup, and decide whether to hand them both numbers or make them find one — tell me why you chose), then a model answer of maybe 600-700 words, then a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and a B answer won't, with a one-line note for each move on how students usually fluff it.

Two things. They're writing this by hand in a timed exam with no internet, so don't ask them for names, dates or citations — the model answer can gesture at the recieved arguments without attributing them. And for the answer and the scheme, no headings, bullet points, bold or tables, just continuous prose the way you'd write me a long email. Also don't let the model answer drift into a history of the paradox; the new case is the whole point.
