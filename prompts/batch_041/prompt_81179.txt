I am applying for a one-year fellowship at an interdisciplinary centre for philosophy and the cognitive sciences, and I need a two-page research proposal. The selection committee is half philosophers and half clinicians; they have said they will fund conceptual work, but that they want to be convinced it could not be replaced by a literature review, so the proposal has to be legible to people who do not share my vocabulary.

Here is my half-formed idea. The clinical cases in which pain and its unpleasantness come apart are almost always put to a single use in the philosophical literature: to show that the badness of pain is separable from its sensory character. Asymbolia is the standard exhibit — patients who report that a stimulus hurts, who can locate and rate it, and who are untroubled by it, sometimes to the point of amusement; the reverse dissociation has also been reported, in a patient with a postcentral lesion who appeared to retain the affective dimension while losing the sensory-discriminative one, and there are the familiar phenomena of hypnotic and pharmacological analgesia. I think the standard use is the least interesting one available. What these cases actually give us is a set of dissociations among things philosophers tend to run together: the sensation itself; its location and intensity; its unpleasantness; withdrawal and protective behaviour; the subject's judgement that something bad is happening; and the subject's caring whether it continues. The competing accounts of why pain is bad — the ones that locate the badness in a distinctive phenomenal quality, in an imperative or command built into the content of the experience, in an evaluation the experience represents, or in the subject's desires and aversions — differ, I suspect, not in what they say about any one case but in which patterns of preservation and loss they permit. As far as I can tell, nobody has worked out those patterns and then asked whether the clinical record actually contains them. There is a further wrinkle: the standard clinical definition of pain builds unpleasantness into the definition, which makes the asymbolia cases look, definitionally, as though they were never cases of pain at all — and I want to know whether that is a real problem or a bookkeeping one.

So the project I want to propose is this: derive, for each of the main accounts of pain's badness, its dissociation signature — the combinations of preserved and lost elements that the account rules out and the ones it predicts — and then check those signatures against the dissociations that have actually been reported. The question should be stated so that it has a determinate answer: which accounts survive contact with the full pattern, and does any of them predict a dissociation that has never been observed, or, worse, exclude one that has? I also want to know whether the pattern is rich enough to distinguish the accounts at all, since if it is not, I want to be able to say so, and to say what the accounts are then really disagreeing about.

Please write the proposal. It needs: a precise statement of the question; why the existing positions leave it open — not that the field is divided, but what the standard moves are and where they leave a gap; the method, with a realistic month-by-month plan for a year; an argument for why this is philosophical work rather than a job for more data; what a good result would look like and what would count as the project failing, including the outcome in which the whole thing turns out to be a verbal dispute; and an honest statement of scope, distinguishing the part that is doable in a year from the part that is not. Don't worry about citations; I will add those. And please do not let the proposal presuppose that asymbolia settles anything — the point is that it might not.
