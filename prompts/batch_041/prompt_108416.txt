Six months ago I stopped flying and stopped eating meat. Since then the same exchange has found me roughly weekly — someone agrees with everything I say about the harm and then adds, kindly, that my own contribution is a drop in the ocean — and at Christmas a relative put it to me as though it were decisive: that individual action on climate is not merely futile but a category mistake. I have now been asked to run a seventy-five-minute session on the question at our local climate group's monthly evening. About twenty people will come; none of them is a philosopher, and several are already persuaded that the objection settles the matter. I do not want the argument rehearsed for me. I want a plan for teaching it.

Give me the order in which the components must arrive, and why each depends on the one before it. I have come to think there are two quite different objections travelling under one name — an arithmetic one (my share of the harm is one part in many millions, hence negligible) and a modal one (whether I act or not, the outcome is identical) — and that the familiar replies tend to hit one and miss the other. If that distinction is worth building the session on, build it on that, and tell me exactly where in the sequence it has to be introduced if the room is not to hear it as a technicality.

Then tell me what single case should carry the whole thing. I keep oscillating between a threshold case (a bus route that only runs once enough people commit to using it) and an accumulation case (a beach gradually stripped because every visitor takes one pebble), and I want to know which one does the work, what it must have and lack, and what to say about the ones I set aside — including voting and the many torturers each turning the dial a fraction, which I suspect this audience has already heard and discounted.

Set out the misconceptions you expect in the room, in the order they will surface, marking the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I am already fairly sure that someone will conclude that if the responsibility is collective then it is nobody's, and someone else that talk of complicity is just consequentialism in a better suit. Finish with one question to put at the end that will show whether they have grasped the shape of the problem rather than merely been made uncomfortable, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover in seventy-five minutes. Please write the whole answer in British English.
