I'm a philosophy PhD student putting together a short-story unit for an undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of history, and I need the centerpiece story. Write one set in a mid-sized city preparing to mark the centennial of a founding event it now finds embarrassing: a land grab that was publicly dressed up as a gift, which the city has spent a century half-celebrating and half-forgetting. Three of the people on the centennial committee each unknowingly live out one of Nietzsche's three ways of relating to the past. One wants the founders' nerve to serve as a usable model in a fight the city is having right now. One wants every ledger, fence-post, and contradictory anecdote of the founding preserved, catalogued, and displayed exactly as it was. One wants the centennial to be an act of repudiation, in which the city stands in judgment over its founders and cuts itself loose from them. Do not name these modes, do not mention Nietzsche, and do not let any character or narrator explain the theory. Give each of the three their strongest case before you let their characteristic failure show: the hero-worshipper whose usable past is quietly getting falsified in the telling, the antiquarian whose devotion curdles into accumulation that serves nothing living, the judge whose verdict turns out to be as absolute and unappealable as the myth it destroys. Have the story turn on one concrete decision the committee must make together — whether to keep the founder's name on the city's main bridge, and what it costs them, in money and in loyalties, to remove it — and let the ending make visible, without anyone articulating it, a fourth relation to the past that serves the city's present life rather than idolizing, embalming, or executing it. Around 2,000 words, no afterword.
