I'm in this weird spot where I have to write a short paper for a grad seminar on collective epistemolgy, and I've basically got it drafted but I'm not confident in it. The topic is information cascades and rational herding. My draft goes roughly like this.

Thesis: when agents update on each other's actions in sequence, a run of a few public choices can push everyone into a "cascade" where each person rationally ignores their own private evidence and just copies the crowd. The punchline I want is that this isn't irrationality — every step is a correct Bayesian update on what's observable — so you can't fix it by telling people to think harder or be more independent-minded; the failure is structural and lives in the order and observability of decisions, not in anybody's head. Then I have four claims riding on that. (1) The classic illustration is the urn: everyone knows it's majority red or majority blue, each person draws a ball privately and then announces a guess in sequence. Once the first two announce red, the third should announce red even if they drew blue, because two reds outweigh one blue, and after that everyone downstream is in the same spot — their own draw is drowned out and they just follow. (2) Once you're in a cascade it's basically locked in; the crowd keeps confirming itself, and the only thing that breaks it is a run of contradictory public signals big enough to overwhelm the accumulated weight, which in practice means it almost never happens. (3) So the wisdom-of-crowds result — lots of independent guesses averaging toward the truth — is really a statement about independence, and cascades are exactly the mechanism by which independence gets destroyed. (4) Practical upshot: for things like peer review, prediction markets, or a whole field converging on a paradigm, we should worry less about individual bias and more about whether the sequence of decisions lets later people see earlier people's choices before they commit.

I think it's decent, but I have a nagging feeling I'm mis-describing something, especially claim 2, and that I might be quietly sliding between two different phenomena that both get called herding. Can you go through it and tell me where it's accurate, where I've got the mechanism wrong or oversimplified, and whether the thesis survives the corrections? If any of the four claims is doing more work than it can actually support, say so — I'd rather cut a claim myself than have a referee gut it. And if there's a cleaner way to frame the whole thing that keeps the punchline intact, I'd take that too.
