I chair the bargaining committee of a manufacturing local of about 1,400 members, and in six weeks we vote on how to answer our employer's plan to automate the assembly and inspection lines. The new cells would raise throughput by roughly a third, eliminate around 30 percent of the current jobs, and convert most of those that survive from skilled setup and fault-diagnosis into machine-tending. Management presents the change as unavoidable modernization and offers a buyout plus a modest retraining fund; it warns that refusal means the work moves to another site. The committee is split. One faction wants to oppose the installation outright, holding that cooperation would betray members whose lives were built on these jobs. The other wants to accept the technology and bargain hard for a shorter week, a share of the productivity gain, and retraining, arguing that the machines are not themselves the enemy and that reduced labor time is the real prize. A third member keeps invoking the old assurance that displaced workers are eventually reabsorbed, while another says that assurance was discredited long ago and the record is at best mixed.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. Recommend a concrete course -- reject, accept, or accept subject to enumerated conditions -- with a time frame attached to each demand, and supply reasons the committee can actually defend on the floor rather than a compromise that offends no one. Engage seriously the thought that a machine's character depends on who owns it and what it is used to extract, without collapsing into the view that resisting a particular installation is the same as resisting the shortening of the working day. State what the displaced members are owed, and why, and give the strongest case for the faction you rule against its due weight.
