A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Gottlob Frege's political diary — the notebook he kept in 1924, with its antisemitic and anti-democratic outbursts — and whether it has any bearing on his philosophy. I found myself defending a line I take to be common among Frege scholars: the diary is a private document, the residue of a bitter and disappointed old man, and it does not touch the logical and philosophical work, because the validity of a proof and the truth of a claim about sense and reference do not depend on the moral character of the person who produced them. My friend called that evasion, and I have not been able to stop turning it over. I want to know what would actually count against my position — not what would embarrass me, but what ought to move me.

Take the role of a philosopher of logic with a strong grounding in the history of analytic philosophy and in the methodology of the history of philosophy. Think carefully, and brainstorm privately before you commit, about the difference between an ad hominem irrelevance and a genuine interpretive relevance: when, if ever, does an author's life bear on the content of their work, and is there anything in Frege's case that connects the diary to his actual doctrines rather than merely to his character? The response must consist of exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion — each one or two sentences long.

Items 1 through 3 should be the considerations that count against my position, ranked from most to least decisive, including anything already on the record that I ought to be weighing. Item 4 should say which of these a defender of my line would concede and which they would dismiss as beside the point. Item 5 should say whether my position risks anything at all or has quietly been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind — and should name the single consideration that would move you most personally.
