Last month I was present, purely as a bystander, at an argument between two colleagues, Dana and Ravi, that ran well past the end of the session and that I have not been able to settle in my own mind. I am not asking you to tell me which of them to side with; I am asking you to go through the exchange as it actually went and say what is right and what is wrong at each stage. Neither of them, incidentally, knew which way I was leaning, because I did not lean.

The case they were arguing about. Outcome S: one person endures a year of the most intense suffering a human being can survive. Outcome D: a very large number of people — take 10^30, or any larger finite figure — each endure one brief episode of mild discomfort, say a dull ache in one finger lasting about two seconds, at an intensity nobody would rearrange a morning over. Dana held that for some finite N, D with N people in it is worse than S. Ravi held that S is worse than D however large N becomes.

1. Dana opened by saying that if no finite number of finger-aches outweighs a year of agony, then a finger-ache counts as nothing at all against agony: it is ranked below every amount of the agony-kind of badness, however small. But a finger-ache is a real harm, not a zero, and a real harm repeated often enough must eventually tell against a larger one. Without exchange rates of that sort, she said, we could never say when it is justified to impose small harms on many people in order to spare one person a severe harm, and that question comes up constantly.

2. Ravi replied that he does not call the ache nothing; he denies that quantities of one kind add up into another kind. Counting finger-aches gets you more finger-ache and never agony, in the way that adding points to a line never yields a plane. A year of agony is not an enormous number of finger-aches but a different order of thing, and its being worse than any number of them does not require it to be infinitely worse in any numerical sense.

3. Dana pressed him. If the agony-kind outranks the ache-kind in that way, then a one-second twinge of the agony-kind — a pain far milder than the year-long torture, but still of the higher kind — outweighs any number of finger-aches, 10^30 of them included. And the line between the kinds has to fall somewhere on what looks like a smooth continuum of intensity, so two pains a hair apart in severity will differ in weight by more than 10^30 to one. Worse, she said, an ordering with a step like that cannot be represented on any numerical scale at all, so on his view there is no answer to how much worse one outcome is than another and no way to compare two policies that trade harms of different kinds.

4. Ravi granted that his threshold is vague, but denied that vagueness is arbitrariness: we cannot say precisely when a man becomes bald, yet not every man is bald, and the vagueness of the predicate does not empty it. He located his threshold at a qualitative break — suffering that takes over a life, that destroys the sufferer's agency and closes off everything else, as against suffering that does not — and said that a hair's difference in intensity near that break is a difference in what the pain does to a life, not merely in how it feels. He added that the moral unit is a life and not a quantum of feeling: 10^30 lives each slightly worse off do not add up to one life destroyed, and the reason the year of agony matters is that it is one person's life being ruined, which is not the sort of thing that can be assembled out of parts distributed across persons.

5. Dana then offered a case meant to test that. Outcome S again: one person certainly endures the year of agony. Outcome R: each of 10^30 people independently faces a one-in-10^30 chance of enduring it. In R nobody's life is ruined with any appreciable probability, since every individual is overwhelmingly safe; yet the probability that at least one person endures the year is about 63 percent, and who it is will be settled by lottery. By Ravi's own test, she said, R must be better than S, because R is the outcome in which no one's life is destroyed with anything approaching certainty. But R does not prevent the agony, it redistributes it, and it lays a small risk on everyone. She asked whether he would accept that R is better than S.

6. Ravi said R is better ex ante and worse ex post, and that if he must take a stance he will take the ex ante one: under R no one can complain of having been singled out, each person's risk is negligible, and no life is ruined by design, whereas under S one person is destroyed. He added that Dana's view fares no better in this case. Since S and R have the same expected number of victims, she must either call them equally bad, which seems plainly wrong given how different they are, or help herself to the ex ante stance that she was just objecting to. He closed by asking why the mere size of the population should change the moral weight of a single event.

They broke up without conceding anything. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken, through defensible but incomplete, to clearly right, and say briefly what is right or wrong in each. Then identify the deepest point of disagreement between them — the thing that, if it were settled, would settle most of the rest — and say what each of them is missing that neither noticed. If either of them has to give ground on some particular move, say which and why. And if the exchange really does favor one side overall, say so and say on what grounds.
