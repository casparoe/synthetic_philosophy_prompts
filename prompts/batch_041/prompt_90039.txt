I'm planning a 90-minute session on the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument — Lucas's and Penrose's versions of it — for a mixed group: six philosophy graduate students whose last logic course was two years ago, plus two computer science students who know the incompleteness theorems but no philosophy of mind. I don't want the explanation itself; I want the teaching plan.

Lay out the order in which the components have to arrive so that each is earned rather than asserted: what a formal system is, and what "the mind is a machine" has to mean before the theorems can be aimed at it at all; why the move from "the system doesn't prove its Gödel sentence" to "that sentence is true" needs soundness and not merely consistency; what the second theorem does and does not settle about a machine knowing its own consistency, and what kinds of machine it leaves untouched; and only then the premise the whole argument actually rests on — that the mind can recognize the soundness of any formal system it can grasp. Tell me if my ordering is wrong.

For the carrying example I want a machine programmed to print "I am sound": it can say it without having earned it. Does that bear the weight of the session, or what should carry it instead — and what must the example have and not have?

Then map the misconceptions onto the sequence, saying where in the sequence each is headed off: that the theorems show truth outrunning proof in general; that any physical system is thereby a formal system; that the machine can simply be handed its consistency sentence as a fresh axiom; that the argument shows the mind is not a computer; that human fallibility settles the matter. One item is not a misconception and should be placed as the mechanist's strongest move rather than corrected — the reply that our mathematical practice is captured not by a single formal system but by an open-ended sequence of them, each extending the last. Say where it belongs and what the anti-mechanist has to answer.

End with a single question I can put to them that would show whether they can state the load-bearing premise and what it costs, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so openly — arithmetization, the diagonal lemma, Rosser's trick, the technical refinements of Penrose's later arguments, the quantum speculations.

Where the plan touches matters that are genuinely contested among people who work on this, separate what is settled from what is contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. American English.
