I do cost-effectiveness work for a public health insurer — when a drug comes up for coverage I run the numbers, and the numbers are QALYs, which means at the end of the day I'm adding up years of life across people and funding whatever comes out biggest. Some patient groups have started pushing back on this, and one of them sent a long letter I can't really argue with: they say our method treats the population like it's one person who can trade a bad year here against a good year there, when there isn't any such person — the one who eats the bad year and the one who gets the good year are different people, and the second one's gain doesn't make the first one's loss up to anybody. I basicly agree, which is a problem, because we still have to decide. I get about a week per decision, I'm not a philosopher, and whatever I produce has to go to a committee that signs off.

So write me a procedure I can actually run on a funding proposal. Questions in order, what a good enough answer to each looks like, where the procedure stops with a no, and — the part I really need — where it stops being technical and becomes a value call the committee has to own out loud. It can't just say never aggregate, because then it's useless and everyone ignores it. I'm pretty sure some kinds of adding-up are the objectionable kind and some aren't, and I can't say what the difference is. One more thing that bugs me: trading a bad year for a good year inside one life is obviously fine, that's just what treatment is, so why is it supposed to be outrageous across two lives? Either there's a real difference there or their isn't, and I'd rather the procedure take a side on that than paper over it.

The procedure should end by producing a short written justification I can send to the people who lose out, addressed to them specifically. If all I can say is that it's better on the whole, that's the very answer they're already rejecting.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a cheap drug that gives a small benefit to a huge number of people versus an expensive one that gives a big benefit to a handful; (b) closing a clinic that serves one identifiable group in order to fund something with more QALYs spread thinly across everyone else; (c) a case where the procedure tells me the thing I'm doing right now is the bad kind — don't soften it. And say plainly which steps are arithmetic, which are moral philosophy in a lab coat, and what the procedure can't settle at all.
