I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, working on the ethics of affective engineering, and I keep colliding with a summary of the hedonistic imperative that I can't quite adjudicate on my own. A friend of mine — a science journalist, sharp but with no philosophical training — has drafted an explainer for a general-audience outlet and has asked me to check it before he files. His central paragraph runs roughly as follows: the hedonistic imperative is the utilitarian proposal that we should use biotechnology — genetic engineering, designer pharmacology, direct neural stimulation — to eliminate all negative emotion and all pain from human life, replacing them with a permanent and unbounded state of bliss; its author holds that suffering is the only thing that matters morally and that pleasure is simply its mirror image and should therefore be maximized without ceiling; and since the same technology could be deployed coercively, we are obliged to re-engineer ourselves whether or not anyone consents. Critics, he continues, object that this is just wireheading writ large — a population tethered to pleasure machines with no projects, attachments, or grief — which shows the project is self-defeating rather than merely risky; and the imperative also forecasts that this transformation is inevitable, which is why it is framed as an imperative rather than a choice.

I am fairly sure at least a couple of these sentences are doing something wrong, but I can't say crisply where the line runs between a defensible popular simplification, a subtle misattribution, and a genuinely contested philosophical claim. Could you go through the paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me, for each, what a sympathetic and well-informed defender of the view would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would call simply false — flagging in particular any place where two distinctions the position is at pains to keep apart have been collapsed into one, any objection that is being presented as a refutation when it is at most a standing challenge, and any term used in its ordinary sense where the position uses it in a technical one? And when you have finished, I want to know whether the conclusion — that the project is best rejected — follows from whatever in the paragraph turns out to be accurate, or whether it survives only by leaning on the parts that do not.
