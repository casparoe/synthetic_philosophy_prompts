Write a dialogue of roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words between two legal philosophers who are intellectual equals: one a latter-day defender of the view that the criminal law may legitimately enforce a society's shared morality, the other a latter-day defender of the view that the only sound ground for criminalization is harm to others. The exchange should take place at a hearing of a fictional law-reform commission that is considering whether to criminalize a private, consensual practice that harms no third party but that a large majority of citizens find degrading. Invent the practice yourself, but make it something other than a thinly disguised version of a real contested prohibition, so that neither character can win by invoking a familiar political controversy.

Neither character should merely recite the canonical positions in the historical debate. Require them to do real philosophical work under pressure, and let each of them concede or revise something in response to the other. Build the exchange around the following materials, and make sure each is actually contested rather than resolved:

First, a stipulated forecast that the commission has commissioned: if the practice is decriminalized, public disapproval of it will fall from roughly four-fifths of the population to roughly two-fifths over thirty years, with no other measurable social change. Have the moralist argue that this forecast, if granted, strengthens the case for the ban, and have the liberal argue that even granting the forecast, it does not. Make them disagree about what follows from it rather than about whether it is accurate.

Second, a fresh argument the moralist should put forward: treat the shared morality as a common-pool resource, analogous to a shared language or a clean environment, whose value is degraded by individual defections, so that the aggregate erosion of the moral climate is itself a harm that the liberal's own principle must reckon with. The liberal must then either deny that a changed moral climate counts as a harm, or grant that it is a harm and explain why it nevertheless cannot be met with the criminal law. Let the liberal's response here be the hinge of the dialogue, and let the moralist press the strongest reply available.

Third, the question of social identity. The liberal should press that defining a society by its shared morality is circular, since we have no way to identify the society independently of the morality we are asked to protect, and should test the moralist's best non-circular answer (for instance, an analogy between morality and a constitutive practice such as a shared language). The moralist should defend the analogy rather than abandon it, and the liberal should probe where it breaks down.

Close the dialogue without a clean winner, but with the central crux made explicit: whether the moralist's common-pool argument gives the liberal a harm he must count, and whether counting it is enough to license coercion. Keep the prose self-contained and free of citation; the characters should not quote, name, or allude to specific texts, authors, or court cases. The dialogue should be readable by someone who has never encountered this debate, while giving someone who has the sense that the participants are arguing past the textbook versions of their own positions.
