For a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind I have been asked to circulate my position in advance so that the others can attack it, and I would rather find the weak joints myself. Here is the set of things I believe, stated as plainly as I can manage.

1. Causal closure. Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, and no non-physical item ever enters into the production of a physical event.

2. The status of phenomenal properties. They are real; they are not identical with any physical property; and they are caused by brain states. But they are causally inert: a quale never causes anything, not even the neural events that produce my reports about it. The connection from brain state to quale is a brute, contingent law of nature — nothing in the physical facts necessitates that a given brain state be accompanied by any phenomenal property at all.

3. Intentional content. A state represents a feature only if it is causally sensitive to that feature — that is, only if it would not occur, or would not occur in the same way, were the feature absent. A state lacking that sensitivity does not represent the feature, whatever it may feel like from the inside.

4. Introspection. My judgments about my own experience are typically true and typically justified, and I see no mystery in how I come to make them: the brain state that produces the quale also produces the judgment, so the judgment reliably covaries with the quale it reports. I take this to dispose of the familiar worry about how a causally inert property could ever be the object of a judgment.

5. The knowledge argument. Mary learns something when she first sees red. What she acquires is a fact about a phenomenal property, not merely a further fact about her brain, and her exclamation expresses a true judgment about that property.

6. Zombies. The zombie scenario is conceivable. Conceivability is not a reliable guide to possibility, so the argument from that scenario to the falsity of physicalism fails, and I am not moved by it.

7. The science of consciousness. A mature neuroscience will discover the laws linking particular brain states to particular qualia, and subjects' reports will be the primary evidence for those laws.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these items are genuinely in tension with which, and how severe is each conflict? Which pairs only look tense and can be shown to cohere? For each genuine conflict, give me the cheapest revision that removes it and state what that revision costs me elsewhere on the list. Then order the conflicts by severity and tell me which single change would leave the greatest part of the list intact. I have no antecedent preference among the seven and no idea which of them is doing the damage, so please do not weight any item by how confident I sound about it. Write in American English.
