In Norway the tax lists are public: anyone can look up a named person's income, net wealth, and tax paid for the year, and the person searched can see who has been looking at them. Set that alongside the rest of the Nordic package — marginal rates that take close to half of a high earner's income, benefits that flow to the middle class and not only the poor, and unusually high voluntary compliance — and tell me what conception of the relation between the individual and the community the whole arrangement presupposes. Does making earnings searchable presuppose that income is a public matter rather than a private one, or is transparency merely a device for sustaining trust among people who already conceive of themselves as participants in a joint enterprise? Which rival accounts of the person–community relation does the arrangement rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Is it internally consistent — can a society treat everyone's earnings as everyone's business while also insisting, as these societies do, on a strong right to privacy and personal autonomy? And if the presupposed conception is load-bearing, does that explain why the model resists export, or could the institutions be adopted on quite different commitments about the person and the community?
