I have just come from a long argument with a close friend about the meaning of success. He accused me of dressing up envy as sociology; I accused him of confusing luck with merit. The argument has sharpened a thesis I am scheduled to present next month to a regional chamber-of-commerce breakfast in a mid-sized Midwestern manufacturing town. The audience will be mostly family-business owners, a few local bankers, the mayor, and one extension economist from the state university. They are practical, proud of the town's self-made successes, and allergic to what they hear as academic resentment; a few are first-generation immigrants who will hear any critique of the American Dream as a denial of what they earned. I am a tenured professor at the state university in that town, and my salary is public information.

My thesis is that the American Dream and Jantelagen are not opposites but mirror-image permission structures for envy. Both begin from the premise that exceptional success is a problem that must be justified. The American Dream justifies it by converting success into evidence of merit and failure into desert; Jantelagen, the Scandinavian norm that one should not think oneself above the group, justifies the community's suspicion by treating exceptionality as a debt or theft. Tall poppy syndrome is the enforcement mechanism when the permission structure fails. I want to replace the usual individualism-versus-collectivism framing with a distinction between two exemptions: the merit exemption, which lets the successful stand apart because they earned it, and the humility exemption, which lets the group pull them back because no one is entitled to stand apart. On this view, the real question is not whether we should celebrate or cut down the successful, but who is granted an exemption and at whose expense. I call this the permission-structure thesis.

Prepare me for the question period: the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones I would get from a graduate seminar, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this audience would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Make sure the objections engage the two-exemption distinction, especially the charge that the humility exemption is just envy with a good conscience. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. I will have no notes or sources in front of me, so keep the replies speakable.
