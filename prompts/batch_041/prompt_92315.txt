I'm marking the midterms for a 300-level seminar I run on perception and the senses, and there's one short-answer question on Molyneux's problem where I keep flip-flopping on a students answer. I'd rather not commit to a number until someone else has looked at it, so here it is.

The question (15 marks, ~400 words, closed book, and they've been told not to bother with citations):

State Molyneux's problem. Then pull apart two things it tends to run together: (i) the claim that the man could not recognise the shapes by sight, and (ii) some claim about what his visual experience would be like. Next: I've been calling a certain intuition the one-way valve — the thought that touch can teach sight what a shape is, but sight can't teach touch, i.e. that cross-modal learning only ever runs in one direction. Say whether answering Molyneux's problem in the negative commits you to the one-way valve, and whether it commits you to any view about whether shape concepts are innate. Finally, the mirror case: a person who has been sighted from birth loses their sight in adulthood and now has to sort cubes from spheres by touch alone. What does your answer to Molyneux predict about them, and why?

And here's what the student wrote:

Molyneux's problem was put to Locke in a letter, and Locke thought enough of it to print it in the Essay along with his agreement with Molyneux's own answer. A man blind from birth has learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch. If his sight is restored, could he tell which is which by sight alone, before he touches them? The before-he-touches-them part is doing real work — the question isn't whether he could ever learn to match up what he sees with what he feels, only about the first moment.

Molyneux and Locke both say no. Their reason is that the man has all the tactile experience in the world of how a cube feels, but none of how a cube looks, and none of the correlation between the two; the link between the look and the feel of a cube is something you build up, so at the first moment there is nothing to go on. Leibniz and the rationalists say yes, on the grounds that the geometry is common to both senses and can be reasoned out.

Berkeley pushes the empiricist line further than Locke and ends up on the rationalists' side of the ledger, though for entirely different reasons. For Berkeley the ideas of sight and touch are so unlike that they can't be compared at all, so what the man sees when he looks at the cube is just a flat arrangement of colours with no spatial content whatsoever, and we only think we see shapes because we've learned to read visual cues as signs of tangible things.

I think this shows the negative answer is really a claim about concepts rather than about perception. If the man can't recognise the cube by sight, then the concept of a cube can't have come from the senses at all, so it must be innate.

That's also why I think the no answer commits you to the one-way valve. If sight can't identify a shape without touch having got there first, then the teaching can only run from touch to sight, never the other way. The mirror case is then the same problem with the arrow pointing the other way, and the valve predicts the same failure there: having learned shapes by eye won't help you sort them by hand.
