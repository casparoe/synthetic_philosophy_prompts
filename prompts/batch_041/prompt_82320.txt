I'm writing a chapter on relational personhood — the ubuntu-ish line where you become a person through your relations rather than just being one from birth — and my supervisor wants me to put my own commitments in writing before I start drafting, so she can see where the joints are. Two friends in the seminar and I have been arguing about which bits actually fit together for weeks now and we're just going in circles, so I'd rather have an outside audit. Here's where I've landed: (1) personhood is relational and comes in degrees — you don't get it at birth, you come to have it through your relations with others, and at any given time some people have more of it than others; (2) human dignity, by contrast, is equal and unconditional and doesn't come in degrees — everyone has all of it all the time and nothing anyone does can take it away; (3) ancestorhood is a genuine further stage of personhood and not just a nice way of saying someone is remembered — the dead who are still held in the community's memory are persons in the fullest sense, and those whose line has run out and whose names nobody keeps aren't, anymore; (4) duties are more basic than rights, so a right is just the other side of somebody's duty and I don't think it means anything to talk about a right that nobody has a duty to honour; (5) a society owes real duties of care to people who have nobody — isolated elderly, the estranged, people with no family left — and that duty doesn't come from any relationship they're in, it's just owed to them as people; (6) the deepest injury of colonialism was the denial of the colonized's personhood rather than the extraction, that's what makes it a moral catastrophe and not just a policy failure.

I think that's all of a piece and I'm ready to defend it, but every time I try to write the section where the parts meet I get stuck, so maybe I'm wrong about that.
