I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and next week I am to present a short paper at a departmental seminar on self-knowledge. The spine of the paper is an argument I built myself, and it reaches a conclusion I cannot accept: that no belief of mine is luminous — that is, that I can never be in a position to know what I currently believe. I am convinced something has slipped, but I cannot see where, and I would rather have the argument dissected than quietly withdrawn. Please treat the numbered claims below as the entirety of the argument, and do not rescue it by supplying premises I have not stated.

(1) If I know that p, then I know that I know that p.
(2) If I know that p, then I have a reason in support of p, and that reason is available to me on reflection.
(3) Let p be the proposition that I now believe that q, and suppose that I know that p.
(4) By (2) I have a reason, available on reflection, for p; and by (1) I know that I know that p, so by (2) again I also have a reason, available on reflection, for the claim that I know that p.
(5) The only reason reflection can supply for either claim is that it introspectively seems to me that I now believe that q.
(6) But that seeming is exactly the same whether or not I actually believe that q; it does not vary with the fact it purports to report.
(7) A consideration that would be exactly as it is whether or not p were true cannot justify p.
(8) So reflection supplies no reason for p, and none for the claim that I know that p.
(9) Hence, by (2), I do not know that p; and since q was arbitrary, no belief of mine is luminous.

Which step should I give up? Tell me why that step looked so compelling, what giving it up costs, and what the chief rival accounts of self-knowledge would each have to concede in order to take that exit. I should say plainly that I would most like to keep (1) and (2), so if the fault lies in the steps that draw on them, make the price of that especially clear. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want the diagnosis.

Be genuinely critical. If the argument is valid and every exit is costly, say so rather than manufacturing a tidy flaw; if my framing of the problem is confused, tell me so plainly instead of answering the question as posed. Do not present all the exits as equally respectable if one is clearly the best. Please write your answer in British English.
