ok so I'm a fourth-year in philosophy and I'm supposed to workshop a short story for this thing my dept runs where the philosophers and the fiction people get thrown in a room together, and I voluntold myself into doing one on the repugnant conclusion. Quick recap incase we're not on the same page: it's Parfit's problem where for any world of, say, a billion genuinely happy people, you can always describe another world with vastly more people whose lives are only barely worth living, and if you just add up total welfare the huge mediocre world comes out on top. I've been doing population ethics for two years and I'm so sick of the standard version where it's basically "sad philosopher stares at a graph," so I want to write into a specific soft spot instead.

Here's the soft spot, and I think it's actually the interesting part that nobody dramatizes: the whole paradox assumes you can score a life on a welfare scale from the outside. So in my story, the barely-worth-living billions are at the floor by every objective measure you like — gruel, routine, short lives, no variety, nothing anyone would call flourishing. But they definately are not miserable. They're content. They'd tell you they're happy, they'd be genuinely offended if you called their lives barely worth having, and they have no idea anything better is possible. Then I've got the other world, the small thriving one, and everyone there is quietly tormented in a totally different way: they know exactly how good things could be and they measure themselves against it constantly, so they're free and rich and miserable.

The gap between how a life scores on the metric and how it scores to the person living it — I've been calling it the contentment gap, which is half-baked but whatever. If welfare is just how the life goes for the person living it, then my billions might not be low-welfare at all and the repugnant conclusion kind of dissolves. If welfare is objective, then it stays repugnant no matter how happy they insist they are. I want the story to sit in that undecided space and make a reader feel the pull of both sides without resolving it.

So — can you write it, like 1500 words or so? I want it from the POV of an outsider sent to audit the vast mediocre world, someone with a metric and a clipboard and a mandate, who keeps running into inhabitants who politely refuse to be counted as deprived. Give me at least one scene where the outsider's own measure gets turned back on them, and one where you can feel why someone would still call the whole thing a moral catastrophe even though nobody in it is suffering. Please don't end on a verdict, and no character standing around explaining the philosophy out loud — I want the idea to be in the furniture of the world, not in a monologue.
