Right, so I want to run a real episode through Dennett's tower of generate-and-test and see where the tower creaks. The four rungs, as I'm using them: Darwinian creatures are born with their behaviour; Skinnerian ones learn by reinforcement, so their bad hypotheses die in the world instead of in them; Popperian ones can run a hypothesis inside their own head and let the hypothesis die instead of them; and Gregorian ones think partly with tools and notations they pick up from their culture -- maps, tables, words, other people's ideas. Each rung is supposed to sit on top of the one below it.

The episode: the longitude problem in the 18th century and what the British actually did about it. A prize was on offer from 1714. Harrison spent decades building sea clocks good enough that you could get longitude by comparing ship time with home time. Maskelyne pushed the other route -- lunar distances, where you measure the moons position against the stars and read off the time from tables. And from 1767 the Nautical Almanac was printing those lunar distances for the year ahead, so a skipper didn't need to be an astronomer any more, just someone who could read a table, do a bit of arithmetic and follow the recipe. Same navy, same ocean, far more people who could get the job done.

Analyse that with the tower. Who is the actual agent in 1770 -- the individual navigator, or the ship-plus-almanac-plus-tables system? Is the almanac Gregorian scaffolding in Dennett's sense, or does it break his ordering, because here a tool is doing work that only a Popperian could do unaided, and handing it down to somebody operating at roughly Skinnerian level (follow the steps, get reinforced by arriving)? If the rungs are meant to be cumulative, is this a ladder, or a menu where good enough scaffolding lets you skip?

Then push it back the other way, I want the case to test the theory and not just illustrate it. Two things in particular. First, owning versus renting a tool: the mariner can use the almanac but couldn't build one or tell if it had gone wrong, and Dennett's Gregorian creature is supposed to have a genuinely augmented mind, not just be taking orders from a book. Does that distinction matter to the framework, and does the mariner pass? Second, whether "creature" is even the right unit of analysis when the competence is spread across a crew, a book and an instrument, or whether that's a metaphor doing no work. Then tell me what the tower actually explains here that a plain history wouldn't, where it's just redescribing, and whether it has anything to say about why the navy kept betting on tables for the many rather than clocks for the few -- or whether thats just cost and politics.
