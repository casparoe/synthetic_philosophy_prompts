Ok so this is kinda in the weeds but its been bugging me for weeks. In my philosophy club (I'm 16, we meet on Thursdays) we got onto whether you can know that you know something, and whether there are facts about yourself that you can always tell are true just by paying attention. Our advisor said theres a whole literature on this and that it's basically stuck, which made me want to know why.

Here's the debate the way I understand it. Some people think knowledge is transparent to the person who has it -- if you know something, you can know that you know it, and if some condition holds of you (youre tired, your in pain, the room is bright) you can always tell that it holds. Other people say no, and they run an argument like this: knowledge needs a safety margin, so if you're right at the edge of a condition you cant know you're in it, and since conditions have fuzzy edges (there's no sharp instant when a slowly dimming room stops being bright), it follows that no interesting condition is like that, so you basically never get to know you know. Then they say this kills the transparency idea.

What bugs me is that when we argued it nobody moved an inch. The transparency side says the safety-margin thing begs the question by assuming one picture of knowledge; the other side says the transparency people are just refusing the conclusion. And one kid said it's all about what know means and how strict you're being, which might make it verbal, but I dont know how you'd even tell. Somebody else made up a case: you're driving and the gas gauge is sliding from a quarter down toward empty -- at some point you know you're low, but you can't point to the exact moment you knew, and then everyone just talked past each other about it.

My guess -- tell me if its a bad one -- is that two things are mashed together: (1) a real fight about whether knowledge has to come with a safety margin, and (2) a fake or verbal fight, where you can know you know means have enough evidence to know it for some people and could figure it out if you tried for others. Is that right? If it isnt, what kind of disagreement is it actually -- about facts, about what counts as a good argument, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for?

Also our advisor mentioned a distinction between being in a position to know and actually knowing and said the whole thing hinges on it, but I didnt follow, so use that if its relevant.

So: diagnose why this has stalled, say what kind of disagreement it really is, and tell me what would actually count as progress -- a new distinction, a counterexample, a formal result, or honestly nothing, and if it's nothing then why should I believe that instead of just that everyone's being stubborn.
