Imagine you are grading a short essay in a graduate seminar on social ontology. The question was: Lewis's account treats a convention as a regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, where each person conforms because they expect others to conform and prefers to conform on that condition. Consider a case in which a population first coordinates on an arbitrary rule under genuine coordination pressure. Later the coordination problem disappears, but the rule survives because children are taught that it is binding and deviants are punished. Is the later regularity still a convention in Lewis's sense? Can Lewis's framework by itself explain how the transition from convention to enforced norm occurs, or does it need an additional account of normative internalization? Answer in roughly 400 words.

A student submitted the following answer:

Lewis defines a convention as any equilibrium in a coordination game. On his view, a regularity is conventional when everyone follows it, everyone expects everyone else to follow it, and everyone prefers to follow it provided others do. The original rule therefore counts as a convention. Once the coordination pressure disappears, the regularity is no longer a convention in Lewis's strict sense, because the conditional preference for conformity is gone; it has become a norm backed by sanctions. Still, Lewis's framework can explain the transition. A convention, once established, becomes a focal point. Because people expect others to expect them to conform, the regularity can acquire normative force and be transmitted as a rule that people treat as binding. So the later rule is a degenerate convention, or a norm built on a convention. The common mistake is to think conventions must be arbitrary through and through; they can be arbitrary in origin but need not remain so. Lewis thus explains both the persistence and the moralization of the rule.
