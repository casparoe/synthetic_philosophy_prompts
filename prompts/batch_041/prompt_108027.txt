I have been asked to open the discussion at our reading group's session on the first part of Parfit's Reasons and Persons, and I am stuck, because the illustration everyone reaches for — the hitchhiker stranded in the desert who can only get his lift by convincing the driver that he will pay on arrival, when the driver will have no way of making him pay — has been used so often in that room that the group will simply recite it back at me. The subject has also become oddly personal. A few months ago I accepted a considerable favour from someone I had only just met, on the understanding that I would return it later at some cost to myself, with nothing behind it but my word and no prospect of ever seeing him again. I did what I had said I would do, and when I tried afterwards to give an account of why, every reason I could produce either made the promise sound like a poor investment or made me sound like someone I do not much recognise. So I would like you to build me a fresh case of the same general kind, designed to specification, that I can put in front of the group.

The case has to do a very particular job. The self-interest theory — the view that what it is rational for a person to do is whatever will make her own life go best considered as a whole — must return opposite verdicts at the two moments of the story: it must approve of her taking the arrangement up, and disapprove of the very act that makes the arrangement worth anything to the other party. None of the familiar escapes may be left open. It must be a single encounter, with no future dealings and no one who will ever learn what she did; nothing may turn on her reputation, her self-image, or her feelings, so stipulate that she is untroubled by guilt, takes no warm glow from doing the decent thing, and does not care about being any particular kind of person. She must have no way to bind herself in advance, no way to alter her own desires or character by an act of will, and no way to decide to become the sort of person who would go through with it. There must be no concealed self-interested payoff of the sort that would let the theory be reconciled with itself in hindsight. And the whole thing should be ordinary — contemporary life, ordinary stakes, no deserts, no perfect predictors, no mind-reading devices, no medical intervention; the only read that anyone takes of anyone else is the sort of read an ordinary person takes of a stranger on ordinary evidence. Keep the case itself to two paragraphs or so, since I have to read it aloud.

The part I actually care about is this. I do not want the case to turn on weakness of will, where she simply fails to do what she had settled on; that is a story about her rather than about the theory. What I want is a case in which deliberation itself is what spoils the outcome — where the good reaches her only if she acts without stopping to ask what the action is worth to her, and where a theory that tells her to maximise her own interest is thereby committed to requiring the very step that destroys the good. It must be visible in the case why thinking about it spoils it, and not merely stipulated: the motive has to be of the kind that dissolves when it is examined for its usefulness, the way an unforced concern for another person can evaporate the moment one starts asking whether the concern is paying for itself. She must not have to believe anything false, and she must not have to be ignorant of anything she could easily have found out. It also has to be a case in which what she loses is genuinely good for her, so that the theory cannot reply that the prize was not worth having. Once the case is built, I would like the working shown: what the self-interest theory says at each moment and why, what the present-aim theory says (the view that what is rational is to do what best achieves the aims one has at the time of acting), and what common-sense morality says; then an honest account of which single feature of the case is doing the work, what becomes of the case if that feature is removed, and how someone wanting to defend the self-interest theory would try to break it. The point of the exercise is to be able to say that the verdicts fall out of the theories rather than being built into the scenario, so if it turns out that the case works only because I have quietly rigged it, tell me that instead. Finally, say where your own reasoning is likeliest to fail, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about whether the case does what I am asking.
