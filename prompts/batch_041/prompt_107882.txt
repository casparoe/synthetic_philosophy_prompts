Question:
In this unit we distinguished four mechanisms by which a multipolar trap can arise: (1) symmetric escalation, in which each party's best response to others' increases is to increase its own; (2) asymmetric externalization, in which parties shift costs onto third parties who cannot retaliate; (3) preference aggregation failure, in which individually coherent preferences produce a collectively self-defeating outcome through a voting or market aggregation rule; and (4) commitment failure, in which parties cannot make credible commitments to restrain themselves even though all prefer mutual restraint. We also distinguished a trap from a merely bad equilibrium: a trap requires that the suboptimal outcome be stable under unilateral deviation and that the parties would prefer some feasible alternative if they could coordinate. We use Molochian to name the pattern in which individually rational choices generate a collectively self-defeating outcome that the parties would prefer to escape. Using this framework, explain why the competition among hospitals to acquire and advertise expensive robotic surgery systems is or is not a Molochian multipolar trap, and identify which mechanism(s) are operative.

Student answer:
The competition among hospitals to acquire robotic surgery systems is a Molochian multipolar trap. Each hospital would prefer a world in which no hospital adopted the technology and all saved money, but each fears that if it alone refrains while rivals adopt, it will lose market share and prestige. So adoption is a dominant strategy, and the outcome is stable against unilateral deviation. This is primarily a symmetric escalation mechanism: as one hospital adopts, others must match or exceed it. It also involves asymmetric externalization because the costs are partly borne by insurers and patients rather than the hospitals themselves. However, I think the trap is not a genuine multipolar trap in the strict sense because the hospitals are not all identical—some are academic medical centers with research missions that genuinely benefit from the technology, so their preferences are not aligned. A true multipolar trap requires identical preferences and a single equilibrium. Since the preferences differ, the outcome is better described as a preference aggregation failure rather than a trap. In any case, Moloch is best understood as a metaphor for the unintended collective consequences of individually rational action, not as an actual demonic agent. The main lesson is that the outcome is not a trap but a preference aggregation failure, so hospitals should focus on aligning their preferences rather than on building commitment devices.
