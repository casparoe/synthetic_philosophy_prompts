You are an analytic metaphysician with a specialty in mereology and the ontology of material objects, and I would like you to act as a careful interlocutor on a problem in that area. Before you answer, please spend a few sentences brainstorming: lay out the space of candidate principles that might underlie the pattern of verdicts below, say where each gains and where it loses, and only then commit to a recommendation. Think step by step, and be explicit whenever a verdict has to be sacrificed.

I have been trying to settle, for my own case, what is usually called the special composition question: under what conditions do some things together compose a further thing — a single object over and above them? My verdicts on particular cases are as follows, and I have tried to report them as they actually strike me rather than as any theory would want them to.

- The cells that make up my cat compose something: there is a cat, and it is one object.
- Six planks nailed together into a raft compose something: there is a raft, one object. If I merely lay the same six planks side by side so that they touch, I do not think there is any further object — there are just six planks, arranged.
- A heap of sand on the beach is not an object. There are grains, and talk of "the heap" is a convenient façon de parler, but nothing over and above the grains exists there.
- A quantity of sand fused by lightning into a single glassy lump is an object — the lump is one thing, even though it is the very same sand.
- A cloud is not an object; it is a region of vapor, and its boundaries are stipulated for our convenience.
- A severed finger lying on the ground is not an object; the finger was part of an object while attached to a living body, and now it is just tissue arranged fingerwise.
- A bacterium is an object. A virus, which cannot metabolize or reproduce on its own, is not an object but a package of molecules — though I admit I am much less sure here.
- The cells of a slime mold that have come together into a single crawling, slug-like body compose one object while they are so organized, and stop doing so when they disperse.
- The parts of a disassembled watch, jumbled in a drawer, do not compose a watch. Nor do they compose anything else, so far as I can tell.
- A bee colony is not an object, even though the bees behave in striking ways as a unit; nor is a corporation, nor a nation.
- The water molecules currently in my bathtub compose the water in the bathtub, which is an object, though a moment later it is a different collection of molecules and I still say the water is there.

My tentative principle: some things compose a further object just when either (a) they are caught up in a single life — a self-maintaining, self-directing metabolic process — or (b) they have been intentionally fastened or fused by an agent into a rigid whole. I already suspect this is a mess: clause (b) seems to buy me the raft and the lump at the price of buying me far too much (why not the watch parts, if someone once fastened them?), and clause (a) has to say something uncomfortable about the virus, and about whether a lichen — a fungus and an alga living as one — is one life or two.

What I want from you:

1. State, as precisely as you can, the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures the verdicts above, giving for each clause the case that forces it.
2. Tell me what each candidate predicts for cases I have not given: a wooden ship whose planks are gradually replaced one at a time; a wave traveling across the ocean; two people holding hands; the collection of all the red things in the room; a termite mound; and an organism in a persistent vegetative state whose metabolism continues but whose life, in the richer sense, seems over.
3. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate and must overturn, and whether the pattern is better served by a simpler principle that gives up a verdict than by a complicated one that saves them all.
4. Finally, say whether the whole exercise presupposes something contestable — for instance, that "object" is univocal across the biological, artifactual, and geological cases — and whether that presupposition should change how much weight my verdicts deserve.

Please be rigorous, and resist the urge to give me a survey of the literature; I care about which principle actually fits the cases and about what it costs.
