The question, as set (20 marks in total):

Scanlon's contractualism is often said to face an aggregation problem.

(a) Reconstruct the problem. What feature of contractualist justification is supposed to generate it, and what does it seem to imply about the moral relevance of numbers? (4 marks)

(b) Say what the view implies in the case below, and why. (6 marks)

The case. A charitable trust holds a sum of money that must be spent in one of two ways. It can fund a course of treatment that will save the life of one patient, R. Or it can fund a programme that will relieve, for each of 250,000 people, a chronic itch that is constant and distracting but harmless and not life-shortening; each sufferer would describe the itch as a serious burden on her days, but not as a threat to her life or her livelihood. The sum cannot be divided between the two, the treatment cannot be postponed, and no other effects are relevant.

(c) Scanlon does not accept that his view makes numbers irrelevant. Set out the resources the view has for giving numbers weight, and assess how far they reach. (6 marks)

(d) Is the resulting position stable — does it avoid both a collapse into ordinary aggregation and the anti-aggregation implications you identified in (a)? Defend your answer. (4 marks)

The student's answer:

Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject. The test of a principle is therefore whether some person affected by it has grounds for rejecting it that it would be unreasonable to ask her to set aside.

Because the test is put to each person in turn, the view is individualist. It compares the complaint of one person with the complaint of another, and it does not permit complaints to be added together across persons. This is the source of the aggregation problem. If the only question is whether some single person has a complaint stronger than some single person on the other side, then no number of people with weak complaints can ever outweigh one person with a strong complaint. Applied to the Fund case, this means the trust must fund the treatment and save R. Each of the 250,000 has a complaint — a chronic itch is a real burden — but no one of them has a complaint as strong as R's, and their complaints cannot be summed to outweigh his.

This is not a version of contractarianism. The question is not what each party could rationally demand in a bargain, but what could be justified to each party given her equal standing. The strength of a complaint is the weight of the reason the person has to object to the principle, not the amount of welfare the principle would cost her; contractualism is not a disguised utilitarianism with a pairwise comparison rule.

Scanlon does not think this makes his view indifferent to numbers. First, he allows aggregation within a life: where a principle would burden one person in several distinct ways, those burdens may be combined into a single complaint. Second, the number of people who share a complaint matters, since a principle that many people have the same reason to reject faces a stronger objection than one that only a few have reason to reject; and once we see that the same reasoning extends across persons when the complaints are of the same type, contractualism can give the numbers their due without becoming consequentialist. In the Fund case, though, neither consideration changes the outcome: R's complaint is the strongest single complaint, and the itch sufferers' complaints are of a different and weaker kind, so R cannot reasonably be left to die for their aggregate comfort.

Is the position stable? I believe it is. The individualist restriction blocks the cases in which aggregation gives the wrong answer, while the two resources above keep the view from being absurdly insensitive to numbers when the complaints on each side are of the same kind. So contractualism is neither a form of aggregation nor a view on which numbers never matter.
