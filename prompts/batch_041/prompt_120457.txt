I am revising a paper on the epistemic case for and against democracy, and I have reached the stage where I can no longer tell whether my central move is a contribution or a redescription of a familiar deadlock. I would like an adversarial reading of the argument below. I care most about accuracy: whether the technical machinery is used correctly and whether any of my claims are overstated. I care second about whether the reframing earns its keep, and third about what to cut. I do not need a reading list or citations; I know the literature, and I would rather have your judgment than a bibliography.

The argument runs as follows.

First, the dispute between epistocracy and democracy is normally conducted as a dispute about who should hold authority: the many, or a subset selected for competence. I claim this is the wrong framing, and that both sides are really making claims, usually tacitly, about the error structure of collective judgment.

Second, the aggregative case for democracy rests on a result of the following shape. If each of n voters is more likely than not to judge correctly, and their errors are independent, then the probability that the majority is correct increases with n and tends to certainty; if each is more likely than not to judge incorrectly, the majority tends with equal certainty to the wrong answer. Two conditions carry the weight: the question put must have a determinate correct answer, and the errors must not be systematically aligned.

Third, politics supplies neither condition cleanly, and I press on the second. Aggregation cancels noise; it does not cancel bias. Where errors are correlated — shared outlets, shared cues, shared partisan identity — the majority converges on the bias rather than on the truth. So the empirical question that decides the epistemic case for democracy is not how ignorant the median voter is, but whether the error is mean-zero and roughly independent across voters. There is a structural reason to expect that it is not. The costs of becoming informed are borne individually while the benefits are diffuse, so the information that circulates is disproportionately supplied by those with concentrated stakes. The resulting ignorance is not random but tilted. Aggregation therefore fails precisely where the stakes are highest, and the familiar complaint about voter ignorance is really a complaint about a directional distortion that aggregation cannot repair.

Fourth, the epistocratic reply is to restrict the franchise to the competent. But identifying the competent requires a standard of correctness independent of the judgments in dispute; otherwise the criterion is circular, since we would be identifying competence by agreement with the answer we take to be correct, which is the very thing at issue. The available proxies — political knowledge quizzes, education, occupation — are not track records of sound judgment on novel policy questions; they measure exposure to the prevailing information environment, and that environment is itself endogenous to the interests that supply it. The knowledgeable are knowledgeable in part because the environment is built to inform people like them. The selection criterion is therefore not independent of the distortion it is meant to correct.

Fifth, the strongest epistocratic intuition, and the one I find hardest to answer, is not the philosopher-king but the oracle: an arrangement that, when consulted, reliably returns better policy answers than any electorate, and which it would be perverse to ignore. My response is that the oracle's competence is certified by an independent record of success, and that no actual epistocratic arrangement has such a record available at the moment of decision. I am not confident this is a genuine asymmetry rather than a standard of ex ante certification that I impose on epistocracy and not on democracy.

Sixth, if the question is error structure, the design question becomes which institutions decorrelate error, and at what cost: sortition, deliberative bodies furnished with opposing briefs, supermajority requirements, divided jurisdictions, adversarial audit of policy. My conclusion is deflationary. Decorrelation is hard, and the obvious proposals either reduce correlation by reducing participation, which reintroduces the selection problem, or widen participation while inheriting the same common shocks.

What I want from you: (i) is my use of the aggregation result correct, and would a referee accept the way I state the independence condition? (ii) Is the noise-bias distinction doing the work I claim, or is it the familiar point about biased voters dressed in technical vocabulary? (iii) Is the circularity charge fair, or does the epistocrat have a non-circular route — retrospective track records, prediction markets, accountability at the next election? (iv) Is the oracle asymmetry real? (v) If the reframing dissolves the dispute rather than resolving it, is that a defect in the paper or the honest result — and if a defect, what would a version that resolved it look like? Tell me which section you would cut, and where the argument is thinnest.
