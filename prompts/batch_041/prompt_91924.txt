I have recently begun sitting as a lay member of a tribunal that applies statutory rules, and my appointment carries an undertaking to apply the law as it stands, whatever I think of its merits. Twice now I have had to apply a rule I believe to be seriously unjust, and I have found that I no longer know what I take myself to be doing when I do it. The question I keep circling is the old one about whether an iniquitous enactment is law at all, and I would like a decision tree that sorts the positions on it — built out of the questions that actually divide those positions rather than out of a list of names. I have no philosophical training, so I need to be able to walk the tree myself, answering each branch without knowing where it leads.

Please return a single JSON object with the following fields.

"entry": the id of the question the tree begins with.

"nodes": an array of branch questions, each an object with "id" (a short string such as "N1"); "question" (the branch question, phrased as a concrete case or a claim about one that I can answer Yes or No without knowing the name of any position — no branch may simply ask which label I accept); "yes" and "no" (each the id of the next node or of a leaf); and "divides" (one sentence naming which positions come apart at this branch).

"leaves": an array of terminal positions, each an object with "id" (such as "L1"); "position" (the conventional name of the view, or "unnamed" if no one has defended the combination); "path" (the sequence of answers that reaches it, written as, for example, ["N1: yes", "N3: no"]); "core_claim" (one sentence on what the view holds); "awkward_commitment" (one sentence on the least comfortable thing the view has to say); and, for unnamed leaves only, "diagnosis" (one of "incoherent", "unattractive" or "unexplored", with a sentence saying which it is and why).

"inseparable_pairs": an array of objects with "pair" (two leaf ids) and "why" (one sentence explaining why no question in the tree separates them).

"notes": a single string for anything structural that fits nowhere else — a distinction of mine that turned out to be two, two that collapsed into one, or a branch I could not phrase without giving away a label.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: settle the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and then locate the positions in that space. The distinctions I care about, roughly in the order I would put them, are these. First, whether legal validity depends on a norm's content or only on its pedigree. Second, if content matters, whether what matters is procedural — how the norm was made, and whether it can actually guide conduct — or substantive, turning on whether the norm serves the common good. Third, whether a moral failure voids the norm outright or only makes it defective or presumptively invalid. Fourth, whether the moral condition attaches to the individual norm or to the legal system as a whole. Fifth, whether a moral failure bears on validity at all, or only on whether one has a duty to obey. Each of these should appear somewhere in the tree as a branch, though they will not all be reachable on every path. Where a position turns out to be committed to an answer on one of these points that its usual defenders have not noticed, say so in that leaf's "awkward_commitment".

The tree should reach at least these leaves: (a) validity depends only on pedigree, and this is a necessary truth about the concept of law rather than a feature of some legal systems; (b) validity depends only on pedigree, but a system's criteria of validity may themselves contain moral tests, so that in some systems morality is a condition of validity; (c) the moral condition on law is procedural, so that a system whose rules cannot guide conduct — because they are unpublished, retroactive, contradictory, or impossible to comply with — fails to be law; (d) a norm contrary to the common good is not law, whatever its pedigree; (e) only extreme injustice voids a norm, ordinary injustice leaving validity intact; (f) law is not exhausted by rules but includes principles, so that the question whether a particular enactment is law is the wrong shape; and (g) at least one view that grants the enactment is law and denies any general duty to obey it. Add any further leaves the logical space contains.

Use these cases as branch questions wherever they will carry weight: a statute that was validly enacted, is clear, published and prospective, but gravely unjust; a rule that is morally admirable but was never enacted; a retroactive criminal statute; a statute validly enacted but never published; a rule enacted by a regime whose authority is contested and which has since been applied without complaint; and a norm that a community treats as binding although no institution ever enacted it.

Keep the tree to at most eight branch questions, so that no path runs longer than six questions, and to between eight and fourteen leaves. Do not cite or name any works or authors — I want the positions, not the literature.
