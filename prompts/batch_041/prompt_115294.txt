I'm in a seminar on information ethics and we keep having the same argument: one person says privacy is just control over your information, someone else says it's about being let alone, someone else says it's basically a property right in data, and then the conversation dies because nobody has agreed on what the question is. Before we fight about which one wins I want the spec first. So write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the nature and value of privacy has to deliver — the cases it must get right, the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must not collapse, the facts about how data actually works that it can't contradict. For each requirement tell me what's behind it, the case or consideration that generates it, and say whether you think it's non-negotiable or just a prejudice of one camp. And if you think some subset of these requirements can't be satisfied together, say so and show the tradeoff — thats the part I actually want.

Three things from class I want the list to handle, since they're the ones that keep breaking the simple views. First, two worlds with exactly the same information available about me, differing only in how cheap it is to get at — in one, a stranger has to spend a week in a records office, in the other a search box spits it out instantly. Same facts, same disclosure, but only one of them seems to wrong me, and I want the list to say why or whether that intuition is junk. Second, information that isn't mine alone: my half of a conversation, a photo of me and you, my genome, which is also my sister's. If privacy is control, whose control is it, and can I waive yours by telling my half? Third, the person who genuinely doesn't mind — the guy who has decided he has nothing to hide — inside a system that predicts other people's traits off his data, so his likes end up forecasting his friends' politics. He's fine with it; are they wronged? A theory that can only shrug and say he consented so there's nothing to see looks like it's missing something, and I'd like the list to tell me if it is. Related, and maybe this is its own item: cases where nothing bad happens to anyone and yet something still seems to have been done to me — is a violation a different thing from a harm, or is that just muddy talk?

One more distinction I keep fumbling, tell me where it goes: between being unknown and being un-knowable, i.e. between nobody having looked and nobody being able to. One phenomenon or two, and does a decent theory need both. Then, briefly — a paragraph total, or a short table — how the control view, the limited-access view, and the property view hold up against the list, and which single requirement does the most damage to the most candidates. Don't bother with reading lists or citations, I just want the criteria and your reasoning about them.
