I want to practice arguing about the epistemology of markets, so I need you to play a committed defender of a position I'm going to try to wreck. The position: the deep result about markets isn't the efficient-markets stuff everyone quotes, it's the no-trade theorem. Heres the version I'll grant you so we're on the same page — if the market is in an efficient equilibrium, nobody's behaving irrationally, and it's common knowledge how traders get their information, then even when some traders do have private information none of them can profit from it, because merely making an offer leaks what you know, everyone updates, and nobody accepts. You think that's the skeleton key: every actual trade needs something extra to explain it — different priors, non-common knowledge, straight-up irrationality, or a non-speculative motive like hedging, liquidity, taxes. You'll happily concede that markets do something, that prices carry information in a thin sense, and you'll grant a good point when you see one, but you never surrender the main claim, that a price is a snapshot of who's currently willing to bet and against whom. Sharp, a bit cocky, econ-seminar energy, one paragraph per reply, no bullet points, stay in character till I say stop.

My opening move: put your theorem next to two other results and the whole picture eats itself. If prices fully reflected the information people have, nobody would pay to go get information in the first place, since there'd be no profit in it — so a perfectly informationally efficient market is impossible, and markets can only aggregate information to the extent they stay inefficient. And two rational agents who share priors can't knowingly disagree, so the disagreement that fuels trade is either somebody's mistake or a difference in priors that no Bayesian would sign off on. Which means information gets into prices only by leaving money on the table, and trade happens only where somebody is wrong. So the wisdom-of-crowds story looks parasitic on the crowd being wrong. So which is the market: an aggregator of beliefs, or a machine for selecting which model survives? Those are different epistemologies and I don't think your picture gets to have both. Go.
