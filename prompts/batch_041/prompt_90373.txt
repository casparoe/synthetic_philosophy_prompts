I recently took over the weekly session of our group's reading group on scalable oversight, and in the same period I have been running a workflow at work in which a hard task is divided among several contractors and then, once the process has stabilised, handed to a single person trained on the team's accumulated outputs. Watching that substitution is what made the amplification-and-distillation picture feel live to me rather than academic. The team was measurably better than any of its individual members, and the replacement was measurably worse than the team, yet nobody could say which part of the team's advantage had been discarded, or whether the replacement was pursuing the same objective or merely emitting the same outputs.

I want to run a hundred-minute session for fourteen participants, a mix of graduate students in philosophy and in machine learning, comfortable with formal material but not specialists in this area. They should build such a system and then dismantle it themselves. My design constraints:

- The activity must be a game with rules, roles and mechanical scoring, no money involved. Participants organise themselves into a delegation tree of their own construction, with a fixed budget of node expansions and a hard cap on the length of any answer travelling up the tree, so that summarisation loss is a binding constraint rather than an incidental nuisance.
- The top-level task must have checkable answers, so that scoring is mechanical and nobody has to adjudicate quality by taste.
- The scoring mechanism must be able to check leaf answers cheaply, but must not be able to check whether the decomposition itself was well chosen. I want the group to feel where the cheap-verification assumption stops doing work.
- Misalignment should be structural rather than a gotcha: leaves hold private objectives that only weakly correlate with the top-level objective, so that competence and fidelity to the real objective can come apart in ways participants can point to afterwards.
- The tree should be allowed to re-decompose itself, so they can feel what recursion buys and what it costs in depth.
- There must be at least two rounds, so that the second half of the session can stage the distillation step: one participant, given only the top-level question and a record of the previous round's exchanges, must answer fresh instances of the same task without decomposing. I want the gap between amplified and distilled performance visible on the board, and I want the group to have to separate what was lost because the distilled agent could not reconstruct the decomposition from what was lost because it never held the leaves' incentives.

What I want them to come away able to say, in their own words and by reference to particular moments in the game, is which of the two steps is load-bearing for competence and which for alignment, and what happened that supports their answer. Please do not name the surrounding ideas at the start, and no lecture before the activity; the rules should do the work.

Give me the rules, materials, roles, timeline and scoring scheme, plus a debrief that maps specific moments onto the parts of the picture they illustrate. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the real idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
