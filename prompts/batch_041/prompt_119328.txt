Ok, here's a position I've talked myself into over years of reading blogs and comment sections, and I want you to try to talk me out of it, or at least show me where it's exposed.

I'm a realist about free-floating rationales -- the Dennett-ish idea that you can say a bird's wing has a rationale for generating lift, or a spiderweb has a rationale for catching prey, with no reasoner anywhere in the picture. Fine, that part is standard. Where I go further: I don't think you need a selection process at all. I call these flat rationales. On my view, any stable causal structure that supports the right counterfactuals about a task has a rationale for that task -- regardless of whether anything ever selected for it, whether it ever actually performs the task, and whether there's any history behind it. So a rock arch that would hold up a roof if you set one on it has a rationale for roof-holding. A randomly-initialised net that happens to compute something useful has a rationale for that. A river bed has a rationale for getting water to the sea. And I want to be a realist, not an instrumentalist, about all of it: flat rationales are out there, they can be correct or incorrect (a wing can have a bad rationale for lift), and this is the right ontology for a pile of stuff people are confused about -- biological function, what a trained model "knows", market efficiency, all of it.

So what would count against this? I want the real defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, not the drive-by "that's just instrumentalism" thing people say in comment threads. Things I already suspect are load-bearing but can't tell how badly: the indeterminacy problem (if a structure supports infinitely many task-descriptions, what makes one of them *the* rationale rather than a gerrymandered one?); whether the no-selection cases are actually doing any work or are just dispositions with extra steps; and whether anything normative survives once you drop the selection history, since it's not obvious a rationale can be *wrong* without something having been selected for getting it right. I'd like each defeaters sorted by whether it hits the flat/no-selection extension specifically or the whole free-floating apparatus, and I want to know which of them a committed defender (including me) would concede as counting against them versus which they'd wave off as a rival framework rather than a refutation. Also: does my view predict anything that plain disposition-talk or the selection-history view doesn't? And be honest if it turns out I've qualified the thing into unfalsifiability -- and if so, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just the price of the ontology.

The one that would personally move me most is a case where my account has to declare two incompatible rationales both correct with no principled tiebreak, or where it forces me to call something a rationale that I'd then have to admit isn't one.
