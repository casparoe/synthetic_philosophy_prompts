I'm in a metaphysics seminar and we just covered Kripke on proper names as rigid designators. I think I basically follow the idea — a name like 'Nixon' picks out the same person in every possible world, whereas a description like 'the guy who invented the telephone' just picks out whoever happens to satisfy it in that world, so the name and the description can come apart. Fine. But here's where I get stuck, and my prof's answer didn't really land for me: if 'Nixon' refers to Nixon in every possible world, then there can't be any possible world where Nixon doesn't exist, because then the name would have nothing to latch onto. So doesn't it follow that Nixon exists in all possible worlds — that his existence is metaphysically necessary? Which seems obviously false, since his parents could easily never have met, or met at a different time, or whatever.

And then a second weird consequence falls out of that. If Nixon's existence is a necessary truth, and the only way we ever found out about it was empirical history — archives, birth certificates, all that — then it looks like historians have discovered a necessary truth a posteriori. I've heard the phrase "the necessary a posteriori" thrown around, so maybe that's just literally what people mean by it?? But it feels insane to say that some guy being born in a particular California town is a fact that holds in every possible world. My friend says I'm conflating two different things and then refuses to eleborate, which is annoying.

Can you untangle this for me? And once you've sorted out where I went wrong, I'd also like to know whether the rigid-designator idea is actually doing any real work here, or whether it turns out to be kind of trivial once you clean up my mistake.
