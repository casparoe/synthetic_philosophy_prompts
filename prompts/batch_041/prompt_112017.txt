I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about competence and comprehension, and I would like you to help me predict where she will come out on a case we never reached. She holds, roughly, that doing the right thing reliably and understanding what one is doing are two separate achievements, that the first can exist without the second, and that both Darwin's account of natural selection and Turing's account of computation turn on that separation.

Her settled verdicts, delivered with great confidence, are these. A thermostat that holds a room at a fixed temperature: competence, no comprehension; it does not understand temperature. A chess engine that beats her: competence, no comprehension; it does not understand chess. A bacterium swimming up a sugar gradient: competence, no comprehension. Natural selection producing the vertebrate eye: no comprehension anywhere in the process, yet the eye works. A pocket calculator: competence, no comprehension. A human adult who solves a logic puzzle and can give the reason for each step: comprehension, the real thing. A child learning her first language: comprehension, though she insists the interesting question is how much of it was comprehension and how much was competence.

The case we never reached: a program that proves theorems it was never given the strategy for. It searches, discards and revises its own failed intermediate steps, and can print a human-readable trace in which each step is offered as the reason for the next. Everyone agrees that it is not conscious and has no inner life.

Tell me what my friend will say about this program — comprehension, or competence without comprehension — and defend the prediction. To do that, lay out the several distinct rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, including at least these three: that comprehension requires consciousness; that comprehension requires the system's competence to be its own achievement rather than the gift of a designer or of a selective process; and that comprehension requires only that the system's behavior be caused by representations it can deploy as reasons. Say what each rule predicts about the program, give your best guess at her verdict and your reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and then name the further case, or the smallest set of further cases, that would most cleanly separate the rules.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of the question.
