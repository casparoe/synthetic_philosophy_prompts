I'm a novelist — I run a weekend workshop once a year for about fifteen other novelists, and this year the theme is what your character knows. I've got two hours on the Saturday morning for the session I actually care about, and I've spent the last few weeks chewing on logical omniscience: the idea that if you model what someone knows as everything that follows from what they believe, then everyone knows every logical truth, knows all the consequences of everything they believe, and can never fail to know something that their beliefs already commit them to. I want my group to feel how absurd that is in their bodies before I say the word, and since they're novelists, they'll feel it through characters. So design me the activity.

Here's the shape I have in mind, take it, wreck it, or replace it with something better: everyone gets a character dossier — eight or ten flat assertions about a fictional case, say a stolen violin — plus a rule card with four inference rules. From P and P implies Q, get Q. From P, get P or Q, for any Q at all. From P and Q, get P and Q. And from P, get the sentence that says the character believes P (believing implies believing that you believe it — I want that nail in there, though tell me if it ruins the game). I'm the scorer, with a key, and the scoring rule is the whole point: a character is committed to every sentence that follows from their dossier by those rules, whether or not anyone has ever entertained it. Round one, I fire yes/no questions at them and they answer yes, no, or that their character would have to say they can't tell — points for correct, a heavy penalty for can't-tell when my key says it follows. Round two, a bounty: two points for any consequence of your dossier nobody else has turned in, which I verify on the spot. Round three, I reveal that one dossier has a contradiction buried in it — two facts placed pages apart — so under the rules that character is committed to every sentence in the language and could have swept the bounty round by writing down literally anything. Then the debrief. No money: the prize is picking the restaurant that night and a cheap brass trophy shaped like a magnifying glass.

Give me the dossiers (write them out, they need to actually work), the rule cards, the exact wording of the scoring rule as I should read it aloud so nobody misreads it as being about what they can work out in the time available, a two-hour timeline, the scoring numbers, and the debrief — which should map each moment of the game onto a piece of the problem (the closure rule, the cheap disjunction trick, the contradiction, the introspection rule), and then say what each of the standard repairs actually buys and what it costs: the awareness-style fix where you only count sentences the agent has some grip on, impossible worlds, and the resource-bounded approach where reasoning takes time. Don't say logical omniscience to the group until the debrief.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time. I'm already expecting someone to find the disjunction trick in minute four and turn the bounty round into a joke, so give me the ones I'm not expecting, and how to handle them, including how to keep the whole thing from feeling like a gotcha I sprung on them. And be straight with me about what the game can't do — I assume it won't reproduce the actual argument that any possible-worlds model forces this on you, and I suspect it can't show why closure is exactly the right idealization in the places where it is right, like a proof or a perfect logician. Say what I have to supply out loud myself, and where the game will leave people with a false impression if I don't.

Don't lay out three versions and leave me to pick. Choose the one you'd run on the day, commit to it, and tell me why that one over the others and what you're giving up by choosing it. And start with the central mechanic in a single sentence — the thing the players are actually doing — then everything else after that. No throat-clearing, and don't restate the question back at me.
