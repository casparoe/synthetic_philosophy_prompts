Write a satirical client-facing FAQ page, of roughly 650–750 words, for a fictional firm that sells interval-valued probability forecasts to institutional clients. The piece should be deadpan corporate prose — numbered questions, reassuring headings, the occasional note from a compliance or marketing department — and the humour must come from getting the underlying epistemology exactly right, so that someone who works on imprecise probability recognises the author knows the material rather than winces at it. The target is the widespread assumption that interval forecasts are simply a conservative wrapper around a single probability, and that collecting more data must always narrow an interval.

The central joke should be the phenomenon known in the imprecise-probability literature as dilation. Make sure the piece gets the following right, without ever stating them as a lecture: that a credal set can widen after conditioning on evidence which is independent of the event of interest under every distribution in the set; that the set of posteriors for the event can therefore be more spread out than the set of priors, even though each individual distribution in the set is unchanged in its attitude to that event; that this is a theorem rather than a coding error, so the firm's spokespeople must defend it as a feature; and that dilation is not universal — whether it arises depends on the particular credal set and on whether the independence judgement holds for every member of it, so a firm offering a no-dilation guarantee would be overpromising. It should also be clear, somewhere in the fine print, that dilation is not the trivial case of refusing to update: the updating happens, and the widening is a genuine consequence of it.

The satire should let a reader feel the real philosophical stakes — that dilation sits awkwardly with the intuitive principle that information irrelevant to a hypothesis ought not to change one's attitude to that hypothesis, and that it is a live question whether the principle or the imprecision should give way — without ever resolving it, and without naming any real living person. Invented colleagues, clients and competitors are welcome; the piece may include a testimonial from a sceptical precise Bayesian, provided it is consistent with the position being satirised rather than a straw man. Use British spelling and punctuation throughout.
