I've been reading rationalist blogs and lurking in the comments sections for about a decade, and the thing I keep circling back to is how much of our actual reasoning happens inside made-up stories — Einstein on a beam of light, Galileo's balls off the tower (which I'm told he maybe never actually dropped), somebody saying 'imagine a frictionless plane' and then acting like they've just told you something about the world. I got into a long argument about this last week and I can't let it go. My friend thinks he has the devasting line: a thought experiment is just an argument in a costume. Strip off the narrative, write down the premises, and what's left is either a valid argument you already had or an invalid one that fooled you with vibes. The story adds nothing. I think the story is doing real work — that a thought experiment is closer to an actual experiment, where you build the apparatus in your head, run it, and look at what happened. He says that's a metaphor. I say his view can't explain why the good ones land like discoveries.

So build me a case that pulls these two apart, to spec, and build it fresh — no Maxwell's demon, no Schrödinger's cat, no twins, no elevator, no brain in a vat, no Mary's room, no trolley, no Chinese room, and nothing involving the king of France. I want two thought experiments, both scientific (the conclusion has to be a claim about how the physical world actually is — not about what our words mean, not about what we should do), both about the same real, well-established piece of physics (your pick), and both the kind of thing a working physicist might actually have put in a paper rather than a philosophy-classroom fantasy — so no gods, no magic, and no infinite-precision idealization carrying the load. The one variable I want isolated is the polarity of the conclusion. One of the two has to conclude that something is possible: the experiment succeeds, you watch it happen, and whatever theory said it couldn't has a problem. The other has to conclude that something is impossible: you set it up, it fails, and the failure is supposed to generalize to every version of the setup — and the failure should be genuinly surprising, not a 'well obviously'. Otherwise match them as closely as you can: same kind of cast, same length, same amount of detail, same shape of inference, so that if you handed someone only the stripped-down arguments they'd say the two are the same kind of thing with the same standing. Because that's my prediction about what my friend will say — that the disguised-argument view treats them as evidentially on all fours, same premises, same inference, same force, and any felt difference is just that one conclusion is exciting and the other is a downer.

Then do the bookkeeping and be honest about it, and don't tell me to go read someone, just do the thing. For each of the two, write out the argument the disguised-argument view extracts and say what it thinks that argument is worth. Then say what the run-it-in-your-head view thinks you learn from each, and why — and don't just declare that the two views disagree; the disagreement has to fall out of what each view is committed to, so if you have to bend one of them to get the verdict you want, say so. Then tell me which feature of the cases is actually doing the work. My guess is it's the asymmetry between one successful run and a claim about all runs, but either confirm that or tell me I've picked the wrong feature, and show it by removing or flipping it: if I rewrite the impossibility case as a possibility case (or the reverse), do the two collapse into the same case? If they do, I've isolated the right variable; if they don't, tell me what I actually isolated. Then steelman my friend — I assume his best move is something like 'the impossibility case needs a generality premise, and that premise is where every bit of the work is, so the thought experiment is contributing nothing' — and say whether that reply saves his view or just relocates the puzzle. And if a third story does better, say so: that a thought experiment just pumps an intuition and the intuition is the evidence, in which case the two cases come apart for a totally different reason, because imagining a thing succeeding and imagining it failing aren't symmetric intuitions. Pick whichever of the three is the real fault line and defend it.
