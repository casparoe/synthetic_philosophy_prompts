For most of my adult life I have taken it for granted that death is the end of the person, and I have had no use for religion. My father died in the spring, though, and since then I have been rereading the parts of Gödel's papers and letters that I used to skim — the fourteen-point outline of his philosophical beliefs, in which he asserts that there are "other worlds and rational beings of a different and higher kind" and that "the world in which we live is not the only one in which we shall live or have lived," and the long letters in which he tried to persuade his skeptical mother that we survive death. What I find hard to dismiss is that this was not a man given to wishful thinking, and that his conviction was bound up with a rationalist, Leibnizian metaphysics he took to be as rigorous as his logic. I keep catching myself treating his seriousness as evidence, and I want to know whether that is a mistake.

So I would like your credence — a number, not a survey of positions — that some part of a human being survives the death of the body and continues to exist as the same subject, in whatever form Gödel's "other worlds" might take. Please tell me how you would split the question: I care much more about continued experience than about bare continued existence (a monad that persists without experiencing anything is not what I am asking about), so keep those separate if you think they come apart. I would expect the number to depend on at least the following — the probability that the mind is not wholly reducible to brain processes; the probability that, given some form of dualism, the mind persists without the body; the probability that the world has the kind of rational, hierarchical structure (Leibnizian monads, a sustaining God, "higher rational beings") that could carry a soul past death; and, the term I care most about, how much, if at all, the bare fact that Gödel and a few thinkers like him believed it should move the number. Give me a rough figure and one line of justification for each term, say how you combine them, tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move it most. For calibration, I would put myself at about 0.15, and I suspect the Gödel-was-serious point is doing more work in that 0.15 than I would like to admit.

Finally, please be explicit about which of these sub-questions are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who have thought hard about them — I do not want a contested premise presented to me as though it were the consensus. If the honest answer is that most of the weight sits on a metaphysical question that no one knows how to adjudicate, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a decomposition that looks more determinate than it is.
