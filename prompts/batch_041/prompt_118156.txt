I have been trying to understand updateless decision theory, and a friend in my reading group has produced the following argument against it. Every step strikes me as independently plausible, the conclusion strikes me as clearly false, and I cannot find where the two come apart. I would like you to identify the faulty step, explain why it looked compelling, and say what a defender of the view would have to concede in order to block the argument.

By way of setup: an ordinary causal decision maker evaluates each action by the outcomes it would cause in the situation she now takes herself to be in; she conditions on her observations, then chooses the action with the best expected consequences. Updateless decision theory is meant to differ in this respect. The agent instead chooses her entire policy — a rule mapping observations to actions — by maximizing expected utility from an ex ante standpoint, prior to conditioning on which observations she will in fact make. The motivation is to handle cases such as Newcomb's problem, where what matters is the kind of chooser one is rather than what any single action causes, and counterfactual mugging, where the reward one receives in one branch of the world is made to depend on how one would have chosen in a different branch.

The argument:

(1) A policy is a function from observations to actions. To choose a policy from the ex ante standpoint is to choose it without conditioning on the observations one will later receive.

(2) To choose an action without conditioning on one's observations is to choose it in ignorance of one's evidence.

(3) An action chosen in ignorance of one's evidence can be justified only by the average of its consequences across all the observations one might have made, weighted by their prior probability.

(4) Hence the action an updateless agent performs in any given situation is the action that is best on average across all the situations she might have believed herself to be in, rather than the action best suited to the situation she is actually in.

(5) So if she observes that the building is on fire, she should do whatever would have been best before she knew whether the building was on fire — which may well be to stay in bed.

(6) A decision theory that directs you to act on an average over the evidence you might have had, rather than on the evidence you actually have, is not a theory of rational action at all; it is a recipe for ignoring everything you know.

(7) Therefore updateless decision theory is not a viable decision theory.

I am reluctant to give up the ex ante evaluation of policies, since that appears to be the view's central idea, and I would rather surrender the conclusion than that. But I cannot say which of (1) through (6) does the damage. Please lead with your verdict in the first sentence — naming the step that fails — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the argument. Explain why the faulty step looked plausible, and what my friend would have to give up in order to repair it.
