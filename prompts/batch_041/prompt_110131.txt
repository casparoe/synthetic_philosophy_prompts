I'm taking this seminar on Buddhist philosophy of language and we keep circling back to Nagarjuna's tetralemma — the four corners where, for any claim, you can say it holds, it doesn't hold, it both holds and doesn't hold, or it neither holds nor doesn't hold — and I've noticed somthing that I honestly cant tell is a real parallel or just me pattern-matching. There's this thing called Belnap's four-valued logic (at least that's the version I keep seeing cited) that was cooked up so a computer could answer queries against inconsistent information without the whole system exploding into triviality. It has exactly four values: true, false, both, neither, where 'both' means some sources say true and others say false, and 'neither' means no source has anything to say. So the shape looks identical to the four corners. My question is a comparison and I'd like it done properly: how far does the analogy between the tetralemma and a four-valued logic like Belnap's actually go, and where does it break down?

The reason I care is that there seem to be two very different stories about what the tetralemma is even doing, and they pull in opposite directions. On one story Nagarjuna is basically a proto-paraconsistent logician — the third and fourth corners are genuine live options, so he's refusing the law of excluded middle (and maybe non-contradiction too), and 'emptiness' is just what's left when none of the four corners can be occupied. On the other story the tetralemma isn't a semantics at all, it's a therapeutic device: you march through all four options not because 'both' and 'neither' are real truth-values but to exhaust the space of views until the interlocutor gives up the whole habit of taking a position, and reading him as a logician revising logic misses the point entirely. These two readings disagree about what 'both' and 'neither' are actually doing in the arguments, and probably also about whether Madhyamaka can be squared with classical logic at all.

So can you lay out the comparison for me — what each reading has going for it, what each has to give up, and which one makes better sense of how Nagarjuna actually argues? And then the part I really want: tell me where the parallel with four-valued logic is genuinely illuminating and where it's a false friend. I have a hunch that the interesting disanalogy is that Belnap's 'both' and 'neither' are about what the information sources say, while Nagarjuna's corners are about something else entirely, but I cant put my finger on what. Help me pin that down, and say which of my two readings you'd keep.
