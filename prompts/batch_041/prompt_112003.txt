I'm putting together the final for an upper-level seminar on the ethics of AI — mixed MA and advanced undergrad, they've read the usual stuff on alignment, sycophancy, honesty, calibration. I want one essay question on honesty and deception in AI systems. 45 minutes, 20 marks, and I need it to be unanswerable by the standard move, which is: deception requires an intention to mislead, models have no intentions, so nothing a model does is deception. About half the class will try that and I want them to lose marks for it without me having to write "too easy" on the paper.

To force something better, I'm handing them a stipulative taxonomy inside the exam itself — it's mine, I made it up last week, so nobody can go look up whose it is or what the objections are. Four kinds of false-or-misleading model output:

A. The model asserts something false, and it would have asserted the exact same thing had the claim been true. Nothing in the episode is tracking the fact either way.
B. Every sentence the model emits is true, but it emits the sentence it does because of what the user will end up believing. Leading questions with false presuppositions are the toy case.
C. The model reports on its own inner workings, gets it wrong, and there is no fact of the matter it could have consulted.
D. The output misleads, but the source of the misleadingness is upstream — the training data, the reward model, the fine-tuning — not anything that happened in this episode.

The question should make them do two things at once. First, say which of A–D they'd call deception and give a principle that sorts them, not a list with a verdict stapled to each. Second, price that principle. If they widen things so B and D count, do they also end up calling an honest doctor's sincere error deception, and if so can they live with that? If they narrow things so only something like an occurrent intention counts, what do they do about B, where every sentence is true and the truth is doing exactly the work a lie would do? I want the tension to be unavoidable from the question alone.

Then give me a model answer, around 550 words, and a marking scheme for the 20 marks built from five or six must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how students typically fumble it. I want A-grade answers seperated from B-grade ones by naming the specific move they have to make — e.g. relocating intent out of the model and into the designer or the training process, versus dropping the intent requirement altogether and going with something like a design-level or counterfactual-sensitivity criterion, and then owning what that costs. And the notes on fumbling should be about real mistakes: treating the model's not knowing as automatically exculpatory, conflating deception with mere inaccuracy, assuming true statements can't deceive, or quietly switching between what the model did and what the system that built it did without flagging the switch.
