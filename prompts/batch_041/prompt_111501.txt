I retired from geology about six years ago — spent most of my career on sedimentary basins, nothing philosophical — and since then I've been sitting in on a seminar on Nietzsche and the philosophy of history at the local university. The professor asked me to help grade one of the midterm questions because I've been reading the second Untimely Meditation pretty closely, and I want to be sure I'm not missing anything. So I've pasted the question and the student's answer below, and I'd like you to grade it.

The question was: Nietzsche's essay On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life distinguishes three species of history — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — and also speaks of the unhistorical and the suprahistorical as remedies for the historical disease. Explain what each species is for, what danger or excess is internal to each, and what the unhistorical and suprahistorical remedies consist in. Finally, does Nietzsche think the dangers of history can be avoided, and if so, how? Be sure to say what he means by a culture's plastic power.

The student's answer: At the center of Nietzsche's essay is the claim that history is not valuable in itself but only insofar as it serves life. He identifies three ways of doing history. Monumental history looks to the great figures and deeds of the past as models, so that people who cannot find greatness in their own age can take courage from the fact that greatness was once possible. Its danger is that it invites hero worship and can be used to make the present look contemptible; Nietzsche also warns that it can be seized by the weak and the resentful, who then become fanatics and tyrants. Antiquarian history, by contrast, is the reverent, preserving attitude of the person rooted in a place and a lineage; it gives a people a sense of continuity and gratitude toward its origins. Its danger is that it turns into a blind lust for collecting, so that the past becomes an idol and everything new is measured by whether it fits the old. Critical history is the third species: it drags the past before the bar of judgment, interrogates it, and condemns it, cutting off the roots of inherited customs and beliefs that stand in the way of living. Its danger is that it is hard to find a limit in denying the past; taken too far, it produces skepticism about all inherited values, and even about the possibility of value as such.

The two remedies are the unhistorical and the suprahistorical. The unhistorical is the power of forgetting, the capacity to live in a kind of bounded horizon in which only some things matter; the animal, which grazes and forgets, is the example of a creature that is happy because it is unhistorical. The suprahistorical is really just the unhistorical raised to a higher level: it is the attitude of the person who has given up on history altogether and looks at existence from a standpoint above the flux, so that everything becomes equally meaningless. Nietzsche thinks art and religion are the main suprahistorical forces, because they turn the eye away from becoming and fix it on what is eternal.

As for plastic power, this is the capacity of a culture or an individual to preserve the past faithfully — the stronger the memory, the greater the plastic power, since a strong people is one that can hold on to everything that has happened to it.

Does Nietzsche think the dangers can be avoided? Yes, but only by a proper balance. The historical and the unhistorical must be mixed in the right proportion, and the suprahistorical must be kept in check by the historical. He does not want us to abandon history; he wants us to use it as a means to life, and he thinks critical history, because it is the most clear-eyed of the three, is the species that best serves life in a modern age. The real disease of modernity is that history has become a science for its own sake, so that we know everything about the past and can do nothing with it.
