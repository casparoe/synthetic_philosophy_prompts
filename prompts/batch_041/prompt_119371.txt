I teach a seminar on Mill and the limits of expression, and a puzzle about the shape of modern free-speech law has been nagging at me all term. The law — constitutional doctrine, and in a looser and more erratic form the speech codes of universities and the content policies of platforms — sorts expression into a regulable pile and a protected pile. Into the first go fraud and false advertising, perjury, true threats, incitement to imminent lawless action, obscenity, child sexual abuse material, and some categories of private information; into the second go insults, blasphemy, racial and religious slurs, denial of historical atrocities, and grossly offensive images and speech. The sorting plainly does not track the magnitude of the distress or the social damage, since a slur can wound more deeply than a misleading advertisement. There is a further wrinkle that I find especially telling: the very same words — a threat, a slur, an unwanted sexual remark — can be actionable when aimed at a subordinate in a workplace or at a fellow student in a school, and wholly protected when shouted on a public street, so that the relationship between speaker and audience, rather than the content of what is said, appears to carry much of the weight.

I have a half-formed distinction that I would like you to test against the practice. I have been calling some harms message-constituted — the injury is inseparable from the fact that a particular proposition was communicated and taken up — and others message-independent, where the speech is merely the occasion for an injury that could in principle have been produced another way. My suspicion is that the doctrine is best read as permitting regulation only of message-independent harms, and that the context-sensitivity is what lets it do so while still banning some content. Please tell me whether that reading survives contact with the details. What conception of harm does the practice actually presuppose — a Millian one on which only a distinct, non-message-bound injury to a person's security or interests can justify regulation, a relational or positional view on which the harm consists in the audience's dependence or captivity rather than in the speech itself, or something else? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can; separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit comfortably; point out any parts of the practice that presuppose incompatible things, or that are philosophically underdetermined so that several rival theories would license them; and say what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed theory were replaced with a straightforwardly Millian one. Write in American English.
