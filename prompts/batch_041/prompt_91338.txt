I'm a PhD student in philosophy of social science, drafting a chapter on whether there can be laws of history, and I'd be grateful for your comments on the argument sketch below.

The argument I want to make is a qualified defence of laws of history against the case usually mounted against them. As I understand that case, it runs: the growth of knowledge is among the principal drivers of historical change; we cannot predict the future growth of knowledge, since predicting it would amount to already having it; therefore we cannot derive unconditional predictions of long-run historical outcomes, and there are no laws of history of the kind that would license prophecy.

I think this establishes something narrower than it is usually taken to. What I want to introduce is a category that falls between law and mere trend: a self-undermining regularity, one whose holding depends on its not being widely credited. Take the regularity I use as my running example: in a small research field, a norm of replying to preprints within a fortnight holds only while most participants assume that everyone else is prompt. Circulate a survey showing the true distribution of response times and the norm erodes, because each person now expects delay and adjusts accordingly. The regularity was real, it explained why submissions were answered when they were, and it supported counterfactuals — had you submitted then, you would have waited a fortnight — yet it could not be used to predict response behaviour across any interval in which the prediction became known.

If that is right, the dichotomy between laws, which support prediction, and trends, which do not, leaves no room for regularities that are genuine but epistemically fragile. The answer to whether there can be laws of history is then a qualified yes: there can be, but they are systematically unavailable for the prediction that is usually taken as the mark of lawhood, and that is a feature of the subject matter rather than a defect in our methods. Prophecy fails, but not for the reason usually given.

My worry is that this is either a real refinement or a piece of verbal legislation. What I'd like from you is an assessment of whether the distinction between a self-undermining regularity and an ordinary trend can be drawn without circularity — in particular, whether the counterfactual support I claim for the norm does any independent work, or whether I have simply defined lawhood down until my example fits. I'd also like to know where the sketch overreaches, and whether the conclusion genuinely qualifies the original case or merely restates it. Please respond in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or numbered lists, in the manner of a letter, and in British English.
