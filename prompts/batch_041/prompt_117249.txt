I want you to stress-test a position for me, and I don't want a survey of who said what; I want a defeater map. Think it through properly before you answer.

Here's where I've landed. Omnipotence should be read as a modal notion: a being is omnipotent just in case it can bring about any state of affairs that isn't intrisically incoherent. And intrinsic incoherence is supposed to be a feature of the state of affairs itself — a married bachelor, a square circle — not a fact about the being's abilities or its nature. On this reading the stone paradox doesn't get answered so much as dissolved. The phrase "a stone too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift" doesn't pick out any state of affairs, because the description eats itself; and there's a scope ambiguity buried in it anyway, between "too heavy for any omnipotent being" and "too heavy for the one doing the lifting," where only the second is even a candidate task and it's incoherent for the same reason. So no limit on the power has been found, and on this view none can be found, since every failed task gets reclassified as an incoherent task rather than a task.

What would count against this? I care less about the classic can-he-make-a-rock back-and-forth than about where the view is actually exposed. Specifically:

1. Is the intrinsic-incoherence line principled, or is it just an escape hatch? Every counterexample gets bounced as incoherent. Is there an independent criterion for intrinsic incoherence that isn't just whatever God turns out not to be able to do? If not, does the view buy immunity at the price of vacuity — does "omnipotent" on this reading end up being a property that anything sufficiently capable has trivially, or one that says nothing at all?

2. What does my reading predict that rivals don't? Compare it against (a) the Cartesian universal-possibilism option, where God can do even the logically impossible, and (b) the unrivaled-power reading, where omnipotence is about no being being able to resist you rather than about task completion at all. What case would seperate these, and which way would mine have to go to stay distinct?

3. Push on the modal presuppositions. My reading needs a domain of states of affairs with intrinsic modal properties that hold independently of God — otherwise "intrinsically incoherent" is just whatever God's nature rules out and we're back to the escape hatch. Does that external modal domain sit badly with the rest of classical theism? I'm thinking of the modal collapse worry (divine simplicity plus necessary existence seeming to make everything necessary, which would gut any talk of what God could have done) and of the no-best-world line of thought. Do those actually bear on my view, or am I importing problems that belong to other doctrines?

4. The Augustine-style move: God can't sin, can't lie, can't cease to be, and these aren't defects because they're defects. That's the same shape of move as mine. Which of these is a genuine concession by the view's own lights and which is a rationalization? What's the difference between can't-do-it-because-it-isn't-a-thing-to-do and can't-do-it-because-of-who-I-am?
