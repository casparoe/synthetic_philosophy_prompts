Here's the question I set:

"Everyone teaches Chesterton's fence and Burke's case for deference to inherited institutions as the same idea in two idioms. Are they? Say what each one commits you to, and then say whether either of them gives you a way to tell an institution that has lasted because it's doing real work from one that has lasted only because the people it serves have the power to keep it."

And here's what the student turned in:

"Short version: they aren't the same principle, but they are the same argument, and I think the difference is smaller than it's usually made out to be.

Chesterton's fence is a rule about justification. You're walking along a road and there's a fence across it, and you can't see what it's for. The modern reformer says he doesn't see the point of it and proposes to clear it away. Chesterton says that's backwards: the fact that you can't see the point of the fence tells you something about you, not about the fence. Go and find out why it was put there. If it turns out the fence was keeping the cows out of the vegetable patch and the cows are long gone, then by all means take it down. So the principle isn't a ban on reform; it's a demand that whoever does the reforming understand what he's removing. It's a rule about evidence, and in that sense it's self-liquidating — once you know what the fence was for, you're free to act.

Burke runs the same move at the level of a whole constitution. Property, the church, the courts, the balanced constitution of Crown, Lords and Commons — none of these was designed by anybody. They're the sediment of centuries of trial and error, with each generation adding a bit. Burke's point is that the reason stored up in them vastly exceeds anything a single person could work out for himself, which is why he's so savage about the French revolutionaries, who thought they could rebuild the state out of a few abstract propositions about the rights of man. He also describes society as a partnership between the dead, the living and the unborn, which I take to be a vivid way of saying that the accumulated information in an institution is held in trust and can't be unilaterally thrown away by the people who happen to be alive now.

So Burke's point is Chesterton's point with a longer time horizon and a bigger object. Chesterton sends you off to recover the reason for one fence; Burke tells you that for a constitution the reason is scattered across so many generations that no living person can recover it, so the presumption in favor of what already exists is correspondingly stronger. That's a difference of degree, not of kind.

On the last part of the question, I think Chesterton's version comes out fine: 'go and find out what the fence was for' just is the test for whether it's doing work, so if the answer is that it's there to keep the tenants from leaving, you've got your answer. Burke's version is in more trouble. If the wisdom is dispersed and mostly inarticulate, then the main evidence that an institution is doing something useful is that it has survived, so the longer it's been around, the more confidence it deserves. That does make it hard for him to avoid simply deferring to whatever happens to have lasted, but he'd say the burden is on the critic to show that the thing is a tollgate and not a fence, and that strikes me as a fair reply."
