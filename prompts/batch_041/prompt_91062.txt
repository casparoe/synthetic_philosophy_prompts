I am a third-year philosophy student preparing to take the anti-Molinist side in a seminar debate on divine foreknowledge and human freedom, and I would rather rehearse against a live opponent than against a summary in a textbook. Please play a committed Molinist: someone who holds that God possesses middle knowledge — that is, that God knows counterfactuals of creaturely freedom prior to any act of divine will, that these counterfactuals are contingent truths about what free creatures would do in circumstances that may never obtain, and that they are not made true by God's decree. Stay in character until I say stop. Constraints on the exchange: keep each reply to about a paragraph; do not step outside the role to hand me a balanced survey of the literature; you may mention historical figures, but you may not win a point by appeal to authority or by observing that some other Molinist has an answer — argue the position yourself; you may ask me a clarifying question about a case if you genuinely need one, but otherwise answer directly. Concede what is genuinely lost; do not concede that middle knowledge exists. And tell me which line you actually take, not a catalogue of the lines that others have taken.

My opening move is the grounding objection, stated as tightly as I can manage. Consider a counterfactual of freedom that is true but concerns someone who never exists in the world God actually creates: that a certain possible woman, Cassia, would freely betray a friend if placed in a particular set of circumstances. On your view, that counterfactual was true before God decreed anything at all. It is true, so by the truthmaker principle something makes it true. It is contingent, since Cassia in those very circumstances could have freely done otherwise, so what makes it true cannot be the divine decree, on pain of turning her betrayal into something God settled rather than something she freely did. It cannot be Cassia's own choice, since she never chooses anything; and it cannot be her essence, since an essence fixes what she can do, not what she would freely do. So what is the truthmaker? If you say there is none, explain why the truthmaker principle fails for precisely the class of propositions Molinism needs it to fail for, and why that is not an ad hoc exemption written to order. If you say that God's ideas or God's nature make these counterfactuals true, explain why that is not the decree under another name. If you say they are brute contingent facts, explain where such facts are located, given that they are about no part of the created world. Choose one, and I will press it.
