I teach a final-year metaphysics option and every year the same thing happens: the dispositions week is the one the students actually enjoy, and the week on situationism in social psychology is the one where they walk out convinced character is a fiction. This year I want to force the two together, and I want you to do the forcing.

Here's the episode. Milgram's obedience experiments at Yale in the early sixties. You know the shape of it: a volunteer turns up for a study of learning and memory, is cast as the teacher, watches a man get strapped into a chair in the next room, and is then instructed by a bloke in a grey lab coat to deliver what he believes are increasingly painful electric shocks for every wrong answer. In the baseline condition something like two thirds of them go all the way to the end of the dial. The proximity conditions are the interesting ones: as the victim gets closer -- same room, then having to press his hand down onto the plate -- obedience falls off a cliff, and when the experimenter leaves the room it drops further again. I know the archive work complicates the published numbers and that plenty of subjects suspected the shocks weren't real, so let's bracket all that. Take the published findings as the case; the point here is conceptual, not historical.

What I want is the episode read through the disposition / mask / fink machinery. Quick reminder of the kit, since I dont want the answer to collapse into the standard situationist sermon: a disposition can fail to manifest because it has been removed by the very thing that would normally trigger it (a fink), or because something blocks the manifestation while the disposition stays perfectly in place (a mask), or because the behaviour that looks like the manifestation is actually being produced by something else altogether (a mimic). The whole point of that literature is that the naive conditional analysis -- x is disposed to M when S just in case, if S then x Ms -- fails for exactly these reasons, and that you therefore cannot read dispositions straight off the behaviour.

So: is the obedient subject's failure to intervene a masked compassion-disposition, or a finkish one, or is the obedience itself the disposition in question, and if so what do the proximity and experimenter-absent conditions tell us about which of the three we're dealing with? Does the dispositional vocabulary actually earn its keep here, or is it situationism with more furniture? And run it the other way too: does Milgram's design hand the metaphysics anything it needs -- for instance a real case in which you can prise a mask apart from a fink by the fact that the disposition comes back once the masker is removed, or by whether it comes back at all? Then push on where the analogy breaks. My suspicion, which I'd like tested rather than confirmed, is that the trait the situationists are denying is not the same kind of thing as the disposition the metaphysicians are talking about, and that if so the whole dispute is closer to verbal than either side wants to admit -- which would be a boring result and I'd rather find out now than in front of twenty undergraduates.

Give me the argument in prose first, then finish with a table so I can put it straight on a slide. Columns: Episode element; Disposition in question; Threat type (fink / mask / mimic / none of these); What this reading explains; Where it strains; Confidence (high / medium / low). Six rows or so, and open the whole thing with a single-sentence bottom line.
