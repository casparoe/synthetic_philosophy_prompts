ok so i keep having the same argument with a friend and i want to see it written out properly, so write me an essay.

the question is whether a trained network really 'represents' anything, or whether that's just a useful way of talking. heres the setup i want you to work with. take some feature of the input -- call it F -- and suppose interpretability work gives you two kinds of evidence for the claim 'the network represents F'.

evidence of the first kind: you train a linear probe on the activations at some layer and it decodes F with 95% accuracy. the information is in there, linearly readable, and it lines up with the model's own behaviour.

evidence of the second kind: you find a direction and you intervene on it -- ablate it, or steer along it -- and the model's outputs change in exactly the way you'd predict if that direction were carrying F. causal, not just correlational.

now suppose the two come apart, in both directions.

case A: the probe decodes F at 95%, but when you project out the direction it's using, the output barely moves. the network has a redundant copy somewhere else, or it just recomputes F from scratch two layers down. kill the probe direction and the behaviour survives.

case B: you find a direction whose ablation reliably wrecks the behaviour and whose steering moves the behaviour the way you'd predict -- but no linear probe gets F out of the activations above chance, because whatever is doing the work is smeared across a dozen features at once. the causal role is there; the readable encoding isn't.

so: does 'the network represents F' have a determinate truth value? do cases A and B come out differently? which of the two kinds of evidence is the real test of the claim, or is insisting on either one of them just an arbitrary choice about which decomposition we happen to like? and if neither is privileged -- if for any behaviour you can find some direction that 'explains' it, since the space is huge and the network is redundant -- does realism about interpretability collapse, or does it just turn out that 'represents' here means something weaker than people want it to mean?

i dont want an essay that ends with 'well it depends what you mean by representation'. thats the opening move, not the conclusion. pick a sense of 'represents', say which of the two cases it makes decisive and which it makes a red herring, defend that choice against the obvious objection that you've just defined the answer into existence, and be honest about what you're giving up. write it as prose, not bullet points, and dont bother with citations -- i care about the argument, not the reading list.

end the essay with a final line of the form: Verdict: <one sentence saying whether a network can literally represent a feature, and under what condition>.
