My verdicts on when a credibility discount amounts to an injustice, and I want the principle behind them. A doctor waves off a woman's account of her own pain as anxiety: injustice. A doctor gives less weight to a patient with a documented history of exaggerating symptoms: fine. A search committee discounts every candidate's self-description because candidates inflate: fine. A hiring manager discounts an applicant because of her accent: injustice. A jury disbelieves a confident eyewitness after learning the witness was coached: fine. A journalist treats residents of a company town as unreliable about the company's pollution, on the grounds that they have over-reported it before and the base rate is real: injustice, though I can't say why, since the discount is statistically sound and improves accuracy. A doctor who disbelieves a patient's report of pain out of conscious racial bias and happens to be right, because the patient is exaggerating this time: still injustice. A clinic that corrects group-level pain reports because published studies show that group over-reports at any given injury level, and so doses more accurately on average: injustice, and yet the doctor who instead relies on the known individual exaggerator strikes me as fine. The case I keep going back and forth on: a triage algorithm that down-weights symptom reports in certain dialects because it was trained on data in which those reports were less often corroborated, with no prejudiced hearer anywhere in the loop.
