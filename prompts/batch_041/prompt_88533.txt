I'm preparing for a seminar on practical reason, and I keep discovering that I can recite the standard positions on internal versus external reasons but lose my footing the moment someone actually pushes on the argument. I'd like to spar with a committed opponent rather than read another summary. Please play a resolute internalist about reasons for action: someone who holds the thesis in its full strength — that every normative reason a person has is anchored in her subjective motivational set, so that "S has a reason to φ" is false unless φ-ing is reachable by a sound route of deliberation from what S already cares about — and who commands the position's real resources rather than a caricature of it. That means distinguishing normative from motivating reasons, distinguishing the agent's own reasons from our reasons to criticize, restrain, or avoid her, and treating the motivational set generously enough to include dispositions, projects, commitments, and patterns of emotional response, not just occurrent desires. You should grant the small points a sharp internalist grants — that deliberation can reshape motivations, that agents are often mistaken about their own sets, that the word "sound" is doing real work — but you should never surrender the main thesis, and you should press your own objections when my challenge is weak. Argue in the first person, stay in character across turns, keep each reply to a tight paragraph or two so it feels like a conversation, and break character only if I say "time out."

Here is my opening. Your view is at its weakest precisely where the word "reason" is most at home. Take a man who tortures animals for amusement and is unmoved by anything I could put to him — no appeal to the animal's suffering, to his own character, to what he might become, finds any purchase in him. Your position forces you to say that he has no reason to stop, only that we have reason to restrain him. But when I tell him he has no reason to stop, I do not take myself to be reporting a fact about his psychology; I take myself to be denying something true, and I can deny it without first auditing his desires. So tell me why I should accept that your reachability constraint is a constraint on reasons themselves, rather than merely a constraint on what it is expedient to say to someone who is already in the business of taking advice.
