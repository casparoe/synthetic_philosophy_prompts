My father is ill. There is a treatment that would cost roughly my entire savings and that offers him, on the best evidence I have, perhaps a one-in-five chance of several more years of reasonable health. If I do not spend the money that way, I will give it to an organization that I have strong reason to believe would use it to save several lives outright. I have thought about this for months, and I find that I cannot say that either course of action is better than the other. It is not that I take them to be equally good; I would not say that either. It is rather that they do not present themselves to me as items on one scale, and no amount of arithmetic laid over them produces a verdict I can endorse. I know which one I will do. I cannot say that doing it is better.

A friend who has studied decision theory tells me that this is not a position a rational person can hold, and he offers a demonstration. If I truly have no ranking, he says, then when he offers to take the first course of action off my hands and give me the second in exchange, less a small handling fee, I have nothing to object with, since I do not hold the first to be better than the second; and when he then offers to take the second off my hands and give me the first back, less another fee, I again have nothing to object with, since I do not hold the second to be better than the first. He may do this as often as he likes. At the end I am where I began, minus everything I have paid him, and at no single step can I say that he has made me worse off. He adds that I am in the position of a person whose preferences run in a circle — A over B, B over C, C over A — whom everyone agrees to be irrational, since such a person can be walked around the circle, paying a little at each step, and left exactly where he started with nothing to show for it.

I want to resist, but I need to know precisely what to resist. And one thing troubles me, because it cuts against me: depending on my mood, and on which of the two I happened to think about last, my inability to rank them sometimes hardens and sometimes nearly dissolves. If it is that unstable, perhaps it is not a considered judgment about value at all, but only noise, and my friend is right that there is nothing there to defend.

So: is he entitled to his conclusion? If he is not, name the step he is not entitled to and say what it assumes. If I hold my ground, tell me what I am taking on — what discipline I owe myself that someone who simply picked a scale would not owe. And if I cannot have both of the things I want — my refusal to rank the two, and my confidence that my choice is defensible — tell me which one is cheaper to give up.
