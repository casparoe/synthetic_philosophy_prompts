I am a novelist, a little over three-quarters of the way through a book whose protagonist is a diplomatic interpreter — a woman trained to render other people's sentences with total fidelity — who has never in her adult life uttered a sentence she believed to be false, and who has nonetheless wrecked four lives by saying only true things in an order that made each of her listeners believe something untrue. The book has become a study of what I have come to call, in my notes, the difference between fabrication and curation: fabrication puts a falsehood into the world, whereas curation arranges truths already in the world so that the audience supplies the falsehood itself. A defence barrister I dine with insists the two are morally indistinguishable, that any felt difference is an artifact of how easy it is to point at a false sentence and say, there, that is the lie, whereas curation leaves no sentence to point at; a screenwriter in the same circle insists the reverse, that curation is the graver of the two because it corrupts the listener's own reasoning instead of merely handing her a wrong answer. Neither of them will give me a number, which is why I am asking you.

Take the proposition to be assessed to be this: holding fixed (a) the false belief the listener ends up with, (b) the speaker's foresight and intention that she end up with it, (c) the harm that ensues, and (d) whatever role-based entitlement to rely the speaker's position confers on the listener, a true utterance knowingly curated to produce that belief is pro tanto less blameworthy than a false assertion producing the same belief in the same listener. What is your credence that this is so — that the difference between fabrication and curation lies in the acts themselves rather than merely in the circumstances that usually attend them? I want the number decomposed, at minimum into: the probability that asserting what one believes false carries a wrong of its own, over and above the wrong of producing a false belief; the probability that a listener's entitlement to rely attaches to the content of what is said rather than to the uptake she is invited to take, so that the curator exploits a convention rather than breaking one; the probability that the usual exculpation for the curator — that the listener could have asked a follow-up, that she was a co-author of her own credence — is a genuine mitigation rather than a second and distinct wrong; the probability that the difference survives the hardest case, in which no implicature is exploited at all and the curator merely puts four true facts in an order that does the work; and the probability that what we are tracking is not a moral difference at all but a difference in how easily a bad will can be evidenced and narrated — a difference, that is, in the prosecution's case rather than in the crime.

Then combine the terms, tell me which one your number hangs on most, and say what argument or evidence would move it furthest. One further thing, since it is the thing I actually cannot decide: my own credence is about 0.6, the barrister's is about 0.2, and the screenwriter's about 0.8, and I suspect we are not disagreeing about the moral psychology so much as about whether curation without implicature is even an act of deception or merely a piece of storytelling that a listener mistook for testimony — so tell me where the disagreement most plausibly lives, and whether the term that carries my 0.6 is the same term that carries their 0.2 and their 0.8.
