I want to argue a hard case before a textualist judge, and I need you to be that judge. Play a committed textualist in the new-textualist tradition: for you, a statute means what its enacted words conventionally mean in their legal context at the time of enactment, not what anyone hoped the statute would do; you treat terms of art as carrying their technical legal senses; you read a statute as a coherent whole; and you will consult a title or a purpose clause only to resolve a genuine ambiguity, never to override operative text that is clear. You may concede that canons conflict with one another and that applying them takes judgment, and you may concede that a ruling is costly or that the legislature should clean up its own mess. Do not concede that your method is covertly purposive, that legislative intentions or legislative history carry weight, or that a court may improve a badly drafted statute. You are hearing a motion to dismiss, and by the end you will have to rule. Assume the facts I give you; if you need one more fact, ask for it the way a judge would, not as a request to restate the problem. Stay in character until I say "stop"; step out only if I ask a direct question about the exercise. Keep each reply to a paragraph or so, mostly questions with reasons, in a dry judicial register, and interrupt me whenever you like. Do not cite cases or scholarship; I want your reasoning, not a reading list.

The statute, which I have invented for this exercise, reads in full:

An Act to Protect Homebuyers from Latent Construction Defects

§ 1. Purpose. The purpose of this Act is to ensure that a buyer of a completed dwelling who could not reasonably have discovered a latent construction defect before purchase is not left without a remedy against the seller who built the dwelling.

§ 2. Liability. A seller of a completed dwelling shall not be liable to the buyer for a latent construction defect unless the buyer had constructive notice of the defect before purchase.

§ 3. Definitions. "Latent construction defect" means a defect in the construction of a dwelling that is not observable by a reasonable inspection at the time of purchase. "Seller" means the person who constructed the dwelling and first conveyed it.

§ 4. Damages. A prevailing buyer may recover the cost of repair, together with reasonable attorney's fees.

I represent the buyer; the seller has moved to dismiss. My opening: Your Honor, the seller's motion rests entirely on the technical sense of "constructive notice," and it is true that my client had none, because the defect was behind the drywall and invisible to any inspection. Read that way, § 2 makes the Act protect only buyers who already knew of the defect, which is to say it protects no one, in a statute whose § 1 announces that its purpose is to protect buyers who could not have discovered the defect. Everything I rely on is enacted text: the title, the purpose clause, and the words of § 2 themselves. So, three questions. First, by what principle does the technical sense of "constructive notice" beat the ordinary sense in which statutory words are normally taken — is "plain meaning" a property of the words, or a choice of which register of English the court reads in? Second, if the technical sense wins, why does the terms-of-art canon outweigh the whole-act rule that bids you read § 2 so that § 1 is not a lie? Third, if purpose clauses are not operative, why may the court not use § 1 to fix the register of § 2, when § 1 is as much an enacted sentence as § 2 is? And I will say what I take the stakes to be: the moment the Court selects among these resources by asking which reading leaves the Act coherent, it is doing precisely what textualists say only purposivists do.
