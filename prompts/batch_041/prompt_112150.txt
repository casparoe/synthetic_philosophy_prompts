I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, and there's a recurring move in seminars and conference Q&As that I can describe in detail but cannot name. It concerns the phrase "Western values" and its cousins — "the Western tradition," "our values," "Enlightenment values." The shape is this. Someone asserts that a particular value, say individual liberty or the rule of law, is a Western value. You observe that the societies supposedly native to that value ran slave empires and inquisitions, and that their jurists and divines defended both at length; the reply is that these were betrayals of the value rather than instances of it, since the West's values are what the West aspires to and its crimes are failures to live up to them. Very well. But now you observe that something recognizably like the same value was articulated, at length and with sophistication, in a quite different setting — in Chinese or Islamic political writing, for instance; and here the reply changes shape: that was not really the same thing, because the concept there was embedded in a wholly different metaphysical and legal framework. Internal counterexamples are thus demoted to failures, external parallels demoted to coincidences, and the extension of the term contracts on one side and expands on the other depending on which direction the objection arrives from. A second feature is that the phrase is used descriptively and normatively in one breath. "These are our values" is offered as a report on what a population actually holds, so that criticism of the values as bad meets the answer that this is what we in fact believe; the same phrase is then offered as a standard, so that the fact that many people in the relevant population do not hold them meets the answer that they are not living up to our values. Each claim shields the other in turn. A third feature: no one can say what makes the items on the list items of one list. Sometimes the unifying thread is Christianity, sometimes the Enlightenment, sometimes Athens and Rome, sometimes simply "not Islam," sometimes "not Confucianism," and the criterion shifts with the argument being made.

I don't think this is merely the no-true-Scotsman manoeuvre, since the speaker need not be revising a definition under pressure or acting in bad faith — the retreat is usually sincere, it is distributed across many speakers and texts rather than located in one person, and it includes the outward-facing move as well as the inward one. Nor is it quite the thesis that certain concepts are essentially contested, since that is a claim about what a concept is like rather than about how this particular argumentative pattern works. I have a vague sense that there is literature on traditions being invented, and on definitions being re-engineered so as to do persuasive work, but neither seems to capture the two-sided asymmetry or the descriptive/normative shuttle.

Is there an established name for this configuration? If there is, tell me what it is and where it stops being the right description of what I've described. If there isn't, coin a term, define it precisely, and tell me what it excludes: the nearest neighbours it must be distinguished from, and one or two boundary cases where it is genuinely unclear whether the pattern is present. The label matters less to me than the carving — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
