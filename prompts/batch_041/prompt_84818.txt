A friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about disagreement — we had each read the same things, reached opposite verdicts, and each of us thought the other was being stubborn in precisely the way we would have forgiven in ourselves — and we got nowhere, which I gather is itself supposed to be evidence of something. I would like to turn the argument into a quiz for my blog, so that readers can discover which position on peer disagreement they actually hold, as opposed to the one they would recite if asked directly. Please write that quiz.

To save you from having to reconstruct the literature, here is the logical space I want it to cover, though you should feel free to add a position or an axis I have missed.

The scope axis: does the pressure to adjust fall on your credence, on your outright belief, or on both — and is the question what you are required to believe or merely what you are permitted to believe, given that two people can be equally reasonable in holding different views? The trigger axis: what makes the other party a peer — identical evidence, comparable competence, the same reasoning, or something you cannot check from the outside at all; and do facts about their track record, their mood, their incentives, or the stakes for you count as evidence about their peerhood? The response axis: equal weight; weight by relative competence; treat their verdict as simply one more item in the total evidence; stand firm; stand firm only when you can point to the specific error in their reasoning; or split the difference in what you say and do while privately keeping your belief. The domain axis: whether the answer is the same for science, mathematics, morality, matters of taste, and philosophy itself — including the question you are writing about, which is where the self-undermining worry bites.

Positions I want to appear as leaves, at minimum: strict conciliationism or the equal-weight view; the total-evidence view; steadfastness; the right-reasons view; permissivism about rational belief; a two-level view that conciliates credences while keeping beliefs, or the reverse; a domain-relative view; a no-shared-ground view for disagreements about the standards of assessment themselves; a self-trust view on which you are entitled to your own reasoning because you have no alternative but to rely on it; and the view that the whole puzzle is an artefact of treating a social predicament as a purely evidential one. If you think any two of these are the same position, do not pad the list — say so and merge them.

Every question must be one concrete case, not a request for a general policy. Use at least these, and invent others: two doctors with identical evidence, one of whom has twenty years more experience; two people with identical evidence, one of whom you know has been awake for thirty hours, so you have a defeater about their processing but not their data; a lone dissenter against nine colleagues who have all read the same paper; a disagreement with your own past self, who held the opposite view on the same evidence; a disagreement whose other party you suspect reached their verdict because of how they were raised, in a way you cannot demonstrate; a disagreement about whether the two of you count as peers; a case where you agree on all the evidence and disagree about what counts as evidence; a peer whom you know to be systematically biased in a direction you can model, so you can partially discount them; a case where you are the one out of step and have no way to check; and a singular prediction that will not be settled until later.

No question may name a position or use the vocabulary of the debate — the words conciliation, peer, and defeater should not appear in the question text — and every answer option must be something a reader could say without knowing which position it belongs to. No position should be identifiable from a single answer, and no question should have an option that is obviously the right one.

The distinction I care most about, because my friend and I kept sliding over it, is between adjusting your confidence and adjusting what you are willing to assert or act on; make sure the quiz catches the reader who conciliates in the second half of that sentence and stands firm in the first. Assume readers of a general-interest philosophy blog: some have read a little epistemology, none know the literature.

Deliverable: ten to fourteen questions, three options each; then a scoring key as a table with one row per leaf position, giving the position's name or "unnamed", the pattern of answers that lands a reader there, the commitment that position has the hardest time explaining, and whether anyone actually holds it. Before the questions, one short paragraph on the two or three axes that do the real sorting; after the table, a note on which pairs of leaves the quiz cannot separate and why. Where a combination of answers matches no position anyone defends, say so and diagnose it: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Keep the whole thing under 1,400 words.
