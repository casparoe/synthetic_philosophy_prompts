I am writing a reported essay on how courts handle statistical evidence, and I want to build it around the case of Sally Clark: the English solicitor convicted in November 1999 of murdering her two infant sons after the paediatrician Sir Roy Meadow told the jury that the chance of two cot deaths in a family like hers was about one in seventy-three million, a figure he reached by squaring his estimate of the chance of a single cot death. The Royal Statistical Society later said publicly that the figure had no statistical basis and objected to the misuse of statistics in court; the conviction was upheld on appeal in 2000 and quashed on a second appeal in 2003, when it emerged that the prosecution pathologist had not disclosed microbiological findings suggesting the younger child had died of natural causes.

Every telling of this case turns it into the prosecutor's fallacy — the transposition of the probability of the evidence given innocence and the probability of innocence given the evidence — and that lesson has been thoroughly drilled. I want the problem one level beneath it. The reference class problem is what interests me: a frequency is a frequency in some class, and Meadow's figure was less false than unattached. He drew his rate from a class defined by whatever the underlying study happened to record — affluence, smoking, the age of the parents — with nothing to say that those are the features that matter.

So do this. Reconstruct the competing reference classes that were genuinely available in that courtroom, say what each one yields, and then tell me whether any principled rule — take the narrowest class for which you have adequate data, or take the class defined by the features causally relevant to the outcome — actually selects one, or whether the choice is underdetermined and the single-case probability is therefore indeterminate rather than merely unknown. Tell me whether the squaring error is better described as a mistake about independence or as a mistake about the class, given that the relevant class for a second death is not the class for a first. Then run the analysis the other way: what does the episode establish about the reference class problem itself? In particular, does the fact that the conviction finally fell on a non-disclosure point rather than on the statistics show that the law routes around the problem — through admissibility rules, through its insistence that proof be about this defendant and not about a class — rather than answering it? And test one reframing for me: that what was wrong with Meadow's evidence was not its probability but its weight, a large loose class being thinner support than a small close one. Does that distinction illuminate the case, or does it merely relocate the reference class problem?
