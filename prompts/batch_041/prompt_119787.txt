I'm reading Hume's Of Money with a reading group and theres a passage early on I cant get past. He says money isn't really one of the subjects of commerce, only an instrument for exchanging one commodity for another, "none of the wheels of trade" and "the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy." Then he says that considered within a single kingdom the greater or less plenty of money is of no consequence, since prices are always proportioned to the plenty of money, and a crown in Henry VII's time served the same purpose as a pound does now. I think I follow the arbitrariness-of-units point well enough. But later in the same essay he talks as if an influx of gold and silver can actually get industry going and keep people busy, which sounds like money having real effects — at least while prices and wages are still catching up. How are these two halves supposed to fit together? Is the "of no consequence" line meant only as a long-run comparison once everything has adjusted, with the real effects belonging to the transition, or is Hume just inconsistent? And what exactly is the oil metaphor supposed to rule out — does it commit him to a strict quantity theory of money, or something weaker?
