You are grading a written response to an exam question in a graduate seminar on the interpretation of quantum mechanics. The question and the candidate's answer are reproduced in full below.

Question:

In the Everettian (many-worlds) reading, quantum mechanics is governed by a single deterministic, unitary law; the Schrödinger equation never selects one outcome over another. Address the following.

(a) State precisely what is meant by the "probability problem" (sometimes called the incoherence problem) in this setting, and explain how it differs from the preferred-basis problem.

(b) A classmate proposes the following resolution: "Because every outcome is realized in some branch, the probability of an outcome is simply the number of branches containing it divided by the total number of branches. Since decoherence tends to generate branches of comparable size, the resulting statistics will match the Born rule." Evaluate this proposal. Is branch counting a viable route to the Born rule? Justify your answer.

(c) Outline the leading alternative strategy that Everettians use to recover the Born rule, and state what, if anything, that strategy assumes.

Candidate's answer:

(a) The probability problem is this: if the dynamics are deterministic and unitary, then nothing in the physics privileges one outcome; the theory says that all outcomes occur, one in each branch. It is therefore unclear what it could mean to say that one outcome is more likely than another, or on what grounds a probabilistic prediction could be made at all. This is distinct from the preferred-basis problem, which asks why decoherence singles out a particular basis in which branches become stable and non-interfering. The latter is a question about which decomposition of the state is physically significant, not a question about probability.

(b) I take the proposal to be basically right, and to capture the standard Everettian strategy. If every branch is equally real, there is no basis on which to favor one over another, so the natural measure is branch count; and because decoherence multiplies branches rapidly and roughly evenly, the relative frequencies come out as the Born rule predicts. On this picture the amplitudes are really just a calculational device for tracking interference, with no further physical significance once the branches have separated. The one refinement I would add is that the count must be taken over branches as defined by decoherence, rather than over arbitrary superpositions.

(c) The leading alternative is the decision-theoretic approach. One considers an agent who is about to bet on the outcome of a measurement and requires that the agent's preferences satisfy ordinary rationality constraints; from these constraints together with the physics, one derives that the agent must bet as though the outcomes carried the Born-rule weights. This is essentially Everett's own derivation, and its crucial assumption is a principle of indifference: because the agent has no information favoring one branch over another, she must assign them equal credence.
