In February 1959, Swiss men voted by roughly two to one — 67 per cent — against granting women the federal vote. In February 1971, an electorate that was still exclusively male approved the same reform by 65.7 per cent, and women voted in a federal election for the first time that October. In the intervening years a number of cantons had already admitted women to cantonal votes; one canton, Appenzell Innerrhoden, refused until the Federal Supreme Court ordered it to comply in 1990; and an organized federation of women campaigned against the reform in both federal votes.

I want this episode analyzed through the democratic boundary problem: the claim that the composition of the demos cannot be settled democratically, because any procedure for deciding who belongs presupposes an answer to who is entitled to decide. Please keep the two directions of the analysis apart.

The theory on the case. Who is the demos at each vote, who is bound by the outcome, and what exactly does the boundary problem predict here? Set out the two principles usually offered as the criterion for inclusion — that all whose interests are affected should have standing, and that all who are subject to the decision's coercion should — and say which one the Swiss case fits and which one it embarrasses. Note that the excluded were not of one mind: say whether the existence of an anti-suffrage women's movement weakens the claim to standing (standing being independent of what the affected think) or genuinely complicates the all-affected principle as a criterion. Say also whether it matters that this boundary was internal — a class of persons already inside the polity's households and workplaces — rather than the external boundary of immigration or secession about which the problem is usually posed. Where does the boundary problem misdescribe what happened, or fall silent?

The case on the theory. The 1971 vote looks like a demos enlarging itself, which is what the impossibility claim says cannot happen. Was it? Consider whether that vote can be an act of self-government for the women it bound; whether the inclusion is legitimate because later ratified by the enlarged demos, or illegitimate because granted by a body with no standing to withhold it; and what the 1990 court order, which completed the inclusion by non-democratic means, does to the analysis. Then test the result against a second, shorter case: the 2014 Scottish independence referendum, whose franchise ran on residence — EU and Commonwealth citizens living in Scotland and sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds included, Scots living elsewhere in the United Kingdom excluded. Show what the all-affected principle would demand there and why that is usually taken as a reductio, and say what the contrast reveals about which principle was doing the work in the Swiss case.

End with a verdict on whether the Swiss episode confirms the boundary problem, is an anomaly for it, or exposes a distinction the theory has been missing. I want an argued case analysis, not a survey of positions, and I would rather see the theory declared silent at a point than stretched to cover it.
