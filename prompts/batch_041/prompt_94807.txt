My mate Dan and I got into a long, increasingly loud arguement last night about whether it matters who discovers something first, and I came away with a pattern I can't name.

Take Darwin and Wallace. When someone wants to take Darwin down a peg, the move is: Wallace worked the whole thing out on his own, on the other side of the world, having never seen a word of Darwin's notes — which supposedly shows the idea was in the air and Darwin was just the guy with the leisure and the connections. Now watch the same fact get used the other way. That Wallace got there entirely on his own is also the entire reason we call him a co-discoverer rather than a footnote: it's what proves he was a real discoverer and not a borrower. One fact, two jobs, and it happens to shrink one man's credit and inflate the other's. I see the same shape in the calculus mess — the story everyone tells is that Newton had it first and sat on it for years, Leibniz published first, and the fact that Leibniz built his version independently gets used to deflate Newton (so the whole thing was ripe, no big deal) while simultaneously being what earns Leibniz his place in the story.

And it's not only the independence fact. When the second person obviously *did* know about the first — no independence available — people switch to "well, someone was bound to find it", which still only ever seems to dock the earlier person, while the later one gets praised for "recognising its importance". So whatever the ripening fact is, it's treated as a debit for whoever came first and a credit for whoever came second.

Is there a name for that? I know there's a literature on discoveries happening twice (some mid-century sociologist made a list of them), and I know there's a "everything's named after the wrong person" law, and the whole great-man-versus-the-times debate. But those are about how common it is, or about who ends up remembered. I'm after the specific both-ways move with a single fact. If it has no name, name it, and be precise about what it is and isn't — how it differs from just saying the discovery was inevitable, from the genetic fallacy, and from a plain double standard. And where are the edges? Is it still the same thing if the two of them were corresponding and one was being cagey? What if the later person is clearly the weaker thinker but got into print first? I dont want a label slapped on it, I want to know what the actual thing is.
