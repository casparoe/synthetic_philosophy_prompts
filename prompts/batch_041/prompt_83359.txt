I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on formal epistemology and decision theory, and I need to turn a nagging objection into a proposal for a one-year fellowship (the deliverable would be a single paper of roughly 25–30 pages; there is no lab work, no empirical component, so everything has to be argument and formalism I can do at a desk). The objection began as a disagreement with a friend in an economics seminar, where everyone treated it as a platitude that free information cannot hurt you: if someone offers you a free look at the coin before you bet, or a free test result before you choose a treatment, you could always just ignore what you are told and act as though you had never been told it, so your situation cannot get worse. I accept the formal result behind the platitude — the one usually called Good's theorem — and I accept that it is a theorem under its own assumptions: for an agent who maximises expected utility with a fixed prior, a fixed and state-independent utility function, and a fixed set of available acts, expected utility is weakly increasing as the information partition is refined. What bothers me is that the informal gloss does no real work in the proof, and looks false as stated, because declining to use information as evidence is not the same as not having it as part of one's situation. Once the utility of an act depends on what the agent believes — the patient who would rather not know, the person who pays a premium not to find out, the agent who is stung by anticipated regret, anyone whose confidence or peace of mind enters the payoff directly — the information changes the value of the very act you were going to choose anyway, and no act of ignoring can restore the old payoff. My suspicion is that the phrase "the value of information is non-negative" is really three or four different claims that the literature has run together (about the ex ante expectation versus the outcome once you have looked; about refining a fixed partition versus enlarging the decision problem itself; about what a rational rule must permit versus what a particular representation happens to deliver), and that once they are separated, some of them turn out to be substantive constraints on any acceptable account of rational choice while others are artifacts of the expected-utility representation. I have a hunch that non-expected-utility rules, and rules whose evaluation is sensitive to the partition an agent faces, will supply counterexamples, but I have not yet built a case I am happy with, and I do not know how to state the target claim so that the question has a determinate answer rather than collapsing into terminology. That is the part I need help with. I would rather see the roads not taken than a single confident framing.
