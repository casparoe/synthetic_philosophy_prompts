I retired from experimental biology a few years ago and have been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, where I recently watched two members spend an entire evening on a case I still cannot adjudicate. The case: a man treats his wife badly, and by his own account nothing about her, or about being a decent person, moves him — he has no desire to change, no interest in her feelings, and nothing he wants that changing would serve. One member, A, held that the cruelty is nevertheless a reason for him to stop. Reasons, she said, are considerations that count in favour of acting, and whether a consideration counts does not depend on whether the man happens to care; if it did, "he has a reason to stop" would amount to no more than "we do," and criticism of him would be a report about the critic. The other member, B, answered that a consideration which could not possibly move the man is not a reason for him but only a reason for the man he would be if he cared, and that what makes something a reason for someone is that it bears on something he already cares about, or would come to care about through deliberation beginning from what he already cares about. A replied that this deliberation clause either does no work or does too much: if it means whatever ends he would endorse after reflecting from where he stands, then a man who reflects and cheerfully endorses his cruelty has no reason to change, which cannot be right; if it means reflection that corrects his ends, then external reasons have been smuggled in under another name. B replied that the clause is not empty — the man is not merely indifferent but inattentive, and the facts he ignores are ones he would care about if he saw them clearly, given concerns he does have (being thought decent, not being a hypocrite) — and that a reason which could move no one is not a reason but a disguised demand. A answered that a demand can be true: some considerations count in favour of acting even when no one is disposed to be moved by them.

I gather this is the internalism/externalism debate about reasons, though my own training treats "reason" as a kind of cause, so I may be missing the point. Please adjudicate: tell me which of A's and B's moves are right and which are mistaken, and what each side is missing. I would especially like to know (i) whether A's claim that considerations can count in favour independently of anyone's motives has content beyond restating her conclusion, and (ii) whether B's talk of what the man would care about after deliberating from his existing concerns can be made precise, or whether it inevitably collapses either into A's position or into a bare description of the man as he already is. Where people who know this literature agree, say so plainly; where the question is genuinely contested, do not present one side as the consensus.
