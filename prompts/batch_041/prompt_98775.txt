Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about objective chance and ended exactly where we started. He works in statistical mechanics and holds a deflationary line: chance talk is just a summary of what a well-informed agent would believe, so the requirement that rational credence equal the known objective chance is not a substantive norm at all — it collapses into the advice to set your credence to your best information, and an agent who knew the microstate would have no chance left to defer to. I hold that chance is an objective feature of the world, not reducible to anyone's information, and that there is a genuine requirement to let credence track it; I do not think this is merely a special case of deferring to an expert, since identifying what counts as the chance-maker already seems to presuppose the very principle we are trying to state. Two things kept the argument stuck. First, on the deterministic case — a half-life or a Mendelian ratio in a world whose every event is fixed by its initial conditions — he insisted we were quantifying over our ignorance while I insisted we were tracking a real propensity, and we could never agree on what the disputed term picks out. Second, when he pressed the point that the principle only applies when the agent has no further information that screens off the chance, and that in any realistic case the agent always has such information, I could not say whether the principle is thereby vacuous, or circular, or merely in need of a careful account of which extra information is allowed; and when he pressed the self-undermining case — a chance that depends on the whole history including the future, so that the occurrence of the very event the chance concerns would change what the chance was — I could not say whether this refutes the deference requirement or only shows that it must be restated.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal disagreement about what the word chance denotes, so that each of us is right about a different quantity? A substantive metaphysical disagreement about whether chance facts supervene on the non-chance facts? A normative disagreement about whether deference to chance is a basic requirement of rationality or something derived from a more general rule about experts? Or are we answering different questions under one heading — he a semantic or decision-theoretic one, I a metaphysical or normative one? Tell me which of these is actually doing the work, drawing on what each of us actually says; separate the parts that genuinely conflict from the parts that only look like conflict; and say what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result about whether any account of chance can satisfy the principle, or a reason to think the question is ill-posed and ought to be replaced.
