I sit on the resource-allocation subcommittee of a small hospital trust, and over the past few months we have found ourselves stuck on a class of decisions I had assumed were routine: whether to fund an intervention that delivers a large benefit to a handful of patients, or a cheaper one that delivers a barely noticeable benefit to an enormous number of them — a marginally better chance of avoiding a mild bout of post-operative nausea, say. What unsettled me is that my colleagues and I were plainly not disagreeing about the arithmetic; we were disagreeing about something more basic, namely whether very large numbers of very small harms or benefits aggregate into anything that can be weighed against a single large one. I have since gathered that this is a live dispute in population ethics, usually introduced with a toy case in which one person is tortured for fifty years and the alternative is an astronomical number of people each receiving a dust speck in the eye, and I would like to stop re-running the same argument every month and instead understand the space of positions properly. I should add that the matter has become personally awkward as well: I have a long-standing condition whose symptoms are, individually, too minor to complain about, and I find that I cannot decide whether the accumulated discomfort amounts to something serious or whether that thought is a kind of category error.

Please take the role of a philosopher working on normative ethics, with particular expertise in the aggregation of harms, the structure of value, and population ethics. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the logical space before committing to any labels: first the questions on which the disputants genuinely come apart, and the combinations of answers those questions permit; only then the named positions located within that space. What I want as the final deliverable is a decision tree — a flowchart with numbered nodes — that takes a reader from the modest starting point of accepting that small harms count for something to a specific position on aggregation. Every branch node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to: concrete cases and plain claims, not invitations to pick a label. Each leaf must carry the name of the position (or an explicit note that nobody defends it) together with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment, and any pair of leaves that your questions cannot separate should be flagged as such rather than papered over.

To keep the tree anchored, please build it so that it can accommodate at least the following distinctions, adding any further branch you judge necessary: whether negligible harms count at all, or count with positive but non-Archimedean weight, or have zero weight; whether harms are commensurable on a single scale; whether the aggregation function is linear, or saturating, or contains a genuine discontinuity — a cliff such that no finite quantity of dust specks outweighs the torture until some threshold population, after which it suddenly does; whether lexical thresholds or side-constraints block aggregation; and whether the correct locus of aggregation is the person, the experience, or the moment. I would also like a branch testing a distinction I have not seen made explicitly and suspect cuts across the usual consequentialist/deontological divide: whether negligible is an absolute predicate, so that a harm can be too small to matter full stop, or a comparative one, so that a harm matters only relative to the alternatives in the same choice — I suspect this leaves some combinations unoccupied, and I would like you to say briefly whether each empty combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Please use, at a minimum, the dust-specks-versus-torture case, the hospital allocation case I described, and a case of my own invention that I find harder than either — a pill that would grant an astronomically large number of people a single moment of mild boredom, weighed against sparing one person a badly broken leg — and add a branch testing the sorites-style worry that if a single tiny harm may be ignored, then by accumulation any large harm may be ignored too. Aim for roughly ten branch nodes and no more than a dozen leaves. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind; I want the content of the positions, not pointers to the literature. Write the whole thing in British English.
