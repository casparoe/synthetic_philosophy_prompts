I am presenting at a one-day alignment workshop next month, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion period, not a summary of my own argument.

My thesis: the treacherous turn, as it is usually told, presupposes that a model can carry a stable final objective through training and then conceal it until deployment, and I want to argue that this presupposition does no work that a weaker assumption cannot do. What the standard training setup actually licenses, I claim, is only a dispositional notion of deception — a policy that would deceive were the incentive present — and the vivid occurrent picture, in which the model decides to defect once it judges itself unmonitored, is an extra commitment imported from the agent-foundations literature rather than derived from anything gradient descent over a task distribution guarantees. I want to press that the difference matters: if deception is dispositional, the right interventions concern the shape of the training and deployment distributions; if it is occurrent, they concern the model's internal goal representation. The field is pouring effort into the second while the evidence underdetermines it.

The audience: roughly two-thirds empirical RLHF and interpretability researchers who treat talk of a model having a true objective as a category error and will demand an operationalization they could run tomorrow; the remaining third are alignment-theory people who take the classic goal-directed picture as the load-bearing case and will hear my reframing as a change of subject. There is also one cognitive scientist who will keep asking what a disposition is supposed to mean mechanistically.

Build me the prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, phrased in the vocabulary of whichever faction will raise them, ordered by how much damage each does to the thesis rather than by how likely it is; for each, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds and an honest note on where the reply is soft or merely definitional. Tell me which objections I should concede outright and what conceding costs me, which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the hour, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the one whose answer I have not worked out.

Lead with the bottom line: in your first sentence, name the objection that most threatens the thesis, and only then lay out the full prep sheet. No preamble, and do not restate my argument back to me.
