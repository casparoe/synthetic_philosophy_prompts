I'm a features writer covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm assembling an application for a one-year fellowship that funds a single long-form piece; mine would come in around ten thousand words. What I need from you is the proposal itself, roughly 1,500 words, in the sections and order I've listed at the end. Please write it in the first person, as my application — a writer's proposal, not a philosopher's abstract. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list: I'll add those myself, and I'd rather you describe positions in plain terms than attach them to names, because I'd have to verify every attribution and I don't want to inherit your guesses.

Here is the half-formed idea I'm trying to turn into a project. For several years my beat has been preventable harm — heat deaths, a delayed inspection regime, a benefits rule that was known to be wrong, a staffing formula that everyone could see was going to fail. In each of these, the harm was predictable, cheap to avert, and traceable to no one's particular failure to act at a particular moment. There is no inspector who skipped a building on a Tuesday; there is a schedule that could never have covered the buildings, agreed to over years by people who each did their job as defined. When I try to write "who failed to act," the sentence has no subject.

So the question I want to put at the centre of the piece: does the concept of causing harm by omitting to act have application to harms produced by a standing arrangement rather than by an individual's non-action — or is that concept essentially agent-shaped and moment-shaped, so that in my cases the causal vocabulary simply runs out and we are left saying that the deaths happened, that they were foreseeable, and that no one omitted anything? If the second answer is right, I think that is a much more interesting finding than it sounds, and I want a proposal that can defend it rather than assume the first.

Two things I'd like you to handle carefully. First, I have read enough to know that the general debate over whether omissions can be causes is old and crowded — absences aren't events, so how can an absence stand in a causal relation; if it can, is it in virtue of counterfactual dependence, and does picking out which non-events count as the relevant omission smuggle in a normative baseline about how things should have gone. I also know the phrase "problem of many hands," but as far as I can tell that is about apportioning blame among people who each did something, whereas my cases have no one who did anything in particular. If the question I've posed turns out to be settled, or turns out to be one of those two debates wearing a new coat, say so plainly in the first section and then reformulate it into the nearest question that is genuinely open. I would much rather have a smaller real project than an ambitious confused one.

Second, be realistic about me. I have an undergraduate philosophy degree and no formal training; I cannot do technical work in logic or metaphysics, and the fellowship pays for reading, thinking, reporting and writing, not for a lab or a survey. The method you describe has to be something I could actually execute in a year and express in magazine prose without jargon, which is part of why the proposal has to say what the essay's argument would look like in ordinary language. The deliverable is the essay, so the proposal should make the case that the piece is worth funding, not merely describe it.

Use these section headings, in this order, with these rough lengths:

The question (about 200 words). State it in one paragraph, so that it has a determinate answer and a reader can see what would settle it. Name the two or three answers on offer.

Why the existing positions leave it open (about 300 words). Lay out the standard positions on whether an omission can be a cause, without names, and identify precisely which step in each one presupposes an individual agent with a determinate moment of non-action. Then say what none of them settles about my cases.

The angle (about 400 words). The route in that is mine and not the standard one: which of my reported cases I would use and how I would strip them down to thin descriptions that keep the causal structure and drop the names and intentions; what candidate criteria for causal status I would test them against; what the argument would actually claim if it goes well. Say why this beats armchair conceptual analysis on one side and simply reporting more cases on the other.

Plan and schedule (about 300 words). Quarter by quarter across the fellowship year: what I read and when, when I build the case set, when I draft, when I hand drafts to two or three people who do this for a living and when I revise in light of what they say.

What success and failure look like (about 250 words). What would make this a piece worth publishing rather than a muddle; what outcome would count as the project failing; and what I should do if the cases turn out not to cooperate.

Scope and risks (about 200 words). The honest case that this fits a year and ten thousand words, what would make it too big, and what I would cut first.

Keep it continuous prose under those headings, and don't add sections of your own.
