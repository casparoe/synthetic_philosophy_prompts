I took early retirement from a chemistry department a few years back and have spent the time reading philosophy, which is the one thing I never had room for while I was working. Right now I'm stuck on the Molinism fight and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a real disagreement or two teams talking past each other, so I'd rather you diagnose it than pick a winner.

As I understand it: Molina, a Jesuit in the 1500s, said God knows a third body of truths alongside the necessary truths and the truths about what God decides to do — counterfactuals about what any possible free creature would freely do if placed in such-and-such circumstances. Since these are true before God decides which world to make, God can use them to pick a world in which people freely do what God wants. The Dominicans (Bañez and company) said no: grace works by a physical premotion that determines the act while leaving it free, and the counterfactual truths, if true at all, are true because God decreed them, not because some free creature would have done something. Each side accused the other of something awful — the Molinists said premotion makes God the author of sin, the Dominicans said middle knowledge makes God depend on his creatures and slides toward Pelagianism. It went to Rome, they spent years on it, and in the end the pope just told both sides they could each keep teaching their own view and had to stop calling the other side heretical. So it's been sitting there unresolved for four centuries, and there's a third camp (open theists) who say there are no true counterfactuals of freedom at all, so God couldn't know them even in principle.

What strikes me is that both sides agree on almost everything — God is omniscient, creatures are free in the libertarian sense, grace is efficacious — and then they draw opposite conclusions. My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than assume: this is really a fight about grounding, not about God. If you think every true proposition needs something in the world to make it true, then a counterfactual about a free act that never happens has nothing to make it true, since the act is free and nothing before it settles it — and Molinism is dead. If you're willing to let some truths be brute, it's fine. That would make it a disagreement about a general metaphysical principle rather than about theology. But maybe that's wrong, and it's actually a dispute about what freedom means, or about what divine causation is, in which case it's substantive. In my old field we had a similar-looking squabble about dispositions and probabilities — whether the fact that this particular nucleus would decay in the next hour is a fact about the nucleus or just a summary of the theory's predictions — and I can't tell whether that's the same shape of disagreement or whether the theology does real work my analogy misses.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a partly verbal one where the two sides mean different things by freedom or by causation, or two different questions wearing one name (the Molinist asking what God knows, the Dominican asking what makes the act happen)? Is a hidden disagreement about truthmakers or about the principle of sufficient reason doing the work? Or is the question ill-posed because it assumes there's a fact of the matter about what a free creature would do in circumstances it never faces? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or should we expect this to sit there forever the way it has? Please argue it out rather than giving me a survey, and skip the reading list; a seperate bibliography is no use to me, I just want the argument.
