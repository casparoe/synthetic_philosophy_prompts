I spent forty years as a palaeontologist and have been teaching myself philosophy since I retired; the questions I cannot leave alone turn out to be the ones I spent my working life on without naming them. I now sit on the acquisitions and interpretation committee of our county museum, and I must circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

The case is a constructed one, though of a kind that recurs. A private collector offers the museum, on favourable terms, a collection of worked stone said to have been recovered from a named cave in the uplands. If the findspot is accepted, the collection becomes the earliest evidence of human presence in the region by a considerable margin, and our displays would say so. The findspot rests on nothing but the collector's account of what a now-dead acquaintance told him; there are no excavation records. The pieces themselves are not forgeries, and their form and raw material are consistent with the claim — as they are with several other origins. Our development office wants them shown prominently; one faction of the committee wants them refused on principle, holding that material without a recorded findspot ought not to be admitted at all; the curator proposes to accept them and catalogue them as of unknown provenance. The legal and ethical questions are, I am satisfied, settled — the collector's title is good and the export was lawful when it took place — so what remains is the question I have been reading Herodotus and Thucydides for: how much can testimony establish about a distant past, and what does silence prove?

Please draft the opinion I shall circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — accept, refuse, or accept on stated conditions that the museum can actually administer and review — give the reasoning for it, and treat the strongest case against your recommendation as seriously as your own. Three things I particularly want worked out. First, the evidential standard the committee should apply to a claim about the distant past, and whether the same standard should govern what we assert on a public label. Second, the argument from silence: when an absence of evidence is evidence of absence, and what we must know about survival and recovery rates for that inference to hold. Third, two thoughts from my own trade, which I should like tested rather than simply adopted: that the incompleteness of a record is a fact about preservation and sampling rather than about what once existed; and that a find stripped of its context is not merely weak evidence but a different kind of thing from evidence — able to illustrate a claim, but not to locate or date it. Say also what further evidence would change the recommendation, and whether we could obtain it.

Begin directly with the opinion, its first sentence the recommendation, and then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of this letter. I do not want a reading list; the argument must carry it.
