I am a third-year philosophy student taking an advanced module on normative ethics, and the section we are on at the moment is the paradox of deontology — the thought that a view which forbids us to violate rights appears, on reflection, to be committed to minimising the violation of rights. I have been trying to work out what I actually think by going case by case, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to, and which of them I ought to give up.

Here is where I land.

Transplant: a surgeon kills one healthy patient and distributes his organs to five dying patients. Impermissible, with no hesitation.

Tram: I divert a runaway tram onto a side track, killing one worker there, so that five on the main track survive. Permissible.

Footbridge: I push a large bystander off a bridge onto the track to stop the tram. Impermissible. What distinguishes this from the tram case, I tell myself, is that the bystander's being killed is precisely what stops the tram.

Loop: the tram will loop back and kill the five unless it strikes a person on the side track whose body absorbs the impact. That person's body is doing the stopping, just as in the footbridge case, but he is already there and I am merely diverting the tram. I find that I think this is permissible, which wrecks the explanation I gave for the footbridge case.

Attacker: someone is about to kill two people, and the only way to stop him is to kill him; his death is both intended and the means by which the two are saved. I think this is permissible.

Torture: a villain will torture three people unless I name one of them for him to torture. I think I should name one.

Notice the arithmetic: the torture case and the transplant case offer the same choice between one and three or five. Yet I think I should name the one and that I must not kill the one.

Prerogative: I may spend money on my own child's education rather than send it to save the lives of two strangers, and I do not take this to be a moral failing.

Threshold: I must not kill one to save five, but I ought to kill one to save a thousand, and I am uneasy about the second verdict.

Consent: a competent, terminally ill patient, certain to die within the day, volunteers to be killed so that his organs can save five. I want to say this is impermissible, but I cannot see what the constraint is protecting, given that he will die regardless and asks for it. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

Agency: unlocking a door so that another person kills one in order to save five, or setting a machine to do it after I have gone to sleep, strikes me as no better than doing it myself.

Assistance: letting five drown because saving them would ruin my suit strikes me as monstrous, but I do not think I am obliged to give a kidney to save one stranger.

The tentative principle I have been trying out runs roughly like this: an action is wrong when it brings about a harm that is either intended or is the means by which the action's benefit is secured, subject to a threshold at which the numbers of lives at stake override the constraint, and subject to a permission to give special weight to one's own projects and attachments. I suspect this is a patchwork, and that it cannot survive the loop, attacker and consent cases.

What I would like from you is the following: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to cases I have not thought of; for each clause, the verdict that forces it and the verdict that makes it awkward; the account's verdicts on two or three further cases that you construct yourself and that I have not mentioned; and a candid statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why that verdict, rather than the principle, should go. I am more interested in the account being worked out than in a survey of positions, so please argue rather than report. Please write your answer in British English.
