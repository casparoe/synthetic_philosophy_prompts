I was at a lunchtime reading group where two colleagues, Dana and Ellis, got into a dispute about the old Carroll dialogue in which Achilles tries to get the Tortoise to accept a conclusion from premises and the Tortoise keeps demanding that the conditional be added as another premise. The exchange went like this. (1) Dana argued that the Tortoise is right: any attempt to justify modus ponens by adding the conditional as a premise just produces a longer argument that still needs a rule to apply, so deduction is either circular or infinitely regressive and has no non-circular justification. (2) Ellis replied that the Tortoise only gets the regress by treating a rule of inference as if it were another premise; in a proof system, modus ponens licenses the move from two lines to a third, and it is not itself a third line. (3) Dana countered that this is relabeling: if you accept a rule, you have accepted something, and one can still ask what justifies accepting it, so calling it a rule does not answer the demand. (4) Ellis said accepting a rule is practical rather than propositional, since it shows up in what you do rather than in a further belief, and compared it to a chess player being bound by the rule for bishops without needing a premise stating that rule. (5) Dana answered that the chess analogy cuts the other way: chess rules are conventional and optional, so if logical rules are like that, logic is just a game and could have been otherwise, which saves deduction from regress only by making it arbitrary; Ellis began to reply that a rule can be constitutive of an aim rather than merely arbitrary, as when a proof assistant's kernel defines what counts as a proof, but Dana said that still leaves the choice of kernel unjustified. I have no philosophy background and could not referee this. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say who has the better point, how strong the move is, and what, if anything, both parties are missing. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
