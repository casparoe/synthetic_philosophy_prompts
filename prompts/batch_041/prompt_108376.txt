I'm in a small reading group that has spent the term on Kierkegaard, and the last session is on his writings about the press and what he calls the public. The idea I keep circling is that the press does not merely report on a crowd; it conjures a new kind of entity — an anonymous abstraction that is somehow everything and nothing at once, that no one actually is, that cannot be addressed and cannot be held to account, and that lives on chatter: talk that circulates with nobody standing behind it, in which anything can be said and therefore nothing is ever settled. His companion idea is levelling, the slow grinding-down of every distinction — the exceptional, the passionate, the single individual — onto one flat and indifferent plane, with the press as the mill that does the grinding. What I find hardest to hold onto, and what I would like the poem to hold onto for me, is that he refuses the easy version of the complaint. The levelling is nobody's plot; it happens because everyone does nothing in particular. He will not let the exceptional individual play the hero who halts it — anyone who sets himself up as the one who will stop it has, on his account, already misunderstood it — and he does not simply mourn it either, since there is a strand in him that treats levelling as the negative condition on which a genuine equality might later be built. My friend in the group, who knows about the years when the satirical weeklies had Kierkegaard as a standing joke, thinks the whole thing is a wounded man's revenge on the papers dressed up as philosophy, and I would like to read something aloud at the final session that makes him feel the difficulty rather than the snobbery.

So: a sestina, and I want the form to carry the argument. The six end-words should all be apparatus rather than persons — chatter, press, public, level, everyone, moment — so that the machinery of publicity occupies every slot in the poem, and each of those words should be allowed to shift grammatical job as it recurs, a noun turning into a verb and back, because a word that will not stay in one kind of work is part of what levelling is. The speaker should be the public itself, the abstraction that speaks as no one, addressing a single reader in the second person: courteous, patient, faintly hungry. The phrase 'the single individual' should appear exactly once in the whole poem, in the middle of a line and never at the end of one — the one thing that resists levelling must not be handed a slot. No villains anywhere: no minister, no editor pulling strings, no cabal; the poem has to implicate the reader by sheer ordinariness. And no elegy — the ending must not be a lament for lost greatness, since that is precisely the move I want the poem to make unavailable. The envoi has to gather all six end-words, and I would like the thirty-ninth and last line to land on 'everyone' rather than on the individual; I want to see whether the form survives that. Please don't put any of his own phrases in quotation marks — I don't want to be sitting there wondering whether a line is really his — and don't lean on any biographical detail beyond what I've given you. Render the ideas accurately enough that someone who has read him would not wince, and clearly enough that someone who hasn't would still be reading a good poem. Give it a plain title, then the poem, then two or three sentences on which formal choice is doing which conceptual work, and finish with a single line beginning "In one sentence:" that states what the poem claims about the press and the public.
