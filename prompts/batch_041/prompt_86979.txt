On 3 September 1967 Sweden switched, in one coordinated step, from driving on the left-hand side of the road to the right: a change the electorate had rejected in a 1955 referendum, which the Riksdag nonetheless approved in 1963, and which was executed after a national publicity campaign and an overnight halt in which all traffic stopped and then resumed on the other side. Treat this as a test case for David Lewis's account of convention -- a regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, is sustained by common knowledge and mutual expectation rather than by any authority, and is arbitrary in the sense that another regularity would have served just as well. I want a disciplined mapping in both directions. From the theory: what the coordination problem was, why the old arrangement qualified as a convention and whether the new one does, and what becomes of the arbitrariness condition once the neighbouring countries' practice and the left-hand-drive cars had given the change a definite direction. From the case: where Lewis is silent or wrong -- the centrally orchestrated replacement of one equilibrium by another, the manufactured salience and common knowledge, the role of law and enforcement in getting across, and the impossibility of a partial switch. Close with a definite verdict, defended rather than hedged, on whether the episode confirms, refines, or refutes the account, and say what a theory of how conventions change would have to add.
