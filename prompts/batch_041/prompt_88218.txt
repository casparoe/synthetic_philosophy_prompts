At a seminar dinner, two colleagues of mine — call them Dana and Marcus — fell into an argument about judicial review, and I was left trying to referee without being able to settle it. The occasion was a constitutional court's decision to strike down a statute requiring large new residential developments to set aside a share of units at below-market rents; the court held that the statute imposed a burden on owners that the constitution does not permit. Neither was arguing about rent policy as such. They were arguing about whether the court should have had the last word at all.

Dana (1) opened: an unelected body of judges with secure tenure has just overridden a deliberate choice of an elected legislature, and nothing makes their reading of an abstract text more authoritative than the legislature's; where the text does not settle the question, the democratic branch should win by default. Marcus (2) replied that the whole point of entrenching limits is to remove certain questions from ordinary majorities, and a limit that no institution can enforce is not a limit; someone must apply the text, and "the legislature decides" is itself a contested reading, not a neutral baseline. Dana (3) granted that courts must apply determinate rules, but said there is a difference between enforcing a rule the constitution clearly states and choosing among several reasonable readings of a vague clause — which is what this decision did; when the court is choosing rather than applying, its choice should be reversible through ordinary politics, by override or sunset or something of the kind, since otherwise a transient majority of judges binds everyone indefinitely. Marcus (4) answered that a charter majorities can override whenever they care enough is just a statute with extra steps, though he conceded that courts should defer more when a right is genuinely vague, and that he cannot draw the applying/choosing line cleanly either. Dana (5) then shifted ground: even granting that the court's reading is defensible, its authority rests on being seen as legal rather than political, and every contested moral question it resolves spends that authority and invites retaliation that will eventually cripple its ability to protect the clear cases. Marcus (6) called this circular — authority is earned by doing the job well over time, not by ducking hard cases, and a court that refuses the contested ones will look evasive rather than neutral.

Adjudicate their exchange move by move rather than declaring an abstract winner, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing. Return your answer as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: "moves", an array in which each element has "move" (the number above), "speaker", "summary" (the claim in one sentence), "verdict" (one of "clearly mistaken", "mostly mistaken", "mixed", "mostly right", "clearly right"), and "justification" (two or three sentences); "strongest_move", giving the move number and a sentence on why it is the strongest; "weakest_move", likewise; "what_both_are_missing", a short paragraph; and "overall_verdict", two or three sentences on who is more right overall and on what specific question each side should concede.
