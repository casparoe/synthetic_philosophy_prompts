Problem: A robot watches a human expert cross a warehouse floor and take a long detour around a puddle. We want to infer the human's reward function from the trajectory. (a) Explain why the trajectory alone underdetermines the reward function -- and be precise about the family of reward functions that would rationalize the same behavior. (b) A colleague suggests fixing the underdetermination by assuming the human is Boltzmann-rational with a known temperature, i.e. at each state actions are chosen with probability proportional to exp(beta * Q(s,a)), with beta known. Does that help, and at what cost?

Student's answer: "IRL asks which reward function the observed behavior is optimal for. The trouble is that behavior only reveals the ordering of the human's preferences. Any reward function can be shifted by an additive constant without changing the optimal policy, so the trajectory pins the reward down only up to a constant; and since we only see which option got picked, not by how much, the scale is free too. So from the detour we learn that the human dislikes wet feet more than they value the extra time, but nothing about how much. For (b): yes, this helps a lot. Assuming a known temperature turns the problem into fitting a distribution over actions, and since the softmax is invertible you can recover the reward exactly from the observed choices. The cost is that it bakes in a strong assumption about human rationality, so if the worker is actually sloppy the inferred reward will be biased."
