I'm a backend engineer with zero philosophy training, and a friend nerd-sniped me with an old puzzle I can't stop chewing on. The version he told me has a soldier who parachutes into enemy territory split into Red and Blue, and each half has a Headquarters area and a Second Company area. She figures she's 50/50 on Red vs Blue, and 50/50 on HQ vs Second Company inside whichever half she's in. Then she gets a garbled radio message and the only part she can make out is "if you're in Red territory, the odds are 3 to 1 that you're in Headquarters." What should she think about Red vs Blue now?

My gut: nothing, it shouldn't budge. The message told her how things split up inside Red. It didn't tell her anything about Red vs Blue. (If the message had just said "the odds are 3 to 1 you're in HQ," flat, no if, then fine, conditionalize, Red stays 50/50, no problem. It's only the conditional ones that get me.)

Then I built a mirror version and my gut flipped, which is the part bugging me alot. Same setup, but now the garbled message is "if you're in Headquarters, the odds are 3 to 1 that you're in Red." Here my gut says Red should get *more* likely — HQ is where the Red basically is, so if she's in HQ she's probably in Red, so Red goes up. But that message is also just telling me how something splits inside a sub-area, so by my own logic I should've said "no change" again. Why does my gut move on one and not the other? Is there a real difference or am I picking whichever answer feels nicer?

Two more of my reactions, both shakier:

- If instead of odds the message flatly said "if you're in Red, you're in Headquarters," I feel like Red should get *less* likely, because now Blue has two live options and Red has only one, so Blue has more room. I don't trust this one at all.
- When a person says "if X then probably Y," part of me thinks I've learned something about X — like they wouldn't bring X up unless they thought it was in play — so in the first case the mere mention of Red should nudge Red up a little. The other part of me thinks that's just me reading in, and it's a seperate question anyway.

What I want: the rule, or a couple of competing rules, that get as many of these four verdicts as possible, stated clean enough that I could code it up. The rule I keep reaching for is "hold fixed whatever the message conditions on — Red, in the first case — and just re-split the inside of it." That gets my first two verdicts, which is suspicious, because it feels like I'm choosing what to hold fixed so my gut comes out right. Is there a non-arbitrary reason to hold *that* thing fixed rather than the other? Or is "move my probabilities as little as possible while respecting what the message says" better? I've messed with KL divergence at work so that phrasing is in my head, but I have no idea if it's the right tool here or whether it even reproduces my guts.

Tell me which of my four verdicts I have to give up, and what the rule you favor predicts for stuff I haven't thought about. What if the message is garbled so bad that all I get is "if you're in Red, the odds are 3 to 1" with the rest cut off — do I leave everything alone? And what if the radio is from a company I don't trust at all — does that change the shape of the problem, or just shrink how far I move? My friend says philosophers have a whole literature on exactly this; I don't care about that, I care whether my guts can be made consistent.
