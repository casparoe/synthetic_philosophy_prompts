I'm teaching a unit on Augustine's ethics of speech and I want to close it with A Man for All Seasons — use the Bolt play, or the 1966 film if it gives you more to work with. You're a philosopher of religion who knows the early Christian debates about lying inside out. Think carefully before you write, and spend a few lines up front brainstorming what Augustine's actual position is and what the play is actually doing, so you don't just match them up by vibe.

Here's the puzzle. Augustine, in the earlier treatise On Lying and then again in the later Against Lying, won't permit a lie under any circumstances — not to save a life, not to protect someone's faith, not to keep heretics from deceiving the faithful. What makes something a lie for him isn't that the statement is false; it's that the speaker has a double heart, saying one thing while holding another in mind. He'll even say that a person who utters something true but believes it false is lying, while a person who utters something false in good faith is not. He carves out jokes, on the grounds that the joking tone makes the absence of deceit plain. But he also seems to hold that simply not saying something — keeping a truth to yourself, letting a question go unanswered — isn't the same act as lying, and I think he's a lot more permissive about that, though I'm not sure how far he'd let it run. The earlier book he apparently came to regret writing, which is its own interesting fact about how hard he found the position to state cleanly.

Enter More. In Bolt's play, More's whole survival strategy is silence. He won't swear the oath, he won't say why, he won't deny the King's supremacy, he won't affirm it — he tries to live inside the fact that he has said nothing. The trial turns on whether his silence can be construed as a denial. Rich perjures himself to kill him; More won't tell even a small lie to save himself. On the face of it he's Augustine's dream: a man who would rather die than utter a falsehood.

So what I want from you: is Bolt's More actually an Augustinian, or is he exploiting the silence loophole in a way Augustine should have been much more worried about? Push on these:

- What does More's silence communicate, and to whom? He knows perfectly well what people will infer. If he intends them to infer it, has he deceived anyone, and does the double-heart test even reach that case? Is there a real difference between intending someone to believe something false and merely foreseeing that they will?
- More says he can't swear the oath without damning himself, but he also says he doesn't know whether the marriage was lawful. Is the play giving us a man who loves the truth, or a man who loves his own integrity? Those come apart, and I think Augustine's whole framework hangs on the first rather than the second.
- The play has a big sub-theme about the benefit of law — More's speech to Roper about giving the Devil the benefit of law. Does that sit uneasily with Augustine's insistence that the truth is owed to everyone regardless of consequences? Which does the play actually value more, truth or law?
- Where does the play resist the Augustinian reading? My suspicion is that it dramatizes something Augustine misses: that silence can be a way of honoring the truth rather than evading it, and that a world where everyone has to answer every question isn't a more truthful world. Is that right? If so, is it a point against Augustine or just a case he never considered?
- And the ugly part: More's silence gets other people hurt. His family suffers, and in Bolt his refusal to explain himself to them is part of the tragedy. An absolute rule seems to leave no room for the thought that the people who love you have some claim on your speech. Does the play show the rule is wrong, or show the cost of it being right?

Don't just tell me More is a martyr for conscience — I know that reading and I want the harder one. Finish by saying what the play adds to the philosophical debate that the treatises can't, and what it gets wrong about it.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — is More an Augustinian, yes or no and in what sense — then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question.
