I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend — a game theorist who left academia for a systematic hedge fund — about a thesis I have been nursing toward a conference talk, and I want to be ready for the question period before I commit to it. The thesis is this: the no-trade results, read together, are not the narrow modeling curiosities they are usually taken to be about when rational agents refrain from speculative exchange; they amount to an impossibility result about disagreement itself. The consequence I want to draw is that any exchange properly called speculative must rest on either heterogeneous priors or some non-epistemic motive — hedging, liquidity, a misreading of the common-knowledge structure — so that the epistemology of markets is not a theory of how prices aggregate dispersed knowledge but a theory of the residue left once genuine disagreement has been subtracted. My friend's objection, which stung more than I let on, is that I have smuggled a metaphysical conclusion out of a modeling assumption: that the common prior and the common knowledge of rationality are doing all the work, and that once you state them plainly the theorem says nothing about markets at all, only about a stipulated set of priors. I half believe him, and I would rather find out now than from the audience.

I am presenting this at a joint workshop, and the room is really two rooms. The larger group is financial economists — asset-pricing and market-microstructure people — who build models with heterogeneous priors as a matter of course, regard the common-prior assumption as a convenient fiction, and will judge the thesis by whether it has any empirical or explanatory content; for them the live question is whether the no-trade result is a substantive claim or a vacuous benchmark, and their instinct will be to ask what work the "epistemology" framing does that a plain statement of the assumptions would not. The smaller group is formal epistemologists who work on common knowledge, agreement, and the dynamics of persistent disagreement; they will care about exactly which assumptions are load-bearing, whether the common prior is needed or only the common knowledge of the partition structure, and whether the result is about markets at all or about Bayesian rationality as such. There is also one senior attendee, known for the view that prices aggregate information whether or not anyone actually trades, who I expect will say that I have mistaken the market for the trades inside it.

For each, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft, question-begging, or merely a restatement of the thesis. Tell me which of my friend's charges I should simply concede rather than fight. And include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
