I am a novelist, well into a book set in Benin City on the eve of the punitive expedition of 1897, and before I commit to its politics I would like my own position audited for coherence rather than encouraged. This is roughly what I believe. (1) The Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles are owed back — to Benin City and to Athens — and no amount of exemplary stewardship since discharges that debt. (2) What actually grounds a claim to return, though, is continued use: an object that remains ritually or ceremonially alive belongs with the community that keeps it alive, whereas an object that has become purely aesthetic is better housed where the greatest number can study it. (3) An object's meaning is constituted by its entire history, so there is no pristine original context to which a returned object could be restored; the bronze that spent a century in Bloomsbury is now also, in some measure, that. (4) The modern nation-state is a poor proxy for the community that made and used a thing, being largely a nineteenth-century European export, so return to the country of origin is a shakier formula than it sounds. (5) My method as a novelist is to inhabit the looters' reasons from the inside, with genuine sympathy, since flat villains make bad fiction — and I do not take this to be a moral failing.

Tell me which of these actually pull against each other, which only appear to, and which single one, if I surrendered it, would resolve the most — and what the surrender would cost. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list.
