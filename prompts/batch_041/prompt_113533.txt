My brother in law dragged me to a charity do last month where the man running the auction explained the rules in a way I'd never come across before. The prize was a twenty pound note, and the catch was that the two highest bidders both have to pay — whoever's on top pays what they bid and gets the twenty, and whoever comes second pays what they bid and gets nothing at all. I thought that was obviously a mug's game and kept my hands in my pockets, but my brother in law got sucked in and ended up paying £34 for a twenty pound note, and the fella who beat him paid £35. We argued about it the whole way home. He reckons he couldn't stop, because once he was sat at £33 as the second highest bidder, quitting meant throwing £33 away for nothing, whereas going to £34 meant either the other bloke folded and he got his twenty back (so he's only £14 down, which beats £33 down), or the other bloke went again. And then he said the other bloke was in the exact same spot, so it was just a question of who blinked first. I told him that's just the sunk cost fallacy with extra steps and he told me to write it down properly if I was so sure.

So I did, and now I'm the one who's stuck, because every line looks fine to me and the end of it is obviously rubbish. (1) When you're deciding what to do next, only what happens from here on counts — money you've already put in is gone either way, and letting it sway you is the sunk cost fallacy. I'm certain about this one, it's the one bit of economics I actually know. (2) Say I'm second highest at £33 and someone else is top at £34. If I stop, I pay my £33 and get nothing. If I go to £35 and the other fella stops, I pay £35 and get the twenty, so I'm £15 down instead of £33 down. (3) So going to £35 beats stopping. (4) There's nothing special about £35 — the same comparison holds at £50, at £500, at any number you care to name, because whatever the figure, stopping means paying my last bid for nothing while carrying on at least keeps the prize in play. (5) So a rational bidder never stops, and the auction runs for ever. (6) Except these auctions plainly don't run for ever, they're over in a few minutes, and my brother in law did stop, and he's an accountant, not an idiot. (7) So it looks to me like the argument proves that something which definately happens can't happen, which is wierd.

I'd rather keep (1) than give it up — I'm not about to start telling people their sunk costs matter, that way madness lies — but I want to know what it costs me if I do. Which line do I ditch, why did it look so reasonable when I wrote it, and what am I buying if I go the other way and keep it? And please be straight with me about how confident you actually are, and which part of your answer you're least sure about — I've put this to three people now and got three different lines back, all delivered very confidently.
