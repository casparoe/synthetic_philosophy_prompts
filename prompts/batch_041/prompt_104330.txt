I am a retired physical chemist. My working life was spent in spectroscopy and statistical mechanics — partition functions, ensembles, coarse-graining, phase space, microstates and macrostates, entropy read as missing information, probability read sometimes as ignorance and sometimes as objective chance — and I have spent the three years since retirement reading philosophy of physics on my own, with no formal training in it. In my old trade the wavefunction was an instrument: we used it to compute spectra and electron densities, and I do not recall anyone in the building asking whether it was real.

I would like you to take the contemporary dispute over the reality of the quantum state — the contrast between psi-ontic and psi-epistemic readings of the wavefunction, together with the no-go results that constrain the epistemic side (Bell-type theorems, contextuality results of the Kochen–Specker family, and the 2012 theorem of Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph, which I understand rules out preparation-independent hidden-variable models of the epistemic kind) — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in. Then give me the residue report.

Specifically:

1. Which claims of each camp translate into ensemble language without loss? Where the analogy holds, say why it holds rather than merely asserting it.
2. Where does the translation distort? I am suspicious of one thing in particular: the technical sense of epistemic in this literature, which I take to concern whether a single underlying physical state can be compatible with more than one pure quantum state, does not look to me like the statistical mechanician's ignorance of a definite microstate. Tell me whether that is right, and whether the convex structure of the state space — mixed states admitting many distinct decompositions, and the absence of anything like a canonical underlying support — has any counterpart in a coarse-grained classical distribution, and what is lost if it does not.
3. What has no expression at all in my vocabulary? Does anything in the dispute — about completeness, about the role of the agent or of information, about what it would even mean for the state to be real — resist translation into ensemble talk?
4. Does my vocabulary smuggle in a premise that the classical case makes look innocent, namely that there is always a definite underlying microstate about which the statistics are statistics? If so, is that a defect of my framework or evidence that the original disagreement is less substantive than it appears? I would rather be told the exercise is a category error at some point than be flattered with a tidy analogy.
5. Where does the ensemble interpretation of quantum mechanics sit relative to both positions, and why is it not simply the epistemic view under another name?

I would also like you to be explicit about the status of what you tell me: mark clearly which parts of this are settled, which are live disputes among people who work on it, and where you are giving your own view or a reconstruction of your own rather than reporting a consensus. Do not present a contested reading as the received view, and flag any point at which the translation forces a choice the literature does not license.
