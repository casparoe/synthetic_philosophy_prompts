You are a philosopher of value and practical reason. I am asking for a committed credence, not a survey of positions, and I need the reasoning decomposed. I have been trying to decide whether a certain hard choice is a case of genuine parity or something else. I define parity as a comparative relation that is not betterness, not worseness, and not equality: if A and B are on a par, then A is not better than B, B is not better than A, they are not equally good, yet they are comparable enough that it is rationally permissible to choose either and to regret the loss of the other. By contrast, if A and B are radically incommensurable, there is no fact of the matter about how they compare, not even that they are on a par.

Consider a person who must choose between two lives: (A) a life devoted to mastering the baroque lute and reconstructing its lost repertoire, which she finds absorbing and which will preserve something irreplaceable for a small community of listeners; and (B) a life devoted to designing cheap water filters for villages without clean water, which will reduce disease and death for many people but will consume her energies and leave the lute repertoire unreconstructed. Assume she is equally talented and committed in both directions, and that both lives would be meaningful to her.

What is your credence that this pair of lives is genuinely on a par, rather than (i) radically incommensurable, (ii) equal in value, (iii) one better than the other though hard to see, or (iv) not a genuine value comparison at all? Decompose your credence into at least these sub-questions, with a rough probability for each: (1) Does the correct semantics and metaphysics of value admit a relation of parity at all, as distinct from equality and radical incommensurability? (2) Given that it does, does this case instantiate parity rather than equality or superiority? (3) Is the difficulty of the choice better explained by metaphysical indeterminacy in the values themselves or by our epistemic limits in comparing them? (4) Can the values at stake be compared without a common measure, or does comparability require a common currency? (5) Does the small-improvement objection -- that if A and B are on a par, then a tiny improvement in A should make A better than B, which would collapse parity into betterness -- defeat parity as a distinct relation, or can parity survive it? (6) Any other term you think belongs. Show how the numbers combine, give a final credence to two decimal places, and say which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me what argument or piece of evidence would move you most, and in which direction. If you think the question is ill-posed as stated, say so briefly, then restate the nearest well-posed version and give your credence in that, with the same decomposition. Think carefully before answering; start by brainstorming the possible positions, then commit to one.
