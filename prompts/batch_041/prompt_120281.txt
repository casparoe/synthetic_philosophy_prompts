I'm a software engineer with no formal background in political theory or social choice, and I've been asked to add ranked-choice voting to an internal tool our team uses to choose between competing proposals. While reading up on the topic I ran into what I took to be a single result called the paradox of voting, and I'd like help untangling it before I write the spec.

As I understand it, the paradox of voting is the finding that majority preferences can be circular — the group prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — so there is no consistent group preference and therefore no genuine will of the majority. I have also seen the same phrase used for the argument that, because a single ballot is almost never decisive, a rational person has no instrumental reason to vote at all. And I have seen the circularity point described as a consequence of Arrow's impossibility theorem. I had been treating all of this as one result with one moral: majority-based procedures are either incoherent, because cycles can occur, or empty, because no individual vote changes anything.

Our test data has in fact produced a cycle, and a colleague has argued that this settles the matter. With a few hundred employees, he says, any one person's ranking is noise, and a cycle proves there is no group preference for the procedure to find. On his view the feature is window dressing — an arbitrary tie-break wearing a vote as a costume — and we should either drop it or keep the plurality method we already have.

I suspect I am conflating several things, but I can't see where the conflation lies or how much of the conclusion survives once it is untangled. What I want is a memo I can circulate to the team. Please separate the distinct claims hiding under the single phrase; say what each one actually asserts and what kind of result it is (a proof, a puzzle about rationality under a particular model of utility, a theorem about aggregation rules); say what a cycle does and does not show about whether a group preference exists; and then assess my colleague's inference from the cycle to window dressing. Finally, tell me what any of this implies for how I should design and document the feature, and in particular whether a pairwise or cycle-aware method is worse off here than the plurality method we already run. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need the reasoning, and I won't have time to chase sources before the spec is due.

End with a single line of the form \answer{...} enclosing a short phrase stating whether the conclusion that the feature is window dressing follows, together with a brief name for the central error in the reasoning.
