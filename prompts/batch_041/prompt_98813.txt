I took early retirement from the chemistry department a couple of years ago and, with the time on my hands, I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university here. There's a retired actuary in it, Alan, who's become my main sparring partner, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule he's actually running when it comes to this 'principle of indifference' lark — you know, the idea that when you can't tell a bunch of possibilities apart you should spread your belief evenly across them. He's been definately consistent over the puzzles we've done so far, but I can't for the life of me see the principle underneath, and next week there's a new one coming up that I'd like to call before he does.

Here's what he's said so far.

- Three boxes: one holds two gold coins, one two silver, one one of each. You pick a box at random, pull out a coin, and it's gold. He says the chance the other coin is also gold is 2/3. That one I could follow.
- A coin is either 60-40 in favour of heads or 60-40 in favour of tails, and you've no idea which. He says the chance the next toss comes up heads is 1/2.
- A dart lands uniformly at random somewhere in a one-metre square. He says the chance it's in the left-hand half is 1/2.
- A machine turns out cubes, and all you're told is that every edge is at most 2 cm. He won't give a chance that a cube's edge is at most 1 cm — says there's no determinate answer. That one suprised me; I'd have said 1/2 without thinking. His point, as far as I can follow it, is that you might just as well have been told the volume is at most 8 cubic centimetres, and then you'd get a different number, so the problem simply hasn't told you enough.
- A bottle of wine and water is known to contain at most three times as much water as wine. Chance it contains between one and two times as much water as wine? Refuses again, much the same reason — it comes out differently depending on whether you take the unknown to be the water-to-wine ratio, the wine-to-water ratio, or the proportion of water.
- A chord is drawn 'at random' in a circle. Chance it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? Refuses, same reason again.

Now the new one. A chord is drawn by picking two points independently and uniformly at random round the circumference and joining them. Same question: chance it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle?

What I'd like from you:

1. What he'll say, and why.
2. What rule he's actually running. I can see at least two that fit everything above and that come apart on the new case, so set them out.
3. For each candidate rule, what it predicts for the new case.
4. Your best guess, with your reasons for preferring it.
5. A case I could put to him the week after that would separate whichever rules are still standing.

No need for references or for who-said-what-historically — I just want the reasoning. And could you write it in British English, the group's a bit pedantic about that sort of thing.
