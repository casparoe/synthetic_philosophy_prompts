I was at a ideas festival last month and ended up at a dinner table wedged between a political theorist who's spent years arguing for sortition and a former council leader who thinks the whole thing is mad. They went at it for the better part of an hour and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning, which is embarrassing because I write about politics and ideas for a living. So I'm handing it to you to referee.

Here's roughly how it went. She opened with this: elections are a machine for manufacturing one particular kind of person, the kind with the money, the appetite for campaigning, and the ability to pretend to agree with everybody. Her counter-proposal was a body of 150 people picked by a stratified lottery, one-year non-renewable terms, and a veto over a defined list of constitutional questions, overridable by a two-thirds vote of the elected chamber. That, she said, would give you an assembly that actually resembles the city. He said resembling the city on paper is not the same as representing what the city thinks, and anyway the point of a lottery was never wisdom, it was the absence of bias, which is why we use it for juries, and even then we let the lawyers strike people off. And a jury at least has to decide on the evidence and the law; a citizen assembly vetoing a budget has no such anchor.

She came back that the jury analogy actually helps her: we let twelve random people decide whether someone loses their liberty for years, on the theory that ordinary judgement plus deliberation is enough, and most political questions aren't technical puzzles, they're disagreements about values, which is precisely what a sample of the public is good at. He said some of them are technical, and a body that can veto but can't be voted out is a body nobody can hold to account, so if it makes a catastrophic call there's no remedy, you just wait out the term. She said accountability needn't be retrospective, rotation and the lot are their own form of accountability because you can't capture what you can't predict, and elected politicians aren't meaningfully accountable anyway, they answer to donors and party machines. He said that's the standard move, but at least an election hands you a lever, whereas sortition hands you a lottery ticket and a shrug.

It went round like that a few more times. She finished on: it all depends what you think democracy is for, and if it's about being responsive to what people want then elections win, but if it's about the people actually ruling rather than choosing their rulers then the lot wins. He finished on: even by her own definition it fails, because a sample isn't the people, it's a group of strangers handed power over the rest of us without any of us having chosen them.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what, rather than just who came out ahead overall? I want to know where each of them is standing on solid ground, where they're overclaiming, and what they're both missing. Please give it to me as plain prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just write it out the way you'd write me a long email. And in British English, please.
