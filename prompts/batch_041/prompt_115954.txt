I am a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on probability and evidence, and I would like to put an argument in front of you for diagnosis. It is not my own; I assembled it from the standard discussion of the Doomsday Argument for a class presentation, and I have since come to suspect that it proves far too much. The difficulty is that I cannot find where it goes wrong. Each step, taken on its own, seems to me difficult to deny, and yet I cannot bring myself to accept where it lands.

Let N be the total number of human beings who will ever have been born, and let r be my birth rank, that is, the number of people born before me, plus one. On the usual estimate, r is presently on the order of a hundred billion.

(1) My rank r should be treated as a uniform random draw from the set {1, 2, ..., N}. I have no reason to think myself specially early or specially late among all the humans who will ever live.

(2) For any hypothesis about the value of N, then, the likelihood of my observing a rank as early as r is inversely proportional to N. Hypotheses that posit a much larger total make my observed rank a correspondingly more surprising draw.

(3) Combining (2) with a vague and modest prior over N, my evidence shifts substantial probability toward small values of N, so that I should give considerable credence to the total's being no more than a couple of orders of magnitude greater than r.

(4) The same reasoning was available to every human who has ever lived. Anyone alive at a past time t could compute their own rank r(t) and conclude, by (1) through (3), that N was probably not vastly larger than r(t).

(5) Take the earliest humans. Their rank was a small fraction of the present figure, and N is at least a hundred billion; the conclusion they would have drawn therefore undershot the truth, for the earliest of them, by orders of magnitude. Generation after generation, all the way down toward the recent past, the same inference would have led to a conclusion that what actually happened refuted.

(6) So this is a rule of inference that has failed at essentially every trial. A rule that has been wrong on nearly every occasion on which it was applied is not one we are entitled to trust. It is as if I had a method for guessing an object's weight from its volume, and every object I tried it on turned out heavier than I had guessed; I would stop using the method.

(7) Therefore premise (1) is false, and my rank places no constraint whatever on N.

I would prefer to keep (1). I do not see what else could license any inference from my rank to the size of the human future, and without something like (1) I seem entitled to complete indifference about whether humanity has a long future, which strikes me as wishful thinking wearing the costume of epistemic humility. But I cannot buy (7) with (6) if (6) is as strong as it appears. Please tell me which step I should abandon, why it looked compelling, and what the remaining options cost. If the argument is valid and the price is simply the abandonment of some premise, I want to know which premise is cheapest to give up; if instead one of the steps is doing work it is not entitled to, I want to see exactly where the illicit move is made.
