okay, I'm putting together my notes for the seminar on the calculation debate and I want to lay out where I've landed before I subject everyone else to it. Here's my working set of views — tell me if they actually hang together:

(1) You can't have rational economic calculation without prices that track opportunity costs. This is the Mises/Hayek point and I think its basically right, not just a rhetorical flourish.
(2) A central planner with enough computing power could in principle reach a sensible allocation by trial and error, adjusting notional prices in response to shortages and surpluses until things clear — Lange's move, more or less.
(3) The knowledge that actually matters for production is mostly tacit and dispersed; it lives in people's heads and habits and can't be written down in a central plan.
(4) The right way to judge an economic system is welfare — how well it satisfies people's preferences — not whether it honours some set of property rights.
(5) Markets have a genuine epistemic virtue: they aggregate and transmit information that no single mind possesses.
(6) The preferences markets reveal are largely endogenous — shaped by advertising, norms, and the market itself — so "satisfying preferences" is a shakier yardstick than it sounds.
(7) Externalities and public goods are pervasive enough that actual market prices systematically fail to track true opportunity costs.

So: which of these are actually pulling against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest revision in each case? Order the real tensions by how bad they are and say what I'd have to give up to fix each one, then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I suspect I'm being too generous to myself somewhere but I honestly can't see where.
