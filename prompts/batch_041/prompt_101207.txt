I'm in a reading group on AI safety and we keep going around in cirles on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, because every example anyone reaches for is either a thought experiment (paperclip maximizer) or straight sci-fi, and we can't tell whether the claims are about real optimization presure or just about the stipulations of the thought experiment. So I want to try the reverse move: take a real, boring, well-documented case of an institution under intense optimization pressure and see how much of the theory actually survives contact with it. My candidate is the Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal — a bank that set aggressive sales quotas for branch staff, a culture where employees opened checking accounts, credit cards, and other products customers never asked for, millions of unauthorized accounts, thousands of low-level employees fired, a CEO who resigned after the initial settlement, and (this is the part I care about) no mastermind who set out to defraud anyone, just a metric everyone was told to hit. Treat the bank, or the sales apparatus inside it, as the "agent."

I want you to map that onto instrumental convergence and the orthogonality thesis the way you'd map a game onto a crisis: who or what the agent is, what its final goal supposedly was, what its options and resources were, and then which of the convergent instrumental drives — self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, resistance to shutdown or correction — actually show up, which show up only by loose analogy, and which are just absent. Then run it the other way and tell me what the case does to the theories. Does it support orthogonality, since capability and values came apart and the most effective salespeople were often the worst offenders? Or does it show that in human organizations the two are coupled by culture in a way the thesis doesn't anticipate? And is this really a case of instrumental convergence at all, or is it Goodhart / proxy divergence wearing convergence's clothes — a final goal we already endorsed going wrong, rather than an exotic goal producing convergent drives?

Take a definite line instead of giving me a survey of the options; I'd rather have a committed verdict I can argue with, including a clear statement of which parts of your mapping you're least confident about and what would change your mind. You can play an alignment researcher who's also read too much organizational sociology, and start by brainstorming a few candidate mappings before you pick one and commit. Don't sand off the analogy to make AI safety look good — if the case turns out to be a bad fit, say so and tell me what a better one would look like.
