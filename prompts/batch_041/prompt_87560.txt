I am a second-year student in a course on Buddhist philosophy and the philosophy of mind, and for our unit on the not-self doctrine I tried to reconstruct the traditional argument in a form tight enough to defend in a tutorial. What came out is below. Each step looks acceptable to me on its own, yet the final step is a conclusion I cannot accept, and I have not been able to locate the misstep. I would like a diagnosis.

(1) If anything is to count as a self, it must be permanent — not subject to change — and it must not be a standing source of affliction to the one who owns it. Call this the ownership condition: a self that could alter or hurt you is not the kind of thing the word was ever meant to pick out.
(2) The body, feelings, perceptions, dispositions, and consciousness are each impermanent, constantly changing, and bound up with affliction.
(3) Therefore none of them is the self.
(4) Nor is the self something over and above them. No such thing is ever found, and if it were wholly apart from these, it could bear no relation to them or to anything we call our experience.
(5) Therefore there is no self.
(6) A person just is whatever a self would have been. So if there is no self, there is no person — no one who acts, no one who persists from one moment to the next, no bearer of the consequences of action, no one to be reborn, and no one who could be liberated.
(7) Therefore Buddhist practice and Buddhist ethics, which presuppose precisely such a person — someone who suffers now, is answerable for what they do, and can be freed — are incoherent.

I find (5) illuminating and (7) intolerable, and I suspect I cannot keep both in the form stated. Which step should I reject, why did it look unobjectionable, and what does each available rejection cost? I am less interested in being told that the tradition has a distinction that dissolves the worry than in seeing exactly where the argument trades on something it has not earned, and what a defender of the doctrine has to pay to repair it.
