I have been circling an argument that surfaced in a comment thread months ago, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a tour of the literature, so let me write it out as carefully as I can. Each step looks individually defensible, the conclusion is one I am fairly sure I do not want, and I cannot tell whether the trouble is a bad inference somewhere in the middle or a premise I will simply have to pay for.

The setup is the standard Frankfurt case. An agent, A, decides on her own, for her own reasons, to do X. Unbeknownst to her, an intervener has installed a device that would have forced her to do X had she been about to decide otherwise. The device never fires. Nearly everyone agrees that A is responsible for X even though, given the device, she could not have done otherwise, and this is taken to refute the principle of alternative possibilities, on which responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise.

Here is the argument.

(1) A is responsible for X, and A could not have done otherwise.
(2) So in this case the absence of alternatives did not by itself undermine responsibility.
(3) What secures A's responsibility is the way X issued from her own deliberation: the device contributed nothing to the sequence of events that produced X.
(4) The device contributed nothing because it never operated; its entire role is a counterfactual fact about what would have happened had A been about to decide otherwise.
(5) So no fact that fails to enter the actual sequence producing the action can affect responsibility.
(6) How A came to be the kind of agent who would decide as she did — her upbringing, her settled temperament, a designer who built her from scratch — consists of facts that do not enter the actual sequence producing this particular action.
(7) So how A came to be the agent she is cannot affect her responsibility, provided the action flows from her deliberation in the ordinary way.
(8) Now take the Sculptor, a designer who, before A ever acts, builds her values so that she will one day decide to X on her own, with no device and no coercion. By (7), she is responsible for X.
(9) The Sculptor's agent is not responsible for X.

The argument delivers (8); I cannot give up (9). Tell me which step to reject. I would most like to keep (1) through (4) and (9) together — the Frankfurt verdict compels me and the anti-Sculptor verdict feels non-negotiable — so tell me whether that combination is available and at what price. If a step has to go, say which one, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives, including the option of keeping the argument intact and swallowing (8). Cap the answer at 600 words.
