For several years I have worked on large distributed systems, and I keep running into a pattern in how we reason about them for which I have no name. I would like to know whether philosophy of science already has one, and if the nearest established concepts do not fit, I would like a careful account of what the thing actually is and how it differs from those concepts, rather than a bare label.

Here is the pattern. Such a system admits two descriptions. The upper one is coarse — throughput, queue depth, the shape of the latency distribution, the tendency of a cache to stay hot — and it is the description we use to predict and to explain. The lower one is the exact trajectory: which instruction ran, which packet arrived when. We act on the upper description, and it is remarkably stable: move the system to different hardware, rewrite its internals, change the timing of individual events, and the upper-level claims still hold. The lower-level story is not merely unwieldy; it appears to be the wrong place to look for the general truths, since almost everything in it is incidental.

What I cannot classify is this. The clean upper-level behaviour is not something the lower-level description delivers at any actual operating point. It appears only as a limit — as the number of requests grows, as the workload becomes stationary, as the noise averages out — and at every finite, real, messy operating point the lower-level description yields something that does not exhibit the clean regularity, or exhibits it only approximately and by accident. Yet we treat the upper-level law as true of the real system, and we are right to: it is the reliable thing to reason with, and it does not care which of many very different lower-level arrangements is doing the work. So the object of interest is a general truth that (i) is stated at the coarse level rather than the fine one, (ii) is robust across an enormous range of fine-grained differences, and (iii) cannot be read off any single finite fine-grained situation, only off a limit that no actual system attains.

I have heard the words reduction and emergence used in this vicinity, and neither seems to be the thing. Reduction suggests that the coarse truth ought to be derivable from the fine description, whereas here the derivation goes through only in a limit we never reach. Emergence, as it has been explained to me, suggests that the coarse level has novel causal powers of its own that push the fine level around, and I neither need nor want to claim that; the fine level seems to be doing all the pushing. Nor is this simply multiple realizability, if I understand that term, since that says one coarse kind can be realized in many ways, whereas my puzzle is that the coarse law is exact only in a limit and merely approximate everywhere else.

So: does this configuration have an established name, and what is the best-established concept under which it should be filed? I would like you to do three things. First, tell me whether there is a standard term for it and, if so, what it is. Second, if there is no single term, give me the two or three nearest concepts and say precisely where each one diverges from the pattern above — specifically, which of the three features I listed each concept captures and which it misses. Third, in either case, propose a definition of the phenomenon as I have described it that would let me decide, of a given case, whether it is an instance, and supply a boundary case that has some of the three features but not all, so that I can see where the edges lie. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations; I want the carving.
