For two years my metaethics reading group has been circling the same issue — whether the is–ought gap is a substantive result about ethics or a triviality about inference — and I would like a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than another defense of one side. One camp holds that no set of purely descriptive premises entails any normative conclusion; its critics answer that this is true of inference everywhere (nothing about molecules entails anything about organisms without a bridge principle) and so carries no special metaethical weight; a third group thinks the gap can be closed by analytic bridge principles, pointing to the alleged derivation of an ought from the practice of promising, or to thick concepts said to be descriptive and evaluative at once. Take a stock case: from the premise that an act causes suffering one reaches the conclusion that one ought not to do it only by adding something like a principle that suffering is to be avoided — and each side reads that observation differently. My hypothesis is that the parties are not disagreeing about a fact but are using the word entailment in different senses, one deductive and formal, the other closer to justification, and that a second, concealed disagreement about whether bridge principles are themselves descriptive or normative is doing most of the work. Please test that hypothesis against what each side actually claims, say whether the dispute is verbal, a case of different questions sheltering under one heading, or a genuine disagreement about the existence of analytic bridge principles, and tell me what would count as progress — a formal result, a distinction, or an argument that none should be expected.
