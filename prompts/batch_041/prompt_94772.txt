I'm not an academic — I fell into this sideways. I write the words on the wall: exibit panels, plaques, audio-guide scripts, the little cards next to objects. Small museums, battlefield sites, town heritage trails. Maybe twelve jobs a year, a week or two each, and the client is almost always a volunteer board of trustees who also have strong feelings about the story I've been hired to tell.

The problem is that maybe one job in three turns into a fight about a story that isn't true but that people need, or that's doing work for somebody right now. I know the famous ones — Confederate stuff in the States, Masada, the Kosovo battle, Dunkirk — but I keep hitting small local versions: the regiment that held the line when it actually ran, the town that was never bombed, the founder who was a crook.

I've been groping toward my own way of sorting these and I want you to turn it into a procedure I can actually run in the first week of a job, in order, with the questions to ask and what counts as a good enough answer to move on. Right now I go on gut, and I've got it wrong twice in ways that cost the client.

What I've got so far, and tell me where it's muddled:

The present-tense test. A story is live if somebody is using it in an argument happening now — about who owns a thing, who owes an apology, whose budget gets cut, what kids learn. If nobody's using it, maybe it's just a false story and I don't have to pick a fight over it.

The job test. These stories seem to do one of about three things: turn a defeat into a moral win (we lost but we were right, so the loss doesn't count), hide who did what to whom (the doer vanishes into tragedy or hard times), or make the current setup look like it was always going to be this way. If I can name the job, I know what I'd be taking away from someone.

The audience test. Who has to stand in front of the panel — the people the story flatters, or the people it's about?

I also want the output to be one of a small set, and I don't trust my set: tell it as fact, tell it as a story plus what it does, present the dispute as an open dispute, or route around it. Is that the right list? And which of my three tests should I drop, because I'd rather have two that work than three that don't.

Then run it on three cases: a small-town plaque to a local Confederate unit that a heritage group wants restored and a chunk of the town wants gone; Masada at a site where the script currently just tells the mass-suicide story as fact (as I understand it the whole thing comes down to one ancient writer who wasn't there); and Dunkirk at a museum where the gift shop and the school program are built on it and the trustees will be upset if I complicate it.

Two things I need from you. One, mark clearly where the procedure stops and a person has to decide, because that's a values call and not a facts call — I don't want that buried in a footnote. Two, be honest about where it fails, and where it hands me an answer I won't like, including if the answer is that I should leave something alone. Don't give me a reading list or citations — I won't read them and I can't put footnotes on a panel anyway.
