Across a number of democracies a distinctive two-stage institution has taken hold. A body on the order of a hundred citizens, chosen by lot and stratified so that it matches the wider electorate on age, sex, region, and often education and ethnicity, meets over several weekends, hears testimony from experts and stakeholders, deliberates in small facilitated groups, and votes on a set of recommendations. Those recommendations are then handled in one of three ways: enacted by the legislature, put to the whole electorate in a referendum, or quietly shelved. The same political systems that create such bodies also routinely entrench decisions beyond the reach of any majority — constitutional courts, independent central banks, treaty commitments, sometimes balanced-budget rules — and when a court strikes down legislation that a majority supported, it will often describe itself as vindicating the people's own deeper or longer-term will rather than as overruling it.

What conception of 'the will of the people' does this cluster of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Is the will treated as something already present in the electorate and merely discovered by the institution, or as something produced by the institutions that elicit it? Is 'the people' treated as a single agent with one will, or as a plurality of preferences requiring aggregation? And what is supposed to make a body of a hundred people an acceptable stand-in for millions?

Then do three further things. First, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with: which claims about popular will would have to be true for the practice to be coherent, and which are optional glosses that some other theory could equally well supply. Second, identify the rival conceptions the practice rules out — pure preference aggregation, the view that a majority's vote simply is the popular will, the deflationary view that 'the people' has no will at all — and say which parts of the practice would survive under each. Third, examine whether the practice's own justifications are consistent with one another. A mini-public is commonly defended both on the ground that it is a statistically faithful microcosm of the electorate and on the ground that deliberation improves the quality of the opinions it reaches; if deliberation genuinely transforms preferences, is the body still a sample of anything, and if it is not, on what does its authority rest? Why, moreover, should a referendum that re-asks the undeliberated public be treated as either confirming or overriding its judgment?

Finally, say what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed conception of popular will were replaced by a straightforwardly aggregative one, and what would have to change if it were replaced by the view that there is no popular will for any institution to express.
