I am a PhD student in moral and political philosophy working on the ethics of aggregation, and I have been trying to use the 2013 revelations about the NSA's bulk telephony metadata program as a test case for a distinction I have been developing. The distinction is between two ways of aggregating tiny harms. On the summation view, each individual harm, however small, is a fixed negative unit; the moral status of a policy is determined by adding those units across persons, so that enough dust specks can outweigh a torture. On the threshold view, individual tiny harms are morally inert below some structural threshold; what matters is not the sum of the specks but whether the pattern of collection crosses a threshold that produces a qualitatively different, systemic harm—what I call a surveillance artifact—that is not reducible to any individual's experience. I want you to analyze the NSA program through both views. The salient facts are these: the program collected metadata on a vast scale; each individual disclosure was trivial, but aggregated it allowed the government to reconstruct private associations and habits; defenders claimed it was necessary to prevent terrorist attacks, which would be torture-level harms; critics claimed the privacy loss was either not tiny or not aggregable in that way.

I am not interested in a summary of the Snowden debate or in a legal analysis of Section 215. I want a disciplined mapping: what does each view say about whether the program was justified, which features of the episode does each explain, and where does each break? In particular, does the threshold view collapse into the summation view once the threshold is crossed, or does it preserve a categorical difference? And what does the episode show about the original torture-versus-dust-specks thought experiment—does it confirm that the thought experiment is a useful idealization, or does it reveal that the thought experiment misdescribes the structure of real aggregation problems, for example by ignoring the difference between harms that are experienced individually and harms that are constituted by a pattern? I would also like you to say whether the framework itself is doing any work, or whether it is just a redescription of the familiar distinction between act and rule utilitarianism.
