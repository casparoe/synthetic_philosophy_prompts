I'm midway through a doctoral seminar on population ethics, and I have grown tired of adjudicating between theories by trading intuitions about one imagined world at a time. Before I judge any of the contenders, I want the specification: write me the desiderata, i.e. the full list of requirements that any adequate axiology of variable populations must satisfy — a theory whose purpose is to rank outcomes in which the number, identity, and quality of lives differ. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, stated concretely enough that I can see exactly which pair of outcomes it is supposed to rule on and what work it is doing; mark where one requirement is really a special case of another, where one is stronger than it needs to be, and where two are pulling in opposite directions. I also want the list sorted, not merely enumerated: which of these are genuine constraints on any such theory, and which are the commitments of one camp dressed up as adequacy conditions. I have my suspicions — that the demand for a person-affecting structure, the demand for a thoroughly impersonal one, and the demand that a larger population never by itself count against an outcome are all candidates for the second category — but I would rather you decide which is which than take my word for it. For what it is worth, a friend in the program argued last week that the repugnant conclusion only looks repugnant because we compare a vast mediocre population with a small flourishing one from the standpoint of someone choosing which life to live, and that comparing the outcomes from the standpoint of each person in them would dissolve the repugnance; I do not know whether to believe this, and I would like the desiderata written so that the answer to that question is visible in them.

The part I care about most: I suspect that several of these requirements cannot be satisfied together, and I want you to identify the subsets that are jointly unsatisfiable, with the argument that shows it set out in your own terms rather than by gesturing at the literature. The tension I have seen hinted at involves three things at once — the avoidance of the repugnant conclusion, the thought that adding a life that is worth living is never in itself a bad thing, and the thought that an outcome of uniform misery is not to be preferred to an outcome of uniform flourishing with the same number of people — and I want to know whether these really are inconsistent, what a proof of that would look like, and which of the three has to give. Close with a short ranking of which single requirement excludes the most otherwise attractive theories, and a sentence of justification for your choice. Two constraints on how you write it. First, assume I know the standard positions by name and do not need them introduced or attributed, and do not give me a bibliography or reading list; I will supply the references myself, and an argument that leans on who said what is useless to me here. Second, build in rather than bolt on the fact that the theory is meant for sentient beings generally and not for humans alone: the great majority of sentient lives are short, non-human, and without anything like a life plan, so every requirement on your list has to be phrased in a way that survives that, and you should flag any requirement that quietly presupposes a human-shaped biography.
