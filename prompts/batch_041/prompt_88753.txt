For the last six months I've been running a reading group — eight or nine people, mostly software engineers, plus a philosopher of science and one recovering product manager — working through the conceptual foundations of decision theory, and next month the session is mine to lead. The topic is revealed preference and the meaning of utility, fifty minutes, whiteboard, no slides. What I want from you is not an explanation of the material but a plan for teaching it: the order in which the pieces have to come so that nothing is asserted before it has been earned, and why the dependencies run that way. I've been assuming the load-bearing distinction is a three-way one — utility as a feeling, utility as the satisfaction of whatever the agent actually wants, and utility as a mere bookkeeping device for representing choices — but I can't tell whether that trichotomy should be the opening move or the payoff, and I suspect that getting the order wrong is precisely how the whole session collapses into either Benthamite hedonism or a flat behaviorism. I'd also like to know what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud, given that I'd rather leave them with a clean puzzle than a half-digested formalism.

Pick the one example that should carry the session, and tell me what it has to have and what it must not have. I've been toying with the canteen case, where a person's order changes the moment a third dish appears on the menu, and with the case of someone who keeps choosing the thing they insist they don't want, but I'm not convinced either does the right work; if the obvious toy case — you picked the apple, therefore you preferred the apple — is a trap, tell me why. Then give me the misconception map. I'm already braced for half the room hearing revealed preference as the claim that people always get what they want, for the ex-product-manager to read it as a license to treat A/B testing as a theory of welfare, for someone to object that a framework which can rationalize any choice by positing a matching preference is unfalsifiable and therefore empty, and for the philosopher to ask whether this is behaviorism with extra steps. Tell me which of those are really the same mistake wearing different clothes, where in the sequence each one should be headed off, and which I should let the room arrive at on its own rather than pre-empting. Finish with the single question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that would show the idea has landed rather than that they can recite a definition, and close the whole response with a final line of exactly the form \check{...} containing nothing but that question.
