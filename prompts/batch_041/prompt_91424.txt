For a long piece I'm writing on the end of Bretton Woods, I want to read the 1971 closing of the gold window through Aristotle rather than through Keynes. Start by setting out what his account of money and exchange in the Politics and the Ethics actually commits him to — money as a measure that exists by convention rather than by nature, the two uses of every commodity, the gap between managing a household and acquiring wealth for its own sake, exchange as proportional reciprocity grounded in need, and interest-taking as the least natural form of acquisition — and say where the texts leave it genuinely open whether he thinks money must be a commodity with a value of its own. Then map the episode onto that: the dollar as the world's reserve unit, foreign dollar claims outrunning the gold stock, the unilateral suspension of convertibility, the slide into floating rates. Which parts of his analysis does the episode vindicate, which does it leave without resources (credit, a unit of account that is also someone's liability, a monetary order run for the issuer's benefit), and does the natural/unnatural distinction survive? I would also like your view on whether the dilemma later writers call Triffin's has an Aristotelian analogue. Paraphrase the texts rather than quoting them, and flag where you are reconstructing an argument rather than reporting one.
