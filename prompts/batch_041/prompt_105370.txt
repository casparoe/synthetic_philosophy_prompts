I'm a policy analyst at a regional environmental protection agency, where I help run permit reviews for novel technologies and industrial projects—about thirty a year, each on a two-week turnaround, with a memo that has to survive the director's sign-off. I've spent years in rationalist blogs and forums, so I'm allergic to decision procedures that smuggle value judgments into probability estimates or that dress up institutional squeamishness as "the science isn't settled." Our authorizing statute says that where there is evidence of a threat of serious or irreversible harm, lack of full scientific certainty shall not by itself justify inaction, but that any measures must be proportionate and subject to review. Our general counsel has floated a four-rung "precautionary ladder"—plausibility screen, reversibility test, proportionality test, burden-shifting—but hasn't said what counts as clearing each rung, how the rungs interact, or what to do when one says stop and another says proceed. Counsel also keeps collapsing quantifiable risk, unquantified uncertainty, and genuine ignorance under the single word "uncertainty"; I suspect the statute's force depends on which of those is actually in play, and I want the procedure to keep them apart. I also want it to distinguish precaution as a first-order decision rule from precaution as a second-order rule about who bears the burden of proof, because I think our counsel has been sliding between them.

Please turn that into something my team can actually run. I need the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit about which steps are expected-value reasoning and which are deontic constraints, and about the failure mode where precautionary reasoning becomes so permissive of blocking that it gives no guidance at all. Then run the procedure on two permit applications: (a) a proposal to release a gene drive targeting an invasive rodent on an island where the rodent is driving several endemic birds extinct, and (b) a deep-sea mining application for battery metals in an area with poorly studied benthic communities and a plausible but unquantified risk of irreversible sediment damage. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and end by saying what the procedure cannot decide and where a human has to own the judgment.
