I need to write and deliver the after-dinner speech at the retirement dinner for my colleague and former supervisor, who has spent thirty-five years working on numerical weather prediction and the theory of chaotic dynamical systems. She is a determinist of the uncompromising kind: she holds that the equations she has spent her life on describe a world in which nothing is genuinely chancy, and she is at the same time a thoroughgoing pessimist about long-range forecasting. The room divides cleanly in two. Half of it is her scientific colleagues — forecasters, numerical analysts, a few people who have taught the mathematics of nonlinear systems for decades — and they will hear every technical allusion and will notice immediately if the speech quietly contradicts something she believes. The other half is her family: her brothers, her grandchildren, neighbors, people who know her as the person who is never quite listening during a thunderstorm. She has one standing prohibition, which I intend to honor rather than tease: she cannot abide the butterfly metaphor, which she regards as a journalistic distortion of the mathematics she actually did, and she has been known to leave rooms over it.

Please write the speech, roughly five minutes spoken. It has to be warm enough to work on the family and precise enough that her colleagues hear a coherent position rather than a set of nice noises. That means it must not assert anything she would judge false — in particular it cannot console anyone with the thought that the future is still open, and it equally cannot offer the triumphalist line that more computing power would let us know it. The philosophy should be doing structural work, not decoration: what the speech does say should make visible why it cannot say those other things, so that the same sentences land as a tribute for both halves of the room. I would like the argument to turn on a distinction she drew constantly in the lab and that I think is the real heart of the matter — between our not knowing, finely enough, where a system is, and the system's not having a stable answer to the question of where it is at any precision we could ever possess. Build the speech around that distinction rather than around sensitivity to initial conditions as a slogan. Please avoid the standard set pieces: no Laplace's demon, no dice-playing deity, no mention of the butterfly.

After the speech, add a brief note, clearly marked as not part of the speech, saying where its philosophical framing is most likely to be mistaken. Which of its claims about determinism, chaos, and predictability is the most contested, and what would have to be true for this to have been the wrong framing to choose? If you think a defensible reading of the physics would have licensed a different speech, say what that reading is and why you did not adopt it.
