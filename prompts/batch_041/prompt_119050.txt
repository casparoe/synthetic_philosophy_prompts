I'm putting together an episode of a small podcast on AI and philosophy, and the guest works on mechanistic interpretability and is a full-on realist about it — she thinks the features and circuits these tools turn up are real structure in the network, not useful fictions we project onto it. I want to interview her propperly and not just let her do the usual "look, we found the Golden Gate Bridge feature" routine.

Act as a philosopher of cognitive science who also knows the interpretability literature well, and think hard before you write anything. Start with a short brainstorm — just a few bullets — mapping where the live fault lines actually are in the realism-versus-deflationism argument, then draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question each on:

- the gap between information being linearly decodable from an activation and information actually being used by the network (probes, causal scrubbing, activation patching — does she think the causal methods close that gap or just relocate it?), and whether a concrete case like induction heads is a real mechanism or a description that happens to fit
- whether "a feature" is a natural kind or an artefact of choices we made — the sparsity penalty, the dictionary size, the very fact that we hunt for directions in activation space
- a twin-networks thought experiment I want to spring on her: two networks that behave identically on everything we can test but have systematically different internal structure (one looks like it represents a tidy set of features, the other like a lookup table). Does the realist have to say both have representations? And if so, is the word "representation" earning its keep, or is it just a label for "there's structure in there"?
- whether her view is purely structural or needs a consumer — does something have to use a feature, for some purpose, for it to count as a representation, or is being there and making a causal difference enough?
- what would actually count as evidence against her realism, and whether the deflationist alternative (interpretability posits as useful instruments) is even empirically distinguishable from her view.

Finish with a question that makes the standard deflection — "it's early days, it's a matter of degree" — hard to use.

One more thing: in the brainstorm, be straight with me about what's settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't sell a contested position as the consensus. I don't want to get ambushed live.

British English please.
