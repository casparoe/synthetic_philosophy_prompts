For nine years I have been building a research program around an approach that has never quite delivered what it promised, and nearly everyone close to me has converged on the same advice: that wanting to continue because of the nine years is the sunk cost fallacy, and that the only question worth asking is which option looks better from here. I have come to suspect this advice is less an argument than a slogan, so a friend who reads philosophy set out the following case for me, to show that when you take it seriously it cannot be right.

(1) What you ought to do now depends only on the expected consequences of your options from here on.
(2) The past is over; whatever it was going to do it has already done, so which option has the better expected consequences depends only on facts about the future.
(3) So no fact about the past as such can bear on what you ought to do.
(4) Continuing because of what I have already spent is to act on a fact about the past as such.
(5) So continuing for that reason is never rational, and my friends are right.
(6) But the same reasoning covers the fact that I made a promise, that this is my child, that I was wronged, and that I have loved one particular person for twenty years.
(7) Each of those is a fact about the past as such.
(8) By (3) none of them can bear on what I ought to do — yet that cannot be right, since at least some of them plainly do.
(9) So the argument proves too much, and something in (1) through (3) has to give.

My friend will not hear of a fix; the promises and the love, he says, are sunk costs too, and I am merely sentimental about them. I am certain I will not give up (8), and I would rather not have to give up (1) or (2), but I can no longer tell whether that preference is even coherent. Tell me which step is doing the illegitimate work and why it looked innocent, then price the ways out. And then answer the question I actually have: on whatever view survives, is my reluctance to abandon the nine years still the fallacy under a cleaner name, or can it be a reason in good standing — and what would have to be true of my situation for it to be the latter?
