We were up till like 1am arguing about Newton and I still can't let it go, so I want to try to write it out as a poem instead of another text.

Here's the fight. My friend says Newton was basically a physicist who had a weird hobby, and that his theology is a separate compartment you can ignore — the Principia on one shelf, the prophecy stuff and the church-history stuff on another, and the second shelf is just embarrassing noise. I say that's backwards: for Newton the two are one project, and the tell is his God. His God isn't the deist's clockmaker who winds it up and walks away. Newton needs God acting *now*, continuously, holding the world in being and correcting it when it runs down, and he identifies that sustaining presence with space itself — space as the medium in which God is present, the way the world is "in" God. And then there's the other half: he thought the Christianity of his own day was a corruption, that the doctrine of the Trinity was a later imposition, that the pure original religion had been buried, and that reading Scripture's prophecies was a way of recovering it. So the physics and the theology aren't two hobbies; the same move shows up in both — nothing is allowed to run on its own without God's ongoing presence, not the solar system and not the church.

Anyway. I want a poem out of this. Can you write me a dramatic monologue in the voice of Newton, late in life — say in blank verse, maybe 30-45 lines, no fixed rhyme scheme — where he's talking to himself in his study at night, and the *turn* of the poem is the moment the two halves collapse into one? I want the poem to move from the physical picture (the planets, the comets, the machinery that would drift if left alone) into the theological one (the corrupt church, the lost original faith, the God who is *here* and not absent), and I want the reader to feel that it's the same insistence doing the work in both halves — no autonomous clockwork in nature, no autonomous tradition in religion. Don't have him "reveal" the anti-Trinitarian thing in a gotcha way; let it surface as something he can't say out loud, the thing he writes and buries. And the last line or two should land on the presence-of-God-in-space idea in a way that a reader who knows Newton's theology would recognize as *his* position and not a generic pantheism.

Since it's a poem I'm not expecting a bibliography or anything, but I do want a short note after it: tell me which parts of this are actually well-supported in what Newton wrote versus which parts are the contested reconstruction that historians argue about (the extent of his anti-Trinitarianism, how literally to take the "space is God's sensorium" line, whether the prophecy work was central or marginal to him). Don't smooth over the disagreements and don't tell me it's all the consensus view if it isn't — I'd rather the poem be honest about where it's leaning on a reading that's up for grabs.
