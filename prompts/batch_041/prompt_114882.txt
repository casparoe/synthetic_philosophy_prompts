I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and I've ended up reading about the tragedy of the commons because of a work thing. There's this one small open source library that our whole stack quietly depends on, and it's maintained by basically one guy who is very obviously burnt out, and my managers solution is that we should just fork it. I've read enough pop stuff to know the usual shape of this discussion (Hardin's herders, Ostrom's fisheries and irrigation systems, the design principles checklist everyone pastes), and honestly I don't want the checklist. What I want is the most important implications for a company like mine, roughly ranked by how much they should actually change what we do.

Two things I keep chewing on that I think the usual discussion glosses over. First, I think there are two different goods tangled up here: the code itself, which doesn't get used up when I use it, and the maintainer's attention, which absolutely does get used up, issue by issue. If those are two different kinds of goods, what follows from them being glued together like that? Second, I don't think public goods are all the same shape. Some are additive, where every contribution adds a bit. Some are best shot, where one person doing it is enough and everyone gets the benefit. Some are weakest link, where the whole thing is only as good as the least willing contributor. Which shape is open source maintenance, and does the answer change what actually fixes it?

So: the most important implications for us as a consumer, for the maintainer, and for whether forking, or paying, or formalizing the thing is a real fix rather than a way of moving the problem around. And which of the standard takeaways are genuine implications versus slogans people just repeat.
