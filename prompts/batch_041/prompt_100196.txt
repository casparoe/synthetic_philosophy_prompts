I've been following, across a few online communities and a couple of academic departments I'm adjacent to, the recurring fights about call-outs, pile-ons, and deplatforming, and I'd like to understand the thing at the level of moral theory rather than as a series of hot takes. Here is the practice I want you to focus on, because it strikes me as having a strange shape. When someone in one of these communities violates a norm, the response is typically not a fine, not a trial, and not a fixed sentence; it is an open-ended social sanction — a public denunciation, a demanded apology, a withdrawal of platform or collaboration, an entry on some informal list — with no clear point at which the sanction is served. There is no proportionality schedule, no statute of limitations, no formal right to confront one's accusers, and, as far as I can tell, no discharge: the screenshots and summary posts persist, and anyone can relitigate the case years later. Yet the very same communities tend to hold quite specific and quite critical views about state punishment — that it is disproportionately carceral, that it should aim at rehabilitation or restoration rather than retribution or denunciation, that due process is a hard-won safeguard rather than red tape, and that someone who has served a sentence deserves a clean slate. As a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, I'm wary of a motte-and-bailey in which "accountability" means something defensible in the abstract and a pile-on in practice, and I want to know whether the informal practice is a coherent application of a different theory of punishment, an inconsistent borrowing of the worst of both worlds, or something for which our usual vocabulary of punishment simply does not fit.

Please act as a philosopher of punishment and of social norms — someone at home both in the retributive/denunciatory/consequentialist/restorative taxonomy and in the sociology of informal sanction — and give me a careful diagnosis rather than a survey. Start by brainstorming the candidate readings before you commit to one: a denunciatory or expressive theory, on which the point is to reaffirm the norm rather than to change the offender; a retributive theory in disguise; a purely deterrent or signaling story; a restorative theory corrupted by the absence of any forum; or the possibility that calling this "punishment" at all is a category mistake and that it is better understood as the price of continued membership in a voluntary association. Then do the real work: reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice — what it presupposes about who has standing to sanction, about what makes a sanction deserved, about whether severity should track the wrong or the outrage, and about whether sanctions can ever be discharged — and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, since I suspect several theories would license it. Tell me which parts of the practice are internally inconsistent, and what would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with a rehabilitative or restorative one; be concrete about the awkward cases, especially the sanction that is cheap to impose, impossible to discharge, and borne most heavily by the people with the least institutional protection. Please write in American English, and skip the citations and reading lists — I want the argument, not the bibliography. And don't read this as a request to defend or condemn the practice; I'm asking what it commits its practitioners to, whether they know it or not.
