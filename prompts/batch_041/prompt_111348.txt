I'm giving the commencement address at my old college in June — small liberal arts place, about four hundred graduates on the lawn, parents and grandparents fanning themselves, a few trustees, and the whole economics department up front, including the professor who taught me game theory twenty years ago and who will absolutely be sitting there listening to see whether I get anything wrong. I want to do something with the twelve minutes that isn't the usual "follow your own path" business, and I've been chewing on an idea my brother-in-law (he does economics) calls an information cascade, or rational herding — the thing where each person in a line of people is behaving perfectly sensibly given what they've heard so far, and the upshot is that by the end nobody's actually using their own information anymore. What gets me about it is that the people in one of these aren't being stupid or spineless, and they don't conciously experience themselves as conforming at all; they feel like they've independently concluded something. And because the thing getting thrown away is everybody's private information, the collective result ends up hinging on nothing more than who happened to speak first and how confidently — you could reseat the room and the same people would arrive at a different answer. It also stays balanced on a knife edge: one credible person saying something out loud can flip a room that's been settled for years, because all the swallowed doubts are still sitting there. And the absence of dissent means nothing at all, since a cascade is precisely a machine for making dissent look pointless.

Here's what I want the speech to actually do, and this is where I keep getting stuck. I'm standing in front of four hundred people saying the same thing to all of them at once, which is more or less the definition of the thing I'm warning them about. So I can't just end on "so think for yourselves" — that's one more public signal from a guy with a microphone, and they'll all nod and agree with each other about how important it is not to agree with each other. I'd rather the speech *enact* the point than announce it, if that's possible — maybe by getting them to notice something about the room they're sitting in, or by sending them off with one small specific thing to do in their first week at a new job or a new program. Something like being the first person in a new group to say something uncertain out loud, since being early is most of the leverage, and since saying "I don't know, but I noticed X" adds information where agreeing adds only weight. I spent a while investigating industrial accidents for a living, so I can also tell them about a report I once read where forty-odd people had each noticed one small thing and said nothing, and how the summary line — "several employees had concerns" — is what one of these looks like from the outside, afterwards.

So: twelve minutes, no jargon (the family half of the audience has to enjoy it, and I don't want it sounding like an economics lecture), at least one real laugh, and it should land for the game theory professor without him thinking I've mangled it. Don't do the "journey" stuff, and don't put any quotations in it — I'm not going to stand at a podium and attribute a line I haven't read. Tell me if the ending I'm imagining is too cute to work.
