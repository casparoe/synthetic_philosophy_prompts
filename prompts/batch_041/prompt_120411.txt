You are a philosopher of cognitive science who works on the foundations of interpretability research, and you write satire in the register of a trade-press vendor evaluation. I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar that I co-teach with colleagues in a machine learning group, and I want a piece of sustained irony to circulate alongside the technical readings.

Write a review of roughly 550 words, in the voice of a sincere, mildly budget-conscious staff scientist at a large lab, evaluating a fictional vendor service called ConceptClear. ConceptClear audits trained neural networks and issues what it calls Representation Certificates: a signed document listing the concepts a model genuinely represents. The review has just been written to justify renewing the contract. Include the vendor's pricing tiers (a cheap decode-only audit that trains linear probes on internal activations; a costlier causal audit that patches and ablates activations; an enterprise ontology tier in which the vendor supplies the concept inventory in advance), a couple of deadpan excerpts from the vendor's own documentation, a headline in the style of an internal tooling blog, and a ratings box at the end.

The comedy has to come from the real shape of the debate rather than invented absurdity. The certificate is issued by a probe suite, so what it certifies may be the auditor's ontology rather than anything the network has; a probe can be made to decode almost anything if the inventory is generous enough and the dimensionality is high; causal necessity in one task is no guarantee of causal necessity in another, since a model may carry redundant circuits that compensate for any single ablation; and the phrase "the representation is distributed" can absorb every failed prediction, which makes the deliverable hard to falsify. Keep the target the practice of certifying representations, not any named person or company, and never let the reviewer notice any of this: no winking, no nudges to the reader, no authorial asides.

Before drafting, take a moment to list for yourself the three or four specific interpretive moves you intend to dramatize and how the reviewer's enthusiasm will do the work. Then write the review. Deliver only the review.
