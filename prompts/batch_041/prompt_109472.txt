I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and I've been asked to close out a graduate seminar on Rousseau that has spent the term on the Social Contract and, for the past three weeks, on the Letter to d'Alembert on Spectacles — the 1758 reply to d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article proposing a theatre for Geneva. Rather than end with the usual summary of the term, I'd like to read a poem aloud with three colleagues, and I'd like you to write it for me. Please take the role of a political theorist who also writes verse, and work in stages: begin by setting out for yourself the three or four claims in the Letter that a poem of this kind has to get right, then draft, then revise once against those claims before showing me the result.

The poem should be in the voice of Rousseau himself — warm, declamatory, personal, addressing his fellow Genevans, and quietly aware that he is doing something scandalous, since the man who argues against the stage is himself a man of the stage and his letter is itself a performance. Architecturally I want a two-movement poem of roughly twenty to twenty-six lines, no fixed metre or rhyme. The first movement should be a soliloquy: one voice speaking to an audience that does nothing, which is precisely what Rousseau thinks the playhouse trains us to be. The second movement should be a round, or canon, for three voices, laid out with staggered entrances so that a reader can see the overlap, and written so that the lines change meaning as they return in the next voice — the same words should be able to mean I am watching and I am being watched depending on who is singing them. The formal point is that in the fête Rousseau prefers there is no audience left over, because everyone singing is also hearing and the spectators are the spectacle. End on a single line that could be spoken either by an actor on the boards or by a citizen in the crowd, so that it is undecidable which of them has said it. Please also include one stage direction in parentheses, addressed to the audience, instructing them to do something that would dissolve them as an audience.

Accuracy matters more to me than prettiness. Do not make Rousseau a simple enemy of pleasure or a generic moralist about art. The claim I care about, and the one usually missed, is that the theatre's first principle is to please, so it must flatter whatever audience it finds rather than correct it: a comedy can make the honest man ridiculous and the manipulator agreeable, and even a play that portrays virtue well loses its force because we know it is fiction. The objection to actors is not that they are wicked but that their art is a trade in counterfeiting themselves, in displaying feelings they do not have. And the argument is local — it concerns a small, austere city, not the abolition of the Paris theatres, and it targets the playhouse rather than spectacles as such. A reader who knows the Letter should recognise it in the poem; a reader who does not should still be able to sing the second half.
