Here's a pile of my gut reactions about sunk costs, and I want to know what rule, if any, they add up to. Pouring more money into a failing restaurant because of what I've already sunk in: clearly a mistake. Staying in a war because of the soldiers already lost: mistake. Finishing a novel I hate just because I'm 200 pages in: mistake, though a harmless one. Now the other pile. My uncle keeps his late dad's failing hardware store open, and I don't think he's being irrational, even though the books look like any other money pit — though if a stranger were thinking of *buying* that same store with the same prospects, I'd say don't. Someone three years into learning piano and still bad at it: quitting feels like a small betrayal of the person who did all that practising, not just a sunk cost. And I keep going back and forth on the PhD case — five years in, dosen't like it, no real career payoff either way — does the fact that those five years happened give any reason at all to finish?

My half-baked principle is that sunk costs count when the past spending built something that's still there (a skill, a relationship, a customer base, a promise) and don't count when it's just gone. But I can't tell if that's the real distinction or if I'm just relabelling. I also wonder if the right test is something like: if you woke up tomorrow with no memory of having spent anything, would you still choose it? That seems right for the restaurant and wrong for the hardware store, which is maybe the whole problem.

So what principle actually captures these verdicts, what does it say about cases I haven't listed (someone who stays in a town they moved to for a job that ended years ago, or a scientist who keeps funding a line of research after a decade of null results), and which of my verdicts would I have to give up to keep the rest? Write it out in plain continuous prose — no headings, bullets, or bold, just the way you'd say it in an email.
