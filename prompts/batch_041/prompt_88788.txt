Three weeks ago my sister died, at forty-six, after fourteen months in what the neurologists called a minimally conscious state. Her eyes opened and closed on something like a sleep schedule, she tracked a moving hand, she startled when a door slammed, and in all that time she did almost nothing that anyone in the family could reliably call a response — though twice she squeezed a hand when asked, once in a way that could easily have been coincidence. On Saturday I am giving the remarks at her memorial. The room will hold about forty people: our parents and cousins, her friends from the rowing club, and — because they asked to come, and because I want them there — four of the clinicians who looked after her, including the neurologist who first tried to explain to me what the scans do and do not show.

Reading since then has made the ordinary memorial speech impossible for me, because I keep falling into a distinction that has turned into a trap. In one sense, consciousness means roughly this: information is taken up, held, and made available to the rest of the system, so that it can be reported and can guide what the person does. That is what the bedside exam and the scanner are testing, and it is what the global workspace picture is a theory of. In the other sense, it means that there is something it is like to be her — experience itself, whether or not it is ever broadcast, reported, or used. And then everyone, clinicians included, slides from the observation that the broadcasting machinery is not producing a signal to the conclusion that there is nothing it is like to be her. I understand the pressure: the only evidence anyone can have of experience is behavior of some kind, so an absence of behavior reads as an absence of experience. But there is a further step I cannot get past. Even the striking studies — the patient who cannot move a finger, asked to imagine walking through her own house, with the corresponding brain region lighting up on cue — show that a command is being received and acted on somewhere; they do not show who, if anyone, is there to receive it. And I have read the argument that no study of that kind could ever show it, because every such paradigm asks the patient to do something, and doing something is already the first sense of consciousness rather than the second. If that argument is right, nothing I could ever have been shown would have settled the question. If it is wrong, the question gets settled one patient at a time, and never, in advance, for her.

So: write the memorial remarks. They will be delivered to that mixed room, so they have to work for people who will find the word consciousness strange in my mouth, and for four people who will hear exactly which literature I am gesturing at and will notice if I misuse it. I will not tell her friends that she heard them, and I will not tell the room that she was already gone, or that we spent fourteen months tending a body; both are claims about the one thing I cannot check. Nor may the speech dissolve into pure hedge — a memorial in which the speaker will not say whether the person was there has failed at its job. The difference between the two senses of consciousness must be the load-bearing structure of the speech rather than its ornament: it should be the reason some sentences are available to me and others are not, and the reason I can offer that room something better than either false comfort or cold withholding. Five to seven minutes, and let the closing line be one I could defend to the neurologist. If you conclude that the two senses cannot in fact be kept apart in a speech to this room, say so plainly and then write the best speech that is still available to me.
