I am giving a seminar paper to an education faculty next term and want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defence. My thesis is that the standard reception of Whitehead's philosophy of education — avoid inert ideas, and observe the rhythm of romance, precision and generalisation — misses the load-bearing claim, namely that the three stages are meant to recur, so that precision is worth having only when the learner is repeatedly re-entered into a fresh romance; and that on this reading Whitehead is committed to something most of his admirers would deny, namely that a curriculum which never returns a pupil to romance is worse than one that never reaches precision at all. To give the argument a target I have coined a name for the failure I am attacking — the precision trap, the condition of a learner who is technically competent and permanently unable to care about the subject again. The audience is mostly assessment and learning-science specialists committed to outcomes-based curricula, measurable learning objectives and standardised testing; two Whitehead sympathisers from the philosophy-of-education side; and a handful of practising teachers who are sceptical of both camps. Prepare me for the roughly six to eight questions I will actually get from this room — not the ones a Whitehead conference would ask — ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary that faction would use, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede before the first question is even asked, and include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises.
