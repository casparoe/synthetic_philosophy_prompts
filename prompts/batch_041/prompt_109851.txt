So I got into this arguement with two friends, Sam and Alex, and I'm honestly not sure who's right. It started because I told them about a puzzle I'd been chewing on. Imagine a lamp with a switch. At 1 minute before noon you flip it on. At half a minute before noon you flip it off. At a quarter, on. At an eighth, off. And so on, halving the time each flip. At noon, has the lamp been flipped infinitely many times, and is it on or off? (I think these are called supertasks but I'm not sure.)

(1) Sam said the lamp can't be either. The sequence of states is on, off, on, off... and that sequence doesn't settle down to anything, so there's simply no fact of the matter about the state at noon. The description is incomplete; you could stipulate that it's on, or off, or that it explodes, and nothing in the setup rules any of those out.

(2) Alex said that can't be right, because the lamp is a physical object. At every moment before noon it has a definite state. At noon, the switch is in some definite position. There's no such thing as a physical object with no state. If the sequence doesn't settle it, that just means the setup is impossible, not that the lamp is in limbo.

(3) Sam replied that Alex is assuming the setup can be completed. A supertask isn't a physical process; it's a mathematical description. As a mathematical description it's perfectly consistent: for every finite n, the state after n flips is determined. What's missing is only a rule for the limit. So the puzzle shows that "the state at the limit" isn't automatically determined by "the states approaching the limit." That's a real lesson, not a contradiction.

(4) Alex said that's changing the subject. If you say the limit state is just whatever you stipulate, then you're not talking about the lamp anymore; you're talking about a mathematical sequence. The original question was about the lamp, and for the lamp the answer has to be on or off.

(5) Sam said that's like saying a lamp can't be neither on nor off, as if we're breaking the rule that everything either is or isn't. But "on" and "off" just don't apply to the lamp at noon — it's not a third state, it's like asking whether the number 7 is green.

(6) Alex said a lamp is not a number. A lamp that's been toggled infinitely many times still has to be in one position or the other, and if the description can't tell us which, the description is bad, not the world.

(7) Then I threw in a variant I'd made up. Suppose instead of a toggle switch, the lamp has a dimmer knob. At 1 minute before noon the brightness is set to 1. At half a minute it's set to 1/2. At a quarter it's 1/3. At an eighth it's 1/4. So at step n the brightness is 1/n. At noon, the brightness is 0 — the lamp is definately off. And that seems perfectly determinate: the sequence 1, 1/2, 1/3,... converges to 0, and the lamp is off at noon.

(8) Sam said that proves his point. The dimmer case is determinate only because the values converge. The toggle case is indeterminate because they don't. It's all about convergence, and convergence is a fact about the mathematical description, not about the lamp.

(9) Alex said it proves his point. In the dimmer case there's a fact about the lamp because the lamp's brightness is a physical quantity that changes continuously. In the toggle case, the switch is a discrete mechanism, and a discrete mechanism that's been toggled infinitely many times still has to be in one position or the other. If the description can't tell us which, the description is bad, not the world.

(10) Sam said why should physical continuity matter? The dimmer is just a sequence of settings too. Alex is picking the cases where the math gives a limit and calling those the physical ones.

(11) Alex said because in the dimmer case the limit is forced by the physics of a continuous knob — you can't have a knob whose position jumps. In the toggle case there's no such constraint, so the setup is underdescribed.

(12) Sam said so Alex admits the toggle case is underdescribed. That's all Sam said. Alex just doesn't like the word 'indeterminate.'

(13) Alex said no, the toggle case as described is impossible, not underdescribed. An underdescribed possible lamp would be one where you forgot to mention the color. Here the missing information is the state itself, and a lamp that has no state at a time isn't a lamp.

That's where we left it. Can you go through the exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? I'm especially interested in whether the dimmer variant actually breaks the symmetry the way each of them thinks it does, and whether 'the setup is impossible' and 'there's no fact of the matter' are really different answers or just two ways of saying the same thing. Also, is there a version of the toggle case where the answer is determinate after all? If so, what does that show? And what, if anything, are both of them missing?
