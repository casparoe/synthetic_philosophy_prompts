I teach a 75-minute methods workshop for sixteen graduate students in history, anthropology, and information studies, and I want them to feel in their hands how an archive's silences are made rather than merely announced. The topic became personal for me last month when I tried to reconstruct a land dispute in my family's village from a county archive and found only the state's petitions, tax rolls, and surveyors' notes; the villagers' own accounts were missing, and I could not tell whether they had been destroyed, never written down, or simply never described. Design a runnable classroom simulation for that session. Do not tell participants the lesson first. Assign roles: records creators such as a land-office clerk, a missionary teacher, and a village council secretary; an appraisal committee with a fixed retention budget; and researchers who arrive a generation later at a national repository. Give them a fictional documentary universe of about two dozen index cards representing letters, ledgers, photographs, oral testimony transcripts, and administrative forms. In round one, creators produce records and appraisers decide what is kept, destroyed, or retained but left undescribed; access restrictions and metadata choices should be live decisions. In round two, researchers receive only the surviving cards and a finding aid, and must answer a concrete historical question about the dispute for points that convert into a minor privilege in the next class. Arrange the rules so that the researchers' inferences visibly depend on what earlier players discarded or failed to describe. Then design the debrief to map what happened onto archival power: appraisal as gatekeeping, provenance and original order as interpretive frames, description as a second filter, digitization as a third, and the difference between deliberate suppression and routine bureaucratic silence. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce—the slow, generational, often non-intentional production of silence, and the recordkeeping practices of communities whose formats the game cannot represent—and say what I must supply in the debrief to cover that residue.
