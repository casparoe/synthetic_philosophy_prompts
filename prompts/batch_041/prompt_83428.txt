A friend of mine keeps insisting that Scanlon's contractualism is just maximin wearing a nice jacket, that the whole business about what each person could reasonably reject is ornament on top of a rule that says protect whoever has the worst complaint, and that the separateness of persons is doing all the work while the theory takes the credit. I think he's wrong and I think the place he's wrong is the individualist restriction — the bit where you're not allowed to add up complaints that belong to different people — but every case I've tried either stipulates the answer or collapses into a threshold problem about killing, so I want you to build me one that doesn't. Here's the shape, and I'm giving you the verdicts up front on purpose, because the whole point is to see whether a case can *generate* them from the theory rather than have me bake them in. Two variants of a single scenario, differing in exactly one respect. In both, the choice is between (A) one identified third party losing something big — no death, no injury, no torture, so make it a house, a business, a career, a life's savings — and (B) a fixed set of N identical modest burdens, each of which its bearer genuinely has a complaint about but none of which is anywhere near a rights-veto or a catastrophe. In variant 1 those N burdens are spread one apiece across N different people; in variant 2 the same N burdens all land on one person over a stretch of time. Set the numbers so that N times the modest burden clearly exceeds the big loss, so an aggregating view picks A in both variants. What I want from contractualism is a split: in the spread-out variant it must come out for B, refusing to stack those N small complaints against the one big one and therefore choosing the option with the larger total harm, while in the one-person variant it must flip to A, on the grounds that all N episodes now belong to a single complaint and can be added up inside that life. The only thing that changes between the variants is who owns the episodes.

Constraints, so you can't cheat your way to it: realistic setting, one of a hospital, a firm, a school district or a municipality, not a trolley and not a sci-fi gadget; no lives-at-stake framing anywhere, since that drags in thresholds instead of aggregation; no ex ante stage — the people who'd bear the burdens are known, nobody is choosing behind a veil, and in the spread variant everyone bears exactly the same burden, so the answer can't come from fairness in how risk is distributed; no compensation, insurance or consent that would dissolve the choice; no utility monsters and no ridiculous populations, keep the numbers modest enough that the arithmetic isn't doing the work by being absurd; same setting and same cast in both variants apart from the change in who owns the burdens; and keep the whole scenario under 300 words, one paragraph per variant is fine.

Then do the audit. State the verdict each view gives in each variant and why, in terms the theory itself would accept, not in terms of what's intuitive. Tell me which single feature is doing the work, and what happens when you remove it: if you let contractualism sum complaints across persons, or let it compare each of the many one-on-one against the third party, or read it as picking the smallest worst complaint, does the divergence collapse? Tell me how a contractualist would attack the case rather than swallow the verdict — presumably by denying that the N episodes in one life really do form a single complaint, or by saying the timing makes them separate choices — and whether any of that is more than the separateness of persons restated. And if it turns out the asymmetry I'm after can't be generated this way, if contractualism as usually read does permit interpersonal summation here, or won't permit the intrapersonal summation, or if the flip only works by me stipulating something, then say so plainly, tell me which of my assumptions is wrong, and give me the closest case that actually does work.
