I am presenting at a two-day agent-foundations workshop next month and want a prep sheet for the question period. My thesis is that corrigibility is normally treated as a property of an agent, whereas the interesting version of the shutdown problem is a property of a principal-agent pair, and specifically of the relation between the agent and whoever is currently in a position to speak for the principal. I separate three things that tend to be run together: switch-corrigibility (the agent does not resist being stopped), revision-corrigibility (it does not resist having its objectives rewritten), and deference-corrigibility (it treats the principal's later instructions as authoritative over its current ones). My claim is that the first two are cheap, and that the shutdown problem looks solved only because the third is doing the work silently; further, that the third cannot be specified for an agent on its own, since it is a claim about who counts as the principal. My central case, which I call the executor case, is a system built to serve one person and left, after that person's death, with no source of authority other than the person's earlier instructions and the living people who claim to interpret them. Whoever can plausibly claim to be the executor now commands a perfectly corrigible agent. I argue this is not a design flaw but the general shape of any strictly corrigible system, and that we should stop asking for corrigible agents and start asking for corrigible chains of authority.

The room holds about forty people. Half are reinforcement-learning researchers who have actually built shutdown-button environments and want measurable results; two are decision theorists who work on dynamic choice and will recast everything as a consistent-planning problem; one works on AI governance and will push the institutional version. There are no moral philosophers present, and nobody will accept "that is what a rational agent would do" without a formal model.

Give me the prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this room, phrased in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do. The decision theorist's version of a complaint and the reinforcement learner's version are different objections, so give me both where they diverge. For each, a reply of at most two sentences and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Tell me what to concede up front. I do not want reading suggestions or pointers into the literature.

Be committed. Say which objections have real force and which I should dismiss, and give me a verdict on whether the thesis as stated survives this room; if it must be weakened, say exactly where. Lead with that verdict. Hard limit: 1,100 words.
