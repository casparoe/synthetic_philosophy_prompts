For the past two years I've been developing a heterodox answer to the Meno problem, and in three weeks I have to present it as a work-in-progress talk to our department's epistemology reading group. The room is small but unusually mixed, and what I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense. Roughly a third of the group are virtue reliabilists: they think knowledge is an achievement, that the difference between knowledge and mere true belief lies in the quality of the agent's performance, and they will hear any attempt to explain the value of knowledge in non-agential terms as a category mistake. Another third are formal epistemologists who work on the value of information and think of everything in terms of expected utility, credences, and the option value of inquiry; they will want the value claim to be a quantity that can actually be computed. Two people are knowledge-firsters who hold that knowledge is an unanalyzable primitive and the most general factive mental state, so they will deny that there is a gap to be bridged at all. One is an ancient-philosophy colleague who will want to know what exactly I take Plato to have been asking and whether my solution is even addressed to that question. And there is a visiting decision theorist who will probably say nothing for an hour and then ask one question that reorganizes everything.

The thesis I'll defend is that the Meno problem is generated by a presupposition we should reject — namely that if knowledge is more valuable than true belief, the extra value must be a property of the belief state itself. I argue instead that value is always value relative to an activity, and that the relevant activity here is inquiry: knowledge is valuable because it is the state that licenses the knower to stop inquiring — to act without further checking and to be held answerable for error — whereas true belief, however reliably produced, does not by itself confer that license. I model the benefit as a kind of option value: knowledge preserves the option to act while releasing the cognitive resources that continued checking would otherwise consume, so the extra is not a bonus ingredient in the belief but a change in what the knower is entitled to do. My private worry is that this either collapses into the value-of-information story the formal epistemologists already tell, or into a merely pragmatic story that the virtue reliabilists will insist has nothing to do with knowledge's distinctively epistemic value.

Please write the prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from this specific room rather than the generic objections of the literature, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; phrase each one the way that particular sub-group would phrase it, so I can recognize it as it arrives; and for each give me a roughly two-minute reply plus an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credibility, what I must hold firm on, and — the part I most need — the single question I am hoping nobody asks, with a plan for it. Assume I already know the standard literature; what I need is the collision between my position and these people's particular commitments.
