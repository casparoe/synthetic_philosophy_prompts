I direct a small financial coaching program at a community credit union, and we offer a menu of commitment devices—locked savings accounts, automatic transfers on payday, escrowed rent, restricted-access cards, and a few similar instruments—to members who say they want to save but repeatedly fail to follow through. We currently make these calls by intuition, and I want to replace that with a reusable decision procedure grounded in the philosophical literature on weakness of will and in the behavioral economics of hyperbolic discounting. The procedure has to run in a thirty-minute intake by a coach who is not a licensed clinician; it has to ask its questions in a fixed order; it has to say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; it has to mark the points where it stops with a no, where it refers the member to a counselor or a domestic-violence advocate, and where it hands the decision to a supervisor because the question is one of values rather than facts. Please preserve the distinctions that matter: weakness of will as a failure to act on one’s own settled, all-things-considered judgment, versus a genuine change or unsettledness in what the member wants; present bias and preference reversal over time, versus ordinary impatience; and the difference between a commitment device that supports autonomy and one that becomes a new source of coercion or shame. The procedure should not treat every missed payment as akrasia, nor every akratic episode as hyperbolic discounting, and it should be explicit about which steps are diagnostic and which are moral or practical judgment dressed up as diagnosis. I do not need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I need something my coaches can use Monday.

Then run the procedure on three members. The first is a man who says he is determined to stop using payday lenders, has said so for two years, and each month, when the money is in his hand, does it anyway; he describes the moment as if he is watching someone else act. The second is a woman who says she wants to save for a house, but every time she accumulates a balance she sends it to her mother and siblings, and when we press her she says she does not really resent it and is not sure the house matters more than being the one who helps. The third is a member with a stable and clearly stated intention to save, but whose partner controls the household cash and has previously taken her bank card; a commitment device might protect the savings or might provoke retaliation. One of these cases should receive an answer I will not like, and I want you to say plainly which case that is and why the procedure gives it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will misclassify, which distinctions collapse when the coach is rushed or the member is evasive, and where the program should simply accept that it is making a value judgment rather than pretending to apply a rule.
