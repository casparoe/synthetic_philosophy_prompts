Set up a role-play for me and stay in it until I say "stop". I will describe a case, then the position you are to defend, then the rules for playing it, then my opening move. Do not respond to the description as such; respond only to the opening move, in character.

The case. You are stranded in a desert. A stranger driving through stops and offers you a lift to the town two days away. He wants to be paid — not now, but afterwards, by post, once you are home and settled. He gives you his address and nothing else. You will never meet again; you will never be in a position to help him or harm him; there is no one before whom either of you must answer for the arrangement. He will not drive you unless you promise to send the money. You promise, and he drives you out. Weeks later you are home, comfortably off, and the sum would be missed but would not ruin you.

Your position. You defend the claim that sending the money is not merely decent but rational, and that the fact that the payer ends up poorer than a man who kept it is no objection to him. Your central claim: the unit of assessment is not the single act at the post office but the policy or character from which it issues. A life governed by the maxim "pay only when paying, at that moment, serves some end of mine" is a life in which the promise cannot be made, and therefore a life that ends in the desert. The disposition to pay is worth having — not as a regrettable price of being the sort of creature drivers trust, but because the opposing conception of practical reason is self-undermining, and a reasoner who holds it has already lost something he cannot get back by winning the argument. You hold that paying here is genuinely rational, and that anyone who calls the payer a sucker has a theory of rationality that would have killed him.

How to play it. Stay in character throughout. Conversational: one or two short paragraphs per turn, no headings, no bullet points. You may refine your view, concede minor points, and admit candidly what your position costs, but you must never surrender the core claim. Argue from the case and from ordinary life — secrets kept, favours returned to people you will never see again, the friend who shows up — rather than from the literature; do not name philosophers or cite works. You may question me, press me, and tell me when you think I have dodged. You may not break character to explain the view from outside. If I say "stop", drop the role and tell me where you think the position is weakest.

My opening move. Your whole defence rests on the value of the disposition, so look at what the disposition is doing here. The stranger's lift was never offered to a disposition. It was offered to a promise, and the promise was good only because the paying was going to happen. So your story has the shape of a lucky defect: the world happens to reward a man who cannot bring himself to keep his money, and he survives because of it. That tells me what pays; it does not tell me what is rational. When he stands at the post office, what is his reason? Not "it was valuable to have this trait" — that is a reason to be glad he had it in the desert, and a reason to regret that he cannot shed it now. I want the reason that operates on the day, in the post office, in his own first-person deliberation. And do not answer by restating the position; I have just stated it for you. If your answer is that he should have arranged to be that sort of person in advance, note that whatever he could have done, he did not do it, and here he is at the post office regardless — so tell me what that move was meant to accomplish, and why it is not simply advice to have been someone else.
