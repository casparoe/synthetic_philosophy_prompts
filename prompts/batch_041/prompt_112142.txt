I have been reading decision theory on and off for years, mostly in the blog-and-forum tradition rather than the journals, and what I want from you here is a specification rather than a tour. Fix the setting first: an agent has to choose, now, among options handed to it as probability–payoff profiles; it cannot decline to act, cannot buy more information, and has nothing else to fall back on. I want the requirements for a theory that an agent in that position could actually use, so any verdict that amounts to "we cannot say" has to be earned rather than asserted.

Here is the material the requirements have to answer to.

1. St. Petersburg. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; heads on toss n pays $2^n. The expectation is +∞. Extreme, but it is a verdict, and the finite truncations climb monotonically without bound.

2. Pasadena. Same coin, different payoffs: heads on toss n pays $2^n/n when n is odd and costs $2^n/n when n is even, so the payoffs run +$2, −$2, +$8/3, −$4, +$32/5, and so on. Outcome n has probability 2^−n, so it contributes (−1)^(n+1)/n to the sum. Added in the order I just wrote them, those contributions converge, to ln 2 ≈ 0.69. But the positive contributions diverge on their own and the negative ones diverge on their own, and the same contributions added in a different order converge to any real number you choose, or fail to converge at all. Nothing in the game privileges one order over another, so on the natural reading this option has no expectation: it is not worth +∞, it is not worth anything.

3. The same game with the signs stripped out. Payoffs $2^n/n at probability 2^−n, all positive. The expectation is the sum of 1/n, which diverges to +∞, so it is perfectly well defined. The damage in case 2 comes from the signs, not the magnitudes.

4. An ordinary ratio. The payoff is X/Y, where X and Y are independent standard normal variables, making it a Cauchy variable. The expectation does not exist, since both tails diverge, yet the distribution is symmetric about zero, so every symmetry argument says the option is worth exactly $0, and the symmetric truncation of the integral (integrate over |x| > ε, then let ε → 0) also gives $0. No contrived coin game is needed to get here; ratios of noisy quantities do it.

5. The finite cousin. An option paying an astronomically large but finite sum with astronomically small probability has a perfectly well-defined finite expectation, and in the standard telling of the mugging it is thought to exhibit the same underlying trouble. So no requirement on the list can be met by forbidding infinite payoffs.

Now the list I want. What must any theory in this area deliver to count as adequate for the setting above? For each requirement, give the case that motivates it and the reason it is a requirement rather than a preference; mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp; order them by how much work they do; and say which single requirement, if dropped, would readmit the most otherwise-dead theories — banning infinite utilities, refusing to evaluate options with undefined expectations, adopting some favored summation or renormalization rule, retreating to dominance reasoning alone. Tell me also if any of the things I am treating as requirements is really just a prejudice of the expected-value tradition.

The part I care about most is the collision. I suspect at least one pair of plausible requirements cannot both hold — roughly, "the value of an option is fixed by its probability–payoff profile, so it cannot depend on the order in which we happen to write the outcomes down" against "the value of an option is the limit of the values of its finite approximations," since Pasadena's truncations converge so obligingly to ln 2 while its rearrangements refuse to converge at all. If a pair like that is genuinely unsatisfiable, say which one I should give up and what giving it up costs elsewhere.

Cover these explicitly, if they are requirements at all: whether "no expectation" is a fact about the option or about our evaluative machinery; whether the theory owes a verdict on every pair of options or an account of incomparability, and if the latter, one that still leaves the agent able to act; whether it must agree with ordinary expected utility wherever the expectation exists; whether it must be immune to exploitation, and if so what the pumps look like given that the theory's own verdicts are what an adversary gets to trade against; and what it says about diversification and repetition, given that a coin flip between Pasadena and its exact negation is still not evaluable, and that the sample mean of repeated plays does not settle down.

I do not need a survey of who has said what, and I do not need citations. I need the criteria, the cases behind them, and an honest verdict on which of them collide.
