I sit on the curriculum committee of a small secondary school, and we have now deadlocked across three meetings over a proposal to replace our ninth-grade physics unit — currently a sequence of lectures, worked examples, and weekly recall quizzes on density, pressure, and buoyancy — with a semester-long boat-building project in which the pupils are meant to arrive at Archimedes' principle largely on their own, through trial and error on the water. A colleague who teaches history has been quoting A. N. Whitehead at us, chiefly the 1922 address "The Rhythm of Education" and its three stages of romance, precision, and generalization: she argues that our present unit is a machine for producing what Whitehead called inert ideas — notions received into the mind and never used, tested, or combined with anything else — and that we are force-feeding precision to children who have never had the romance, so that they leave able to manipulate formulas they can neither enjoy nor apply. I am inclined to agree with her, but I am uneasy, because the other faction on the committee — the science department, mostly — replies that no novice can rediscover in a term what took centuries to discover, that unguided exploration swamps working memory, and that the evidence favors explicit instruction, worked examples, and retrieval practice, with whatever romance there is coming as a reward for pupils who survive the drill. Both sides are trading slogans, and I would like to see the two positions actually argued out.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent, well-informed advocates. The first is a Whiteheadian: she defends the rhythm of romance, precision, and generalization, the diagnosis of inert ideas, and Whitehead's insistence that schools attempt too much and teach too little thoroughly — and she must be given answers, not evasions, to the cognitive-science objections. The second is a proponent of explicit, teacher-led instruction grounded in cognitive load theory, the expertise-reversal effect, and the empirical case against pure discovery learning — and he must be able to say, honestly and specifically, what he thinks is right in Whitehead's complaint, rather than dismissing it as romanticism. Neither may be a strawman; each should engage the other's actual points and concede where a concession is warranted. I would like the exchange to stay concrete, returning often to the buoyancy unit and to what each of them would actually do on a Monday morning with a class of fourteen-year-olds, and I would like it to press on a question I have not seen addressed: whether Whitehead's rhythm is supposed to describe a single lesson, a single topic, a school year, or an entire education, and whether that ambiguity makes the doctrine hard to test or merely hard to apply. Neither speaker may invent quotations and attribute them to Whitehead; they should paraphrase his views in their own words.

The result should read as a genuine back-and-forth rather than two monologues in alternating paragraphs, roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, with the speakers clearly labeled. Because our committee meets again on Thursday and will want a decision, please close with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" in which you name, in one sentence, which position the exchange left better supported and on precisely which point.
