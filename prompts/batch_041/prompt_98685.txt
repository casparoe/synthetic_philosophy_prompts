ok so I'm in a course on early modern philosophy and we've just done Hobbes on method, and I've got a probem with something on the hand-out.

The picture we were given is this. Hobbes thinks you can only get real, step-by-step demonstrable knowledge of something when you make it yourself, because you properly know a thing's causes only when you know how it was put together by you. Geometry is the model case, since the geometer constructs the figures he reasons about -- you draw the triangle, so you know what's in it. Politics gets to count as a science on the same basis, because we make commonwealths: we assemble them out of covenants and authorisation, so we can know their causes the way we know a triangle's. Physics is the poor relation, because nature was made by God rather than by us, so we're only ever guessing at its causes. (Apparently he also thought he'd squared the circle, which didn't go well for him, but thats by the by.)

Then the hand-out gives us this arguement, and I think it's doing something fishy but I can't put my finger on it:

(1) We have science of a thing only if we construct it, i.e. only if we know how it was made, by us.
(2) Geometry is the model science, because the geometer constructs the figures he reasons about.
(3) We construct commonwealths, so there can be a science of politics.
(4) But we also construct games and their rules, languages, legal systems, made-up worlds, and arguably our own past actions.
(5) So by (1) we can have demonstrative science of chess, of invented languages, of a made-up world, and of what we did last Tuesday.
(6) Having science of a fiction is absurd: what's true in a world you invented is true because you said so, so there's nothing there to be discovered and no error to be corrected.
(7) So (1) is wrong, or at least the maker's-knowledge idea isn't what makes geometry certain.
(8) And if geometry's certainty comes instead from our simply stipulating the definitions and axioms, then its truths are arbitrary conventions -- and the same would go for politics, which would make Hobbes's political science as arbitrary as whatever the sovereign happens to will.
(9) So the geometric grounding of politics gives us either no knowledge at all or knowledge that is arbitrary, and either way it doesn't do the job he wants it to.

I'd quite like to keep (2) and (3) -- I do think there's something to the thought that we can know political arrangements in a way we can't know the deep causes of nature. But (9) feels right to me too, and I can't have both. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If it helps, tell me whether the thing is broken by one bad move or whether every step is fine and I just have to pick the least bad thing to abandon -- and if some step only looked fine because of an ambiguity, say which reading I was sliding between.
