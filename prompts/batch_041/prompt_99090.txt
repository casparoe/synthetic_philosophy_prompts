I'm working through Adam Smith's chapters on colonies in the Wealth of Nations and keep getting stuck on somthing. In Book IV, ch. 7 he grants pretty cheerfully that the discovery and colonization of America increased Europe's enjoyments and augmented its industry. Then, a bit later, discussing the exclusive trade the mother countries reserve for themselves, he writes: "The exclusive trade of the mother countries tends to diminish, or, at least, to keep down below what they would otherwise rise to, both the enjoyments and industry of all those nations in general, and of the American colonies in particular. It is a dead weight upon the action of one of the great springs which puts into motion a great part of the business of mankind." He goes on to call it a clog that embarrasses the pleasures and encumbers the industry of all other countries, "but of the colonies more than of any other."

My question is how to hold those two claims together. Is Smith saying colonization was on balance bad for Europe, or is the "dead weight" point only about the monopoly arrangement and not about the trade as such — and if it's the latter, is the "would otherwise rise to" counterfactual doing all the work? Relatedly, when he says the surplus produce of the colonies is the original source of the increase in enjoyments and industry that Europe gets from the discovery, does that mean he takes the monopoly to be parasitic on an underlying trade that is genuinely productive, so that the harm is a matter of distribution and unrealized gain rather than of absolute loss?

Act as someone who knows eighteenth-century political economy well and help me sort this out. Brainstorm the possible readings first, then commit to the one you think fits the text best and say what would tell against it. Keep it under 350 words, and don't worry about citations — I just want the logic of the distinction.
