I'm a first year PhD student in a consciousness lab and I keep getting into fights with my PI about what counts as data. So heres where my gut lands, case by case. Blindsight patient, says he sees nothing in his blind field, points to the right spot almost every time: I say we've learned something real about his vision, and I'm willing to call it a thin kind of experience. Patient with Anton's syndrome, cortically blind, cheerfully reports that the room is full of flowers and that the doctors are lying about her eyes: I don't count that report as evidence of any experience at all, I count it as confabulation. Split brain patient whose left hemisphere invents a reason for what the right hemisphere just made the hand do: same, the story is confabulation, but I still think there's an experience on each side doing the work. Someone comes back from a dream and tells me about it: I take the report as evidence about the dream, even though by the story's own lights he was in no position to be a reliable witness to it while it was happening. And a meditator who reports that the sense of self drops out: I'll use that as evidence about attention, but I refuse to let it count for or against whether there's a self.

The one I can't settle: hypnotic analgesia. Subject says the pain is gone, but heart rate, sweating and withdrawal reflexes say otherwise. Do I trust the report or the body?

Is there a principle that gets all of this? I want one that says when a sincere report is evidence of experience and when it's just more behavior waiting to be explained, and I want to know which of my verdicts it makes me drop. My PI's line is that all reports are just more behavior, which I hate, but I can't say why. Tell me what your principle predicts for the hypnotic case, and pick one more case I haven't mentioned that would test it. Dont just lay out the options -- commit to one principle, even if it costs me a verdict.
