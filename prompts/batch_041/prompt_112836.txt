I sit on the advisory board of a small foundation that has earmarked roughly four two-year postdoctoral fellowships, plus a workshop budget, for work on the semantic and logical foundations of metaethical expressivism — specifically the cluster of problems usually gathered under the Frege–Geach heading. The board is not committed to any particular camp, and it would rather fund the questions that make the most difference to the rest of the field than the ones that happen to be most discussed. I have a background in formal semantics and the philosophy of logic, and I keep suspecting that the way the problem is usually posed hides what is really at stake, so I would like help framing the agenda before we write the call for proposals.

Here is the tangle as I see it, and I would like you to tell me which strands are load-bearing. On one telling, the problem is not really about moral language at all but about how force and content compose: a sentence must make the same contribution to the validity of an argument whether it appears embedded or unembedded, and the expressivist appears to owe an account on which the very same sentence expresses an attitude when asserted and something else when negated, disjoined, or placed in the antecedent of a conditional. That suggests the live question is whether the recent revival of act-theoretic accounts of propositions — propositions as types of acts, or as things individuated by the attitudes they are for — dissolves the problem or merely relocates it into a general theory of propositional unity that is contested quite independently of metaethics. A second strand is the negation problem: whether the difficulty expressivists have in saying what attitude a negated normative sentence expresses is a special case of the embedding problem or a separate and possibly deeper one, and whether relational or higher-order treatments that seem to work for negation and the conditional generalize to disjunction, quantification, tense, modality, and to normative vocabulary beyond the narrowly moral — epistemic and practical norms, thick evaluative terms, and the like. A third is whether the quasi-realist or minimalist route to truth-aptness genuinely earns the expressivist the right to logical vocabulary, or only postpones the debt to a story about what makes discourse representational. And underneath all of these is a question I think is underrated: whether expressivism is best read as a thesis about mental states or as a thesis about meaning, and whether an expressivist who is happy to accept a thoroughly truth-conditional, compositional semantics is still an expressivist at all, or has quietly become a cognitivist with an unusual metaphysics of content.

What I want from you is a defended research agenda, not a survey. Rank the open questions in this area by how much they matter for a foundation with four fellows and two years — say five or six, ordered — and justify the order by the criteria that should drive it: which questions are upstream of others, in the sense that settling them would settle or reframe the rest; which are tractable for philosophers with a logic and semantics background working largely alone, and which need an empirical or interdisciplinary arm; how many other questions in the area depend on the answer; and which currently fashionable formulations you would actually deprioritize because their resolution would not change much downstream. For each item, say what the competing answers are, what a decisive contribution would even look like, and what would move it up or down your ranking over the next few years. Finally, tell me which of the puzzles I sketched above you think are genuine and distinct, which are the same problem wearing different clothes, and which are artifacts of a framing we could simply abandon — and if the last, what we should say in the call for proposals instead. I am not after a reading list or a set of citations; I want the reasoning about priority, and I would rather you reconstruct the arguments from the content of the problems than gesture at who is supposed to have settled them.
