I'm in a grad seminar on aesthetics and we've spent the last three weeks chewing on aesthetic testimony — whether you can legitimately form an aesthetic judgement on someone else's say-so, or whether you've got to see/hear/read the thing yourself. The pessimists (who trace back to Kant's third critique, and who've been restated in various ways since) and the optimists (who basically say testimony is a normal source of justification and there's no special reason it should stop at the gallery door) have been going at this for decades now, and honestly it feels stuck. Every optimist example gets the same pessimist counter, every pessimist argument gets the same optimist reply, and I've never once seen anyone actually change there mind. What I want from you isn't a survey or a verdict on who's right — I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this partly a verbal dispute about the term 'aesthetic knowledge', where one side means knowledge-that and the other means knowing-what-it's-like, and neither notices? Is it two sides answering different questions under one heading — the pessimist asking whether testimony can transmit the experience, the optimist asking whether it can transmit justification? Is it just the general epistemology-of-testimony debate wearing an aesthetic hat? Or is there a hidden claim about aesthetic properties — that they're response-dependent, or constitutively tied to first-person experience — doing the real work underneath?

Here's my hypothesis, and I'd like you to actually test it rather than nod along: I suspect the real fault line is that the pessimists treat aesthetic judgement as constitutively first-personal, so a 'judgement' you took on someone's word isn't an aesthetic judgement at all but just a belief about the work, while the optimists treat 'aesthetic judgement' as simply a belief with a special subject matter. If that's right, the fight isn't really about testimony at all — it's about what makes a judgement aesthetic in the first place. So tell me whether that's the actual shape of the impasse, and where my framing oversimplifies. Two more things. First, I know the pessimism/optimism labels were imported from the moral-testimony debate (I think it was Hopkins who coined them there?) — does that parallel genuinely illuminate anything here, or is it a false friend, given that aesthetic judgements are supposed to be tied to experience in a way moral ones maybe aren't? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a reframing of the question, some empirical result about how people really form aesthetic beliefs?
