My friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about uploading, and she left me with an argument I can't get out of. Her view is that if you survive gradual replacement, you also survive being copied, and that the second result is absurd enough to sink the premise that produced it.

(1) Replace my neurons one at a time, in five hundred sessions over a year, with silicon units that reproduce the outputs of the neuron they replace for every possible input, matched in timing and in what they connect to. At every stage I remember everything, recognize everyone, pass every test I used to pass, and report no change in what it is like to be me.
(2) A change that leaves every psychological fact about me untouched — no memory gone, no disposition altered, nothing I could notice or report — makes no difference to whether I survive it.
(3) So each replacement leaves me intact. And if each one does, so does the whole series.
(4) So when the last biological cell is gone I am still here, now running on silicon.
(5) But if what survival comes to is just the psychological facts being in place, then a copy assembled from scratch in the next room — same memories, same dispositions, same reports — is in exactly the same position as the silicon version of me.
(6) So the copy is me as well. Yet it would sincerely say that it is me, and it can't be that two people are one person.
(7) So (2) is false: there is more to my surviving than the psychological facts. But I can't see what that extra thing could be without its being something spooky.

I would rather keep (2) than posit a spooky extra fact, and I would rather keep (6) than say that a duplicate in the next room is me. She says that if I keep (2) I must also accept that I would survive being stored as a pattern for a century and then switched on, which I find worse still. So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me?
