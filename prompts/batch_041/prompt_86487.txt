My friends and I are trying to find Sam, who has wandered off somewhere in a large festival site. The site divides into a north half, containing the main stage and the food court, and a south half, containing the campsite and the swimming lake. We have no information about which half he is in, and have been treating the four areas as equally likely. An information volunteer tells us: "I can't tell you anything about which half he's in; I have no information about that at all. But I can tell you this: if he is in the north half, then he is at the main stage with probability three in four." Tom thinks this settles the matter: leave the north–south odds at fifty-fifty, since the volunteer said nothing about them, and redistribute the weight inside the north half so that the main stage takes three quarters — making it our most likely single location at three in eight. I am not convinced. Tom seems to have quietly added a rule that the volunteer's sentence does not contain, and it also seems odd to me that the sentence should tell us anything about where Sam actually is, given that the volunteer admits to knowing nothing about the half the sentence is about. A second volunteer, asked separately, gave us the mirror-image report about the south half, which has not helped us agree. Which of us is right, and is there a single correct answer to be had here? I would like to know whether we learned anything at all, what principle is supposed to fix our new odds if anything does, and — since we can search only one area before the headline set begins — where we should go first. Please say how confident you are in what you claim, and identify the point you are least sure of.
