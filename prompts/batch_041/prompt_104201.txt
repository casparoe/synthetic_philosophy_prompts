My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and we kept talking past each other because we were each running a different theory of what amnesty is for without noticing it. I want to set the thing out properly and have you adjudicate the framework rather than the verdict.

Here is the episode as I would state it, and I would ask you to take these points as given rather than litigating them. The Commission, established in the mid-1990s as part of the negotiated settlement, could grant amnesty from criminal and civil liability to individuals who made full disclosure of gross violations of human rights committed with a political objective. It was an individual and conditional amnesty rather than a blanket one, and it was paired with public victim testimony, the compilation of a historical record, and recommendations for reparation. The bargain is commonly described as an exchange of truth for impunity, and both its defenders and its critics agree that it purchased the transition at the cost of punishment.

The framework I want to test is one I have been carrying around, and I want you to tell me whether it holds. Suppose we distinguish three ways in which amnesty can be justified, which I take to be separable:

First, as a prudential bargain — justified only because, given the balance of forces at the moment of transition, it was the price of any transition at all, and unjustified in better circumstances.

Second, as restorative — justified because it purchases something of intrinsic value (truth, public acknowledgment, the victims' voice) and because retributive punishment would itself have prolonged the violence of the old order; on this reading the justification does not depend on the correlation of forces.

Third, as constitutive — justified because the public act of conditional amnesty itself performs a political transformation, refounding the community on a new basis, so that its value lies in the collective act of redefining the wrong rather than in what it buys or concedes.

What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a survey. For the Commission, say which of the three the design best fits, and what in the design — the conditionality, the individuality, the public testimony, the non-judicial character — tells for or against each. Then tell me what the framework cannot capture about the episode, and where it forces the case to speak when the case is silent. Bring in the Argentine path (a truth report followed by trials, then amnesties, then their later undoing) and the Rwandan gacaca process, but only as tests: do they instantiate different axes, and do they show that the three come apart as cleanly as the framework claims, or that they collapse into one another? Finally, run it the other way: what does the episode do to the framework — confirm it, complicate it, or expose it as a distinction without a difference? In particular, is the truth that amnesty is said to buy ever independent of the bargain that bought it, or is it shaped by that bargain from the outset?

Tell me plainly which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on transitional justice and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.

Please write in British English.
