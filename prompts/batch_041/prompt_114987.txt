For most of my life the philosophy of perception was, I am slightly embarrassed to admit, a subject I had filed under interesting but not mine. That changed this spring. I began having brief episodes in which I see things that are not there: a drift of coloured shapes across one side of the visual field, and now and then something that looks for all the world like a familiar object resting on a table. My neurologist says the episodes are harmless and that I should pay them no attention. What I cannot dismiss is the question they have made vivid: while an episode lasts, the experience is, judged from the inside, of just the sort that occurs when I look at something real, and yet I would not dream of treating it as evidence about the room I am standing in. Before I read any more theory I want the specification. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate account of perceptual experience has to deliver if it is to make sense of this, and of the ordinary cases alongside it.

Give the list in full, with the case or consideration behind each entry. Among the cases: ordinary veridical seeing; seeing something as other than it is, as when a straight stick half in water looks bent; my episodes; after-images and the phosphenes one can produce by pressing on a closed eye; and experiences brought about by direct stimulation of the visual system, which I understand can be indistinguishable, for the subject, from seeing. Among the distinctions the list must not collapse: between the phenomenal character of an experience and its epistemic standing; between what caused an experience and what the experience is; and between the claim that a hallucination is subjectively indiscriminable from a perception and the claim that the two are experiences of one fundamental kind. I want the last two kept firmly apart, since much of the argument I have been reading seems to run them together; and I would like an entry requiring a theory to say what the argument from illusion has going for it without helping itself to the assumption that indiscriminability implies sameness of kind. Say of each entry whether it is a genuine requirement or merely a commitment of one camp, and identify any pair you think cannot be jointly satisfied. The one I keep circling is this: a theory that treats the two experiences as of one kind seems to hand the hallucination the standing of the perception, while a theory that insists they are of different kinds owes an account of why they are indistinguishable from the inside. Tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate theories, and then, in a few sentences each, how the disjunctive view, the sense-datum view, and the representationalist view fare against the list. One further item to consider including: when an episode begins in a certain way I can sometimes recognise it as an episode while it is still going on, and the experience itself does not seem to me any the less like seeing for that. Add a requirement to cover this if you judge one is needed, and say whether it is a requirement at all or merely an artefact of my attending closely to myself. I do not need a reading list; I want the specification, pitched at the level of cases and constraints rather than slogans.
