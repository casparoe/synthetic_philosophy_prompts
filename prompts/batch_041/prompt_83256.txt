I'm putting together the take-home final for my MA seminar on knowledge and rationality — the last third of the term is epistemic logic, and I want one question that seperates the people who've actually understood the material from the ones who've memorised the labels. Obviously it's the logical omniscience question, but I dont want another essay on "agents in possible-worlds models know all the tautologies, isn't that silly." Here's the crux I'm after.

In class I've been drawing a distinction I haven't seen put quite this way, and I want the exam to test whether they can use it rather than just nod at it. Two things get run together under the heading 'logical omniscience'. First, a model can hand the agent every logical truth — all the validities, thought about or not. Second, a model can make the agent's knowledge closed under what she knows: knows p, knows that p implies q, therefore knows q. I've been calling these 'tautology-omniscience' and 'closure-omniscience' in lectures; the names are mine, not standard, so I don't want the question to make them sound like they came from the literature. They come apart — you can have either without the other — and my line in lecture has been that it's the closure version, not the tautology version, that makes reasoning pointless, since if all the consequences of what you know are already known there's nothing left for deduction to do. The real question is which of the usual repairs (impossible worlds, awareness or representation-based approaches, resource- and relevance-sensitivity, or just biting the bullet and calling it an idealisation) fixes which of the two, and whether any of them fixes both without either smuggling the answer back in as an extra primitive or quietly giving up on knowledge as a propositional attitude.

So: write me the question, then a model answer of about 700 words, then a marking scheme. Constraints. (a) The question has to force them to build a small concrete case in which some agent's knowledge has one of the two properties but not the other, not just characterise the problem in the abstract — I want to see whether they can construct the thing, not recite the complaint. (b) No citations and no names-and-dates; I've told them I don't care who said it first, and I don't want a question that someone without library access could only bluff their way through. (c) The marking scheme should be five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, each with a one-line note on how students typically blow that point, plus a sentence at the top on what separates a 17+ from a 13.

One more thing. At the end of the model answer I want a short paragraph naming the single claim in it that's doing the most work — the one that, if it turned out to be false, would flip the whole verdict — and saying where the reasoning is most likely to be shaky. That's the part I'll read closely, because if the load-bearing claim is wrong then the marking scheme is worthless.
