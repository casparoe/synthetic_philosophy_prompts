I am an undergraduate in a seminar on deontology and its critics, and I am trying to state the pattern in my own verdicts for a short response paper. I would like you to help me find the principle behind them. Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I need the principle and its verdicts. Keep your answer to at most 700 words.

Here are my verdicts on eight cases, each involving a choice between one death and five:

1. Runaway trolley: A trolley will kill five people. I can divert it to a side track where it will kill one. Verdict: I may divert it.

2. Transplant: A surgeon can kill one healthy patient and use his organs to save five dying patients. Verdict: I may not.

3. Footbridge: I can stop a trolley from killing five only by pushing a large bystander onto the track, killing him. Verdict: I may not.

4. Culpable attacker: A gunman is about to kill five people. I can stop him only by killing him. Verdict: I may.

5. Innocent shield: The same gunman is holding an innocent child as a shield. I can stop him only by shooting through the child, killing her. Verdict: I may not.

6. Villain’s bargain: A villain will kill five people unless I kill one innocent person myself. Verdict: I may not kill the one, so I let five die. I keep going back and forth on this case.

7. Redirecting the villain: The villain is about to kill five, but I can trick him into killing one person instead. Verdict: I may redirect him, even though one innocent dies.

8. Inherited trap: A long-dead villain left a trap that will kill five. The only way to disable it is to kill one innocent person. Verdict: I may not.

My tentative principle is this: I may intentionally harm a person only if that person is a culpable threat, or if the harm is a foreseen side-effect of redirecting an existing threat away from a greater number of people. I may never intentionally harm an innocent person as a means to saving others, even when the alternative is that a culpable agent will harm more people.

Does this principle capture all eight verdicts? If not, what is the nearest principle that does, and which verdicts must I give up? State the principle precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Then apply it to two cases I have not considered: (a) I can save five by redirecting a threat toward one innocent, but the one is my own child; (b) I can save five by killing one innocent who has consented to be killed. Finally, address the paradox of deontology directly: if constraints are justified by the disvalue of wrongdoing, why may I not kill one innocent to prevent a villain from killing five? Is my principle coherent on this point, or does it treat constraints as brute side-constraints? Please also say honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
