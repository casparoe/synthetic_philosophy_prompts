I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I keep returning to a puzzle about inheritance taxation. The usual arguments I see in comment sections treat inheritance either as just another voluntary transfer or as a uniquely illegitimate form of unearned advantage, and neither seems to fit the actual institutional practice. That practice, in the United States, is a strange hybrid. There is an estate tax, but it applies only above a high exemption threshold, so most estates never pay it. There is a gift tax, but it has an annual exclusion and a lifetime exemption, so ordinary family transfers are largely untaxed. And capital gains are forgiven at death through step-up in basis: the heir's cost basis is reset to the value at the date of death, so appreciation accumulated during the decedent's life is never taxed as income. Wages, by contrast, are taxed from the first dollar, and the estate tax is formally levied on the donor's estate rather than on the recipient's inheritance. The result is an institution that simultaneously recognizes a public claim on dynastic wealth and shields most of the mechanisms by which dynastic wealth is actually transmitted. I want to know what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that the right to bequeath is strong but not unlimited, that the state's claim is triggered by death rather than by holding or receiving, that equal opportunity is compatible with large intergenerational transfers so long as they are taxed once, or something else? Reconstruct the implicit theory of inheritance, desert, and equal opportunity that would make the whole package coherent. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: which parts of the tax code would survive under several rival theories, and which parts are doing real philosophical work? Is the hybrid philosophically underdetermined, or is there a single view it quietly rules out? Finally, suppose we replaced it with a system built on a straightforward equal-opportunity premise: a lifetime, non-transferable inheritance allowance for each person, with transfers above that allowance taxed at or near 100%, and no step-up in basis. What would that system presuppose that the current one does not, and what would have to change in our conception of property, family, and desert for it to be legitimate?
