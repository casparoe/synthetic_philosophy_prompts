I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy — early modern is my main thing, but I'm sitting in on a comparative philosophy seminar this term and I've gotten a bit obsessed with the Mencius vs. Xunzi fight. The textbook version is clean: Mencius says human nature is good, Xunzi says it's bad, they're opposites, done. But the more I read the less that holds up. Mencius's "nature" seems to be a set of little incipient stirrings — the four beginnings, compassion, shame, deference, a sense of right and wrong — which he compares to sprouts, i.e. seedlings rather than saplings. Whereas Xunzi, when he calls nature bad, seems to be pointing at raw desire and the plain fact that if you just let it run you get conflict and disorder, and he's also emphatic that anyone can become a sage, that a person in the street can become a Yu. That sounds way more optimistic than "bad" would lead you to expect. So a friend in the seminar proposed a test I cant decide whether to accept: if two theories of human nature imply the same practical program — the same pedagogy, the same rituals, the same way of raising a child — then the disagreement between them is idle, a dispute about what gets called "nature" rather than about anything that matters. She thinks this dissolves the Mencius–Xunzi thing, since both of them agree that moral cultivation takes sustained effort and a decent environment. She also thinks it dissolves the modern nativism/empiricism fights, which I suspect is overreach.

So: write me an essay on whether the Mencius–Xunzi disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Weigh the case that they're talking past each other (different senses of xing, different explanatory targets, one explaining why we can be good and the other explaining why we aren't already) against the case that they genuinely disagree — about whether moral development is more like tending seedlings or more like steaming and bending crooked wood over a frame, about what moral education is actually doing when it succeeds, and about whether the sprouts are already moral or merely morally relevant material. Then give me a verdict rather than a shrug: which way do you come down, and why. If you think the "same prescriptions implies idle dispute" test is the right test, say so and defend it; if not, tell me what the right test would be and whether the Mencius–Xunzi case passes it. You can lean on the Ox Mountain bit and the child-at-the-well example as test cases if that helps — and say something about whether the two views would design different institutions or just describe the same ones differently.
