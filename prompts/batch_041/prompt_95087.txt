Retired chemist here — 35 years in a lab, now reading philosophy in my 60s, mostly decision theory because my poker group keeps arguing about it. I've noticed a pattern in my own betting that I dont have a name for, and I'd like to know if there's one, or whether the name I half-remember is the right one. No reading list please, I just want the idea and where its edges are.

The pattern: I'm fine not knowing things. Coin flips, sealed envelopes, tomorrow's weather in a city I've never set foot in — I'll take either side as long as the odds are roughly fair. What makes me back off is a particular kind of not-knowing: the kind where the answer is already settled and somebody else already knows it. Last month my buddies and I were betting on whether a drug trial would hit its endpoint. I know nothing about that field and I was fine with it — until I heard that a friend-of-a-friend at the company had seen the data. Nothing about the trial changed, I was never going to ask him anything, and I still wanted no part of that bet. Same thing in the office bracket pool: I'll pick games I know nothing about, but I skip the ones where there's a guy in the pool who actually follows that conference. And here's the part that puzzles me more. If you tell me the outcome is settled but nobody knows it and nobody ever could — the number is in a safe that went overboard, say — the queasiness vanishes entirely and I'll bet happily.

So it isn't how much I know, and it isn't just that I dislike unknown odds. It's whether someone else knows more than me, or whether the thing is knowable at all. That doesn't line up with the Ellsberg urns as I understood them, where the whole point seemed to be that people avoid the option whose composition is unknown. I dont mind that urn. I mind hearing that somebody else has looked in it.

Is there a name for this? I suspect there's a specific one, and that it isn't just plain ambiguity aversion. But I don't want a bare label — tell me how the thing is carved. Is what I'm describing one phenomenon or two (the "somebody knows" case and the "unknowable in principle" case feel like they might come apart)? Where does it stop applying — does it need an actual competitor, or just the thought of one? And is the standard verdict that I'm being irrational here, or is there a respectable line on which refusing a bet because someone else has the answer is perfectly sensible? If nothing quite fits, coin something and tell me its boundary cases.
