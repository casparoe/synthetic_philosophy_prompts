I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree and I'm currently taking a course on knowledge, skill and action, most of which has been spent on the dispute over whether knowing how to do something is a species of propositional knowledge or something else altogether. I have to pick a topic for a 6,000-word term paper, and I'd like that paper to do double duty as the seed of a research proposal I'll be writing over the summer, so the choice matters to me more than an ordinary essay title would. My constraints are roughly these: one term of about ten weeks, a supervisor who works on epistemology, no possibility of collecting data of my own, and a project that has to be doable from a desk with a library.

Two things about the literature leave me unsure where the live questions actually are. First, the examples are almost always the same ones — riding a bicycle, swimming, playing chess — and I'd like you to take seriously the possibility that this narrow diet of examples has distorted the debate. Consider what happens if the central cases are instead kinds of know-how whose content looks fully articulable, such as knowing how to run a particular statistical test correctly, knowing how to construct a proof by induction, or knowing how to work through a surgical checklist; or kinds of know-how that are governed by explicit protocols and transmitted by instruction rather than by imitation. Second, it isn't clear to me that the dispute is about one thing at all: ability, disposition, tacit understanding, skill, reliability, and practical modes of presentation appear to be doing quite different work in different contributions, and I suspect that some apparent disagreements are disagreements about what the term is being used for. I am also unsure whether the debate has anything useful to say about artificial systems that can both produce fluent instructions for a task and perform the task, but where neither capacity tells us much about what is going on inside them — whether that belongs on the agenda or is a distraction is part of what I'm asking you to judge.

What I want is an agenda rather than a survey: an ordered set of the open questions in this area that someone in my position ought to work on, with the ordering defended. For each candidate, say what the question is, why it is upstream of other work in the area rather than downstream of it, how tractable it is for a person working alone from the armchair, and what would move it up or down the list. Then tell me which questions I should leave alone, and why — including any that look central but whose resolution would not change much else. Please say explicitly whether your ranking would shift if the cases I raised in the previous paragraph were treated as the central ones rather than as curiosities at the margin; if the ranking is robust to that, say so and explain why.

I'd like the answer in British English. I am not asking for a reading list or citations — I can find the literature myself, and I would rather have your reasoning than a bibliography. Finally, please be careful to distinguish what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it: where a claim is a live disagreement, mark it as such rather than presenting it as the consensus, and where you are giving me your own judgement rather than reporting the state of the field, flag that too.
