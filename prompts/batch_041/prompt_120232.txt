I have been working through a line of thought in Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and it leaves me with a difficulty I cannot resolve on my own. The idea, as I understand it, is that certain propositions function in our thinking not as things we know or believe, but as the unquestioned framework within which knowing, doubting, and testing take place. Such propositions are not supported by evidence, and, on the account I have been given, they cannot coherently be doubted by anyone who is still in a position to inquire at all. I find this powerful, but it seems to generate a problem rather than solve one.

If a hinge proposition admits of no justification, then nothing available to me can show that I hold the right one; and if it admits of no doubt, then nothing can show me that I hold the wrong one. It appears to follow that which hinges I hold is, from where I stand, a matter of accident, an inheritance from however I happened to be raised. Two communities reared on incompatible hinges would be unable to offer each other any reason to change, and neither could appeal to anything beyond its own training. The view thus threatens to deliver precisely the relativism it was meant to forestall.

What I would like, then, is a method for choosing hinges. Should I favour the ones that make inquiry most productive, or the ones my community can sustain with the least friction, or something else again? And prior to that: by what criterion do I determine which of my commitments are hinges in the first place, so that I can set about replacing the poor ones with better ones? I would rather have a procedure than a picture.
