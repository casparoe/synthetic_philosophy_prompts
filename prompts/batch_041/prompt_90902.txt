I've been going down a rabbit hole on the "singleton" idea — the thought that at some point you end up with a single decision-making agency (or process, or institution) with such a decisive edge that no rival can ever catch up, and after that the history of contest is basically over. I find it slippery in a way I can't shake. Everyone I talk to pictures one machine or one world goverment, but the versions I find most interesting are the ones where it isn't a single agent at all — where what's "single" is a decision procedure, or a standard of legitimacy, that everyone has quietly handed their sovereignty over to because the alternative was a race none of them could survive. And I keep wondering whether a thing like that is stable, or whether it's the sort of arrangement that can only ever look stable from the inside.

So: write me a short story, around 1500 words, no need to be precious about the exact length. You're a sci-fi writer who's also read too much philosophy of technology — think it through before you start, maybe jot yourself a few notes about what the story has to accomplish, then write. Setting, roughly: a few generations after the transition, in a port city that used to be contested and now isn't, where the protagonist works in a small office handling the petitions people file when they think the common procedure has gotten something wrong. What I definately care about is that the story not tell us which kind of arrangement this is. It could be a true singleton — one agency, no rival, ever — or it could be a very stable hegemony that keeps winning and calls itself inevitable. I want the reader to finish not knowing, and to feel both why people would trade a great deal for it and what they'd be trading away. And I want the protagonist to hit a case that makes them ask whether the procedure is doing real moral work or just laundering decisions somebody would have made anyway.

Don't give me a verdict — it's a story, not an argument. But do add a short note (a paragraph or so) after it, on where the story takes liberties with the idea or leans on a contested reading of it. And if the way I've set this up is confused — if "singleton" already means something that makes the ambiguity I'm asking for impossible, or if a story just can't do the work I'm asking it to do — say so plainly instead of writing around it. Start with the story itself, no preamble and no restating the question; the note goes at the end.
