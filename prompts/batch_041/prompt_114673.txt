I cover science and ideas for a living, and next month I am running a session at a small ideas festival: about twenty participants — a mix of journalists, a few neuroscientists and engineers, and interested members of the public — in one room for seventy-five minutes, with no screens and no real money. I am writing a long feature on whether it could ever be defensible to say that a reinforcement-learning agent suffers, and I want this session to make the room feel the force of the distinction the whole debate turns on, rather than hear it asserted from a lectern.

The distinction I have in mind is between a reward signal — a scalar that shapes what an agent does — and valence, the felt quality of an experience. I want participants to run into the gap between the two from the inside. Here is my sketch, which you should feel free to replace with something better: for part of the session, participants act as agents, something scores them, and they come to care about the score in the ordinary way; then the scoring is quietly severed from what it tracked, and the room is split so that half are agents who can report on their own states and half are trainers who see only behaviour and must infer. The moment I am after is the one in which someone notices that they are still working for the token and it has stopped doing anything for them — while the trainers, watching, cannot tell the difference.

What I need from you: rules; materials that fit in one bag; a payoff scheme that needs no money; roles; a timeline that fits the seventy-five minutes; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the philosophical work — the signal's functional role, the felt quality, why behaviour underdetermines which of the two is present, and what would have to be true of a system for either to be attributed to it. No lecture up front, and no naming of the terms until the debrief. The only deception should be about the scoring mechanism, never about anything personal to a participant. Tell me concretely what will go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure as it happens. And be candid about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which the facilitator must supply in words. Write the plan in British English.
