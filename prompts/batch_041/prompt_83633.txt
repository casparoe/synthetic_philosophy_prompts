My father died in February and the book I wrote about him came out in the autumn; since then people have been telling me, always kindly, that they read it in two sittings, that they loved it. I have been reading around the paradox of tragedy ever since — Hume's essay on the subject, and the standard answers that run through eloquence, through make-believe, and through the pleasure we take in our own responses — and none of them quite covers a sad book that is sad because it is true. Write me a poem about it, in the voice of the person who made the sad thing, addressed to the reader who said she loved it, and let the form carry the argument: a pantoum, in which the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third of the next, and the last quatrain reaches back to the first, so that the same words keep arriving under a changed pressure — doing the work of the lament in one place and of the pleasure in another. Let it take seriously the thought that what pleases is not the sorrow but being handed a sorrow entire, from the position of someone who already knows how it comes out; then let it find where that thought fails, since a real grief has no ending yet. It should not be readable as the easy answer, that we enjoy the beauty and not the sadness, and it should not settle which of the two of us has understood it better. Someone who knows the literature should recognise the problem rather than the names; someone who does not should simply find it a good poem.
