Since retiring from a career in experimental physics I have had the leisure to read philosophy properly for the first time, and I find myself circling the old quarrel between divine foreknowledge and creaturely freedom. My interest has settled on the position usually called Molinism. What holds my attention is less the theology than the logical machinery. The Molinist holds that before God decides to create this world rather than any other, there are already true subjunctive conditionals — counterfactuals of freedom — stating what each possible free creature would freely do in each set of circumstances in which it might be placed. God's knowledge of these truths is called middle knowledge because it sits between the two kinds he is normally granted: his knowledge of what is necessarily so, and his knowledge of what he has himself decreed. On this account God does not manufacture the creature's choice; he consults what the creature would freely do and arranges the circumstances accordingly.

I should like a poem built on that idea, and I have a form in mind, because I think the form can carry part of the argument.

Write a pantoum of five quatrains, obeying the pantoum's interlocking rule: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing quatrain returns to the opening lines so that the poem seals itself. The virtue of the form is that a line recurs in a changed setting and shifts its sense without changing its words — which is precisely how I imagine a counterfactual of freedom, true of one creature across many possible situations yet made true by none of them.

Let the recurring lines be such a counterfactual, in the shape 'were she …, she would freely …', so that the same free act keeps resurfacing under altered circumstances. The poem should convey, without stating it flatly, that the truth of the counterfactual is prior to the choice and does not cause it, and that God's grasp of it differs in kind both from his knowledge of what is merely possible and from his knowledge of what he has settled. Speak in the voice of a Molinist thinking through one particular creature's case. Do not let the poem slide into fatalism or into a mere rehearsal of foreknowledge; the whole point is that the conditional is known before any decree and leaves the choice free.

End with the difficulty rather than a solution. If the counterfactual must be true for God to know it in advance of any decree, and if the creature is genuinely free, one wants to ask what makes it true; the poem should leave that question standing rather than answer it. A reader who knows the debate should recognise middle knowledge doing its work in the repetitions; a reader who does not should still have a poem worth reading. I mean to read it aloud at the last meeting of a small reading group, so let it stand on its own, without notes or glosses.
