A colleague has been pressing an argument on me that, if sound, shows that no substantive theory of right action can be built on the idea of an ideal deliberator. I am not prepared to accept its conclusion, but I cannot yet say where it fails.

(1) The view under attack holds that an act is right if and only if an agent who is fully informed and fully rational would be moved to perform it.
(2) Full information and full rationality are limit notions. Any actual agent has finite beliefs and finite powers of reasoning; the ideal agent is approached through a sequence of agents, each of whom knows more, and reasons better, than the one before.
(3) So the verdict of the ideal agent is the limit of the verdicts of the members of that sequence, wherever such a limit exists.
(4) If that limit exists and coincides with the verdict of some member of the sequence, then the ideal agent's verdict is just the verdict of an ordinary agent with rather better information and rather better reasoning. The view then collapses into the truism that better information and better reasoning produce better verdicts, a claim no one denies and one that settles none of the cases we dispute.
(5) If the limit exists but is attained by no member of the sequence, then no process of inquiry can deliver the ideal agent's verdict, and the view cannot be applied: no one can say what the ideal agent would approve, so it issues no verdicts.
(6) If no limit exists, the view issues no verdicts either.
(7) Therefore, on every option, the view is either trivial or unusable, and the notion of an ideal deliberator has no content.

The argument strikes me as proving too much: by the same reasoning there would be no fact about what a perfectly careful reasoner would conclude from a set of premises, which I find hard to believe. But I would rather keep (1) than accept (7), and I do not want to keep it merely because I like it.

Tell me which step I should reject, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs. If you think the argument is valid and the price really does fall on (1), say so plainly and tell me what a defender of idealization can still hold. She also raises, as a separate matter, that perfecting an agent's information and perfecting its reasoning are two distinct operations that can be performed in either order, and that the orders need not agree; I cannot tell whether that is a second objection or the same one in other clothing.
