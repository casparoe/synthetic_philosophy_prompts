I sit on the board of a mid-size city's housing authority, and in three weeks we have to vote on siting a 60-bed shelter on the old rail yard. The neighborhood petition against it has about 4,000 signatures, and one of our members has talked us into running the whole thing through what she calls a deliberative-will process: pull 40 residents at random, give them a balanced briefing packet, let them hear from experts and from each other over two weekends, then ask them what they want and let that outrank the petition. Her pitch is that this shows what the city would want if it knew more, thought faster and were more the people it wishes it were -- she keeps calling it "coherent extrapolated volition" and says it comes from the AI safety world -- so it should count as the community's actual preference rather than its current mood. Two of my collegues think that's paternalism in a lab coat: whoever writes the packet is quietly deciding the outcome, and people who've been on the street for years and stopped expecting anything better will have "considered" preferences that just launder the status quo.

I need to circulate a short written opinion before the vote -- recommendation first, then the reasoning. Should we adopt the process? If yes, with what safeguards (what the packet can't contain, how the panel gets picked, what weight the result gets against the petition); if no, what we do instead. And give the strongest version of the paternalism objection a real hearing, not a courtesy paragraph.
