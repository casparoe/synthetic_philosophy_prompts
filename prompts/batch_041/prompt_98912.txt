Next month I'm giving a colloquium talk in the sociology department where I'm a postdoc, working title something like "Generations Aren't Kinds: Cohort Labels as Administrative Artifacts." The room is going to be mostly quantitative demographers who spend their whole lives on age-period-cohort models, plus two cultural sociologists who write about generational identity and are going to hear the entire thing as an attack on their life's work, and one visiting historian of the postwar US who mostly wants to talk about whether the 60s were really a thing. My line is roughly this: what we call generations -- Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z -- are not natural kinds but what I'm calling "administrative kinds," categories whose boundaries get drawn by whoever happens to need bins (census tables, ad agencies, HR departments) and then get reified into groups people believe they belong to. I lean hard on the APC identification problem (you can't separate age, period, and cohort effects without smuggling in extra assumptions, so the big "cohort effect" everyone reports is at least partly a modeling choice), and I borrow an interventionist criterion for kindhood -- a real kind should be something you could in principle manipulate and get systematic downstream changes from, and you obviously can't intervene on birth year without also intervening on age and period, so the label fails the test. I do want to concede up front that generational consciousness is a real social phenomenon; my claim is only that the phenomenon doesn't require generations to be real kinds, which is exactly where I expect the cultural folks to come at me.
