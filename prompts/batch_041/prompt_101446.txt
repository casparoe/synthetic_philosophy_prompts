I'm a doctoral student in public policy who has been sitting in on a philosophy-of-economics reading group, and a cluster of claims I picked up there has begun to bother me. As I now understand it, the revealed-preference approach is not merely a modeling convenience but an account of what utility actually is: an option's utility just consists in the fact that a person chose it, so a person's preferences are constituted by her choices and she cannot be mistaken about what she prefers. If that is right, several things seem to follow, and I cannot get them to sit together. First, weakness of will becomes impossible, since choosing something just is preferring it; the ordinary case of someone who wants to quit smoking, says so, and lights up again would be a contradiction rather than a phenomenon to be explained. Second, if utility is read directly off choice, then a person's welfare is simply whatever she picks, so there can be no such thing as choosing badly for oneself, and paternalism is conceptually confused rather than merely high-handed. Third, since this choice-based utility is supposed to be the genuine article, it looks as though we could compare it across persons and add it up — which is more or less what my friends in the group do when they rank policies. But I also notice that economists discuss addiction, self-control, and regret in a way that seems to presuppose exactly what I just ruled out, and a friend of mine reliably reverses her choice between two options when a third, clearly inferior option is added, which by my own lights should be impossible. So my question is this: how does the revealed-preference framework accommodate addiction and self-control without collapsing into inconsistency?

Please answer as a philosopher of economics and decision theorist who is scrupulous about the difference between a formal representation result and a substantive claim about the mind. I would like you to begin by thinking carefully about which of the assumptions in my framing are genuinely part of revealed-preference theory and which are not, and to state that diagnosis explicitly rather than taking my question at face value — if some of what I have said is a misunderstanding, I would much rather be corrected than accommodated, and I will not be offended if the honest answer is that my puzzle dissolves once the terms are disambiguated. After that, give me a positive account of what utility means in this tradition — what it is, what it is not, and what work it is and is not doing — and then use that account to address the three consequences I drew, especially the apparent tension with addiction, self-control, and the choice reversals I mentioned. Where you find that my question smuggles in a false presupposition, please name the presupposition and explain why it fails, so that I can see precisely where my reasoning went off the rails.
