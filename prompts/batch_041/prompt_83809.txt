I teach the final-year undergraduate module on causal inference for the health sciences, and I am now writing the summer exam paper. The topic I keep circling back to is the aggregate-versus-stratified reversal — the thing everyone calls Simpson's paradox — and two things this term have convinced me that my usual question on it is far too easy. The first was an argument with a colleague in the department about a small audit I had done for my partner's GP practice, in which the crude figures and the ward-by-ward figures pointed in opposite directions; I spent a fortnight telling anyone who would listen which set to trust, until she asked me, quite reasonably, what question I was actually trying to answer, and I realised I had never specified one. The second was her remark, made in passing and rather too cheerfully, that most of the reversals students bring to us are not paradoxes at all: some are ordinary confounding by a common cause, some are selection or collider bias from conditioning on something downstream of both variables, and some are simply an artefact of the effect measure — a crude odds ratio need not equal any weighted average of the stratum-specific odds ratios, and can fall below every one of them even when the strata are perfectly balanced and no confounding is present anywhere. My old question rewarded students for writing that the aggregate can mislead and the subgroups should therefore be trusted, which I now think is precisely the wrong lesson to send them away with.

So I should like you to draft a single essay question for that paper, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question should put a concrete, compact set of counts in front of the students — a full 2×2×2 table, or a summary from which the full table can be reconstructed — and those counts must genuinely reverse on aggregation: the model answer has to show the arithmetic, giving the crude table and the stratum-specific odds ratios and risk differences, and demonstrating that the reversal is real rather than merely asserted. The crux I want examined is not that the aggregate can mislead but which of three quite different things is doing the work in this particular case — confounding by a common cause, selection or collider bias from conditioning on a variable downstream of both, or the non-collapsibility of the measure itself — and how the defensible answer changes once the causal question is made precise. Ideally a student could only answer well by saying which of the three the case exhibits, what the estimand is, and what they would report if the estimand were a different one. It is a forty-five minute question worth twenty marks.

Please give me three things. First, the question exactly as it would be printed on the paper. Second, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words. Third, a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, with the marks allocated to each and totalling twenty, and against each point a one-line note on how candidates characteristically miss it. I want the scheme to separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each band must contain: in particular, an A-band answer should have to state what would have to be true of the data for the naive instruction to trust the subgroups to be correct, and to say when and why it would be wrong. Keep the whole thing self-contained — no reading list, no references, no names of papers or authors, since the students get a formula sheet and I would rather marks went to reasoning than to recall. Please write everything, including the exam question itself, in American English, as I shall be co-marking with a colleague in the States.
