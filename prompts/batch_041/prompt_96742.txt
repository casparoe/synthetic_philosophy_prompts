I'm giving the commencement address at the small Episcopal college I went to, and I want you to write it. I'm a Rawls scholar at a state university now, I stopped being religious somewhere in grad school, and I'm not going to fake it in a chapel full of devout parents — but the president asked me for ten minutes on "faith and public life," and most of this class is heading into public school teaching and public health nursing, so it's a real question for them and not just a theme. What I want the speech to hinge on is this: in Rawls, public reason isnt the same as secular reason — the restraint falls on everybody's comprehensive doctrine, the church's and the utilitarians' alike — and his proviso lets you bring your deepest convictions into public argument as long as you can eventually cash them out in reasons your fellow citizens could accept. Then the turn: a religious college is the background culture, not the public square, so I'm not telling these kids to leave their faith at the door of the classroom they're about to teach in; I'm telling them what changes when they walk into the school board meeting. Rawls himself nearly went to seminary and then lost his faith in the Pacific, and I want that in there, because the devout half of the room needs to hear that the person saying this isn't their enemy, and the secular faculty in the third row need to hear that I haven't gone soft. So: no sermon, no lecture, at most one "Rawls says." Under ten minutes, and it should end with something they can use in a first-year classroom on Monday.
