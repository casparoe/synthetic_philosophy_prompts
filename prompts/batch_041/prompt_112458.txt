Take the role of a philosopher of mind who works on animal ethics and the metaphysics of consciousness. Think carefully before answering — brainstorm the candidate diagnoses first, then commit to the one you can actually defend.

I'm a retired insect neuroanatomist (thirty years on locust brains) who's drifted into philosophy late, and my reading group has been stuck for months on whether sentience comes in degrees. One camp treats it as a threshold: a creature either has a point of view on its own life or it doesn't, and the real work is finding where the line falls — octopuses and fish in, insects maybe, thermostats out. The other camp says that's a category error: 'sentience' isn't a natural kind but a coarse label for a bundle of capacities — nociception, integration, valence, a self-model, learning — that come apart from each other, so the honest picture is a smear of gradients. A third person insists both sides are answering one question with two meanings of the word.

My hypothesis, tell me if its flattering nonsense: this isn't a factual dispute that better neurobiology would settle, and it isn't purely verbal either. I suspect the two sides agree on most of the facts and disagree about what a moral status is — a natural kind tracking a biological property, or a decision-relevant category we're entitled to draw wherever it does useful work. A friend in the group suggested we just score each creature by how much suffering it could undergo over a lifetime and let status rise with the score, which strikes me as elegant and probably circular.

So diagnose it. Substantive or verbal, or different questions under one name? If some facts are load-bearing, which ones? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a criterion, a translation between the camps — or is the question ill-posed enough that none should be expected? Don't survey the literature at me; I want the structure of the disagreement and an honest verdict on the diagnosis.
