I teach the collective choice unit in a graduate seminar in political theory, and I am marking the problem set for that unit. Below is the question and one student's answer.

Question: The discursive dilemma is usually introduced with a court deciding whether a defendant is liable, given two premises (breach and causation) and a rule linking them to liability. Set out the dilemma for a case of your own choosing, using a different example. Explain the two standard procedures for avoiding the inconsistency it generates, and say what each gives up. Finally, is the discursive dilemma simply Condorcet's paradox under another name? In answering, say precisely what the two have in common and where they differ.

Student's answer: Take a three-member funding panel deciding whether to fund a proposal, where the panel's stated rule is that a proposal should be funded (F) if and only if it is methodologically sound (S) and likely to have significant impact (I). Reviewer 1 judges S and I true, and therefore F. Reviewer 2 judges S true and I false, and therefore not-F. Reviewer 3 judges S false and I true, and therefore not-F. Each reviewer's judgments are internally consistent. Now suppose the panel resolves each proposition separately by majority vote. A majority (reviewers 1 and 2) accepts S; a majority (reviewers 1 and 3) accepts I; and a majority (reviewers 2 and 3) rejects F. The collective judgment set is {S, I, not-F}, which is inconsistent with the panel's own rule. This is the discursive dilemma. It is worth noting that every individual is consistent, and that the majority verdict on each of the three propositions is perfectly determinate.

Two procedures are usually proposed. Under the premise-based procedure, the panel votes on S and I and then applies its rule, so the proposal is funded. Under the conclusion-based procedure, the panel votes only on F, so the proposal is not funded, and the premises are left without any collective verdict. Each procedure buys consistency at a cost. The conclusion-based procedure yields a verdict that a majority endorses, but the group's verdict is unsupported by the group's own judgments on the premises that are supposed to justify it. The premise-based procedure yields a consistent set of judgments, and it stays faithful to the majority's view of each premise; since the conclusion follows from those premises, the group's verdict on the conclusion is the majority's verdict on the conclusion as well.

As to the last part: I take the discursive dilemma to be a version of Condorcet's paradox. What produces the inconsistency is that the group's judgments cycle — there is no consistent collective ordering of the propositions, just as there is no consistent collective ranking of the candidates under majority rule. The difference is only that the dilemma arises for propositions rather than candidates, which is why it shows up in courts and committees rather than in elections.
