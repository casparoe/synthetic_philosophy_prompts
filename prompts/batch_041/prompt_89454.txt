I teach a graduate seminar on perception and cognition, and this term I have replaced the final essay with a short story, a decision I can defend at length: the puzzles in this area concern what experience is like from the inside, and fiction can stage that in a way that a taxonomy of positions cannot. I would like you to write one of these stories for me. We have spent the term on the dispute between those who hold, in the modularist tradition descending from Fodor and Pylyshyn, that perceptual processing is informationally encapsulated and therefore cannot be altered by what the perceiver believes or wants, and those who hold that perception is cognitively penetrable, that is, that a belief, desire, or mood can change not merely what one attends to or how one later describes what one saw but the content of the experience itself. Two distinctions have caused the most trouble in seminar, and I would like the story to live inside them. The first is between what we have been calling the same-content constraint and its denial: does a case count as penetration only if the top-down influence alters the content of experience, or is it sufficient that it biases attention or the subsequent judgement, in which case most of the alleged cases collapse into mere cognitive influence? The second is between synchronic penetration, where a belief held at the moment of looking changes what one sees then and there, and diachronic penetration, where years of training or expertise gradually reshape perceptual processing, so that the expert literally sees what the novice cannot, a case that is far less contested than the first and is often quietly conflated with it. Your story should not explain these distinctions; it should make a reader feel the cost of choosing between them.

Here is the seed I would like you to develop, though you may change the profession or the setting if you can do better. A woman in her fifties has spent two decades identifying wild mushrooms for a regional food-safety authority, work in which her judgement determines whether a harvest is sold or destroyed. Some months after a poisoning case she was peripherally involved in, she begins to find that a patch she has walked for years presents itself differently: where she used to see the edible species she now sees, immediately and without deliberation, the deadly one growing beside it. She is called to account for a call that went wrong, and the question on the table is whether her perception changed or only her judgement did, a question that matters because her employer will keep her only if her seeing is reliable and a tribunal will excuse her only if it is not. Give me at least one scene in which two characters disagree, in good faith and with real resources, about what she actually saw, and resist resolving the matter by fiat; let the ambiguity be felt rather than dissolved. Aim for roughly twelve hundred to eighteen hundred words, keep the philosophy in the plot rather than in the dialogue of visiting philosophers, and avoid exposition of the debate as such.

Please append a short author's note, of two or three hundred words, addressed to me rather than to a general reader. It should say where the story takes liberties with what is actually established about expert perceptual learning and about the alleged cases of penetration, which philosophical claims the story presupposes are contested and how a defender of encapsulation would push back on them, and how confident you are in those claims, singling out the one point you are least sure of.
