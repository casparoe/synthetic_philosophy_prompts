I'm a junior at a large public high school, and I host the podcast for our philosophy club — forty or fifty listeners, mostly classmates and two teachers who are kind enough to pretend that listening counts as their homework. Next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent her whole career defending the idea that a person's origin is essential to them: that the particular sperm and egg that produced you are part of what it is to be you, and that you could not have come from any other pair. I have heard the standard interview version of this more than once, and it always runs the same way — the host asks whether she could have been her brother, she says no and explains why that is less strange than it sounds, and everyone moves on. I would like to do better than that, because the interesting part of the view only shows up once you stop treating one sperm and one egg as a tidy given.

So I would like one question on each of five things, all of them aimed at what a summary would skip. First, where the line falls and why: if the gametes are essential, why aren't the uterine environment, the timing of conception, or the full genome equally essential, and what principled reason keeps the list as short as she wants it to be? Second, twinning: when one zygote splits and two people result, both trace back to the same sperm and egg, so does her view make them the same person — and if she says no, what distinguishes them? Third, a case my biology teacher raised that I cannot get out of my head: sometimes two embryos fuse into one and a child is born with cells from both, which seems to leave that child with either two origins or an origin that is not the gametes at all; I want to know which she says, and whether the child would have existed had the fusion never happened. Fourth, the circularity worry: in order to say that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg, she has to locate me in some other possible world, and I cannot see how she does that without already assuming what makes me me. Fifth, the intuition she is arguing against, taken in its two halves — that if my parents had never met, then either nobody at all would have been born in my place, or somebody else would have been and I might have been that person — and how she tells the halves apart. For each of these, I would like the follow-up she is most likely to steer me toward, since she is a very smooth interviewee and I know I will lose my nerve. Nothing about how she first got interested in philosophy, please.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing paragraph; where a question has an obvious escape hatch, make the second sentence the follow-up that closes it off.
