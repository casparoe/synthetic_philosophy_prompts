hey, weird favor. I'm a third-year, and this term I'm in a seminar on probability and induction. Last week we spent two sessions on the reference class problem and it has honestly wrecked me, which is a problem, because I'm the best man at my best friend's wedding in three weeks and I have to give a toast.

Setup: the groom's been my best friend since first year, we're both stats people, and the bride is an actuary — her actual job is sorting people into risk classes — so two of her officemates are coming, plus her parents, who are retired math teachers. So there'll be maybe five or six people in the room who can follow an argument, and about eighty who are his family from a small town, including his grandma, who I love and who would not enjoy a lecture.

Here's what's killing me. Every wedding toast is a prediction. You two are going to last. I've never met two people better suited. The odds are good. And I can't say any of it anymore, because each of those lines needs a reference class and I can't pick one honestly. All marriages? Marriages of couples who met the way they met? Couples who married at 29 in this state this decade? Couples where one of them spends her workday building mortality tables? The number comes out completely different each time, and the one that feels good at the podium is the one I could least defend to the bride's coworkers.

There's a worse layer too. Our TA said in section that the real problem isn't choosing a class, it's deciding which facts about a case are relevant in the first place — and if you say all of them, then every case is its own class and the whole thing dissolves, which is either the fix or the death of the idea and I definately can't tell which. I tried the move where I just tell them they're a class of one, a case like no other, and it sounded like a dodge even to me.

So: write the toast. Five minutes, spoken, warm, funny in places, real wedding-toast stuff — how we met, one mildly embarrassing story about him, something true about her. Just no claim about their future that rests on a reference class I can't stand behind. And I don't want the fix to be me winking at the actuaries either; his grandma should hear a sincere toast. What I actually want to know is what a toast is for if it can't be a prediction, and I want the speech to be the answer instead of explaining it. Also no quoting philosophers at me — if anyone asks me at the reception who I was talking about, I'm sunk.
