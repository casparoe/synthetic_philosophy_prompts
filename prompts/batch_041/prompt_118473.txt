I'm a science journalist and I'm working on a long feature about mind uploading — the version where nothing gets copied, some very fine-grained instrument just swaps your neurons out one at a time for components that do the same job, so you never notice and can keep talking the whole way through. My editor, who has opinions about this sort of thing, wants the piece to open with a poem rather than the usual anecdote about a startup. So here's what I need: a poem in the voice of the person on the table, six short stanzas of four lines, where the last line of every stanza is the same line — except that with each stanza, exactly one word of that line has been swapped out for a different word. Same part of speech, same syllable count, same rhyme, so it still sits in the ear exactly the way it did before. Pick a refrain with enough words in it to survive five hits. By the sixth stanza I want that line to be made of completely different words and still be unmistakably the same line doing the same job. Don't have the poem point at what it's doing; if it works, the reader should get to the end feeling like nothing was ever broken, then go back and find that the line they'd been hearing is gone and that they can't say which stanza killed it. (One thing I'd love, though it's your call — let one of the words that gets swapped be the one the speaker uses for himself.)

It also has to be honest about the actual philosophy, not just spooky about it. The interesting thing about the case isn't that replacement is creepy, its that at no single step is there any difference you could point to — behavior, memory, the whole causal story just keeps going — and yet when it's over none of the original stuff is left, and if you'd done the whole thing at once we'd all say the person died. So don't cheat by giving me a stanza where the speaker suddenly feels strange, and don't resolve it into either a still-me-obviously ending or a somewhere-back-there-I-died ending. It should sit in the fact that both descriptions of whats going on are true at every moment and can't both be true of the whole thing. Keep it under thirty lines, it has to run in a magazine.
