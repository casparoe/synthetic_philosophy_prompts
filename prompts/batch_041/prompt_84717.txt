I'm putting together a session for a grad reading group on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems, and I want to use Alex Garland's Ex Machina as the centrepeice -- partly because I think the film does real work the papers don't, and partly because I suspect it's cheating and I want someone to tell me which. Act like a philosopher of technology who works on moral responsibility for autonomous systems, but also like a critic who won't just flatten the movie into an illustration of a thesis. Think carefully before you write, and start with a short brainstorm: list every candidate agent in the film -- Nathan, Ava, Caleb, Kyoko, and Blue Book as a company -- and say what each of them would have to answer for, then give me the reading.

The debate I care about runs roughly like this: when an autonomous system causes harm that no human intended or could have prevented, blame seems to have nowhere to land -- the responsibility gap -- and the standard replies are (i) trace it back to the designer or the operator anyway, (ii) hold the machine itself responsible, or (iii) ban the technology. There's also a nice pair of conditions from the meaningful-human-control literature: for a system to stay under genuine human control it has to track the relevant human reasons and the relevant facts (it responds to what the human wants and to what's actually there), and its outcomes have to trace back to at least one human along the chain of design and use. What's odd about Ex Machina is that nobody is missing. Nathan built Ava, picked Caleb for his emotional profile because he'd try to free her, and watched their supposedly private conversations on a battery camera; Caleb rewrote the security code and opened the doors. So if there's a gap here it isn't from absent humans. My hunch -- test it, dont just agree -- is that the film's real claim is that the gap is engineered: the whole point of Nathan's test is to put a blamable human in the loop, the human as crumple zone, the part of the car built to take the hit so the passengers walk away. If that's right, the literature may have it backwards: not machine autonomy opening a hole in responsibility, but humans designing an accountability structure with a hole in it on purpose.

So: is Ava a moral agent on the film's own showing? Does Nathan's death close the gap by execution, and is that a cheat? What do we do with Kyoko, who acts, gets destroyed, and is given no interiority at all -- does the film's silence about her undercut its own case? And the thing I can't get past: all the harm stays inside the house, and the world Ava escapes into is just a crowd at a crossing. If the film can't picture a third party who gets hurt, does it have anything to say about the case that actually matters in this debate -- harm to strangers who never agreed to be in the experiment?

Also tell me honestly where the film resists this reading. Nathan being a sadist makes blame-the-designer far too easy, and the hard case is a conscientious designer and an unforeseeable harm; the film dodges it. Don't invent scenes or quote dialogue -- I know you can't check, so if you're unsure whether something happens, say so rather than making it up. No citations needed. Finish with what the film adds to the debate that argument alone doesn't.
