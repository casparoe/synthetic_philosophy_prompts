I run a small discussion group that meets in the back room of the pub every other Thursday — mixed bunch, a couple of retired teachers, a nurse, a lad who does logistics for a haulage firm, and me, no degree but a lot of time on my hands. This term we've been chewing on the whole buisness of people making threats they've deliberately made it impossible to back out of: cutting off your own escape route so the other side believes you'll really do it. Good term, and since I'm always the one who ends up running these things, I said I'd write the last session's exercise myself and do it properly for once, with an actual answer key, instead of just lobbing a vague question at them and seeing what falls out.

The case I want them to work on I've made up myself, so nobody can go and google it. Two neighbours, call them A and B, are deadlocked over a shared driveway and either of them can escalate to court. Before that happens, either of them can do a thing I've decided to call posting a bond: put a sum of money with a third party on the instruction that the money is forfeited to nobody at all — it just burns — if that person later concedes anything at all. The deposit is public, so the whole street knows who has posted and how much. Once you've posted you are genuinely stuck, because conceding now means losing the fight and the money. So each of them wants to post before the other one does. And once one has posted, the other can post a bigger one, or post one against ever withdrawing their own bond, and it just keeps climbing. Both of them would obviously be better off if neither posted anything, and both of them can see that perfectly well.

So what I need from you is the essay question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme I can actually mark with. Level is a bit above beginner — a few of them did some philosophy at uni years ago, most didn't, so the question has to be answerable from the case and from thinking, not from having read the right books, and there's no reading list to point them at. Give them 45 minutes and mark it out of 20. What I really want the marking scheme to do is tell apart the answer that genuinely understands what the bond is doing from the one that just says "this is the prisoners dilemma, they should cooperate." Two things nagging at me that I'd like the model answer to deal with: I have a feeling the obvious fix — everyone agrees in advance not to post bonds — isn't really available, and I can't put my finger on why; and then the excuse angle, because after A has posted, A is going to say "I couldn't back down, the money was gone either way" — and I honestly can't tell whether that's true or whether it's just the trick working exactly as designed.
