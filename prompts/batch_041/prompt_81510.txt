I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, sitting in on a seminar on addiction and drug policy, and for my response paper this week I want to do something other than the usual "is paternalism justified" routine. What keeps tripping me up is that nobody actually defends one policy; they defend a patchwork, and the patchwork seems to presuppose things nobody will state out loud. Three real-world stances, roughly: prohibition proper, where everything from the poppy field to the guy smoking it is criminal; what Portugal did in the early 2000s, where personal possession of any drug, heroin included, stops being a crime and becomes an administrative thing that routes you to a small panel that can fine you or nudge you into treatment, while production and sale stay squarely criminal; and cannabis legalization, where the state doesn't merely tolerate a market but licenses it, taxes it, sets an age limit, and restricts the advertising — and where, in the same statute book, mushrooms and LSD remain felonies, alcohol stays legal but is squeezed harder every year, and tobacco is being strangled slowly (taxes up, ads banned, plain packaging on the way).

So here's what I want. Treat that whole patchwork as one practice and tell me what it presupposes. Does decriminalizing the user while keeping the seller a criminal presuppose that whatever is wrong with drugs is located entirely in the market — that the user's act isn't even pro tanto wrong, just unwise, whereas the seller is a predator? Or is it just a tractability judgment about who's worth punishing? And what theory of the person does the dissuasion panel presuppose: if you're routed to treatment, that reads as a compulsion view where the user isn't a fit target of blame; but the panel can also fine you, which reads as a choice view where a soft sanction aimed at a chooser is exactly right. Both fit the same institution. Meanwhile, when the state licenses and taxes a drug, does that presuppose it never really believed the drug was intrinsically degrading — that the harm was always about the black market and the unregulated product — or is there room for a view where the drug is bad for you and the state is just refusing to enforce that judgment coercively? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the person, of the state's authority over what we put in our bodies, and of what law is for, and be clear about which parts are strict presuppositions versus which merely sit comfortably with several theories. Then tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, and pick one piece of the patchwork and say what would have to go if we swapped the presupposed view for a rival — a thoroughgoing harm-to-others liberalism, say, or a perfectionist view that the state may steer us toward worthwhile lives. Also, I'm writing this for a seminar where people will jump on any overreach, so say how confident you are in each claim and single out the point you're least sure of. American English, please — I'm at a US department and my advisor is picky about it.
