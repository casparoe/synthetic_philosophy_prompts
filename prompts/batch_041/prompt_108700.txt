I am assembling the first handout for a graduate seminar on confirmation and theory change, and I want to include a short passage that my students are likely to have met before — an introduction to the philosophy of science that is widely assigned and, on this topic, rather more confident than it should be. Before I build a session around it, I would like to know exactly how much of it I should be correcting rather than presupposing. The passage runs roughly as follows:

'Bayesian confirmation theory tells us that a piece of evidence supports a hypothesis exactly when learning that evidence would raise your probability for the hypothesis. The problem of old evidence is the discovery that this account makes it impossible for a theory to be supported by evidence we already have. When a new theory is proposed to explain something long known — general relativity and the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, which had been known for more than half a century — the evidence is already certain, its probability is 1, and dividing by 1 leaves the theory's probability exactly where it was. The Bayesian must therefore say that the most impressive confirmation in the history of physics was not a confirmation at all. Bayesians have responded in two ways. The standard repair is to run the calculation with the probability the scientist assigned to the evidence before the theory arrived, rather than the probability of 1 assigned now; since that earlier probability was less than 1, the evidence can do its work, and the confirmation comes out as strong as it should. The other response is deflationary: the difficulty is an artifact of treating credences as if they were certainties, since the puzzle only bites for an agent who assigns probability exactly 1 to the old evidence, and real scientists never do. The problem of old evidence, on this reading, is a philosopher's fiction.'

What I want from you is an audit rather than a verdict. Take the passage claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether someone working on Bayesian confirmation would accept it as it stands, accept it only with a qualification, or regard it as a misrepresentation — and in the latter two cases, what the qualification or the correct statement would be, and why the difference is more than a matter of emphasis. I am especially interested in any point at which the passage quietly identifies two things that the theory, or its critics, take some care to keep apart, and in whether the two responses it describes are described accurately — in particular, whether the first is as decisive as the passage implies, and what a defender of it would say the passage has left out. The passage closes by announcing that the puzzle is a fiction; tell me whether that conclusion is licensed by the claims that survive your corrections, or whether it can only be reached by helping oneself to something the passage has not established. You needn't supply references — I know the literature, and what I want from you is the substance rather than a reading list. My own inclination is that the problem is genuine and that the deflationary ending is too quick, but I would rather be shown than confirmed, so if the ending can be earned, earn it.
