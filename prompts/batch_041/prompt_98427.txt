I'm putting together a handout for the first meeting of a grad seminar I'm running on the epistemology of skill, and I want the handout to be a decision tree rather than a reading list — partly because half the students will have read the usual stuff and half won't, and partly because I'd rather they work out which view they actually hold before they find out which famous person held it. So: give me a flowchart that starts from a question any competent student can answer without already knowing the labels, and ends at a leaf for each position in the know-how vs. know-that debate. The branch nodes should sit on the real joints — whether knowing how to do something requires being able to do it (and if so, whether being able is a matter of reliably succeeding or just of having the right internal state), whether know-how is a species of factual knowledge about a way of acting or something non-factual, whether it's one broad disposition or a bundle of narrower ones, and whether it comes in degrees or is all-or-nothing. Phrase every node as a plain question about a case, never as "are you an intellectualist?" — someone should be able to answer it while having no idea where it leads.

Two things the usual textbook treatments fudge, and I care about both. First, the threshold cases: someone who knows how to do something but has never actually done it (I can't swim and I'm fairly sure I still sort of know how), a master cook who genuinely cannot put the recipe into words, a person who can only do the thing by consciously reciting steps to themselves, someone who used to be able to do it and now can't but still seems to know how in some sense, and one case where the person can do it perfectly but can't tell which of two different methods they're actually using. Second, build the tree from the logical space first — lay out the questions, work out which combinations of answers are even coherent, then slot the actual positions in and flag the combinations nobody occupies, with a sentence on whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two leaves turn out to give the same answer on every node, say so rather than pretending they're distinct views. Keep it to around six or seven leaves, and please skip the bibliography — describe each position by what it's committed to, since I'll supply the names myself.
