I've been going round in circles about this with a couple of friends, and I would like a straight answer rather than a survey of considerations.

The claim I want tested is that Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016) is the one broadly popular film that seriously attempts to dramatise acausal trade. By that I mean the idea as it circulates in blog form — Hofstadter's superrationality, and the updateless and functional decision theory family descended from it: the claim that two agents who cannot communicate, cannot affect one another, and have no reputation, retaliation or third-party enforcement to rely on can nevertheless each do better by predicting what the other will do and acting as though a bargain had been struck. The strict version of the idea gives the agents no information about each other at all; they are supposed to reason about what a counterparty that reasons as they do would choose. The trade only works if both are good enough at predicting the other, and close enough in power for the exchange to be worth making. The literature also permits the asymmetric case in which one party lies in the other's future.

Why Arrival looks like a candidate. The heptapods hand over their language, which rewires the human experience of time, and they expect humanity's help three thousand years later. That is an exchange across an interval no one can police, between parties one of whom — humanity three thousand years hence — cannot yet be consulted. The film's form also fits: Louise's life is given to us all at once rather than in sequence, and her decision to have a daughter she knows will die at twelve is the kind of whole-life choice that the updateless stance is supposed to license, a policy adopted rather than an act optimised. And the resolution is a coordination story rather than a victory: the crisis ends when the nations stop treating communication as a zero-sum contest, and when Louise draws on what she remembers of the future to talk General Shang down by reciting his wife's dying words.

But I suspect it cheats, and I want to be told whether I am right. In the film nobody is cut off from anybody. The heptapods are physically present; they write, they intervene — one of them ejects Louise and Ian from the craft before the bomb detonates, and Abbott dies of his injuries. Above all, the future is not inferred but seen. Louise does not have to work out what Shang will do; she remembers having already persuaded him. Precognition is an information channel, and once a channel is available the problem that gives acausal trade its point — how to cooperate when there is no channel — never arises. On that reading the film does not solve the puzzle; it quietly deletes it.

Two further things I would like weighed. The first is the adaptation. In Ted Chiang's novella the heptapods never explain why they came or why they left, and so far as I can see no bargain is struck at all: what the story dramatises is teleology — a light ray that must know where it will end up before it sets out — and the acceptance of an unavoidable future, not an exchange. The reciprocal need looks like an invention of the screenplay, and I want to know whether adding it makes the film more philosophical or less, or whether it buys a science-fictional motive at the cost of the philosophy. The second is the reverse of my suspicion: that the gift of the language is itself the gift of the one thing acausal trade requires, namely a shared, non-sequential mode of reasoning, so that the heptapods are less trading with humanity than making humanity into the sort of agent that can be traded with. If that is right, the film's real subject is the precondition of acausal trade rather than the trade.

I am not asking for citations or a reading list, and I would rather have a wrong verdict than a balanced menu. Please write it as follows:

1. "The bar to clear" (~200 words): what a film would have to do to dramatise acausal trade honestly, given the conditions set out above — no communication, no enforcement, no mutual information, mutual predictability, rough parity of power.
2. "The case for the film" (~400 words): the strongest reading on which Arrival earns the idea, with the scenes that support it.
3. "The case against" (~400 words): the strongest reading on which it substitutes precognition for decision theory and thereby dissolves the problem it appears to pose.
4. "Verdict" (~250 words): which reading you take, why, what the film adds to the debate — if anything — that the decision theory does not, and the one scene I should watch again with your reading in mind.

Please use British English throughout.
