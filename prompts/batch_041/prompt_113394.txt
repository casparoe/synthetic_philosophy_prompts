I am assembling an anthology of short fiction on the theme of collective memory, and I would like you to write one of the pieces: roughly two thousand words, literary rather than speculative, set wherever you can make a place convincing.

The premise I want you to build on is a practice, not a theory. In one community — an island, a valley town, a company settlement, whatever the story needs — two accounts of a single founding event have been kept side by side for about a century. One is filed. It is written, dated, signed and submitted to whoever held authority to receive it: a parish register, a company office, a colonial survey, a coroner, a court. The other is recited. It is never written down, it is performed aloud on a fixed occasion each year, and it has exactly one rule, which children learn before they learn its contents: a name may be added to it, and no name may ever be taken out. The rule has been honoured for a hundred years, which means the recited account can only grow while the filed account can only be amended by paperwork — one ratchets up, the other ratchets down, and neither has any ordinary way of shedding its own error.

What interests me is not which account is true. It is the asymmetry of the two failures. The file is exact about what it was designed to record and silent about everything else, and its silences run along the grain of who was in a position to write. The recitation is inaccurate in one direction only, and its inaccuracies are all additions — the dead, the unnamed, the ones who did the work and were never on a payroll. The file cannot lose a name; the recitation cannot lose an error.

The story should turn on an outside demand for a single account: a heritage plaque, a compensation scheme, a land claim, a textbook, an inquiry — some body that requires the community to submit one authoritative version and will not accept two. I want the pressure to come from that demand rather than from a quarrel inside the community. And I want three things held apart throughout: what is false, what is unverifiable, and what is true but unrecorded.

Please keep the two custodians — the one who keeps the file and the one who keeps the recitation — intelligent, unsentimental and partly right, and let each of them see something the other cannot. Do not give me a revelation scene in which a single document settles the matter; do not let the story end in the vindication of either the record or the memory; and do not let it end in the comfortable compromise that both accounts are true in their own fashion. I would rather it end with something irreplaceable actually lost, and with the loss specific enough that the reader can name it. Your own verdict on the community's practice should be legible from what the characters give up, not from a speech any of them makes.

I am not asking for a story that concludes that documents are truth and memory is sentiment, nor one that concludes that documents are power and memory is truth. I want the reader to close it able to say what each account is for, and to see why a community might keep both — and why keeping both might, in the end, be something it cannot afford.
