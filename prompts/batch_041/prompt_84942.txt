I'm a backend engineer at a company that runs a high-volume payments platform, and last month we held a postmortem after a schema migration corrupted a small subset of transaction records. The discussion turned into an argument between two of my colleagues, and I haven't been able to settle it in my own head. Priya, our SRE lead, said flatly that Ravi, the engineer who ran the migration, did not know the backfill was safe, whatever he may have believed — that with a table that size and no dry run against a copy, he had a strong hunch, not knowledge. Marcus, another backend engineer, pushed back: Ravi had run a pre-flight check that came back clean and had said beforehand that he'd verified the backfill was safe; if that same check had been run against a scratch database nobody cared about, everyone would agree Ravi knew, so the only thing that differed between the two cases was how much was riding on the outcome, and it's absurd to say that the size of the blast radius changes what was going on inside Ravi's head. Priya answered that she wasn't making any claim about his head: "knows" is a word whose bar moves with what's at stake, and there's nothing absurd about that. Marcus said that conceded his point — if the bar moves with the stakes, then the same evidence amounts to knowledge in one situation and not in another, so two people can look at the same facts and contradict each other with neither of them confused, and he doesn't believe knowledge is like that. Priya said that's exactly what she means, and it's why she wanted the postmortem indexed to stakes: we should only write "knew" in a writeup when the person's evidence would have justified acting given the damage that was actually possible. Marcus said that policy smuggles consequences into an evidential notion and amounts to convicting people of not knowing things because the outcome was bad — by that rule nobody ever knew anything before a disaster, which he thinks is just blaming people for bad luck. Priya replied that the stakes were knowable in advance, that a ten-dollar version and a ten-million-dollar version of the same migration are not the same epistemic situation, and that she is happy to say Ravi knew in the first case and didn't in the second. Marcus said fine — he'd say Ravi knew in both cases and was reckless in the second, and that "reckless" is the more useful word anyway, because it keeps what someone knew separate from what they should have done about it.

A colleague of mine who minored in philosophy overheard me retelling this and said that Priya and Marcus are each defending positions that have names in the literature, and that in her view they are also quietly answering two or three different questions at once, though she wouldn't stay to explain. I'm not a philosophy person, and I won't tell you which of them I was leaning toward — I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. I'd like you to referee the exchange as it actually happened rather than telling me which camp to join. Go move by move: for each of the roughly eight moves above, say whether it's clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then tell me what each of them is right about that the other never really addresses, and what both of them are missing — including whether the disagreement is really about the word "knows," about knowledge itself, or about when it's appropriate to say or act on a knowledge claim, and whether either of them has the resources to keep the other's insight rather than just denying it. If the two positions have standard labels, name them, but the substantive adjudication matters much more to me than the terminology. Please write your answer in American English.
