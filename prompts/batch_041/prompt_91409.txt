I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend — a former buy-side analyst who now works on policy — about whether markets deserve to be treated as knowledge-producing institutions, and we ended in a stalemate that I suspect is my fault rather than his. Before I go back to him, I want an audit of my own position, because I genuinely cannot tell whether it is a coherent package or just a pile of attitudes I accumulated from different conversations at different times.

Here is what I actually believe.

1. The agreement result. If two agents share a prior and their posterior credences are common knowledge between them, those posteriors must coincide. I accept this, and I accept that a certain kind of persistent, fully transparent disagreement is therefore a symptom — someone is incoherent, or the disagreement is not really common knowledge, or the priors were never shared.

2. There is no privileged prior. Real agents arrive with different priors for historical and psychological reasons rather than evidential ones, and there is in general no fact of the matter about which of two divergent priors was the one a person ought to have had. So durable disagreement among informed and careful people is the normal condition, not a pathology in need of explanation.

3. Markets are epistemically impressive. A functioning price is the best available summary of dispersed private information about the question it concerns, and in most cases a stranger's willingness to trade at that price should move my credence further than my own private reasoning does. When a liquid market says thirty percent, I should be somewhere near thirty percent.

4. It is nonetheless possible to make money by knowing more than the market, and this is not a marginal curiosity — it is the mechanism. Somebody has to be paid for the work of finding out what the price does not yet reflect, or the information never gets found and the price never improves.

5. The no-trade results are usually overread. When someone tells me that rational agents with common knowledge of one another's rationality cannot trade on private information, I read that as a description of an equilibrium no actual market is ever in, not as an argument that speculation is irrational.

6. I am nevertheless a passive investor, and I think almost everyone should be. I do not believe that I personally have an edge, and I think the great majority of active trading is a transfer between people who are each confident that they have one.

What I want from you is not a lecture on market efficiency but an audit of this set. Which of these are in genuine tension with one another, and which pairs only appear to conflict and can be reconciled once the conditions under which each claim holds are made explicit? For each tension you regard as genuine, say how severe it is, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere. For the pairs that dissolve, say precisely which premise was doing the misleading work. Then tell me which single commitment is bearing the most load — the one whose removal would force the largest number of other changes — and whether that is the commitment I should be least confident in.
