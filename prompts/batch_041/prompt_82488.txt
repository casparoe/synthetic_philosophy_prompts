In my philosophy of perception course we have just finished the argument from illusion and moved on to disjunctivism, and I cannot locate the substantive disagreement. The argument runs from cases in which a veridical perception and a hallucination are subjectively indistinguishable to the conclusion that the two share a common experiential core; the disjunctivist denies that the premise licenses the conclusion. Please explain the conceptual issue. What exactly is being denied, and what must be true of the two experiences if indiscriminability is not to entail a shared nature? I would like the two strands kept apart: disjunctivism as a thesis about the nature of the experience itself, and disjunctivism as a thesis about the perceiver's epistemic standing — that in the good case she possesses a reason for belief of a kind she lacks in the bad case. Which of the two does the argument from illusion actually threaten, and why might someone who wants the epistemic thesis have no use for the metaphysical one? Finally, say what the disjunctivist must hold about indiscriminability itself: is it a brute fact about the pair of cases, or something the view owes an explanation of, and how does that choice decide whether disjunctivism is a substantive claim about experience or a redescription of agreed facts?
