I'm a backend engineer and I somehow ended up on the steering committee of a smallish open-source project (a testing library, ~40 contributors). We have to pick between two competing designs for a breaking change in the next major version, and I'm the one writing the memo the committee votes on Thursday.

Design A keeps the current mental model, is less code for us to maintain, but every user has to touch their test files. Design B is a cleaner API, except it leans on a language feature that a chunk of our users' runtimes don't have yet, so we'd ship a compatibility shim forever. We scored them on a rubric we invented ourselves and got 34-34, but honestly the numbers are junk — they're good in different ways and I cant say A is better, or that they're the same amount of good.

One maintainer keeps calling this the donkey thing, the one that starves because it can't pick between two piles of hay, and says lets just flip a coin. Another says that's a cop-out: our governance doc requires a written rationale in the minutes for breaking changes, and a coin flip isn't a rationale. A third says the chair should just decide on his own, since he genuinely doesn't care which way it goes and neither do most of us. And we have to announce something at the release freeze on the 15th or the whole cycle slips.

Draft the memo: recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, say what actually goes in the minutes and why it counts as a reason, and give the fairest version of the argument that deciding with no preference isn't really deciding at all.
