I chair the internal review of my employer's credit-scoring and fraud-flagging models, and next month I must defend, before our standing Model Risk and Fairness Committee, a position I have spent the better part of a year developing. The position is that the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness are best read not as a menu of mutually exclusive metrics but as a proof that fairness is a distributive question rather than a measurement question: because the standard group-fairness criteria cannot all hold at once unless the groups' base rates coincide or the prediction is essentially perfect, any practice of running the whole battery of metrics on each release and adjudicating the discrepancies case by case is not merely imperfect but incoherent as a decision procedure, since the real trade-off is made silently in the adjudication. What I propose instead is an explicit institution: a published fairness budget, modeled on a privacy budget, under which the committee pre-commits to one designated error-rate criterion and a stated tolerance, must account for every departure from it, and must disclose whose errors are being spent to purchase whose accuracy. I will also argue that a firm willing to randomize at the margin can honor such a budget more faithfully than one that insists on a deterministic rule, and that the familiar lament that we cannot satisfy every criterion at once is in part an artifact of that insistence.

The committee is not friendly. Its members are: the chief statistician, who knows the impossibility theorems cold and will not let me gesture at "the theorem" without stating its assumptions and naming exactly which quantities are being traded; a compliance lawyer from a civil-rights background, who thinks in terms of disparate impact, will hear "budget" as a license to discriminate, and will press me on the difference between a tolerance and a right; two machine-learning engineers, who hold that fairness is largely a matter of withholding protected attributes and of letting the model learn the pattern in the data, and who will keep asking why we cannot fix the data and move on; an external ethicist sitting as a member, who will object that "fairness budget" is a category error because fairness is not a conserved resource, and that my distributive framing begs the question of what is being distributed; and a product lead who wants a decision we can ship and will ask what the budget costs in accuracy and who signs when it is exceeded.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, in the order they will come, each phrased the way the member who raises it would phrase it, with a note on how damaging it is; for each, what I should concede, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply, flagged where the reply is soft. I do not need the theorems restated to me; I need to know how they bite. Separate the objections that have real technical force from those that are attacks on my framing rather than on the proposal, include the one that is technically correct but irrelevant to the decision actually before us and the one that is technically wrong but will land anyway, and finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
