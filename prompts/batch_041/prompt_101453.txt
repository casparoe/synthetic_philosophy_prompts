I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book that's mostly a family stuck in a hospital room. Mother's on a ventilator, no real prospect of coming back, and the two kids are split down the middle. The sister keeps saying some version of "we're not killing her, we're letting her die" — she actually said it straight to the doctor, something like, taking her off the vent isn't killing her, it's just letting the disease finish what its already doing. The brother fires back that that's a distinction without a difference and she knows it.

I've been rewriting this scene for a week and I've started to suspect the sister and I are both just saying words. So here's what I want: the actual precise claims packed into "letting her die isn't killing her." I want the whole spread, from the boringly true reading (something about proximate cause, who's got their hand on the switch, what the disease is doing vs what we're doing) up to the loaded ones (that omissions just aren't the kind of thing you can be morally on the hook for at all, or that the disease is the real agent and we're merely spectators). For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a real substantive position, or something that collapses under its own weight. Then tell me which one the sister is most likely reaching for at 2am in a hallway — and, more to the point for my book, which of them would actually get her off the hook and which only sound like they do.

And dont just lay the options out and leave me to pick — you commit. Say which reading you'd actually defend as the true content of the slogan, which are the ones people think they mean but dont, and where exactly the brother's rebuttal lands. I'd much rather you be wrong out loud than balanced and useless.
