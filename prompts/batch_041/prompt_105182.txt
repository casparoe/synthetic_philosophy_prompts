I am a retired physiologist — forty years in the field, most of them on avian sensory systems — who has spent the past several years reading philosophy with far more enthusiasm than training. I have been working slowly through Part Five of Descartes' Discourse on Method, the stretch where he turns to the difference between the human soul and the soul of the brutes. I am reading an English translation from the middle of the nineteenth century, and since I do not want to garble the wording I will give the argument's shape in my own words rather than as a quotation.

He imagines machines that resemble our bodies and imitate our actions as far as is morally possible. Even so, he says, there would be two certain ways of telling that they are not men. The first: such a machine could never use words or other signs, putting them together as we do, to declare its thoughts to others. It might be built to utter words, even a word suited to what is done to it, as when one touches it in a certain place and it asks what one wants; but it could not arrange its speech differently in response to whatever is said in its presence, in the way that even the dullest human being can. The second: though such a machine might do some things as well as or better than we do, it would fail in others, and this would show that it acts not from understanding but only from the disposition of its organs; for reason, on his account, is an instrument of unrestricted scope that can be turned to any occasion, whereas an organ needs a particular arrangement for each particular action. He then says that these same two means give us the difference between men and brutes: no human being is so dull, and none so mad, that he cannot put various words together and make his thoughts understood, while no animal, however perfect and well made, does anything of the sort. He grants that animals may lack the organs of speech, but insists the failure is not merely mechanical, since magpies and parrots can utter words as we do yet cannot show that they think what they say. And he observes that men born deaf and dumb, who lack the organs of speech as much as beasts do, nonetheless invent signs by which they make themselves understood. He concludes that beasts have no reason whatever, and that it is nature in them that acts according to the disposition of their organs, much as a clock tells the hours.

Two things about this I cannot get straight, and I would rather have help than a summary of the familiar view that Descartes took animals to be machines.

First, the two tests look to me as though they are meant to be independent, but I cannot see that they are. The second test — the failure in some tasks, the point about reason being usable on any occasion while an organ is fitted only to one — seems to presuppose that the machine's successes are to be explained by its parts, which is precisely what is at issue when we ask whether it understands anything. And in the case of animals, he seems to use that second test to rule out the very hypothesis he is testing. Is there a reading on which the second test does independent work, or is it doing the same job as the language test in different words?

Second, and more troubling to me as someone who spent decades watching animals solve problems: the inference from the fact that no beast has been observed to declare its thoughts in speech to the conclusion that beasts have no reason whatever seems to require a premise to the effect that thought must issue in declaration of the kind humans produce. He does not state such a premise; he treats the absence of the phenomenon as itself decisive. I want to know whether that premise is argued for anywhere in the stretch of text I have described, or whether it is an unstated assumption, and — if it is unstated — whether it is carrying the entire weight of the conclusion. I am also unsure how much his deaf-mute example can bear, since it appears to show that the capacity for conventional signs is present wherever there is a rational soul, which is arguably what he wants to prove rather than independent evidence for it.
