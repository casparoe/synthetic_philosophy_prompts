My flatmate has been reading up on jury theorems and now he won't shut up about an arguement he says proves that a big enough crowd is basically infallible. I'm pretty sure it's wrong but every time I push back he just says "which premise?" and I don't have an answer. Here it is as he gives it.

(1) Take any ordinary person and any yes/no question of fact. People are, on the whole, better than chance at these. Ask a room full of people a factual yes/no question and the average person is a bit more likely to be right than wrong -- 55/45, say. That's a general fact about humans and questions, not about experts.
(2) Condorcet's jury theorem: if n people each have a better-than-even chance of being right about the same question, and each votes their honest opinion, and their chances of being right are independent given the truth, then the probability that the majority is right rises with n and tends to 1. He says this is a theorem, so we don't get to argue with it.
(3) So for any yes/no question of fact, a large enough crowd voting by majority is right with probability as close to 1 as you like. A hundred thousand people in a hall, a show of hands, done.
(4) Which means expert panels, referees, judges and commissions are a waste of money. If you want to know whether the defendant did it, or whether a drug works, or whether some disputed claim in physics is true, ask a big enough random crowd and take the majority. They'll beat any expert you put against them.
(5) It even means the crowd can settle whether its own votes are independent and whether its own members are really better than chance. Those are factual questions too, so by (3) a majority vote about them is almost certainly right. The theorem certifies its own assumptions.
(6) But that's obviously wrong. A hundred thousand people picked at random and asked whether a particular defendant committed a particular murder, or whether some specific claim in particle physics is true, or whether a vaccine causes a condition, are not almost certainly right; on some of those they're confidently and reliably wrong. And the idea that a crowd can't be wrong about its own reliability is a joke.

So something in (1)-(5) has to go, and I can't figure out wich. I want to keep (2) -- it's a theorem, I'm not going to argue with a theorem -- and I'd rather not give up on crowds being wiser than the smartest person in them, since I use that to defend democracy. Which step do I drop, and what does dropping it cost me? And tell me what the theorem is actually doing here, because right now it looks like it can be pointed in whatever direction you like.
