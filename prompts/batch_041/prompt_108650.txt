I'm a second-year PhD student in computational social science and I've gotten a bit obsessed with the cliodynamics stuff — Turchin's secular cycles, the whole "can history be a science" program. My advisor thinks it's numerology with error bars and honestly I half agree, but I want to write a fellowship proposal that takes the strongest version of the program seriously and tries to break it in a way that would actually be informative. Here's the half-formed idea. There's this whole literature in ecology and complex systems on early warning signals of critical transitions — the claim that as a system drifts toward a tipping point (a lake going eutrophic, the Sahel flipping to desert) its fluctuations get bigger and slower, so variance and lag-one autocorrelation rise before the transition. People have started throwing this at financial crises and epidemics. I don't think anyone has seriously asked whether pre-modern states show the same signature before they break down. So: if secular cycles are a real dynamical phenomenon and not just an artifact of how we count things, the decades before a major state collapse should show rising variance and rising autocorrelation in the proxies people actually use — counts of distinct office-holding families, the frequency of rare given names, recorded revolts, that sort of thing. If they don't, that's evidence the "cycles" are largely an artifact of the aggregation window and the proxy choice. I also know the EWS theory only really predicts warning signs for certain kinds of bifurcations, so part of the question is which regime-shift model even fits a state collapse in the first place.

The wrinkle I can't get past, and the reason I want help rather than just writing it myself: the test is almost circular, because we only know which episodes count as collapses after the fact, and we only know which proxies are "right" after the fact too. I'd like the proposal to face that head-on rather than hide it. Can you write me a two-page proposal? I need the question stated so it has a determinate answer and isn't just "is cliodynamics good or bad"; why the existing standoff (the Turchin side vs. the skeptics who say it's curve-fitting) doesn't already settle it; the method, concretely, month by month over twelve months, including how I'd handle the circularity — I'm thinking some kind of out-of-sample or placebo test where I run the detector on periods that didn't end in collapse, but I don't know if that's enough; what a positive and a negative result would each mean for the bigger claim; and an honest bit about what would make this a failure or just not doable in a year. Don't bother with citations or a lit review, I'll bolt those on myself — I just need the arguement and the plan to be tight.
