I'm putting together a prospectus for a one-semester term paper (probably 7-8k words) on Leibniz's ethics, and I need help turning my half-formed idea into an actual proposal.

Here's what I keep running into. Leibniz defines justice as something like benevolence guided by wisdom -- love of others regulated by knowledge of what's genuinely good -- and he clearly wants this to be a substantive, action-guiding virtue, not just a description of how God loves the world. But he also holds that this is the best of all possible worlds, and that everything that happens is part of a providential order that couldn't be improved on. So what's left for a finite agent to do? If the overall outcome is already optimal and settled, then promoting the good either collapses into going along with the plan, or into a purely local and subjective measure of improvement that the metaphysics undercuts. Neither feels like what Leibniz wants.

My suspicion is that the fix isn't to soften the theodicy but to notice that he's running two different notions of the good -- the perfection of the universe as a whole, and the perfection of finite rational beings -- and that the benevolence of the wise is aimed primarily at the second, in a way that's compatible with the first being already fixed. Roughly: what the wise person wills is the perfection of others, and that's valuable independent of whether the whole is already as good as it can be. If that's right, Leibniz's ethics is less parasitic on his metaphysics than people usually assume.
