For the better part of my adult life I treated the question of how much to stake on a favourable bet as an abstraction with no bearing on my own affairs. That changed over the past year, when I began committing a substantial and growing share of my savings to a long series of independent bets on which I believe I hold a modest edge — positions whose size I choose myself rather than inherit from a market. The practical question of how much to put down has become the most consequential decision I make each week, and it has also become the fault line along which the two people whose judgement I trust most have split. They have argued about it for years without resolution, and I have discovered that I cannot adjudicate it from the inside: each of them is able to answer the other's best points in a way that sounds, at the moment of hearing it, decisive.

I would like you to draft the document they should have written together long ago: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between their two positions.

The first position — call it the geometric or growth-optimal view — holds that when a fortune is exposed to a sequence of multiplicative risks, the governing criterion is the growth rate of the realized fortune over time, so that the rational stake is the one that maximises expected log wealth; that this is not one preference among many but a requirement imposed by the structure of repeated multiplicative exposure; that the relevant object is the time-average of a single trajectory rather than the ensemble average across parallel worlds; and that departures from the growth-optimal stake, fractional staking in particular, must be justified either as responses to estimation error or as explicit deviations from rationality.

The second position — call it the expected-utility view — holds that rational choice is and has always been expected-utility maximisation with respect to one's actual preferences; that the growth criterion is simply expected-utility maximisation under a logarithmic utility function and enjoys no privilege over any other consistent utility; that the maximisation of long-run growth is a theorem about a particular objective rather than a norm; and that the apparent force of the time-average argument rests on an equivocation, since there is no sense in which a single realised path must be the object of rational concern.

Both camps, as I understand them, are willing to grant a great deal to the other — enough, I suspect, that a joint statement would be longer and more interesting than either would predict.

What I want from you:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered, containing the claims both positions can sign — about the mathematics of repeated multiplicative betting, about what each side's central theorem actually establishes, about which of the disputed cases are settled and which are not, and about the limits of what either framework can claim. Include at least claims about: the relationship between the growth-optimal stake and logarithmic utility; what becomes of the growth-optimal stake as the edge grows small; how the two criteria behave in a single bet that cannot be repeated; and the status of fractional staking. Where a candidate claim would be true but vacuous, say so and leave it out rather than padding the list.

2. A disagreements table. For each genuine point of dispute, give the question; the first position's answer in wording its own adherent would accept; the second position's answer in wording its own adherent would accept; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the case, argument, or thought experiment both sides agree bears on that crux; and each side's prediction about it, recorded in advance. Phrase each side's answer so that its adherent would sign it, and do not smuggle in a resolution.

3. Among the cruxes, I especially want these handled: whether the time-average over a single realised trajectory carries normative authority that the ensemble average lacks, or whether the appeal to it is an equivocation; whether the choice between the two criteria in a genuinely one-shot decision is a matter of preference or of correctness; whether the requirement that a fortune survive long enough to compound is a substantive constraint on rationality or merely an implication of logarithmic utility; and whether the two positions are in fact making claims about different things — one about the optimal policy for a class of stochastic processes, the other about the correct theory of choice — so that part of the dispute is verbal. If you conclude that some part of the dispute is verbal, identify precisely which part and why, and state what remains genuinely contested once the verbal element is set aside.

4. A short closing section stating what evidence, argument, or result would actually move each side, and whether there is anything both sides agree would move the debate — and if there is nothing, say that plainly rather than inventing a compromise.

Please keep the document free of bibliographies, reading lists, and citations; both camps are sitting in front of me and I want the arguments themselves, not a survey of who has made them. Favour precision over comprehensiveness: a shorter statement with exact formulations will serve me better than an exhaustive one, and I would rather see a single crux stated so exactly that both of my friends recognise their own view in it than five stated loosely.
