I'm in a fourth-year bioethics seminar (14 of us, mixed — about half pre-med, half philosophy) and I've been assigned to run one 90-minute session on how we decide when someone is dead. I want to do an activity, not a lecture, and here's my idea: we're a hospital committee drafting the protocol for organ donation after circulatory death, and the protocol uses one of these newer machines. After the heart stops and death has been declared, the surgical team puts the body on a pump that sends oxygenated blood back into the chest and abdomen so the organs don't spoil, and sometimes the heart starts beating again in there. They block off the vessels going to the head so none of it reaches the brain.

Give me the rules, roles, materials, a timeline, and a debrief. What I most want them to run into is that writing the protocol forces them to pick a criterion of death without noticing they're picking one: whether you're dead when you can't come back, or dead when we've decided not to try to bring you back. So make them commit to the specifics — how long we stand there doing nothing before we call it, whether restarting circulation counts as resuscitation, what we do if the heart kicks back in, whether we can still take organs from a body whose heart is going again, and how that squares with the rule that we only take organs from someone who's already dead. Then in the debrief show them that the answers they wrote only hang together on one of those two readings and not the other, and that the old protocol they inherited assumed the other one.

Also tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm expecting the pre-meds to just defer to whatever the guidelines say and the philosophers to jump straight to personhood stuff, and I don't know how to stop that from happening. And be honest about what the activity can't do: what I'll still have to say myself at the end, and what about the real thing it definately can't reproduce. No citations needed, obviously.
