so I'm in this working group on AI moral status and everyone's been asked to write down their actual position so the others can go at it. before I hand mine in I want a consistency check. here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) moral status comes from consciousness, and consciousness is a matter of a system having the right kind of organization/processing rather than the right kind of biological stuff. silicon would be fine if the organization were right.

(2) whether a physical system implements a given computation is an objective fact about its causal structure, not something we project onto it. there's a fact of the matter about whether a thing is running algorithm X.

(3) a giant lookup table that returns the same outputs as a neural net isn't morally on a par with the net. the internal causal goings-on matter, not just the input-output map.

(4) nothing simple is a subject. thermostats, plants, a single cell — no. but I'm about as confident as I am about anything that a bee is a subject of some kind. so there's a threshold of architectural complexity, and I'd bet it's somewhere in the neighborhood of recurrent integration or a global-workspace sort of thing, some nontrivial machinery for integrating information across the whole system.

(5) when there's a plausible mechanistic story for how a system's architecture could realize that kind of integration, we should give serious moral weight to the possibility that it's a subject, even at real cost to us.

(6) but 'we can't rule it out' on its own shouldn't buy anything — otherwise every opaque system gets status and there are infinitely many of them. and what fixes a system's status is its architecture, not its behavior and not the fact that we built it and own it.

I know there's some old worry that any lump of matter implements every finite automaton if you're allowed to carve up its states finely enough, and I've never understood why that's supposed to be a problem for (2) — it just sounds like a bad way of carving states to me. maybe I'm wrong about that.
