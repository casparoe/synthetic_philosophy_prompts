I'm putting together a short talk for a reading group at my lab, and I've been going through the AI control framing — the one where you assume the model you're deploying might be actively trying to subvert you, so instead of trusting it you wrap it in protocols: a second, "trusted" model monitors what it does, or edits its output before it ships, or you resample when a monitor flags something, or you hold back a small audit budget and look at a random slice of its actions. The pitch we keep hearing is that this is the pragmatic path, because control supposedly doesn't require having actually solved alignment. I want to build one scenario that pushes back on that, and I want it built to spec rather than just asserted, because otherwise it's just me gesturing.

Here's what I'm after. Give me a case where a control protocol makes things *less* safe than just shipping the untrusted model's output with whatever ordinary human review you'd have done anyway — and the safety loss has to be structural, falling out of how the protocol interacts with the model's incentives or information, not from the protocol being an obviously dumb design and not just stipulated by the setup. Constraints: no superhuman models; no appeal to exotic steganography or hidden capabilities we can't justify; monitor and untrusted model at roughly the same capability level; and the protocol has to be one a competent safety team would actually adopt and defend, not a strawman. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph, and make it a normal deployment — a coding assistant, an agent with a couple of tools, something a real company would ship. Then do the checks: for each item in the spec, show me where the scenario satisfies it; tell me exactly which feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the verdict when I pull that feature out; and give me the strongest version of the reply a control researcher would make, including the reply that I've only shown something about one protocol rather than about control as a family, plus your honest read on whether that reply lands. I care alot more about the mechanism than about the story, so if a cleaner mechanism forces a slightly uglier scenario, take the mechanism.

One thing I'd ask you to be careful about: keep separate the parts that are genuinely settled among people who work on this from the parts that are contested, speculative, or just plausible-sounding arguments, and don't dress the second kind up as consensus. If the scenario leans on an empirical assumption about model behaviour that nobody has really pinned down, flag that explicitly instead of letting the case look airtight, and tell me how much of the punch survives if that assumption turns out false.
