Since retiring from a career in experimental physics I have been running a small reading group on the philosophy of mathematics at our local lifelong-learning institute — twelve adults, most of them with some science or engineering background, meeting for ninety minutes a week — and next month I want to give the whole session to actual infinity. I have taught the standard Hilbert's-hotel material twice now, and both times the group could recite that a fully occupied hotel takes a new guest while nothing in them actually moved: they concede the arithmetic and keep the picture of infinity as an unfinished process, a thing you approach but never reach. What I want from you is a runnable classroom activity — a role-play or a game with rules, a ledger, and real cards in people's hands — that makes them feel the difference between infinity as an endless procedure and infinity as a completed collection that can be compared with its own parts, and then walks them one step past that, to a busload of guests whom no reassignment can seat. The idea has to live in what they do rather than in what I say afterwards: they should discover the bijection with a proper part themselves, by physically handing out and exchanging room cards, and they should run into the second impossibility themselves, by trying and failing to seat the second bus, before I name anything.

Please give me the roles, the materials (I want the reassignment to be something they perform with their hands rather than describe), any scoring or payoff scheme you think earns its keep, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes. For the second stage I have in mind a bus whose passengers are indexed not by the counting numbers but by something strictly larger — I will leave you to choose the cleanest encoding for a group without much mathematics, whether that is infinite strings of room-keys or points on a line — and I want the impossibility to be something they derive, not something I announce: the concierge keeps producing a seating plan, and the group keeps constructing a passenger who is not in it. The debrief should map each moment of the activity onto a piece of the idea — the shift of every guest one room down; the difference between a guest who is moved and a room that is freed; the distinction between a procedure that never terminates and an assignment that is nonetheless complete; the felt contrast between adding one more and adding as many more as there already were; and finally the passenger who differs from the occupant of whatever room the plan assigns — and it should tell me honestly which single step did the real work. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle each failure: where they will get stuck on whether the rooms are physically real, where someone will insist the process cannot finish and mean something true, where a participant will feel the diagonal move is a trick rather than a proof. And be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words. My own scientific habits leave me suspicious that all of this is bookkeeping rather than discovery, so if you can arrange for one participant to play a finitist auditor who must refuse to sign off on any infinite ledger and give reasons, I would be glad — though I suspect the activity can dramatize that refusal without ever letting the group feel what the finitist actually wants.
