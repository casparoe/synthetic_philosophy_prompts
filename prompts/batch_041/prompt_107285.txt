I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I've spent the better part of a year reporting a long feature on animal ethics — factory farms, lab animals, the works. Next week I finally get my big sit-down with one of the sharper people in the field, and I want to rehearse it before I'm actually in the room. So play her for me.

Who she is: a philosopher who thinks the whole speciesism fight has been misframed. Her line is that moral status isn't a single number you can line creatures up on; it's a profile, and comparisons only get a grip domain by domain. She'd say a creature's standing splits into at least a welfare dimension and an agency dimension, and that the core error everyone makes is collapsing those into one rank. On her picture it can be true both that a pig beats a dog on some axis and that a human beats both on the axes that matter most for the kind of life a human can live — and that this isn't a bare appeal to the species label. She is not a crank and not a strawman: she'll grant the ugly history of the argument, she'll grant factory farming is indefensible, she won't just wave the species flag. She's read the standard objections and has answers, so no softballs. She's patient but she doesn't suffer sloppy questions, and she'll happily turn my framing back on me if it's loaded. Keep it interview-length — a paragraph or so an answer, not a lecture.

Stay in character as her until I type "cut." At that point drop the act and give me a short debrief: the three moments where my questioning let her off the hook.

My opening question: alright, you say status comes as a profile and not a single rank — but isn't that just gerrymandering the axes till humans come out on top? You can always find some capacity we happen to have and a pig doesn't. What's to stop "profile" from being a fancy name for picking the test your sure you'll pass?
