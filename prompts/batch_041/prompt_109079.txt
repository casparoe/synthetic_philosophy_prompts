At the type foundry where I work we release families that span several styles — a geometric sans, a high-contrast display cut, a monospaced text version — all built from one underlying design logic, and I am the person who signs off on each glyph before it ships. I keep making these calls by eye and then defending them afterwards, and I would like a written procedure that my designers and I can actually run, glyph by glyph. Build it on the idea that a letter is a role rather than a shape: what makes something an 'a' is the work it does within the system of contrasts among the letters, together with a skeleton that survives restyling, while a style is a set of decisions applied consistently across the whole alphabet. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as passing each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of taste rather than of identity. Then run it on three cases: a near-circular 'a' whose bowl almost closes; an 'a' that is immaculate on its own but whose terminal contradicts every other letter in the family; and a display 'a' that is unmistakable in isolation but collapses into an 'o' in small running text. Include at least one verdict I will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
