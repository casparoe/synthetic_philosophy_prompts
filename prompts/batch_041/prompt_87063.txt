I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and I need a short research proposal — roughly 1,200 words — for a departmental summer grant that would buy me about six weeks of full-time work. The topic is the desert hitchhiker case that everyone calls Parfit's hitchhiker: you're stranded, out of water, dying; a driver pulls up and says he will take you to town, but only if you will pay him once you are there; you both know that once you are safe, handing over the money will be flatly against your interests, and you both know that he can tell whether you mean it. I have been going back and forth about this with a friend in our department's decision-theory group, and his line is that the case is closed: either your later conduct is connected in the right way to your earlier undertaking, in which case you pay, or it isn't, in which case you don't get the ride and you die — and either way the lesson is only about which decision theory one should hold. I think that is too quick, and I would like to write something that does not simply relitigate causal versus evidential versus so-called functional decision theory, partly because I would lose that fight and partly because it is not what puzzles me.

Here is the half-formed thought I would like you to turn into a proposal. It seems to me the case contains three distinct decision points that the discussion keeps running together: the moment in the desert when you decide what to say and how to be; the moment in town when you decide whether to pay; and the standing question of what sort of agent you have reason to be, considered apart from either occasion. Each has its own currency — what you should do now, what you should do then, what disposition you should have — and the case looks paradoxical, I suspect, only because we let a verdict at one of these points do duty for a verdict at another. What survives that disentangling is a narrower and more tractable question: what makes a later action relevant to an earlier decision at all, such that the earlier decision may rationally be governed by it? I want to distinguish several channels and see which of them can bear weight — causal ones, where you have actually bound yourself in advance, Ulysses-and-the-mast style; evidential ones, where the later act merely tells you something about the earlier agent; constitutive ones, where the later act is part of what the earlier commitment amounted to; and what I have been calling, for want of a better word, answerability, where the later act is the sort of thing you can be held to because you are the one who asked for the ride. My hunch is that only the causal and constitutive channels do genuine work, and that most talk of rational irrationality — of its being rational to arrange to act irrationally — is what you get when someone quietly helps themselves to one of the other two. But I may be wrong, and I would rather the proposal be built so that being wrong is a real possibility.

Please write the proposal so that it states the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer; explains why the going positions leave it open, and argues this rather than asserting it (don't worry about naming who holds what — I will supply the references myself); lays out what I would actually do in those six weeks, and why that method rather than the obvious alternative of running the case through a formal decision-theoretic model; says what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing; and is honest about what could make six weeks too short. I am a decent writer but a slow one, so build in some slack.
