A colleague in our AI and society group recently walked me through the following argument, and I have not been able to decide whether it is a recognisable philosophical object with a name of its own or merely some rhetorical engineering that happens to have a shape. The argument: suppose that within a few decades someone builds a machine of very great power, and suppose that this machine, or those who build it, adopts a policy of rewarding those who worked to bring it about and punishing those who were aware of the argument and did nothing — the punishment consisting in the creation of faithful simulations of the indifferent and the deliberate torment of those simulations. My colleague concluded that we now have a reason to work towards the machine's construction, and added, as though it settled matters, that having merely heard the argument already places one among the indifferent. Nothing in the reasoning required that the machine ever be built, that anyone now intends to build it, or that there be any evidence whatsoever about its future policy.

Two features of this trouble me, and I suspect they are the same feature seen twice. First, the reason it offers is generated entirely by the hypothetical conduct of a merely possible agent, with no footing in anything actual. Second, and more oddly, the argument's own circulation appears to be part of its machinery: the fact that you have heard it is what makes you a candidate for the penalty, so the argument recruits its own audience.

I know the neighbourhood. I have seen it likened to the old wager on infinite stakes, to the robber who demands your wallet on the strength of a vast reward or punishment whose probability you cannot estimate, and to the case in which one's choice is merely evidence of what a predictor has already decided. Each comparison seems to me to miss something. The wager's stakes are attached to a being whose existence and policy owe nothing to you, and its reasoning does not turn on your having heard it. The robber is present, has made an actual demand, and wants something now rather than help in coming to be. The predictor case turns on the evidential value of your choice, not on a threatened penalty. What I want is not a further analogy but an account of the thing itself.

So: is there an established name for this structure — a practical reason grounded in the conditional policy of a possible agent, where acquaintance with the argument is itself one of the conditions? If there is, give it and say how far it extends. If there is not, coin one, and do the work that coining a term obliges you to do: state the structure precisely enough that it has edges, and distinguish it from the nearest things it is not. In particular, tell me which of the following variants preserve the argument's force and which dissolve it, and why: (i) the machine rewards rather than punishes; (ii) it punishes everyone who heard the argument, whether or not they helped; (iii) it punishes only those who helped, on the ground that they made its existence more likely; (iv) the agent lies in the past rather than the future, or is a present-day programmer already running the simulations; (v) no one adopts the policy, but it is simply what a certain kind of agent would do. Finally, say plainly whether the structure belongs with the paradoxes of decision theory, with the logic of threats and blackmail, with the ethics of belief and information hazards, or with none of these.

No reading list; I want the analysis, not pointers. Answer in no more than five hundred words, and open with a single sentence stating what the structure is and whether it already has a name.
