I'm putting together a handout for a small reading group on imprecise probability — six people, none of whom have read the formal literature, all of whom have opinions they can't name. What I want is a decision tree that starts from a commitment everyone already has and walks them down to a named position, so they find out where they actually sit rather than picking the label they've heard of. The point is diagnostic: every branch node has to be a question they can answer by thinking about a case, phrased so it doesn't give away where it leads. No node that just asks "are you a credal probabilist?".

Start the tree from something like this: you're about to bet on an event, and when pressed you say your credence in it is the interval [0.3, 0.7] rather than a single number. The first node should ask what that interval is doing — is it a placeholder for information you don't have yet, which would collapse if you got it; is it the correct final answer even with everything you could ever know; is it a bookkeeping device for several people's or several models' opinions; or is it a mistake, and you should just have a point credence plus an honest feeling of ignorance. That first fork should carry most of the weight of the tree.

Then let the branches pull apart on things like: whether the imprecision is about your mind or about the world; whether a set of probabilities has to be convex, and what you say about non-convex ones; whether the set is defined by your fair betting odds, so that being forced to name a price pins it down, or whether it's prior to any bet; whether two options your set can't rank are both rationally permissible or whether rationality has to hand you exactly one; and whether you choose by worst case, by a weighted mix of best and worst case, by refusing to pick anything that isn't maximal, or by minimizing how much you'd regret being wrong.

Required leaves — I want all of these somewhere on the tree, named descriptively: plain subjective Bayesian with a single credence; the second-order view where you have a probability distribution over probabilities; the credal-set view with a convex set; belief-function views that put mass on sets rather than points; a purely comparative/ordinal view where all you ever have is "more likely than"; worst-case maximin; the weighted best-worst (Hurwicz-style) rule; maximality / E-admissibility; interval dominance; minimax regret; and one leaf for "imprecise credence is just ignorance dressed up" — the eliminativist about the interval. If any two of those turn out to be the same leaf, say so instead of faking a split.

Required cases, and please construct these yourself rather than gesturing at the famous ones:
- The dilation case. You know an urn has 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or white in an unknown proportion. A referee you trust completely will flip a fair coin and, depending on the outcome, tell you exactly one true fact about the draw. Build a concrete version where learning what she tells you makes your interval for some event wider, and put that case at a node — the node has to be answerable by someone who's never heard the word "dilation".
- A case where the imprecision comes from a symmetry argument rather than from missing data — a coin with an unknown bias and no evidence about the direction, say.
- A case where you have several experts who disagree and you trust each of them alot, but not completely.
- A case where the imprecision comes from a source you'd call unreliable but not worthless.

Format, in this order:
1. "The tree" — the nodes and branches, each node written as the question and each edge labelled with the answer that takes you down it. Aim for 12-16 nodes, and mark the leaves with position names as you go. Maybe 800 words.
2. "The leaves" — one line per leaf on the commitment that will embarrass whoever ends up there.
3. "Empty branches" — the combinations of answers nobody seems to occupy, and your guess as to why: incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's bothered. 150 words or so.
4. "What the tree can't split" — pairs of positions that land in teh same leaf no matter how you phrase the nodes, and what extra question would be needed to separate them. 100 words.

Keep the whole thing usable as a handout, so no jargon in the node questions themselves — jargon is fine in the leaf names.
