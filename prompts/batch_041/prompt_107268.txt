So I keep reading about this idea that what you beleive can actually change what you see — not just how you interpret it, but the seeing itself. The example that got me was a gray banana shape looking slightly yellow to people because they know bananas are yellow. I find that kind of creepy and I want a short story about it.

Here's a hook I came up with, use it or change it however: there's a small factory that hires people to spot defects on a line — pills, circuit boards, whatever — and during training they tell each new hire the "known defect rate" for each batch before they ever look at it. The narrator has worked there for years and starts to notice that on the batches they've been told are clean, they genuinely can't find the scratches anymore. Not that they doubt themselves or second-guess — the scratches are just gone for them. So they check against the camera photos, and the machine says the scratch is there, plain as day. And the scary part is they can't tell if they've gotten better at the job (tuned out noise, like everyone says) or if their eyes have basically been rewritten to agree with the clipboard.

I don't want a twist ending or a "was it all in their head" cop-out. I want the story to actually take the idea seriously. Is there a real difference between "I see it and I judge that it's fine" and "I literally don't see it"? What would it even mean to be wrong about your own eyes? Can somebody else know better than you what you're seeing, or is that just nonsense? And if your perception can be bent by what you expect, does that make it useless for finding out the truth, or is that just how perception always worked anyway?

Maybe 1200-2000 words, plain prose, no lectures. The character shouldn't suddenly start talking like a philosopher — I'd rather the whole thing come out sideways, through what they do and what they can't do anymore. And if you can, make it so I can't tell at the end whether the character is broken or whether they're the only one seeing straight.
