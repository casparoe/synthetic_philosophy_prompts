Ok, I need a writing sample for my grad seminar on the Scottish Enlightenment, and I want you to produce the model answer so I can hand it out (so make it good). Write an agony-aunt column — the kind of thing you'd find in a mid-century newspaper problem page — except the aunt is Adam Smith's impartial spectator. Three readers write in with contemporary dilemmas, the spectator replies to each, roughly 200 words a reply, 700 words total. Everything the columnist says has to come out of Smith's own machinery: sympathy as an imagined exchange of places with the person concerned, propriety rather than utility, the difference between being praised and being praiseworthy, and the fact that our sense of what's proper is partly settled by custom and fashion. No Kantian or utilitarian backsliding, no naming Smith or citing any book, no scholarly apparatus — it's a problem page, not a lecture.

Choose the three letters so that the advice gets genuinely hard. At least one reader wants a verdict on something where the honest answer is that the spectator can only tell them what they already half-believe, because their sentiments were formed by the very local custom they're now doubting. At least one reader asks about a case where they did the right thing and got no credit for it (Smith has something specific and slightly deflating to say about that). And at least one where the reader has the facts about their own situation wrong, which forces the columnist to say something about what the spectator is supposed to know and who exactly is being imagined in the exchange of places. Keep it deadpan — the joke is that a procedure of sympathetic imagination keeps being asked to behave like an oracle, and the columnist never admits theres a problem even as the hedges pile up. A reader should finish it unsure whether the impartial spectator is a standard or just a very well-mannered mirror of the people doing the imagining.
