Ok so I've been in a running arguemnt with a friend for like two years now about the no-free-lunch theorems and whether they actually have anything to do with Hume's problem of induction, and we keep talking past each other. I want to finally write the document we should have written at the start -- a joint statement that we could both actually put our names to -- so please draft it.

The two of us are basically these two camps.

Camp 1 (the deflationist side -- that's my friend): the no-free-lunch results are, at bottom, a triviality about averaging. They say that if you average performance over all possible objective functions, or all possible prediction problems, with a uniform weighting, then every algorithm does equally well, so none is better than any other in general. But that's a fact about one particular averaging measure, not a fact about induction. Real learning problems don't get drawn from a uniform distribution over all functions, so the theorem has no teeth for the actual problem of justifying induction. Yeah you need some bias or assumption, but that's not the same as induction being unjustified -- everyone always knew you need assumptions, and some assumptions just happen to be true of our world.

Camp 2 (the skeptic side -- that's me): the theorems are the sharpest formal version we've got of Hume's point. They show there's no assumption-free method that beats chance in general, so generalizing at all requires a bias, and the bias can't itself be justified without circularity. So the whole thing *is* the problem of induction, formalized: the free lunch you'd need is an assumption, and you can't get that for free.
