I chair the institutional review board at a university with a large neuroscience program. Next week we review a protocol in which pairs of participants have their brains coupled through continuous bidirectional EEG neurofeedback while they jointly control a single virtual body; neither participant can tell which of them initiated a given movement, and the consent form warns that some participants may leave with a persistent sense that their intentions are partly the other person's. The PI, invoking a Hofstadter-influenced picture of a self distributed across more than one brain, argues that ordinary individual consent is sufficient because each participant understands the procedure and may withdraw at any time. Two board members object: one holds that if the 'I' is genuinely distributed, the unit of consent may be the pair rather than the individuals, so the study cannot obtain valid consent for a state that may not respect individual boundaries; the other calls this speculative metaphysics and notes that we routinely approve research on altered self-experience. I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft it: recommend a concrete decision -- approve, approve with conditions, or reject -- with a time frame and specific conditions, and give the strongest consideration on the losing side a fair hearing. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on personal identity and research ethics, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. No reading list; I need an actionable opinion, not a survey.
