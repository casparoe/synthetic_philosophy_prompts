A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about psychiatric diagnosis, and I want to turn the disagreement into something usable. She works in community mental health and thinks new diagnostic labels mostly recognize what was always there; I think a label can change the people who receive it, so that the next version of a manual is partly describing a population the previous version helped create. I am on the diagnostic review panel of a mid-sized nonprofit health system. The panel is two clinicians, an administrator, a data analyst, and a patient representative. We maintain the local intake and screening forms, recommend whether to add, keep, or rewrite categories, and train clinicians in their use. We meet monthly, our decisions affect about 40,000 patients a year, and the medical director has final sign-off. We have been asked to decide whether to add a new screening category for Problematic Short-Form Video Use; whether to keep our current burnout screening item, which patients have begun to treat as a diagnosis and a basis for accommodation requests; and whether to replace our borderline personality disorder screening label with a trauma-informed alternative. Please write a decision procedure the panel can actually run on a proposal to add, retain, or modify a diagnostic category, one that treats looping effects as a central input rather than a public-relations problem. By looping effects I mean the phenomenon Ian Hacking described: a classification can change the people classified, which then changes the classification. I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I need the procedure itself.

The procedure should ask questions in a fixed order, with a clear criterion for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step and a clear stopping rule for when the panel should decline or defer. It should distinguish at least two kinds of loop: meaning loops, where the label's sense shifts as patients, clinicians, and advocacy groups negotiate what it means, and leverage loops, where people adopt or resist the label to obtain resources, exemptions, solidarity, or a story about themselves. Say how the procedure should treat these differently. It should include a loop audit that runs before the category goes on any form and again at a fixed review interval, with specific evidence the panel should collect from clinicians, patients, and intake data. It should end with a loop-liability rating on a simple scale and a default sunset or review date. It should say where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a person or body because the issue is a value question rather than an empirical one, and it should identify which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It should be usable in a 60-minute meeting by clinicians and administrators, not philosophers, and it should say what the panel should do when it cannot get the evidence the procedure asks for.

Then apply the procedure to the three cases above. For each, give the answers to the questions as far as they can be given, the recommendation, and the points where the procedure is silent. Make sure at least one application yields a recommendation I will not like, and explain why the procedure nonetheless supports it. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do and where it is likely to misfire.
