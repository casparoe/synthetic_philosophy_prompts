For several years I have been unable to dispose of an argument about the welfare of wild animals, and unable to see which of its steps to give up. It runs as follows.

(1) An event harms a being only if it leaves that being worse off than it would have been had the event not occurred.

(2) If an event prevents a being from existing, that being is not left worse off, since in the counterfactual there is no being to be worse off.

(3) Therefore, when a habitat is destroyed and the animals that would have lived in it are never brought into existence, those animals are not harmed.

(4) The animals harmed by habitat destruction are accordingly only those already alive: those killed outright, and those that survive in the degraded remnant.

(5) Suppose, as is now frequently argued, that the lives of most wild animals are on balance not worth living — that the great majority die young, by predation, starvation, exposure, or disease, and that the suffering in a typical such life exceeds its enjoyments.

(6) It follows that the animals whose existence habitat destruction prevents would have had lives not worth living, so preventing their existence is no loss to them, and may be a good thing considered from the standpoint of the world's suffering.

(7) The harm done to the animals actually killed is finite and confined to a single generation, whereas the destruction of the land prevents the existence of every generation of animals that land would otherwise have supported.

(8) Habitat destruction therefore diminishes suffering, all things considered.

(9) The same reasoning applies to any further reduction in the number of wild animals; the limiting case of the process is the elimination of wild animal life, which is consequently the best available state of affairs.

(10) But (9) is monstrous.

Something between (1) and (9) must give way. I would most like to retain (1) and (5) — I am attached to the thought that harming requires a being made worse off, and I find the empirical claim about wild animal lives persuasive — and I am prepared to surrender nearly everything else. Which step should I give up? Explain why it looked compelling, and set out what giving it up commits me to. I do not need to be told that (10) is unacceptable; I already believe that, and I am not asking for a survey of the literature. I want to know where the argument should be resisted, and what each available repair costs.
