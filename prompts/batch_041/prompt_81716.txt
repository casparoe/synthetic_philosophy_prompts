I am a teaching assistant on a second-year course on mind and metaphysics, and I am working through a stack of scripts for the one question on the paper that I wrote myself. The unit closed with the ant-colony case — the one our reader nicknames Aunt Hillary, in which a colony of ants behaves so intricately that an observer can predict it reliably by treating the colony itself as a single agent with its own beliefs and desires. Because I did not want students simply reciting a canned verdict from the lectures, I printed the two criteria on the paper and told them they would be marked on how they used them rather than on which answer they reached. The question, with the criteria as printed, read as follows:

"The Aunt Hillary case asks us to imagine a colony of ants whose collective behaviour is so complex, and so finely responsive to its surroundings, that an observer can predict it reliably by attributing beliefs and desires to the colony itself. Suppose we are also told that the colony's behaviour is exhaustively explained by the behaviour of the individual ants together with their local interactions, and that nothing else is going on. Does the colony have a mind? Answer by (i) stating each criterion below in your own words, (ii) applying each of them to the case, (iii) giving a verdict, and (iv) replying to the strongest objection to that verdict. Criterion 1 (the pattern criterion): a system has a mind just in case attributing beliefs and desires to it yields reliable predictions because the system's causal organisation genuinely tracks the content of those states — that is, the pattern is discovered rather than imposed. Criterion 2 (the aggregate criterion): a system has a mind only if it is not a mere aggregate, that is, only if the behaviour of the whole cannot be predicted from the behaviour of its parts considered in isolation from one another."

Here is the script, transcribed verbatim:

"Both criteria are asking what it takes for a system to be a genuine subject of belief rather than something we merely find it convenient to talk about in that way. Criterion 1 requires that the attributions track something real in the system's causal workings, so that the beliefs we credit it with are the ones actually doing the work. Criterion 2 requires that a mind not be a mere heap of parts: if you can take the parts one at a time and say everything there is to say about each of them, then you have an aggregate rather than a mind.

The colony passes Criterion 1. Its behaviour is responsive in the way that agency is: foragers are redirected when a food source runs out, defence is escalated when the nest is disturbed, and the observer's predictions come off because the colony's organisation — pheromone gradients, traffic, the distribution of work among castes — carries information about the world and channels it into what happens next. That is a pattern being discovered, not imposed.

The colony fails Criterion 2. We are told that its behaviour is exhaustively explained by the individual ants and their local interactions, and that nothing else is going on. Every ant can be described completely without ever mentioning the colony's beliefs or desires: it is following a pheromone gradient, not defending the nest because it believes the nest to be threatened. So the colony's behaviour can be predicted from its parts taken in isolation, which is just what Criterion 2 rules out. Ants are not like neurons, which do nothing on their own and only mean anything inside a whole brain.

Someone might object that the same reasoning would show that I have no mind, since my neurons can be described without mentioning my beliefs. But I know from the inside that I have a mind, and I have no comparable access to whatever, if anything, the colony experiences; that asymmetry is what should decide the case. My verdict, then, is that Aunt Hillary behaves as if it had a mind, but does not have one."
