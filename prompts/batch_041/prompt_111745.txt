A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the finality of legal judgments, and I would like to get the question straight. The practice, as I understand it, is this: once a case has run its course and the appeals are exhausted, the judgment stands even if evidence emerging later makes it very likely that the judgment was mistaken. Courts recognize narrow exceptions — newly available DNA testing, fraud on the court, jurisdictional defects — but the baseline is closure, and in criminal matters the exceptions are drawn tightly. So the same legal system that spends enormous effort getting the facts right at trial then declines to look at facts that arrive afterwards, and I cannot decide whether that is a compromise with truth or a view about truth.

My friend treats it as an obvious compromise: we accept some risk of error in exchange for repose, predictability, and the conservation of judicial resources. I found myself defending something more ambitious — that a properly conducted trial can put a question beyond rational revision, so that later evidence against the verdict is not merely outweighed but disabled, something we are entitled to set aside without assessing it. There is a puzzle in epistemology that looks like that claim in miniature: if you know that p, then any evidence you later meet against p must be misleading, since p is true and you know it, and so you may discount it on that ground alone. My friend calls this the dogmatism paradox and takes it as a reductio of the premise rather than a discovery about evidence; I am not so sure.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of the commitments this practice actually carries. Keep apart two things in particular: the claim that a proposition can be evidentially closed, so that no future evidence could rationally bear on it, and the claim that a proceeding can be procedurally closed, so that we will not reopen the question whatever we privately think of the answer. Does the practice of finality require the first, or is it fully licensed by the second together with non-epistemic values such as stability and the scarcity of judicial time? If it does require something epistemic, is that something the strong thesis that knowledge disables defeaters, or only a defeasible presumption that the procedure which produced the verdict was reliable? And where the practice is silent, tell me how much latitude it leaves: I want to know which incompatible theories of knowledge would each license the same doctrines, and what would have to change — in the law and in the surrounding epistemology — if we took seriously the thought that no verdict is ever evidentially closed.
