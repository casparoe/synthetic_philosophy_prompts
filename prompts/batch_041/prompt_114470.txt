I am trying to formulate a defensible duty to remember without turning it into an impossible archival obligation, and a colleague has offered the following argument, which she calls the triage objection. (1) We have a duty to remember serious historical wrongs, so that their victims are not consigned to oblivion. (2) If we have that duty with respect to one serious wrong, we have it with respect to every serious wrong of comparable gravity. (3) The number of such wrongs vastly exceeds what any person or institution can hold in memory. (4) Therefore we have a duty that no one can discharge. (5) A duty that cannot in principle be discharged is no duty at all. (6) So there is no duty to remember, only an optional practice of memorialising what happens to move us. I want to resist (6), but I cannot see whether the fault lies in (2), in (5), or in the inference from (3) to (4). Where does the argument go wrong? If you think it is valid, which premise is least costly to abandon, and what would abandoning it commit me to? Give me a definite verdict and defend it, rather than setting out the options; in particular, say whether the position you recommend still lets me distinguish remembrance from nostalgia or archival hoarding.
