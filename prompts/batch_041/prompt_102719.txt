I've been reading Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and the literature around it on and off for years, and I keep returning to the same frustration. The argument that Gödel's incompleteness theorems somehow show that minds outrun any formal system — the line Penrose made famous — has been running for over half a century now, and as far as I can tell the same handful of moves get made on each side and nobody budges. The mechanists say the argument requires the idealised mathematician to know her own consistency, which is precisely the kind of self-knowledge the second incompleteness theorem withholds from a formal system; the anti-mechanists answer that this only shows the system cannot prove its own soundness, not that it isn't sound, and that we can see our own soundness in a way no formal system can. Then Hofstadter's own project muddies the water further, because he seems to want the self-reference to do something quite different from Penrose: not to prove that minds transcend machines, but to explain how a machine could come to be self-aware at all, through a strange loop that models itself.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than assume, is that this is not one debate but two questions wearing a single name — a proof-theoretic question about what a formal system can prove about itself, and a philosophical question about what it is for a system to have a self-model at all — and that the apparent impasse is mostly the two sides sliding between "the Gödel sentence" as a fact about formal systems and "the Gödel sentence" as a stand-in for self-awareness, and between "proof" in the technical sense and "knowing" in the everyday sense. If that is right, then nobody is being stupid; they are answering different questions under one heading, and the disagreement is more verbal and equivocal than substantive.

So diagnose it for me. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? Is there a hidden technical result — something in the neighbourhood of the self-trust and provability literature that rationalist types are fond of invoking, where a system's ability to trust its own proofs turns out to be more delicate than either side assumes — that would actually settle the proof-theoretic half while leaving the other half untouched? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here, if anything: a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question, or simply the recognition that it was ill-posed from the start? I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it, not a survey of who said what.

Please give me continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just the way one would write a letter — and write it in British English.
