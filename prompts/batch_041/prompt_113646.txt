I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about higher-order theories of consciousness, and we ended somewhere I now think is a real research question rather than just two people talking past each other. The theory, as we were using it, says that a mental state is conscious just in case a suitable higher-order representation of it is present, with the usual division between versions that require such a representation to actually occur and versions that require only a disposition to produce one. Our disagreement was not about that division. It was about the relation the theory needs between the two states: what it is, exactly, that makes a given higher-order representation a representation of this first-order state rather than of any other.

My friend proposed what he called a tagging solution. On his view the first-order state carries a mark that the higher-order representation is built to read off, so there is never any question about which state is being represented; the higher-order representation just registers the mark. I said this looked circular. If the mark is what makes a state the one that gets represented, and being represented is what makes a state conscious, then either the mark already carries consciousness with it, in which case the higher-order representation is doing no work at all, or it does not, in which case we still owe an account of how the higher-order representation latches onto its target and the tag has merely moved the problem. My own suggestion, which I do not trust, was that the higher-order representation must represent the content of the first-order state. That seemed to fail on cases where two states share a content — an episode of seeing something and an episode of visualizing the same thing, or two successive perceptions of an unchanging scene — since the theory would then have to say the higher-order representation cannot tell them apart, which seems plainly wrong. We also kept tripping over a third constraint: if the higher-order representation can misrepresent its target, the theory owes us an account of what the resulting phenomenal character is, and neither of us could see how any of the proposed relations handles that cleanly.

Please write me a short research proposal that turns this into a project. Assume roughly one semester of work, or a small grant application of comparable scope; assume no experiments. I need the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the answers already on the table leave it open, and why the obvious escapes — deny that any such relation is needed, declare the relation primitive, redescribe the whole issue as one about content rather than about reference — do not settle it; the method, spelled out phase by phase, with a defence of why conceptual work is the right instrument here rather than anything empirical; what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a scope I could honestly finish in the time available, or an honest statement of why I could not. Do not give me citations or a reading list, and do not attribute positions to named authors; refer to views and arguments descriptively and I will add the references myself.

One thing I want to be careful about, because I have already caught myself doing it twice: separate what is actually settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not let a contested view emerge sounding like the consensus — including any view that my own framing of the problem quietly presupposes.
