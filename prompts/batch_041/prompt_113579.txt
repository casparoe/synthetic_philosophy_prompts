I am a novelist, well into a book set in Manchester in the summer of 1819, and I have reached the point where instinct will no longer carry me. The episode is Peterloo. On 16 August 1819 a crowd of some sixty thousand gathered at St Peter's Field to hear Henry Hunt press the case for parliamentary reform; the magistrates ordered the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry to arrest Hunt and the men on the platform with him; the yeomanry charged; William Hulton, chairman of the Cheshire magistrates, then called in the 15th Hussars, who rode into the crowd with sabres drawn. Around eighteen were killed — the commemorative figure has been revised more than once, and the radical press compiled its own lists against the authorities' minimal account — and several hundred were injured. The name by which we know the day was coined in the radical Manchester press as a bitter echo of Waterloo. Liverpool's government answered with the Six Acts, and the prosecutions fell on the reformers rather than on the yeomanry. On the bicentenary, the city unveiled a memorial by Jeremy Deller: eleven concentric circles of local stone, engraved with the names of the dead and the places they came from.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions between this episode and the standing quarrel between the history of the poor and the history of elites and states — microhistory and history from below on one side, the history of the state and its archives on the other.

Begin with a suspicion of mine, which I would like tested rather than flattered. I have come to think that history from below is asked to do three distinct things: to recover the participants, to explain how large outcomes were produced by small actors, and to settle accounts with the present. I suspect that at Peterloo these three come apart, and that the surviving record supports one of them far better than the others. Say which, and on what evidence.

Then the harder question. The crowd at Peterloo reaches us only through mediation: the magistrates' letters, the yeomanry's own accounts, banners seized and inventoried, and a radical press run by men who were not themselves of the crowd — Hunt was a gentleman farmer, not a hand-loom weaver. Does that mediation disqualify a from-below reading of this episode, or merely qualify it? And what could a minute-by-minute reconstruction of that single afternoon, of the kind the method prizes, actually establish about what the crowd took itself to be doing — and what could it not establish, however scrupulous it was?

Then reverse the lens. What does the history of the state explain here that no reduction of scale can — the passage of the Six Acts, the prosecutions that fell on the reformers, the conversion of a magistrates' panic into a national regime of repression? And what does the episode show about the theory in return? Microhistory's wager is that a small case, patiently reconstructed, opens onto a whole world. Peterloo is neither small nor ordinary; it is an outlier that everyone remembers. Tell me what that does to the wager.

Finally, the practical matter, which is what I actually need. A novel requires both a scene and a causal architecture. Which of the two does the record support, and which would I be inventing if I wrote it either way?

Separate throughout what is settled among people who know the period from what is genuinely contested, and do not pass a contested reading off as the consensus — the sequence of the charge is settled; the magistrates' motives, the precise toll, and whether Peterloo changed anything are not. No citations and no reading list; I will do that work myself. Where you are unsure, say so rather than smoothing it over.
