I've been teaching a seminar on creativity and cognition this term and I've dragged the students into the weeds of the old Copycat program — Hofstadter's letter-string analogy thing, where the system gets a puzzle like abc goes to abd, so what does ijk go to, and the interesting part was never whether it lands on the right answer but that it has to settle on a reading of the situation before there can even be a right answer. Half the puzzles are built so that the same input supports two readings at once — bump the last letter of the string, or bump the last element of a doubled group — and until the system has decided which of those it's looking at, it doesn't have a question, let alone an answer. The picture underneath all this is what I keep trying to get across to them: concepts sit in a network where the links between them have lengths that change as the system works, so things that start out far apart — successor and predecessor, letter and group, left and right — can drift close enough to be swapped for one another in mid-problem, which is what the people in that tradition mean by slippage. And there's a global quantity they call temperature that measures how many half-built, mutually incompatible readings are still alive at once: let it fall to zero and one reading has clamped down and you get an answer, keep it high and the system is genuinely undecided about what kind of problem it is in.

The problem is that half my students now work on language models, and every time I say slippage or temperature they hear something else entirely — and I've started to suspect their vocabulary can't even state the thing I'm trying to say. So here's what I want from you. I've picked up enough of their language from sitting in on my colleague's ML course and arguing with my students to follow a blog post — embeddings and vector spaces, attention, the residual stream, next-token prediction, in-context learning, sampling temperature, nearest neighbours in representation space, and the vague things people say about what's happening in the middle layers — but I couldn't write a line of code and I don't want to pretend otherwise. Restate the Copycat picture in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and don't let the new framework's assumptions do the work for you: if you find yourself having to posit something the language-model vocabulary has no room for, say so rather than quietly redescribing it in the newer terms.

Then give me the residue report. Which pieces survive the trip cleanly — my own guess is that the network-of-concepts part goes through fine as an embedding space, and I'd like to know whether that's too glib — which pieces survive only if you let yourself fudge something, and which have no counterpart at all. My suspicion, and I want you to tell me if I'm romanticizing the old program, is that the place the two part company is the business of the system's reading of the problem being something it builds and can revise rather than something handed to it from outside, and maybe also the way its temperature is a fact about its epistemic situation rather than a knob anyone sets. At the end, tell me which way the failure cuts: when a bit of Copycat can't be said in the newer vocabulary, is that because the newer vocabulary is missing something it ought to have, or because the older picture was never as clear as it looked? I'm not after a history lesson or a reading list — I know the literature well enough and you couldn't get at it anyway — I want the translation and the residue, and I'd rather you be honest about where the analogy breaks than smooth it over.
