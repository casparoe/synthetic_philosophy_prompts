Question: Reconstruct the conceivability ("zombie") argument against physicalism. State its premises, explain what has to be true for each premise to do its work, and assess the two main lines of physicalist response.

Student's answer:

"Chalmers' zombie argument is the most-discussed argument against physicalism about consciousness. A philosophical zombie is a being molecule-for-molecule identical to an ordinary human being but with no conscious experience at all — no pains, no tastes, nothing it is like to be it. Because it's physically identical it behaves identically too (it will say 'ow' and write papers about qualia), so you can't answer the argument by pointing to anything the zombie does or says; the difference is supposed to be purely in the inner life.

The argument runs: (1) zombies are conceivable; (2) if zombies are conceivable then they are possible; (3) if zombies are possible then physicalism is false, since physicalism says the physical facts fix all the facts, and here we have a world with all the same physical facts and no consciousness. So the physical facts don't entail the phenomenal facts, and consciousness is something over and above the physical.

Premise (1) is hard to deny, and physicalists have basically stopped trying. The action is all in (2). Since Kripke it's been widely accepted that conceivability is a good guide to possibility, so the physicalist either has to find a counterexample or show that the zombie case is special.

Physicalists divide into two camps. Chalmers calls the first 'type-A': these are the phenomenal concept theorists — Loar, Papineau, Balog — who accept that zombies are conceivable and then argue that the conceivability is only apparent: once you see that phenomenal concepts are special first-personal concepts, the zombie scenario turns out not to be genuinely conceivable after all, so premise (1) collapses. The second camp he calls 'type-B': Dennett is the standard example, and his line is that nobody who understood the neuroscience would still find zombies conceivable; the argument is just an intuition pump, and saying consciousness is a 'user illusion' is changing the subject rather than answering whether the zombie is possible.

The strongest physicalist reply is the one about a posteriori necessity. We can conceive of water that isn't H2O, but water is necessarily H2O, so conceivability clearly doesn't entail possibility. Kripke made the same point with pain and C-fibers, showing that mind-brain identities can at best be contingent; so the physicalist can grant the zombie's conceivability and still deny that it's possible, which kills premise (2) and with it the argument.

Verdict: powerful intuition pump, not a proof. It only works if you accept that conceivability entails possibility, and that's exactly the premise in dispute."
