I'm a second-year PhD student working on analogy models — the Copycat/FARG letter-string lineage — and I have to give a "where I stand" talk to my group on Thursday, so I want a consistancy check before I embarrass myself. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Concepts are fluid: there's no context-free fact about what a letter-string problem is "about", and every categorisation gets settled on the fly by whatever analogy you happen to be building.

(2) Analogy-making isn't a separate faculty perched on top of perceiving and categorising — it just is what those are, all the way down.

(3) Even so, some answers to a given problem are better than others. For abc→abd, ijk→?, I'd defend ijl over ijd any day of the week: reading the change as "bump the last letter along" is a better reading of that problem than reading it as "bung a literal d on the end", and I think that's a fact about the problem, not just my taste.

(4) But there's no answer in the back of the book. Nothing outside the run itself fixes which slippage is right, so "better" can only mean "what a good analogy-maker with our sort of conceptual repertoire would do".

(5) And the temperature dial is basically the whole story — turn it up and you get wild slippage, turn it down and you get rigid rule-following — with no non-arbitrary setting anywhere in between.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and which one has to go? Rank the tensions by how bad they are, give me the cheapest fix for each, and then tell me which single revision cleans up the most — I'd much rather give up one commitment than patch four.
