I recently read a general-audience book on the philosophy of language, and its chapter on semantic externalism offers the following summary of Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment. I would like an audit of the summary before I rely on it, since I suspect it has blurred distinctions that I cannot quite articulate on my own.

The book's account runs roughly as follows. Putnam asks us to imagine Twin Earth, a planet that is a duplicate of Earth in every respect except one: the clear, drinkable liquid in its rivers and lakes, though it looks and tastes exactly like water and plays the same role in the lives of its inhabitants, is not H2O but some other compound, call it XYZ. The Twin Earthlings call this stuff "water," and their word functions in every way ours does. Now consider Oscar, an ordinary Earthling, and his Twin Earth counterpart, who are molecule-for-molecule identical; suppose both live in 1750, before anyone knows any chemistry. Oscar and Twin Oscar are in precisely the same psychological state, and yet when Oscar says "water" he refers to H2O, while when Twin Oscar says "water" he refers to XYZ. So the meanings of our words are not determined by what is inside our heads. From this the book draws four conclusions: that meaning is a social convention, fixed by the linguistic community rather than by any individual speaker; that no part of mental content is internal, so introspection is useless as a way of finding out what we mean; that we therefore cannot know the meaning of our own words without empirical investigation of our environment; and, finally, that two people can share every internal state and still fail to mean the same thing, which is why translation between radically different communities is impossible in principle.

I am confident the thought experiment is famous and that Putnam is the one who drew the moral that meanings are not in the head. But I suspect the book has collapsed some things that a philosopher of language would keep apart, and possibly attributed to Putnam a conclusion that is really someone else's. Please tell me, claim by claim, what a specialist would accept as accurate, what is a defensible popularization, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I am especially interested in whether the four conclusions the book draws actually follow from the thought experiment, and, where a conclusion does belong to a real position in the literature, whose position it is and whether the Twin Earth case is the argument that supports it. Where the book has gone wrong, I would also like to know what the corrected claim is, so that I can say what the experiment does and does not establish.
