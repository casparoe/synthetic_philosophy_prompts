I am a doctoral student putting together a prospectus chapter on privacy, and I have noticed that my whole method of evaluating accounts has been intuition-by-intuition: someone offers a control-based account, I test it against one case; someone offers an access-based account, I test it against another; nothing accumulates, because I have never written down what the accounts are supposed to deliver. Before I read any more of them, I want the specification on paper. What must a theory of privacy — a theory of what privacy is and of why, and whether, it matters — be able to do in order to count as adequate for the purposes of an applied-ethics seminar and a policy-adjacent readership? I am not asking for a survey of positions, a bibliography, or citations; I want the requirements list, each requirement stated as a requirement and each accompanied by the case or consideration that motivates it, together with your judgment about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really preferences of a particular camp in disguise.

Please build the list around the following, adding whatever I have missed. First, the cases the theory has to get right, including several I have been unable to resolve: a person watched through a window while doing nothing of the slightest interest, who is seen to be doing nothing of interest; a firm that logs everything about me but never has a human read the logs, using them only to improve a model that never outputs anything about me (call this the unread-ledger case); two neighbors who install cameras aimed reciprocally at each other's windows, so that each knows nearly everything about the other and neither is embarrassed by it; a fact that is about me and also about my sister — a genetic result, a pregnancy — where the question is whose privacy is even at stake, and whether the theory can locate the right-holder at all; information that was public when first posted, where the wrong, if there is one, lies in its resurfacing years later; and the familiar retort that only people with something to hide object to surveillance, which I cannot tell whether a theory should answer or dissolve. Second, the distinctions the theory must not collapse: privacy as against secrecy; a loss of privacy as against a violation of it; privacy as a state of affairs, a right, a claim, and a good; informational privacy as against physical, associational, and decisional privacy; the instrumental as against the non-instrumental value of privacy; and the privacy of an individual as against that of a group, a relationship, or a pair of people. Third, the considerations it must be consistent with, which I suspect do more constraining work than the cases do: that many people say they value privacy and behave as though they do not; that some losses of privacy are welcome, since intimacy and care consist partly in being known; that privacy admits of degrees without more always being better; that a surveillance arrangement can be fully disclosed and consented to and still be objectionable; and that some complaints we file under privacy look, on inspection, to be complaints about power or standing rather than about information. Fourth, an operational constraint: the account must yield something usable in cases like a care home installing cameras in residents' rooms or an insurer requesting wearable data, without collapsing into the claim that it all depends on local norms, and without reducing to the claim that whatever people report being upset by is thereby a violation.

Finally, I want your view on the shape of the list itself, because I suspect that is where the real result is. If some subset of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say which subset and why, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive accounts — I would rather learn that one requirement is doing all the excluding than receive a tidy list of ten. Flag any requirement you think I have included only because it is a prejudice of my own training, and note any place where a requirement is quietly presupposing a political or cultural commitment that ought to be argued for rather than assumed. I do not need the contenders scored against the finished list; I will do that part myself.
