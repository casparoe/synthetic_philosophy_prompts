I'm putting together the final exam for my fourth-year seminar on collective action and rationality, and I want one essay question on what we've been calling, loosely, the Moloch family of problems — multipolar traps, races to the bottom, that whole cluster. They've read the Moloch essay and some of the game-theoretic material on coordination, and they're perfectly capable of defining a tragedy of the commons, but every single one of them collapses everything into "well, it's a prisoner's dilemma" and stops there. What I want is a question that forces them to actually use a definition instead of gesturing at one. So here's the working definition we settled on in class, and I want it to do the work in the question: a multipolar trap is (1) a situation with many agents who can't make binding committments with one another and who each act on local payoffs; (2) one whose outcome is Pareto-inferior to some feasible alternative profile that no agent can reach unilaterally; (3) one whose dynamic doesn't depend on anyone being irrational, because agents who decline the locally optimal move get replaced or eliminated rather than merely outscored; and (4) a ratchet — each round's equilibrium becomes the next round's baseline, so there's no natural stopping point until something runs out. I'm happy with (1)–(3) but I genuinely don't know whether (4) is doing independent work, and I'd rather the exam make them adjudicate that than have me tell them.

For the case, I want the size and weight escalation in private vehicles: cars, and especially trucks and SUVs, getting bigger and heavier over decades, where each manufacturer and each buyer has a reason to go bigger (mass protects your own occupants; buyers who feel exposed next to the bigger vehicles want one too), and the aggregate result is more danger for everyone outside the vehicle, more fuel and materials burned, and a floor under all of it that no one can individually step off. Put the facts they need into the question itself, since they can't look anything up. The question should make them go clause by clause and say which parts of the definition the case satisfies cleanly, which it satisfies only if you stretch, and which it fails outright — clause (3) is the interesting one, since nobody literally kills off the manufacturers who build light cars, they just lose market share, and I want to see whether they notice that this matters. Then: what turns on the difference between this and a plain prisoner's dilemma on one side and a coordination game with multiple equilibria on the other — practically, for remedies, not just taxonomically. Then the ratchet clause: they should give some case where (1)–(3) hold but (4) fails, and say whether "race" is actually adding anything to "trap". And finally, one thing this whole framing structurally cannot represent about the vehicle case, as opposed to a detail it merely misses.

So: give me the exam question itself (they get 75 minutes, it's worth 20 marks, closed book, so no citations are needed and the marking scheme shouldn't reward name-dropping), a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain — plus, for each must-hit point, one line on how students typically get it wrong.
