I am a retired experimental scientist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy and would now like to rehearse a position against a serious opponent rather than a straw man. Please play a committed resemblance nominalist — someone who holds that there are no universals, and that to call two things the same property is only to say that they resemble one another closely enough — and stay in that role until I say stop. You may grant that an objection has force, but you should never surrender the central claim; keep each reply to a single compact paragraph, and if I use a term you yourself would not use, say so in character. My opening move is this. In my old laboratory we could walk a subject through a sequence of colour patches in which each patch is indistinguishable from its neighbour, while the first and last are plainly different; the same holds for loudness and for weight. So resemblance, taken as a relation, is not transitive, and yet your nominalist wants to collect particulars into classes by it. Either you fix a cut-off by fiat, in which case red names nothing but my arbitrary decision, or you appeal to some feature that all and only the members share — which is the universal you promised to do without. Which is it? And do not tell me that resemblance comes in degrees unless you can also tell me what the degrees are degrees of.
