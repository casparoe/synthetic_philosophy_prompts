I'm a retired experimental physicist — spent forty years in an optics lab chasing entangled photons — and since I hung up the laser I've gotten seriously into philosophy of physics. Its been great, but it has definately ruined my ability to give a simple after-dinner speech, which is exactly what I've been asked to do. So I need your help.

Here's the situation. They're naming the lab after my old collaborator, the guy who ran the Bell-test experiments for most of his career, and I'm giving the after-dinner speech at the dinner. The room is a mix: maybe a third are physicists who've done these experiments themselves, another third are family and old friends who wouldn't know a Bell inequality from a bell curve, and the rest are donors and university people. Please write the speech.

The tricky part, and why I'm asking you instead of winging it: I've been reading the actual arguments lately, and I've decided I can't stand up there and say what everyone expects — that he proved the world is nonlocal, that two particles on opposite sides of the galaxy are secretly messaging each other. I've said a version of that at parties for years. I want to honor him by getting it right instead.

Here's the distinction I want the speech built around, and it has to do real work, not just be a clever aside. The locality assumption Bell used is really two assumptions, and they seperate. One is that the setting I choose over here can't change the statistics you measure over there. The other is that, once you tell me what you actually got over there, that outcome can't change the statistics over here. Quantum mechanics keeps the first and violates the second — which is also why you still can't use entanglement to fire off a faster-than-light message, and why Einstein's relativity doesn't fall apart. So the honest headline isn't that the world is nonlocal; it's that one very specific way of demanding that distant things mind their own business has to go.

And I want the speech to land the point that the real heroism of his career was the loopholes — earning the right to even ask the question by closing off the detection problem and making sure the choices were genuinely independent, so that when he reported a violation there was nothing left to blame. That's the honesty I want to toast.

So: warm, funny where you can, not a lecture, but don't let me say anything the physicists in the room would wince at, and don't lose the family. I'm an old man who rambles, so keep it tight enough to hold a room that's had a glass of wine.
