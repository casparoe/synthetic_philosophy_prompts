I've been sitting in on an elective in the history and philosophy of science this term, and we've just reached Lakatos. The set reading is heavy going, so most of my cohort has been getting by on a well-regarded popular book on the subject, and I've been doing the same. Its chapter on research programmes runs more or less as follows. Lakatos is presented as splitting the difference between Popper and Kuhn: in place of the single bold conjecture or the paradigm, we get the research programme, a long-lived structure with a hard core of central commitments ringed by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. The hard core is unfalsifiable — that, the book says, is precisely what makes it a core — while the belt absorbs the shocks, its hypotheses being adjusted, re-adjusted, and eventually dropped as the programme proceeds. What separates a thriving programme from a stagnant one is the heuristic: a strong positive heuristic tells its practitioners which problems to attack next and how to extend the belt, while the negative heuristic warns them not to aim modus tollens at the core. A programme is progressive, on this telling, for as long as it keeps accommodating the anomalies thrown at it; it turns degenerating once the adjustments stop being fruitful and it merely explains after the fact what a rival had predicted in advance. From there the chapter draws what it takes to be Lakatos's central lesson — that once a programme has gone degenerating and a rival is plainly progressive, the rational scientist must jump ship, since Lakatos's whole purpose was to supply the decision rule that Kuhn's sociology had left out — and it closes with an epilogue claiming that Lakatos, having conceded that partisans of rival programmes can defend their cores indefinitely, came round to something very near Feyerabend's position, on which the choice between them reflects nothing but history, training, and temperament.

I don't know the primary literature well enough to argue with any of this, and my instinct — that the chapter is right about the shape of the thing and wrong about its temper — is not one I could defend in a seminar. So what I want is an audit of the summary rather than a general essay on Lakatos. Go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a specialist would accept as fair, which are defensible simplifications, which are things Lakatos himself explicitly denied, and whether any of it quietly hands to Lakatos a position that belongs to one of his contemporaries. Where the summary is accurate I'd like you to say so plainly rather than hedging, and where it goes wrong, tell me what someone sympathetic to Lakatos would put in its place. I'd also like to know which of the missteps actually matter for the use one would make of the framework — say, in assessing a live scientific dispute where a programme looks to be running down — and which are merely matters of emphasis. I'm not after a reading list or a set of references; I'd rather have your judgement, delivered as a judgement. Please write in British English.
