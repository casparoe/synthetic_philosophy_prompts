I am assembling a unit on the principle of alternative possibilities — the thesis that an agent is morally responsible for what he does only if he could have done otherwise — and before I fix the order of the readings I want the candidate counterexamples ranked by how much pressure each puts on the principle. Every case below is my own construction; none is drawn from the literature. Unless a case indicates otherwise, the agent acts on his own and for his own reasons, the backup mechanism never operates, and the action is a serious betrayal of a friend, so the only question in each case is whether the agent is blameworthy for it.

1. A chip implanted in Jones will fire and produce the betrayal if and only if Jones's own decision fails to produce it; the chip reads the decision at the instant it forms, so nothing earlier than the decision triggers it. Jones decides on his own, and the chip stays dormant.

2. A chip will fire if Jones shows any involuntary sign of wavering in the minute before he acts — a blush, a catch in his speech. Jones shows no such sign, decides on his own, and the chip stays dormant.

3. There is no chip, but a colleague stands ready: had Jones wavered, the colleague would have given him an argument he would have found decisive and acted on. Jones does not waver, and he acts for his own reasons.

4. There is no backup at all. Jones was taken as an infant and subjected to a process that causally determined, decades later, the very decision he now makes. The decision is simply the outcome of that process.

5. The chip is wired to an indeterministic quantum event: if the event occurs, with probability one half, the chip forces the betrayal, and if it does not occur, Jones acts as he pleases. The event does not occur, and Jones acts on his own.

6. The betrayal takes the form of an omission. Jones, a lifeguard, fails to move toward a swimmer he could easily have saved. A device would have paralyzed him had he begun to move; it never operates.

7. Jones knows about the chip and knows that he will betray his friend no matter what he decides. He decides on his own, and the chip stays dormant.

Sort the seven into tiers, from those that should most trouble a defender of the principle to those that should least (ties are fine), where a case is strong to the extent that both of two judgments are firm: that the agent is nonetheless blameworthy, and that the agent genuinely could not have done otherwise. For each case, name the single feature that fixes its place — a residual alternative possibility of even a minimal kind, an indeterministic gap in the backup, a responsibility judgment many will reject, the agent's knowledge that resistance is futile, or the gap between acting and omitting — and state in a sentence where a defender of the principle has an obvious reply. Close with a short assessment of whether the ranking as a whole shows that a principle of this kind can be refuted by cases of this form at all, or only by cases engineered to leave no minimal alternative standing, and why.
