So my reading group has been chewing on Dennett's idea of a "free-floating rationale" — the thought that there can be a reason for something even when no reasoner has it, like the reason a beaver builds its dam (deep water keeps the lodge entrance submerged) even though the beaver isn't thinking any of that. I worte a short position piece for us and I want you to tear into it. Here it is:

---
The phrase "free-floating rationale" is doing two jobs and only one of them is legitimate. Call a rationale vacant when there's a proposition that would rationalize the behavior, a mind could in principle hold it, but no mind does — the beaver case, if we insist the beaver has no belief about safety, though a belief about safety is the sort of thing a mind could have. Call a rationale uninhabitable when the "reason" can only be stated in terms of selection, fitness, or population-level consequences — "the dam is built for the sake of inclusive fitness," or "the receiver trusts the signal because cheap fakes get selected against." No mind could hold that as a consideration for acting, because it's a summary of a selection process, not something that could guide anyone. My claim is that vacant rationales are reasons and uninhabitable ones are metaphors. "Free-floating rationale" sounds like it names a third category — reasons without reasoners — but it doesn't; it names a continuum running from ordinary unspoken reasons (vacant) to a purposive way of talking about selection (uninhabitable), and the far end shouldn't be reified.

The objection I can't get past: the whole selling point of the concept is that we can explain and predict behavior without knowing anything about the creature's psychology. But if I have to check whether a given rationale is vacant or uninhabitable, I've lost that. And the "as if" predictions (the organism behaves as if it's maximizing something) are pretty reliable — doesn't that show the uninhabitable ones are doing real explanatory work, not just decorating a causal story with intention-talk?
---
