A friend and I were reading Berkeley's Querist (1735) for a seminar on money, and we came out with an argument I can't get out of, even though the conclusion is obviously insane. I want to know which step to cut.

(1) Money has no value in itself. Berkeley asks whether the true idea of money "be not altogether that of a ticket or counter," and whether gold, silver, and paper aren't all just "tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring" value.
(2) What money actually does is "stirreth up industry," "enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour." The real source of wealth is "the four elements, and man's labour therein."
(3) So the material doesn't matter and neither does the amount: if "the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone," things "might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained."
(4) He even says less money swiftly circulating is equivalent to more money slowly circulating, so it's circulation that matters, not the number of tickets. Quantity drops out of the picture.
(5) But if money's whole function is to excite industry, and the state can issue tickets at will, then issuing more tickets excites more industry.
(6) So a state can enrich itself by printing tickets without limit: more counters, more wealth.
(7) That's false. Every hyperinflation we've heard of shows the reverse — past some point extra tickets just make each ticket worthless and wreck the industry they were supposed to excite.

Where does this break? I'd rather not give up (2), since I think Berkeley is basically right that wealth is produce and not metal, and I don't want to say money is valuable in itself, because it obvioulsy isn't. So which step do I sacrafice, and what does giving it up cost me elsewhere? If Berkeley's own text already blocks this somewhere, tell me where.
