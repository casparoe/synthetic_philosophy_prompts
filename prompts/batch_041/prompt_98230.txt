In my legislation seminar we have spent the past three weeks on the opposition between purposivism and the new textualism, and I have gotten into a running argument with a classmate that I would like you to referee — not by summarizing the two schools, which I already have from the readings, but by working them through a single case. The case is Bostock v. Clayton County, the 2020 Supreme Court decision holding that an employer who fires an employee for being gay or transgender violates Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the provision that forbids employment discrimination because of an individual's sex, alongside race, color, religion, and national origin. The decision was six to three; the majority opinion was written by Justice Gorsuch, who is ordinarily identified with textualism, and the two dissenting opinions — by Justices Alito and Kavanaugh — argued both that the statute as written does not reach sexual orientation or gender identity and that the majority had effectively smuggled in a purposive reading under a textualist label. You may take those facts as given and need not cite anything.

What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions. First, theory onto case: state as precisely as you can what a committed textualist and a committed purposivist would each predict about this statute, what the relevant text and the relevant purpose actually are here, and which of the three opinions best instantiates each theory. Be careful about the temptation to call the majority textualist simply because it says so; tell me what work the text is doing and where the majority's central move — treating discrimination against a gay or transgender employee as necessarily differential treatment by sex — depends on a premise that is not itself in the statute.

Second, case onto theory. I want to know whether Bostock is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theories never anticipated. My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than accept, is that the episode shows the two theories to be less rivals than they appear, because both are doing the same thing — allocating interpretive discretion — and disagreeing mainly about who should hold it. To give the analysis some bite, add a counterfactual: suppose the statutory language were exactly as it is, but the legislative record contained an unambiguous statement that the enacting Congress did not intend to cover sexual orientation or gender identity. Which theory changes its answer, which does not, and what does that reveal about whether textualism is really a constraint on judges or instead a rule about which materials count as reasons? Finish by saying where each theory is simply silent about this case, rather than forcing it to speak.
