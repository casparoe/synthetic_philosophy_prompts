Write a piece of sustained deadpan satire: a company handbook, roughly 700–900 words, from an invented firm whose official management philosophy is Sartre's account of freedom. It is posted on the intranet, and new hires must sign it. The target is the practice of institutionalizing authenticity — the requirement that employees bring their authentic selves to work, and more generally any rule that instructs a person to be what they already are. Write from inside that practice, in the voice of the people who drafted the document, with no narrator's wink and no authorial commentary.

What makes this funny is the structure of bad faith, so here is the material I want the piece built on. Get it right rather than name-dropping it.

- Bad faith, in Sartre's sense, is not ordinary lying or a factual mistake. It is a flight from the anguish of freedom, and it is unstable by design: it must use the freedom it denies, so it cannot hold still. It slides between treating a human being as a thing with a fixed nature and treating them as pure open possibility, and it needs both faces.
- Demanding sincerity can itself be bad faith. The person who insists that someone else coincide with what they are — be what they are, once and for all — is not thereby on the side of the angels; Sartre's illustration of the friend who champions frankness is meant to show exactly that.
- A cousin of bad faith is the attitude that treats values as ready-made objects lying about in the world, to be discovered and conformed to, rather than as something a free consciousness has to sustain by choosing it.
- Anguish is a recognition rather than a mood: nothing prevents me from doing otherwise, and my resolutions do not carry themselves forward. The gambler who has sworn off the table must remake his resolution when he is standing in front of it; remembering it is not enough.

Mechanics I want: numbered clauses with headings, a definitional preamble, and at least one clause whose footnote contradicts the clause it annotates. At least one clause should be a recognizable transposition of one of Sartre's own illustrations — a waiter whose manner is a little too waiterly, or a hand left lying in someone else's as though it were a thing — without the document ever indicating that it is an illustration. The handbook must never explain its philosophy: no clause may name Sartre, existentialism, or bad faith as such, so the ideas have to survive translation into HR dialect and be recognizable through the euphemism. Each clause taken alone should be plausible enough that a real personnel department could have written it; it is the accumulation, not any single absurdity, that produces the impossibility. Close with a clause, or a short appendix of three or four FAQ entries, that tries to settle whether employees are responsible for who they are and fails in the specific way this philosophy predicts, rather than merely trailing off. A reader who knows the bad-faith chapter should find the piece accurate and fair — exaggerate what is really there, invent nothing — and a reader who has never opened Sartre should still find it funny.

After the handbook, add a short note, clearly marked as outside the fiction and no longer than 150 words, telling me where the piece takes liberties or leans on a contested reading: for instance, if it assumes that everyone is always already in bad faith, or that authenticity amounts to assuming one's freedom, say so and say how contested that is. If I have mis-stated any of the material above, correct me there rather than quietly writing around it. And if you think the brief itself is misconceived — if a compliance document is the wrong vehicle because bad faith is too first-personal to be legislated, or if this can only come out as generic office satire — say so in two or three sentences at the top, then either write the piece as well as it can be written or propose the vehicle you would use instead; do not pad the handbook to disguise the problem.
