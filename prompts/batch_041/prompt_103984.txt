Two friends of mine — both serious readers of philosophy, neither of them a professional — spent an evening arguing over a thought experiment of their own construction, and they have asked me to referee. I followed the whole exchange and could not decide it, so I am handing it to you. I will first state the experiment, then reproduce their moves in the order they made them.

The experiment. Suppose that exactly one of two hypotheses is true, and that, before learning anything else, you would give each a prior probability of one half. Under hypothesis Small, the total number of conscious observers who ever exist is ten billion. Under hypothesis Large, the total is ten sextillion — that is, a trillion times as many. Nothing in your experience discriminates between the hypotheses, and you have no empirical evidence bearing on which is true. You then take note of a fact about yourself: that you exist. The question is what that fact should do to your credence in Large, and whether the reasoning that answers the question is sound.

Move 1 (Dana). It should move you a great deal, and toward Large. A world containing ten sextillion observers offers ten sextillion places for you to have turned up; a world containing ten billion offers ten billion. If you treat your own existence as something that had to land on one observer rather than another, then landing in the crowded world is a trillion times more likely. So you should end up almost certain that Large is true — and, note, you have extracted this from no evidence at all, purely from the fact that you are here to ask the question. This is what Dana called the self-indication idea.

Move 2 (Marcus). That cannot be right, because it would let the size of the world be settled by a philosopher's imagination. If a hypothesis can raise your credence merely by containing more observers, then I invent Large-plus, with ten sextillion sextillion observers, and by your rule I should believe it over Large almost certainly; then I invent Large-plus-plus and believe that; and so on without end. Any number I can write down, I am entitled to believe the world contains at least that many observers. A principle that lets you reach whichever conclusion you prefer by dreaming up a larger number is not reasoning; it is wishful thinking dressed as arithmetic.

Move 3 (Dana). You are attacking the conclusion rather than the argument. That an answer feels absurd is not a premise in anything. If the probability calculus delivers the verdict, then either identify the invalid step or accept the verdict; many true claims are counterintuitive. And your regress does not land: I am not inventing new hypotheses, I am conditioning on the two we granted, each with prior one half. Show me where conditioning on what we both accept goes wrong.

Move 4 (Marcus). The invalid step is the first one. 'I exist' is certain under both hypotheses: each already says that observers exist and that you are one of them, so you have not learned a fact that could have gone otherwise. And if you insist that what matters is the chance that you in particular should be the one doing the reasoning, then notice what that requires — a pool of candidate selves, sized by the hypothesis, from which you were drawn before you existed. Neither hypothesis contains any such pool. A world is not more likely to contain me merely because it contains more other people. What you have done is import an extra, undefended premise and let it do all the work. Marcus said we might as well call this the self-sampling idea.

Move 5 (Dana). You keep saying that 'I exist' is certain under both hypotheses, but that is the very point in dispute. Of course if you help yourself to the claim that my existence was guaranteed, you get your answer; that claim is what a self-sampling view has to argue for, and you have asserted it rather than argued it. I say the size of the pool matters; you say it does not; and we will not settle it by each of us declaring his own rule to be the obvious one. At least my rule explains why I am here at all.

That was where they stopped.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, unsound, or sound but incomplete, and give your reasons; where a move contains several distinct claims, separate them and rule on each. Then say precisely where the real disagreement lies — in particular, whether Marcus's fourth move genuinely refutes Dana or merely assumes the point at issue, and whether Dana's fifth move is a fair reply or itself question-begging. Finally, say what, if anything, both of them are failing to see. I would prefer a verdict on each step to an overall winner, though if one of them is more nearly right overall, say so and say why.
