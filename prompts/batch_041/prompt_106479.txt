I'm putting together a short research proposal for a philosophy-of-language / political-thought workshop — they've asked for no more than about 1500 words, so this is a two-pager, not a whole thesis plan. I've got a half-formed idea and I'd like you to turn it into something that actually holds together.

The idea is this. People keep saying that when an American calls someone a liberal and a European calls someone a liberal, they may not be disagreeing so much as talking past each other — the American sense runs roughly towards social-democratic, welfare-state politics, the European one towards free-market, small-state politics, so the word looks like a false friend across the Atlantic. My suspicion is that the interesting question isn't which sense is correct but what kind of difference this is. Is liberal one word with two related senses, two words that happen to be spelt the same, or one word whose meaning has genuinely drifted in one dialect relative to the other? And does the answer make a difference to whether the disputes we call political are substantive or partly verbal?

What I want to do is take the ordinary tests that lexical semanticists use to separate polysemy from homonymy — zeugma, contradiction, conjunction-reduction, the has-two-meanings test — and run them on liberal in its two dialectal uses, then see whether the tests actually deliver a verdict or whether this is a case where they pull apart and we have to say something more interesting about what's going on. I'd also like to ask whether the divergence is directional: whether one dialect's sense is plausibly the older or unmarked one and the other a shift away from it, and whether any of this survives when we look at the neighbouring terms (progressive, libertarian, social democrat) rather than the one word in isolation. I'm not sure yet whether the deliverable is a verdict or a demonstration that no verdict of that shape is available, and I'd like the proposal to leave room for both.
