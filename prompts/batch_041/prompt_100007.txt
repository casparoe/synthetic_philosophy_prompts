I teach a 200-level seminar called Law, Paradox, and Play -- 14 students, mostly philosophy and pre-law, one 90-minute block, movable chairs, a whiteboard, index cards, and no laptops. I want to run a game that makes them feel the vertigo of self-amending rules and legal self-reference, not just hear about it. I know the basic Nomic idea -- a game whose first rule lets players change the rules -- but I don't want a straight port of Nomic; it's too sprawling for 90 minutes and half the session would disappear into procedural bookkeeping. Design a stripped-down, runnable variant for me: a starting rule set short enough to fit on one side of a page, roles or turn order, a scoring/payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, and a script for the first ten minutes so I don't over-explain before they start playing. I'd like the rules to include a few loaded pieces: an entrenched rule that can only be amended by a supermajority; a rule that lets a player suspend another rule for one round; a rule about retroactivity; and something that makes the validity of a move depend on the validity of the rule that authorized it. Arrange things so that within the first half-hour some predictable crisis lands -- someone tries to amend the amendment procedure itself, or a move is challenged because the rule that permitted it was repealed after the fact, or the group discovers that the rulebook can't validate its own foundations. The point is for them to run into the problem in the doing, before I name it.

Then give me the debrief: a timeline of moments to watch for, how to map what happened onto the ideas I care about -- validity vs. efficacy, the difference between repealing a rule and violating it, retroactivity, entrenchment, the bootstrap problem of a constitution that authorizes itself, and the specifically legal version of self-reference where a rule tries to bind its own amendment -- and a set of questions that gets them to articulate which move was actually legal and why the answer may not be determinate. I also want your honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time: where they'll get confused, where I'll be tempted to referee too much, which student will try to exploit a loophole and whether that's a feature or a bug, and how to keep the game from collapsing into pure chaos or pure procedure. Finally, tell me what this activity can't reproduce -- which part of the Nomic/self-reference puzzle has to be supplied by me in the debrief because no 90-minute game can stage it. Don't give me readings or citations; I need the activity, the handout text, and the facilitation notes. I've been reading alot about this stuff and I keep getting tangled up, so be concrete.
