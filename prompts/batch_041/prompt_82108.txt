ok so I'm midway through a chapter on the aggregation problem for contractualism and I've hit the point where I need someone to actually argue with instead of re-reading the same three objections. So: please play a committed contractualist of the Scanlonian kind. Someone who genuinely holds that the individualist restriction isn't a technicality you drop once the numbers get big, who knows the standard objections and can meet them, who will cheerfully concede the view has ugly consequences and still refuse to move the core idea — that the test is whether a principle could be justified to each person who has to live under it. Argue like a philosopher making moves, not like a bibliography: no "as X argues in chapter three", I don't need citations or reading lists, I need you to make the moves yourself. Concede small points, never the main one, stay in character until I say stop, and if you need something from me ask for it in character. A paragraph or two per reply so it feels like a conversation.

Here's my opening. Take a fixed health budget and put a dial on it. At one setting you spend the whole thing on one patient and save her life — she's got something treatable, but only at ruinous cost. At the other setting the same money funds a cheap intervention for N people, each of whom avoids a moderate, permanent, non-life-threatening condition: painful, isolating, the kind of thing that makes a life go worse without ending it. Now turn the dial. At N=10 I think even you say save her. At N=1000 I'd definately bet most people flip, and I suspect you start hedging. At N=a million its not close. So somewhere on that dial your view changes its verdict. Fine — then say where, and say what's doing the work at that point. If the answer is that the many complaints eventually become "strong" or "urgent" enough in aggregate, you've got aggregation, you've just relabelled it. If the answer is the tie-breaker — that numbers can decide once the complaints are of the same order of magnitude — then tell me what same order means here without smuggling in a threshold on welfare, because a welfare threshold is exactly the aggregative machinery you said you weren't running. And if the answer is that the dial never flips, however large N gets, then own it: say that a million people each facing a real, permanent, avoidable harm have no complaint that outweighs one person's death, and defend that to the million. Which is it? If you think my case is rigged, say how and fix it — but don't dodge the dial.
