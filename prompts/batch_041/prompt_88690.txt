I write novels, and a view I have come to hold about my characters has quietly become a view about people. Call it the idealization thesis: what a person genuinely wants is not what they avow or pursue, but what they would want if they were fully informed, vividly acquainted with the alternatives, and internally coherent in their ends. I use it constantly. It is what lets me say of a character that she is mistaken about her own desire — that the marriage she defends is not what she wants, that the vocation she has served for thirty years answers to a need she would disown if she could see it clearly. I find I cannot write without something like it, and I suspect that is a reason to distrust it rather than a reason to keep it.

What I want from you is a map of its exposure. What would count against the idealization thesis — observations, arguments, formal results, or plain counterexamples — and how decisive would each be? I am less interested in the familiar charge that people are frequently irrational than in the structural difficulties. Is the idealized limit well defined at all? Is it unique, or do many maximally coherent completions of a person's ends satisfy the description equally well? Does the limit depend on the order in which one idealizes — information first and then coherence, or the reverse? Is there a fixed point, an idealized agent whose preferences are themselves stable under further idealization? And what happens when the extrapolation is pushed all the way: does anything determinate survive, or does determinacy survive only by retaining the contingent psychology the idealization was meant to filter out?

Then the concessive part. Which of these defeaters would a committed defender of the thesis already grant — which are priced in rather than fatal — and which would the defender have to treat as a reframing of the thesis rather than a refutation of it? I would like the list ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I would rather the awkward ones were kept in, since I suspect the interesting ones live there.

Finally, a question I care about more than is reasonable. Much of what I take to be a person — the unidealized, incoherent, self-defeating attachment — is precisely what the thesis assigns to error. If that is right, then the drama I have spent my life on is a record of mistakes, and the only fully real desires belong to a version of each person who never appears in any of my books. Tell me honestly whether the thesis must say that, or whether a defender can hold it while still granting that unidealized wanting is where the value is. And if the thesis has been qualified until it can say both, say so plainly, and tell me whether that qualification is a repair or a retreat.
