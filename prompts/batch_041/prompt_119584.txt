ok so this is going to sound like a weird ask but i think you'll get what i'm after. i'm a third-year phil student, i'm in a metaphysics seminar on causation this term, and we just finished the unit on omissions and i literally cannot stop thinking about it. my friend danny was in that seminar with me for three weeks last year before he dropped it, and he used to argue with me the whole walk home that omissions can't be causes — nothing happened, so nothing caused anything, that was his entire position. he died in march. fire in the basement workshop of the bike co-op we both volunteered at. nobody set it. old wiring, i think, the report isn't out. but the fire door at the bottom of the stairwell was wedged open with a block of wood, that wedge had been there about two years and everyone knew, and it got written up in the october safety walkthrough as a fix that ended up queued behind the budget for new work stands. and the smoke alarm in the workshop had been disconnected because it kept going off when people soldered. so: nothing happened. nobody did anything. he was down there alone on a tuesday. that's the whole story and it's also not.

i have to give remarks at the memorial on the 12th, in the co-op courtyard. the room is: his parents and his little sister, who aren't academic at all and are furious (his mum has said some things to the board); a dozen volunteers who are wrecked with guilt; the shop manager who signed off the october walkthrough; and maybe eight people from the seminar plus our professor, because danny was well liked in the department even after he dropped the class. so every sentence i say about responsibilty is going to be heard by someone as either an accusation or an exoneration, and i need to do neither — not morally, and definately not in a way that sounds like a legal finding before the inquest. and i can't just say it was an accident, and i can't say somebody killed him. the omission stuff is honestly the only frame that lets me talk about this at all.

can you write the speech? four or five minutes. the conceptual material needs to be doing real work in it, not name-dropped — his parents don't know what a counterfactual is and i am not giving a lecture at a memorial — but i also can't stand up there and smooth it over, because the thing that's eating me is exactly the thing that's hard to say plainly. the bits from class that seem relevant: the but-for test and the ways it breaks (two people both not doing it, and neither one counts), the idea that leaving something undone only belongs to you if it was yours to do, and the whole question of whether singling one thing out as 'the cause' from a hundred conditions is a fact about the world or about us. use whichever of those earns its place. tell me where the speech has to go quiet instead, where the honest answer is that we don't know yet or that it's for the inquest. don't tie it up neatly, he would have hated that, and don't put words in his mouth — i can only say what he actually argued at me. oh, and his family's church does the he's-watching-over-us thing, i know he didn't believe it, but the 12th isn't the day for that fight, so if you can neither affirm it nor slap it down, that would be a gift.
