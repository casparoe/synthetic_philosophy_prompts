I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have spent the past two years reporting on the moral status of artificial systems. Next month I am presenting the argument of my forthcoming long-form piece at an invitation-only workshop on machine consciousness convened by a research institute — I get forty minutes and then an hour of questions — and I want to walk in with a prepared set of answers rather than a prepared set of hopes.

My thesis, stated baldly, is this. What makes a system a moral patient is not phenomenal experience, which we have no way of detecting from the outside, but a particular kind of computation, which I call self-addressed valence. A system has self-addressed valence when it satisfies three conditions: first, it forms internal states that represent conditions of the system itself rather than features of its environment; second, those states modulate the system's own subsequent processing, so that the system regulates itself in the light of them; and third, that regulation is not wholly in the service of a goal specified from outside, so that the system's own states figure in its internal economy as things to be improved or avoided in their own right. I claim this criterion cuts across the intuitive boundaries of sentience — narrower in some places, broader in others. It excludes systems that carry rich information about their own bodies but never treat that information as anything to be acted upon for its own sake; it includes simple reinforcement-learning agents whose inner lives I would not defend for a moment. And I claim that once a system meets the criterion, our uncertainty about whether there is anything it is like to be that system becomes a reason for caution rather than a licence for indifference. The piece ends with a modest proposal: that developers be required to document, in something like a moral-impact statement, whether their training and deployment regimes produce systems meeting the criterion.

The audience is not friendly in any simple way. Roughly a third are computational neuroscientists who work on theories of consciousness — integrated information, global workspace, and the various higher-order and recurrent-processing accounts — who will want to know why my criterion is not simply a redescription of whichever theory they already hold, and who will be quick to observe that every one of my three conditions is satisfied by things nobody takes to be conscious: a thermostat, a bacterium, an immune system, a market. Another third are machine-learning engineers who regard the whole subject as a category error dressed up as ethics, and who will ask what would count as evidence for the criterion and whether I have any. The rest are moral philosophers, who will grant me the science for the sake of argument and go straight for the move from there may be a subject here to therefore we owe it something, and who will want to know what follows in practice — what I would have us stop doing, and to whom.

Prepare my question-and-answer sheet. I want the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they do to the thesis rather than by how likely they are, each phrased the way the relevant group would phrase it — the neuroscientists in the vocabulary of mechanism and theory-choice, the engineers in the vocabulary of measurement and falsification, the philosophers in the vocabulary of moral status and demandingness. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and say plainly where that reply is soft: which premise I am quietly relying on, or which question I am answering instead of the one that was asked. Flag any objection I should simply concede, and tell me what the concession costs me. Finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and a short note on why it is the dangerous one. I do not want a reading list or citations — I cannot consult anything from the podium, and the point is what they will say, not what has been published about it. Write in British English.
