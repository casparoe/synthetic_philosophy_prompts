I am a political philosopher assembling a short position paper on rights and social choice for a workshop, and before I circulate it I want my own commitments audited for consistency. Here, roughly, is where I have landed.

(1) The weak Pareto principle is not negotiable for me: if every individual strictly prefers x to y, then x is socially better than y. I treat this as a condition of adequacy on any procedure that presumes to aggregate individual judgments at all, not a convenience of formulation.

(2) Individuals have rights, and the correct formal rendering of them is Sen's minimal liberalism: for each person there is at least one pair of alternatives within their own sphere such that, if they strictly prefer one to the other, the social preference must follow. The standard counterexample — the two readers and the indecent book, one who would rather no one read it but, if someone must, would rather it be himself, and one who would rather the first read it than read it himself, and would rather read it himself than have no one read it — strikes me as a genuine result rather than an artifact of a contrived case.

(3) Welfarism is false: the value of an outcome is not exhausted by the utility information it contains. I have occasionally argued that the liberal paradox is a reductio of welfarism, on the grounds that the conflict arises only because a welfarist has nothing but preference rankings to work with — though I have never been confident that this is the right diagnosis.

(4) Rights are grounded in autonomy, and autonomy includes the power to waive or trade what one has a right to. A right that its holder may not alienate is not a right but a duty of self-regard imposed from outside, and I want no part of it.

(5) I refuse to launder preferences — to let social choice respond only to individuals' self-regarding preferences and set aside their preferences about how others live. The filter is ad hoc; it purchases consistency by rewriting the preferences people actually have.

(6) Substantively I am a liberal in the Millian sense: the state may coerce an individual only to prevent harm to others, and it may not act on a conception of the good that some citizens reasonably reject.

Please audit this. I assume some of these pull against each other and that others merely appear to, and I do not trust my own sense of which is which. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each genuine one, say what has to give, what the least costly repair would be, and what that repair costs elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once. I would also like to know which premise is actually doing the work in the impossibility, since I suspect I have misidentified the culprit. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and do not assume that the item I have already earmarked for sacrifice is the one that should go. No references needed; I want the argument, not the literature.
