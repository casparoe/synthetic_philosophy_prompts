I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, applying to a nine-month fellowship for independent researchers working outside universities, and I need a two-page research proposal. The topic is the asymmetry in the ethics of procreation: the cluster of claims behind the thought that bringing someone into existence can be wrong in a way that leaving them uncreated cannot be, so that there is a reason not to procreate but no corresponding reason to procreate. I am bringing a half-formed idea that I want stress-tested and converted into a workable plan, not a general orientation to the topic.

My idea is that the asymmetry, as it is usually stated, is not one claim but at least three, and that the long standoff between those who accept it and those who reject it is partly an artifact of the two sides attacking different members of the bundle. The three I have in mind are: first, a claim about supervenience, that whether a life is worth starting supervenes entirely on facts about that life and its welfare, so there is no one for non-existence to be good or bad for; second, a claim about the structure of reasons, that reasons against creating suffering hold impersonally, while reasons for creating happiness hold only in relation to someone who would otherwise be deprived; third, a claim about deontic status, that the wrongness of procreation is insensitive to the interests or consent of the person created, whereas abstaining is unconditionally permitted. I suspect the first is independently plausible but does not by itself generate the third, that the second is doing the real work and is also the most fragile, and that the practical verdict people actually care about — do not create a miserable life, no obligation to create a happy one — may survive the loss of one or more of the three. I may be wrong about the decomposition itself, and I want the proposal written so that discovering it is merely verbal counts as a real result rather than a failed project.

Turn this into a proposal that does the following:

- States the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer, and says what kind of claim the answer is — about value, about reasons, or about permissibility — and why the others are downstream of it.
- Explains why the existing positions leave the question open, referring to positions by their arguments and their costs rather than by names, titles, or dates. I will supply the references myself; do not write a literature review or a bibliography.
- Specifies a method I can execute alone in nine months. I am comfortable with formal notation and with writing small programs, but I have no training in textual exegesis, so the plan should not hinge on subtle readings of difficult primary texts. I would like the core of the method to be an explicit commitment table: the three components as variables, the intuitions I want preserved as constraints, and a search for the minimal set of components that reproduces them. Say what the constraints are, which cases generate each one, and how I would distinguish a genuine inconsistency from a merely awkward combination.
- Explains why this decomposition-and-constraint approach is preferable to simply arguing for or against the asymmetry head-on, and what the head-on approach must get right that mine can avoid.
- Names the possible conclusions and the considerations that would decide between them: if the components come apart, which one is load-bearing; if they cannot be prised apart, whether the right response is to drop a component or to drop one of the intuitions; and if an intuition has to go, which one and at what cost.
- Names at least two distinct failure modes, and draws the scope honestly, so that what I am promising is a finished paper plus a short note for practitioners — engineers and regulators who make decisions about creating new sentient beings — rather than a down payment on a book.

The intuitions I am starting from, which the proposal may revise but must engage, are these. A life of unrelieved suffering is a reason not to create it. A life that would be happy is no reason to create it. No one is wronged by never being brought into existence. And once someone does exist, the fact that their life is worth living is a reason to keep them alive, even though that same fact was no reason to create them. I am aware that the last of these may be doing something different from the first three; if so, I want the proposal to say so rather than smooth it over, and to treat the question of whether one account can carry both the creation asymmetry and the continuation asymmetry as one of the things the project is meant to settle.
