Having spent my career in experimental science and only recently turned to philosophy, I find myself persuaded by what I take to be the standard reading of Libet's experiments and their successors: that the conscious intention to act does not initiate voluntary movement, but arrives after the brain has already committed to it, so that the experience of willing is a late report on a decision made elsewhere. I would like to know what would count against this position, ranked by how decisive each consideration would be. I am interested in the obvious methodological objections but do not want to stop there: the reliance on averaged EEG, the disputed timing of the reported intention, the difference between the arbitrary finger movements studied and deliberate action in ordinary life, and the fact that Libet himself reported that preplanned movements were not preceded by the same readiness potential. Which of these, if sustained, would actually damage the conclusion rather than merely complicate the experiment? What would the position's own defenders concede as counting against them? And please say plainly whether the claim has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — if it now amounts only to the modest thesis that consciousness is not the earliest causal contributor, I want to know whether that is a real retreat or a clarification, and whether the stronger reading that people usually take from these experiments has any exposure left at all.
