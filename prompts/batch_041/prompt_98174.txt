Right, I've talked myself into a position and I want you to have a proper go at dismantling it, because I genuinely can't tell anymore whether it's deep or just well-defended by vagueness.

Call it tranquilism. The claim: the only things that matter are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. Pleasure doesn't matter in itself — what we call pleasure is just the relief of a craving or the fading of a pain, so it's only good because of what it's the absence of. A life therefore goes well exactly to the extent that it's free of craving and free of suffering, and nothing else — achievement, love, knowledge, beauty, whatever — adds or subtracts anything.

What I want from you is a map of what this position risks. Which observations, arguments, intuitions or formal results would count against it? Sort them by how decisive they'd actually be — fatal, serious, merely awkward — and tell me which of them a committed tranquilist would themselves concede count against them, as opposed to the ones only critics wheel out.

Some places I suspect the pressure is, but don't limit yourself to these:

- Is craving bad in itself, or only because it tends to bring suffering? If it's the second, the view looks like it collapses into plain negative hedonism with an empirical claim bolted on, and a craving that never actually hurts anyone would be fine. Does the tranquilist have to accept that, and if not, whats the story about why craving is intrinsically bad?
- A rock has no craving and no suffering. Does the view have to say the rock is doing as well as the sage — and is that a reductio or a bullet worth biting? If it's a bullet, does the view still have anything to say about lives, or only about states?
- Craving the absence of craving. Second-order cravings, the regress this threatens, and the difference between someone who never craved and someone who craved and then extinguished it — does the view have to call those two equal, and can it even distinguish them?
- Adaptive preference. Someone whose cravings were conditioned out of them by a controlling environment, or by an oppressive upbringing, looks tranquil from the outside. Is the view committed to calling that a good life, and is that a defeater or just a complication?
- Unfelt craving. An addict asleep, or a desire you don't know you have. If craving has to be felt in order to count, does that gut the view's claim to be about the deep structure of a life? If it doesn't have to be felt, how would we ever know a life was free of it?
- Agency. If caring about anything just is a form of craving, then a fully tranquil person can't care about anything, other people included. Cost, or the whole point?

And the meta-question: what does tranquilism predict that hedonism, desire-satisfaction and objective-list views don't? Has it been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just the price of any axiology worth having?

The thing that would move me most, I think, is a case where I'm forced to say either that something clearly bad isn't bad, or that something clearly neutral is bad. But tell me if that's the wrong test to be applying.

Write it in British English please.
