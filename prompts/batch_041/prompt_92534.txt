Is there a name for this? I've been thinking about a pattern I keep running into in arguments, and I can't tell whether I'm describing something that already has a term of art or whether I've just noticed a hole. The pattern is this: someone treats a claim as resting on a presumption of independence among several factors, gets a striking conclusion out of it, and then — in the very next breath — uses that striking conclusion as evidence that the factors were *not* independent after all. So the independence is doing the work at step one and being refuted at step two, and nobody flags the tension because each step on its own sounds fine. A concrete case: you estimate the odds that some complicated object would have a certain feature by multiplying the odds for its parts as if each part were a coin flip, you get something tiny, and then you announce that the tiny number proves the object couldn't have arisen by chance — as though the coin-flip model you just used were the thing you're now refuting. Another: someone assumes a large collection of items are 'randomly scattered' to argue that an observed clustering is too orderly to be accidental, then treats the disorderliness of the rest as further proof of design. Each half is individually reasonable; the two halves contradict each other; and the argument is often *successful*, which is the part that bothers me.

I want to know whether this has an established name and literature, and if not, what the best carving of it is. I suspect it's adjacent to a few things I've heard of but I can't get any of them to fit exactly. It feels related to the general idea of reasoning as if a complicated thing were typical or generic, and to the family of moves where you assume independence to get an estimate — but those are about the *legitimacy of the initial presumption*, whereas what I'm describing is about the *second* step, where the estimate gets turned around and used against the very assumption that produced it. It also feels related to selection effects and to the idea that you can only find a pattern surprising against a background that you then have to keep fixed — but the cases I have in mind don't involve any actual search over many hypotheses; there's just one object and one computation, done twice with incompatible background assumptions. And it feels related to the paradoxes of confirmation, though again those are about evidence *for* a hypothesis rather than about a hypothesis being quietly used and then quietly denied within a single argument.

So I don't think I'm after a bare label. What I want is the carving: what exactly the move is, what it is not, and where its edges are. Specifically — is the sin located in the *inconsistency* between the two steps, or in something weaker, like the second step's treating a heuristic estimate as if it were a rigorous lower bound on improbability? Is there a version of the move that is actually *sound* — where using a random model to get an estimate and then taking the estimate as evidence against the model is not circular, because the estimate is being compared to a *different* background than the one assumed? If the phenomenon has a name, what is it, and which of the adjacent concepts I've gestured at is the nearest neighbor? If it doesn't, give me a term, a definition precise enough to have borderline cases, and at least one case that clearly falls outside it, so I can tell what I'm actually pointing at.
