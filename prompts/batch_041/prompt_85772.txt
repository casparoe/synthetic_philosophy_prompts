My two housemates have been going at each other all week over something from their philosophy of mind class, and they've drafted me as referee. The backstory: our friend Dana was in a drug trial for migraines and got much better, then found out she'd been on the placebo and relapsed; a few weeks later a nurse implied she'd had the real thing after all and she improved again. Meanwhile another participant who got the actual drug didn't improve at all. Here's how it went.

(1) Ravi: so Dana's belief that she was getting the drug caused the improvement. That's not a figure of speech -- take the belief away and the effect goes, put it back and it comes back.

(2) Sam: the improvement was caused by the immune and brain events. You can tell the whole story in those terms without ever mentioning a belief, and once you've told it there's nothing left over for the belief to do.

(3) Ravi: that just shows the belief has a physical basis. The neural story isn't a rival cause, it's the same cause at a finer grain. You don't say the atoms caused the bridge to fall and the bad weld didn't.

(4) Sam: then you've given up the claim worth having. If it's one thing, the belief isn't an extra cause, it's a redescription -- "the belief caused it" is just "the brain state caused it" with hand-waving.

(5) Ravi: but people improve off all sorts of different neural setups. The belief shows up in different brains and the effect follows it, while the physical description falls apart into a hundred unrelated ones.

(6) Sam: so the belief is a grab-bag, a disjunction of whatever the real cause is in each case. Disjunctions don't push anything; whatever does the pushing is physical and particular.

I honestly cant referee this. Go through it move by move and say who's right where, and what they're both missing.
