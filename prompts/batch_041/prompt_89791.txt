I teach a 90-minute session of an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on decision theory (roughly 18 participants, a mix of philosophy and economics students, some of whom have seen the prisoner's dilemma and some of whom have not), and I want a runnable in-class game that makes them feel both the pull and the instability of superrational reasoning. The material I want to build on is the family of puzzles Douglas Hofstadter constructed in his Scientific American column of June 1983, later collected in Metamagical Themas: in the Platonia Dilemma, a fixed number of anonymous participants are each invited to claim a large prize, the prize is voided if two or more claim it, no contact between participants is permitted, and the winner may not share. Do not quote the column at me and do not supply a reading list; I will handle sources myself. I need only the mechanism and the conceptual structure, restated in your own words.

Design the activity in two phases. Phase one should be a clean symmetric version of the dilemma: identical players, identical options, free entry, a fixed prize that is voided or evenly diluted once claims exceed a threshold, and payoffs displayed so that the collective optimum and the individual temptation are both visible. Phase two should introduce an asymmetry that the participants are not told about in advance — for instance, some players privately receive a cheaper or more valuable entry, or one participant secretly holds the role of prize administrator with a stake in the outcome. I want the group to discover that the maxim "everyone who reasons alike will settle on the same answer" delivers a crisp prescription in phase one and something much less determinate in phase two, where the players are no longer interchangeable and there is no settled way to say what reasoning alike even amounts to. The activity should make that gap felt, not merely announced.

Give me: the rules and materials (including how entries are submitted anonymously and how information is released between rounds); a payoff scheme that uses points or a trivial non-monetary reward rather than real money, and that avoids collapsing into a pure free-rider problem; the number of rounds and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; the instructions I read aloud, which must not name superrationality, team reasoning, or any related term until the debrief; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the underlying ideas, including the difference between the symmetric and asymmetric cases and the objection that the game only demonstrates that people enjoy winning things.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle each failure, and which feature of the original dilemma the activity cannot reproduce — what I will have to supply by talking rather than by having them play.
