In discussions of counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, a recurring move is to insist that these cases are not genuinely analogous to the ordinary coercion they are named after. An ordinary mugging or blackmail threat is conditional on your actual non-compliance in the actual world: the harm is threatened unless you pay, and your payment causally prevents it. In counterfactual mugging, by contrast, the harm is located in a branch you are not in, and your payment cannot causally affect the branch you are in; in XOR blackmail, the threat is conditioned on a parity — the harm is threatened if and only if an exclusive-or holds between your payment and some other fact — so that your payment flips the condition rather than simply preventing the harm. The suggestion, then, is that the names "mugging" and "blackmail" are doing illicit rhetorical work: they import the intuition that one should not reward threats, when the structure of the case is different. Others reply that the disanalogy is irrelevant, because the same decision-theoretic structure is present and the names are mere labels.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly, preferably in a table. For an ordinary mugging, identify what plays the role of the threat, the demanded payment, the threatened harm, the conditional structure, the coercer's gain, the temporal order, and the causal connection between payment and harm. Then do the same for counterfactual mugging and for XOR blackmail. Where does the mapping hold, and where does it break? Which disanalogies are mere surface features, and which alter the normative or decision-theoretic status of the case? In particular, consider whether the following features of ordinary mugging survive the translation: the actuality of the threat, the causal efficacy of payment, the identity and continued existence of the coercer, the possibility of resistance or of appealing to a third party, and the role of the coercer's beliefs about your character. Which of these matter for the conclusion that one should not pay, and which are dispensable? Finally, does the conclusion that one should not pay — or that one should pay — survive the audit, or was it being carried by the analogy? If the analogy is defective, propose a better one, or explain why the case is sui generis.

End with a one-line verdict in the form "Verdict: ...".
