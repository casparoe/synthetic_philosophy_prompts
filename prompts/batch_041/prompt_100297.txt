So I'm a philosophy grad student sitting in on a "what is life?" seminar, and I keep getting into arguments at the bar with my friend Priya, who's a second-year in an origin-of-life lab and has very strong opinions about this stuff. I've been quietly keeping score of her verdicts, because I want to figure out what rule she's actually running. Here's where she's landed so far: E. coli, alive; influenza virus, not alive; a prion, not alive; a mule (she insists the sterility is irrelevant), alive; a human red blood cell on its own, not alive; an isolated mitochondrion in a dish, not alive; a bacterial plasmid, not alive; a phage, not alive; fire, not alive; a computer virus, not alive. When I press her on the virus she always says it's just "borrowing" everyone else's machinery and has no metabolism of its own, and when I press her on the mule she says it's plainly an organism even though it can't make more of itself.

Now here's the new case. She just got back from a talk about two lab systems. The first is a little fatty-acid vesicle packed with an autocatalytic metabolic network -- it takes in nutrients, grows, and pinches off into daughter vesicles, over and over -- but there's no nucleic acid in it, no genome, nothing that mutates, so it can't evolve by natural selection; it just keeps churning away indefinitely. The second is a naked self-replicating RNA in a test tube, no membrane and basically no metabolism, except it makes copying errors and the faster copiers gradually take over the tube, so it does evolve.

I want you to predict Priya's verdict on each of those two, tell me which rules actually fit the verdicts she's already given (I can see at least two that fit, and I suspect they only come apart on the new cases), say which rule you think she's really running and why, and then name the one further case I could put to her that would finally force the surviving rules apart. I don't much care whether the answers are "right" -- I want to know what she'll say and what would settle it.
