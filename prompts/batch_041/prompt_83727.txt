My reading group did the justice section of the Pensées last night and two of them went at it for about forty minutes while the rest of us ate the biscuits. I'm a second-year PhD in political theory and I couldnt referee it, so I'm handing it to you.

Setup, for what it's worth: Pascal's line there is roughly that man is ignorant of justice, that the only rule we actually have left is to follow the custom of our own country, that a river or three degrees of latitude flips everything legal, that theft and infanticide have counted as virtuous somewhere, and that custom is the whole of equity only because it's accepted — trace it back to its first principles and you destroy it. He also says we must not look at the fact of usurpation: law was introduced without reason and has become reasonable, so it should be made to seem authoritative and eternal and its origin hidden, and he cites a legislator who thought the people had to be deceived for their own good. Then the famous bit about justice and might — justice with no might behind it is helpless, might with no justice is tyranny — and the remark that, not finding justice, we have settled for force.

(1) Rosa said this is Thrasymachus in better prose: justice is a story the strong tell, Pascal knows it's a story, and he still tells you to keep telling it. A manual for power rather than a critique of it.

(2) Iain said no — the argument is about our ignorance, not about might making right. Pascal says man is ignorant of justice, so the only rule still standing is the custom of your own country; thats a predicament he's describing with dismay, not a norm he's endorsing. And he calls might without justice tyrannical, which is a complaint, not a legitimation.

(3) Rosa: then the ignorance premise is doing no work, and the real premise is the prediction that people who see the origin of the law will throw it off. That's a claim about consequences, and it's the only thing that gets you from we don't know what's just to hide the origin. Skepticism on its own gets you to the reformer having no less standing than the law, not to keeping quiet.

(4) Iain: that's not a bug, it's a second-best argument. There is no stable order that is also honest about its own foundation — you get force dressed as law, or force with nothing on. And the two-tier thing is deliberate: for himself the reflective person knows it's custom, for the polity the belief has to be held. Same shape as his stuff about our being automata as much as minds, where custom is what actually moves us.

(5) Rosa: a two-tier view is a noble lie and it eats itself. His own text is exactly the thing that shows people the origin of the law, and by his own account that's what makes them shake off the yoke. So either the Pensées are for readers who can handle it and the people can't — in which case he isn't talking about justice at all, he's talking about who's allowed to know things — or he wrote the most effective piece of sedition of the seventeenth century by accident.

Can you go through this move by move? Who's reading him right on which point, where is each of them wrong, and is there a reading of those fragments that neither of them has? And who comes off better overall. I don't need citations, I need the argument.
