I am an undergraduate in my second year of a philosophy degree, and this term I am taking a course on the mind-body problem from Descartes to the present. In last week's seminar we read the beast-machine doctrine in Descartes's Discourse on the Method, and our tutor presented it in the usual way: an animal, Descartes says, is a machine — a clock or a hydraulic automaton — and because there is no thinking substance inside it there is nothing there to suffer, so the doctrine is wheeled out to license the conclusion that our moral scruples about animals are misplaced. What struck me is how much of that conclusion the tutor simply let the machine analogy carry. A classmate then pushed the same analogy into the present: she suggested that if the machine picture is right, a dog is in exactly the position of a large language model — it emits the appropriate outputs, but there is nothing it is like to be it — and that Descartes's animal machine is, in effect, an early version of the objection that a chatbot is merely a stochastic parrot, or merely a system shuffling symbols in a closed room. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine continuation of Descartes's argument or a very different one wearing its clothes.

What I would like from you is an audit of the analogy rather than a summary of the doctrine. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the springs, the wheels, the clockmaker, the animal's cry, and the soul — and then say where the mapping breaks down. I am particularly interested in which features the machine picture quietly imports that the animal case does not itself supply, and in the point our tutor mentioned only in passing, that for Descartes the human body is a machine as well, so that the analogy by itself cannot be what marks off the beast from the man. I should also like you to identify the real load-bearing premises: my suspicion is that the language test and the reason-in-all-contingencies test do the actual work, that they concern thought rather than feeling, and that the step from no thought to no suffering is therefore an extra premise smuggled in under cover of the analogy. Does the ethical conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it all along?

Finally, if the machine picture is salvageable at all, tell me what a better analogy would be, and say how far my classmate's chatbot parallel really extends before it, too, starts importing things it has no right to. Please write in British English.
