I'm a novelist — second book out in the spring, which mostly means I'm now insufferable about craft — and I'm stuck about eighty pages into a new one where a missionary family lands in a village that already has a fully worked out moral world of its own, and every draft keeps tipping into an argument about which side is right. My editor says it's becuase I keep asking a novel to answer a question only an essay can answer. So I've gone back to The Poisonwood Bible, the obvious comparison and, I think, sneakier than people give it credit for. What I want is a real reading of it through moral relativism, and I mean the version that's actually interesting: wrong is supposed to work like here, so that my saying it and you denying it isn't even a disagreement, we're just two speakers indexing different frameworks, and the live question becomes whether anything licenses me to criticize your framework from inside mine — the usual worry being that the reformer who fights her own community's code turns out to be simply mistaken by that code's own lights. Does the novel's five-voice structure, Orleanna's retrospective interludes and then Rachel, Leah, Adah and Ruth May taking turns, each with her own vocabulary for what's happening, amount to an argument for that kind of relativism, or a trap laid for it? Because the book badly wants us outraged at Nathan, and at the Belgians, and at the whole arrangement, and outrage seems to need a standpoint that isn't just one more framework. The title is the part I can't stop chewing on: it points at the tradition of misprinted Bibles, editions where one dropped or swapped word turns the text into something else entirely, and the novel's central image is exactly that — Nathan's Kikongo blessing, meant to come out as Jesus is most precious, arriving instead as Jesus is poisonwood. A misprint is only a misprint against a correct text, so I want to know if the book knows that, or whether it's quietly relativist and hasn't noticed it can't have it both ways.

Second thing, and this is the one that matters most for my own book: does Kingsolver actually let the Congolese be moral subjects, people with standing to judge the Prices — Mama Tataba's verdict on the demonstration garden, the elders refusing to let Leah hunt, the village's refusal of the river baptism, their reading of Ruth May's death — or do they end up as scenery for a Western family's moral education? And what does the novel give the debate that argument can't? I suspect the answer has something to do with the fact that you have to live inside one framework at a time as you read, chapter by chapter, which may make the whole question of comparing frameworks look different than it does in the seminar room. Tell me where the relativist reading holds, where the novel resists it and the honest critic has to say so, and what it adds. No reading lists please — I don't need a bibliography, I need the reading.
