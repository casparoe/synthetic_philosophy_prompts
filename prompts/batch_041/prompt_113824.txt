I'm a third-year student and I'm the student rep on the grants committee of a small community foundation in town (they put two students on the board, mostly for optics, but we do actually vote). We have $45,000 to give out before June 30 or we lose an alumni matching gift, and two proposals have split us 3-3 going into Thursday's meeting. Proposal A is from a refugee resettlement office two miles from campus: they want the whole $45k to keep a legal-aid caseworker employed for a year, and the caseworker's numbers are real and the need is obviously real. Proposal B is from a group of grad students and one policy person who want $45k to run a one-year fellowship for people who'd go into "long-term" policy work. Their pitch is what they call the hinge-of-history idea: that the present might be one of the very few moments in human history when a small number of people can shift where civilization ends up going for good, so a dollar spent now on the right thing could be worth vastly more than a dollar spent at almost any other time — the way they put it, most centuries don't matter much, a few matter enormously, and we can't tell which one we're in. Proposal A's backers say that if we can't tell whether we're at a hinge, we should act on what we can actually see; Proposal B's say that's exactly the reasoning that would have made someone in 1900 fund a bake sale.

Our chair wants a written opinion from each of us before the vote and I have to write mine this weekend. Please write it: give me a concrete recommendation (which proposal, how much, over what period, and any conditions I should attach — e.g. what the fellowship people would have to report back to us and when), the reasoning behind it, and an honest statement of the strongest thing the other side has going for it, since half the board will read this. Two wrinkles I keep going back and forth on and want you to actually engage rather than wave away. First, even granting the hinge people are right that this is a hinge, I'm not sure we'd be the ones with any leverage — we're a small foundation in a college town, and it's not obvious that funding three fellows moves anything at all, whereas the caseworker is definately going to keep somebody out of detention. Second, a friend in my philosophy of the future seminar says the whole comparison collapses because we can't know the long-run consequences of anything we do, so any "expected impact" number for A versus B is basically made up — I don't know if that kills the hinge argument or just kills the arithmetic, and I'd like your view. No reading list, I need the memo itself.
