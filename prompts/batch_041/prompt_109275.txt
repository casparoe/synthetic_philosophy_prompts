Hey — I'm a third-year PhD student in logic (set theory side; my own work is on choiceless analysis, which is why the prof keeps roping me into this kind of thing), and I'm TAing our department's graduate foundations course this term. The prof has handed me the job of writing one of the essay questions for the take-home final, and I want something that actually disriminates instead of another state-three-equivalents-of-AC question that everyone can answer by reciting Zorn and the well-ordering theorem. So I'd rather you write the whole package: the question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme I can hand to the other TA so the two of us grade the same way.

The question should be built around a short stimulus proof that you write — maybe 8 or 10 lines — of the claim that a countable union of countable sets is countable, written in a perfectly natural style by someone who never mentions choice at all. Put two selection-looking moves in it, only one of which is a genuine invocation of a choice principle; the other should be a step where a canonical selection rule is actually available, so that a student who flags that one has misdiagnosed things, and I want the marking scheme to say how many marks that misdiagnosis costs. Then the students should have to do the following. (a) Identify the exact step where a choice principle is used and say which principle licenses it at the weakest level — I want them to place countable choice, dependent choice and full AC in the right order and say where this proof sits, rather than just saying it uses AC. (b) Explain why the obvious repair (well-order the union, then take least elements) is not a repair in ZF. (c) Explain why the conclusion isn't merely unprovable in ZF but actually fails in some models, and say what has to be true of such a model, without insisting they name it — bonus marks if they can. (d) Explain what makes a selection rule canonical, and use that to say why the same proof is unproblematic in ZF when the sets are given as sets of naturals, or when the enumerations are handed to you as data, or when the index set is finite. (e) In two or three sentences, say why all this shows that the content of AC isn't "some of the sets might be empty" but something about simultaneity and definability. They'll have their notes but no internet, so nothing should turn on them remembering the details of a particular construction.

For the model answer, I want the quantifier-shift point made explicit — the proof needs the move from for-every-n-there-exists-an-enumeration to there-exists-a-function-giving-an-enumeration-for-every-n — and I want it honest about what ZF does and doesn't give you (finite index sets, well-orderable families and families with a definable selector all being fine). For the marking scheme, do it as five must-hit points, 20 marks total, with the marks for each and, seperate from that, a one-line note on the typical way candidates lose marks on it. Finish with a short A-versus-B paragraph naming what an A answer has to do that a B answer usually doesn't — e.g. keeping eliminable and non-eliminable uses of choice apart, or knowing that the failure is witnessed by a model rather than just by the absence of a proof.
