For the past year I have sat on the allocation committee of a small foundation that funds long-horizon research and advocacy, and next Thursday we vote on the largest grant in our history: a multi-year package to build a forecasting and policy outfit whose entire case for urgency rests on the hinge of history hypothesis — the claim, roughly, that we may be living at the moment of maximum leverage over the long-run trajectory of civilisation, so that a dollar or an hour spent now buys vastly more expected value than the same dollar or hour spent a generation hence. The applicants treat this as settled enough to justify concentrating a large share of our endowment in one high-variance bet. Two of my colleagues, a historian and an economist, are sceptical: they suspect the hypothesis is either unfalsifiable or, worse, a respectable-sounding rationalisation for spending money on whatever the grantee already wanted to spend it on, and they argue that a foundation has no business treating a contested thesis in the philosophy of history as though it were an actuarial input. The rest of the committee is split, and I have been asked to circulate something we can actually act on rather than another seminar.

Please draft the short written opinion I will put in front of the committee before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve subject to stated conditions, with a figure, a cap, or a review date where one is relevant — and set out the reasoning that genuinely supports it. The difficult part is that I do not want to paper over the disagreement. Take my two colleagues' objection at its strongest, including the possibility that the hypothesis functions as rhetoric rather than as a premise anyone could act on, and say plainly what would have to be true for the grant to be justified and how we would know if it were not. Write it as a formal committee document with an actionable bottom line, not as personal advice to me, and keep it short enough to be read aloud at the meeting.
