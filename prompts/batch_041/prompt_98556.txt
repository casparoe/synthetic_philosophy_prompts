I'm midway through a qualifying paper on the side-effect effect — the finding that people are markedly more willing to say a foreseen side effect was brought about "intentionally" when that side effect is bad than when it is good — and I've reached the stage where my problem is too many proposed explanations rather than too few. Before I commit to any of them, I want the standard of adequacy written down first. So give me the desiderata: the specification any theory in this area would have to satisfy, not a survey of the contenders. Which phenomena must such a theory reproduce, which patterns must it avoid over-predicting (the cases where the effect should vanish and the cases where it survives), which distinctions must it preserve, and which constraints from neighboring work must it respect? Pair each requirement with the specific case or result that motivates it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any account of the phenomenon and which are merely house preferences of one camp or another.

To keep this concrete rather than abstract, I'd like the list to be answerable against a handful of cases I've been kicking around, which I suspect do much of the work of separating the accounts. First, the plain asymmetric pair: an executive adopts a policy purely for profit, is told it will also damage the environment, says he doesn't care, and is then judged to have damaged it intentionally — change the side effect to a benefit and the intentionality judgment largely evaporates. Second, a case where the side effect is bad but the agent is conforming to a norm rather than violating one, so the bad outcome is produced by doing exactly what the relevant professional convention prescribes — I take this to bear on whether it is the badness of the outcome or the violation of a norm that is doing the work. Third, a case where the side effect violates a norm with no clear harm at all, and fourth, a case where it violates a norm the respondent herself rejects, to probe whether the effect requires the respondent to endorse the norm. Fifth, a case where the "agent" is an automated system or a firm rather than an individual with attitudes. I want the desiderata written so that they tell me which of these cases each requirement is meant to adjudicate, and I want the tension points named rather than smoothed over.

If some subset of the requirements looks jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly — I have a suspicion that the demand for a single, stable competence account of the concept of intentional action sits badly with the demand to explain judgments that shift with moral valence, and that conflict is the part I most want from this exercise. Skip scoring the named theories against the list; I'll do that myself in the seminar. Close with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing the one requirement you think does the most to exclude candidate theories, followed by the pair you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, separated by a semicolon.
