I'm putting together the last session of a short adult-ed course I run on animal ethics, and I want to end on a film. We're doing The Lion King — the 1994 one, not the remake, I have strong feelings and I'm not interested in being talked out of them. The unit it follows is on wild animal suffering: the idea that what happens to animals in nature, predation but also starvation, disease, exposure, the sheer arithmetic of how many animals are born and die young and badly, is a moral problem in its own right, and that the "it's all part of a harmonious whole, nature knows best" move does a lot of unearned work. So what I want from you is a reading of the film through that lens, taken seriously in both directions — what the film shows or dramatizes or complicates about the issue, where the idea actually illuminates a scene or a character's choice, and where the film resists the reading and an honest critic has to say so instead of forcing it.

To keep this from being a vague thematic ramble, here's a half-baked framework I've been using and I'd like you to test the film against it. I think there are four seperate defenses of predation floating around in popular culture, and they get conflated constantly: (1) necessity, a lion's got to eat; (2) systemic, the balance-of-nature story where removing the predator collapses the whole thing; (3) aesthetic, the thought that a world containing lions is a grander, fuller, more valuable world than a world without them, so the cost is worth paying; and (4) role or stewardship, where every creature has a station and the king's job is to accept and administer his own. My hunch is that the film leans hardest on (2) and (4) and uses (3) as a mood rather than an argument, and that (1) does almost no work because nobody in the movie is ever actually shown eating anything they mind eating. Tell me if that's right, and for each one say whether the wild-animal-suffering critic has a real reply or just a rhetorical one.

Then the specific things I keep getting stuck on. The film's only real evil is *bad* predation — Scar's regime over-hunts, the hyenas eat everything, the Pride Lands go barren, and the good order is restored when consumption is brought back under sustainable yield. That looks to me like a resource-management ethic wearing a welfare ethic's clothes; does the film ever have a concept of how an antelope is doing, or only of whether there are enough antelope? And notice the demography: one cub, no infant mortality, no parasites, no winter, no disease, no r-selected litters dying in the grass. The circle of life as animated is a low-mortality cycle, which is the opposite of the actual complaint. I'd like you to say what happens to the film's emotional force if you restore the real numbers — is it still moving, or is the whole thing resting on the fantasy of a nature where almost everyone survives? Related: the interiority asymmetry. The predators and the comic sidekicks get faces and motives; the antelope are scenery, and the one time the prey do something as a body it's the stampede, and what they do is kill the good king. Also the happiest stretch of the movie is a predator living on grubs with a meerkat and a warthog — so the film's picture of a good life requires an exit from the food chain, which seems to concede the critic's whole point, except that it then treats Simba's return to eating meat as maturity, as growing up into who he is. And the villains are hungry. Scar's grievance is that he gets scraps while everyone else feasts, and the hyenas are driven by not having enough to eat; the film's evil is motivated by precisely the kind of suffering the literature says is the problem. Does the film know that, or does it just need a motive?

I'd rather you tell me where I'm over-reading than flatter the reading, and I'd like it to end on the other direction: what the film sees that the arguments don't. My guess is that it has a real grip on the thing the literature struggles to answer — the sense that a world with no lions in it is a loss, that predation is bound up with everything we find admirable in wild animals, and that any proposal to fix nature has to reckon with the fact that it's asking for a world without the very things it's trying to protect. And if the film is ambivalent rather than committed, say which way the ambivalence leans and which scenes do the leaning. Don't bother with a reading list or citations, I roughly know who says what and I'm not going to assign it. Last thing, practical: if I've got ninety minutes and I want to screen three or four scenes and talk over them, which ones, in what order, and what do I say while they're running?
