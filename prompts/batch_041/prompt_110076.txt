I'm the TA for an undergraduate early modern philosophy course, and I've just finished a first pass over the short-answer section on Leibniz's ethics. A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether Leibniz's God is bound by the good or manufactures it, and I've noticed the argument has left me with strong opinions about which parts of this question matter, so I'd like an independent and fairly granular read before I hand the papers back. The question was worth 10 points, split 3 / 4 / 3 across the three parts:

"Leibniz holds that God has actualized the best of all possible worlds. Explain how this claim fits with his account of the good. (a) Does Leibniz think things are good because God wills them, or that God wills them because they are good? (b) How does he explain the presence of evil in the best of all possible worlds — what kinds of evil does he distinguish, and how does he avoid making God the author of sin? (c) What does his account imply about how we should act — in particular, does it leave room for us to make things better or worse?"

The student wrote:

"Leibniz's starting point is the principle of sufficient reason: a perfectly wise and good God would not choose arbitrarily, so the world he creates must be the best one available to him. This also shows that Leibniz is not a divine command theorist. Things are not good because God commands them; God commands them because they are good, and his will follows the eternal truths that his understanding contains, which he cannot simply rewrite. The existence of evil is therefore the real test of the view, and Leibniz distinguishes three kinds of evil: metaphysical, physical, and moral. Metaphysical evil is the suffering and misfortune that creatures undergo simply in virtue of existing as finite beings; physical evil is pain and hardship; moral evil is sin. His response is not to deny that these are real, but to argue that the whole is better for containing them: a world with free creatures who sometimes sin is more perfect than a world of sinless machines, so God permits evil without willing it as an end. Freedom, for Leibniz, is not a matter of indifference — the will is determined by the strongest inclination, which for God is the best and for us is whatever appears most good. And since the whole is already settled as the best that could be, our choices cannot change the value of the world; the appropriate response is a kind of cheerful acceptance that whatever happens is for the best."

I want a claim-by-claim assessment rather than a general impression: for each substantive claim in the answer, say whether it is correct, incorrect, or half-right, explain briefly why, and flag which errors should cost the student a lot and which should cost only a little. Close by saying which of the three parts is answered most weakly, and what the student would have to change to earn the next grade up.
