I'm a third-year philosophy student writing a paper for a seminar on personal identity and the self. We've spent a few weeks on the idea that the self is not a metaphysical substance but a center of narrative gravity—a constructed unity that emerges from the stories we tell about ourselves and that others tell about us. I want to test that idea against a concrete legal and clinical practice that has always struck me as philosophically loaded: advance directives in dementia care. In many jurisdictions, a person while competent can write an advance directive refusing, say, artificial nutrition and hydration if she later develops severe dementia; when she does, clinicians and courts often treat the earlier directive as authoritative even if the later, demented person seems content, asks for food, or no longer recognizes the document. The practice seems to presuppose something about who the self is across time, but I can't tell what exactly. Does it presuppose that personal identity survives dementia, so that the earlier and later selves are the same person? Does it instead presuppose a narrative self whose author is the earlier, competent person, so that the later self is bound by a story she did not write? Or does it presuppose something thinner—a legal fiction of precedent autonomy that doesn't need any metaphysical view of the self at all?

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of the self that this practice presupposes, and then say how much of that theory the practice actually needs. I'm especially interested in a distinction I've been trying to formulate: between narrative authorship—being the one who writes the story—and narrative ownership—being the one whose story it is, even if later chapters are written by others or by a changed self. Which of these, if either, does the advance-directive practice presuppose? Does it quietly rule out episodic or Buddhist-inspired views on which the later self is not the same person and need not be bound by the earlier self's preferences? And is the practice internally consistent, given that the same legal system often gives weight to the current wishes of a patient who refuses treatment, even when those wishes conflict with an earlier directive? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the presupposed view with a thoroughgoing narrative view in which the self is constantly revised, or with a view on which the later self has full authority over its own story. I'd like the answer to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and to flag the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
