I retired from semiconductor physics about three years ago and have spent most of the time since trying to teach myself philosophy of mind, which is definately going worse than the physics did. What I keep coming back to is von Neumann's The Computer and the Brain — the little unfinished thing he wrote at the very end, where he asks how a system made out of slow, sloppy, wet components manages to compute reliably at all. And what nags at me is that everyone treats him as the man who told us the brain is a computer, whereas when I actually read him he seems much more hedgy than that, almost as if he thought the digital description was a useful first pass that would eventually have to be given up.

So write me an essay on whether the brain is best described as a digital computer at all, and please take a side — don't end on some it-depends-on-the-level-of-description hedge. Here's the distinction I'd like you to run with, and I don't think it's the standard one: the real question isn't whether neurons fire in all-or-nothing spikes (of course they do), it's whether the brain's reliability is engineered — error-correction doing actual computational work, the way von Neumann thought unreliable components would have to be wired up to each other — or whether it's incidental, just brute redundancy and cellular repair that keeps the tissue alive without contributing anything to what the thing computes. If it's the first, the digital picture has a leg to stand on; if it's the second, I think it's in serious trouble. Argue it out and land somewhere. No reading list, no citations, I just want the argument — say 1500 words.
