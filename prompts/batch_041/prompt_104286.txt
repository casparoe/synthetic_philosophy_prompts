I am a novelist, and the book I have just finished is built on a single unverifiable fact that reaches the reader only by word of mouth: each chapter is narrated by someone who heard the story from the chapter before, and not one of them has any independent way to check what they were told. Living inside that structure for a year has left me wanting a number rather than an essay. Here is the proposition I want your credence on: that a hearer who possesses no independent evidence bearing on a speaker's competence or sincerity is nonetheless warranted in accepting that speaker's testimony, so long as no positive reason for doubt has arisen — that is, that the entitlement to believe what one is told is basic, rather than something a hearer must earn by reducing it to perception, memory, and induction.

Give me your credence in that proposition, and then open up the reasoning that produces it. I want the sub-questions the number depends on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification apiece, and how they combine. The terms I can see are these, though you should add or split any you think belong:

- whether testimony transmits knowledge at all, or only produces true belief that is true by luck;
- whether ordinary hearers in fact run a tacit reliability estimate on each speaker, or operate on a default entitlement that only defeaters cancel — in other words, whether the anti-reductionist's picture is psychologically accurate as well as normatively defensible;
- whether the reductionist programme can be completed without circularity, given that we acquire both our language and the great majority of our beliefs from other people in the first place;
- whether an entitlement can be specified that does not simply license general credulity, and what becomes of it in a world where much testimony is interested, careless, or false;
- whether the whole dispute is substantive or dissolves once one separates having grounds for a belief from being permitted to hold it without grounds;
- and, since my book is a chain and not a single link, whether warrant survives, grows, or decays as testimony passes from each teller to the next, and whether the two positions must return different verdicts on a chain that has no independent anchor anywhere along it.

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what single argument or piece of evidence would move it most. My own credence is about 0.7. My editor, who has spent years around courtroom fiction and keeps muttering that none of my narrators would survive a hearsay objection, puts it nearer 0.2. Set your number against ours, and tell me which sub-question the gap between us most likely lives in.
