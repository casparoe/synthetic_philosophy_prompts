For a graduate seminar on the history of monetary thought, construct a thought experiment that puts Berkeley's monetary doctrine under stress. The purpose is to settle whether that doctrine is a single theory or a bundle of separately plausible maxims, so a case that merely illustrates the doctrine is worthless, and so is a case whose verdicts are stipulated rather than derived. The case must compel the doctrine to give a determinate answer at a point where a rival gives a different one.

Take the following as the doctrine, and work from it rather than from further texts. Assume no library and no internet: do not cite, quote, or appeal to anything beyond what is stated here.

1. Money is not wealth. The wealth of a people consists in the power it gives them over labour and the fruits of the earth, and land together with human labour is the true source of riches.
2. Money is useful only insofar as it stirs up and circulates industry; anything else that does the same work may serve as well.
3. The true idea of money is that of "a ticket or counter" rather than a commodity carrying intrinsic value, so that things might still be rated, bought, and sold, and commerce maintained, even if the bullion were gone.
4. Prices are a compounded proportion, directly as the demand and reciprocally as the plenty, from which it follows that a smaller quantity circulating swiftly is equivalent in effect to a larger quantity circulating slowly.
5. Accordingly he wants a national bank and paper credit, and treats the quantity of money as something to be proportioned to the needs of trade rather than fixed by the cost of the metal.

Specifications for the case:

- Setting. A closed economy: no foreign trade, a fixed quantity of land, a population that can be set to work or left idle, coin only, and a single authority able to issue paper. No modern central-bank instruments, no assumption that people forecast accurately, and no appeal to confidence as a free parameter. Keep the scenario itself under 250 words of plain prose.
- Two verdicts from one set of principles. First, a specific issue of paper credit that the doctrine above endorses as an increase in the nation's wealth, while a metallic realist, looking at the same facts, sees only more claims on an unchanged product. Second, an extension of the same policy that the same principles, unamended, license, but that any modern reader would call ruinous. The two halves must share their principles; you may not add a saving clause for the second.
- Quantities. Say what is produced, what the existing stock of coin is, who receives the new credit and on what terms, and what happens to prices, employment, and output on each side's account. Keep the numbers small enough to check by hand, and mark the point at which the two accounts first diverge.
- No smuggled economics. Do not assume that money is neutral, that the quantity theory of money is false, or that value derives from labour alone. If your case requires any of these, say so and count it as a cost.
- The load-bearing principle. Identify the single item in the doctrine that makes the first verdict come out as it does, and defend that identification against at least one rival candidate from the list. Then say what happens if that item is removed or weakened: does the case collapse into bullionism, into an inflationism the doctrine cannot distinguish itself from, or into nothing?

Then the demonstration. Walk through Berkeley's reasoning step by step from the stated principles; give the realist's counter-account; state which principle is doing the work and why the alternatives are not; report what happens when it is removed; and say whether the two halves are consistent with one another. If they are not, say which half must be abandoned and what that shows about the doctrine. Close by stating how a defender of Berkeley would attack the case — by denying that the second half really follows, or by insisting that the demand-and-plenty proportionality and the velocity equivalence are already part of the doctrine rather than an amendment to it, or otherwise.

End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names the load-bearing principle in a short phrase and states whether the doctrine survived as one theory or split.
