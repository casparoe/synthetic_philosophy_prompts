I have been reading around the idea of a free-floating rationale — the thought that natural selection can be said to work through reasons that no one ever entertains, so that a trait may have a rationale even when nothing in the lineage represents anything, and we can say that a mechanism "takes account of" some fact about the world while cheerfully conceding that nobody takes account of anything. I keep oscillating between finding this obviously right and suspecting that it is a manner of speaking which then does illicit work in biology, and I would rather see two people who hold the opposing views argue it out at close quarters than read another round of slogans. Write that dialogue.

Make the first speaker a philosopher of biology who holds that ascribing such rationales is not merely permitted but required — that without them we cannot describe what a mechanism is for, cannot say what has gone wrong when a mechanism misfires, and cannot explain why selection is anything more than a filter on noise. Make the second a philosopher of mind who holds that a reason must be a reason for someone: that where nothing represents anything there is only causal sensitivity, of the sort a river has when it "seeks" the sea, and that the first speaker's vocabulary smuggles in a subject of experience, or at least a subject of normative assessment, that is simply not present. Both are to be competent; neither is to be a strawman; each should meet the other's strongest formulation rather than the most convenient one, and each should be willing to concede a point when the other has earned it.

Let them work mainly through two cases I have been unable to settle. The first is a small marsh bird that goes on stuffing insects into the gaping mouth of a fledgling several times its own size — a nestling that is not its own, whose mouth is a supernormal version of the cue that the bird's provisioning rule responds to. The rationale, if there is one, looks like something on the order of "a wide, brightly coloured, wide-open mouth means one of my chicks, and my chicks must be fed"; it was true of the bird's own young and is false of the parasite, and the bird is not misled about anything in any sense it could be said to grasp. I want the two of them to disagree about whether this is a rationale that has been defeated, a rationale that is no longer there at all, or an episode that never needed rationales in the first place, and I want them to notice what their answer does to the second case: a boiler's safety valve, whose rationale is transparently something like "if the pressure gets too high, let some steam out," and which was fitted by an engineer for precisely that reason. The defender, I take it, will want to say that the valve's rationale is parasitic on the engineer's while selection's is parasitic on no one's, and I want the critic to press on whether that distinction can be drawn without helping oneself to the very notion of a reason's being present in a system. End the dialogue without a verdict, but with the crux the two have jointly exposed clearly in view — I care less about who wins than about locating exactly where the disagreement has to sit.
