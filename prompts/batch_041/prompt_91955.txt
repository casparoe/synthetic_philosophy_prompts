I have been sitting in on a philosophy-of-social-science reading group since I retired from the bench, and last week two members had a disagreement I could not settle. The case they were discussing: a line of people each receives a small private clue about which of two options is better, then publicly picks one, seeing everyone's earlier picks but not their clues; by some point the later people follow the crowd even when their own clue points the other way.

The first member called this irrational herding — people are ignoring their own evidence and deferring to others, which is not thinking — and he noted that a single new public fact can flip the whole line, proof that the agreement was hollow. The second said no: if each person is a good Bayesian, then once the accumulated picks of others outweigh one weak private clue, following the crowd is exactly what rationality prescribes, so the behavior is rational and any defect lies in what the group ends up knowing, not in how anyone reasoned. The first replied that a procedure whose conclusions dissolve at the first new scrap of evidence cannot be rational to rely on. The second replied that this confuses the reasonableness of the step with the durability of the outcome, and that a crowd can converge on the correct option just as easily as the wrong one.

Then the first added a wrinkle that neither could resolve: in the cases that worry him, the early people's clues are not really independent — they read the same report, or talked beforehand — so the later people are treating one piece of evidence as though it were many. The second said that if the clues are correlated, this is not the phenomenon the formal model describes at all; the first said that makes it worse, not better, since it is a cascade wearing the costume of independent confirmation.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: which of the first member's charges stick, which of the second's deflections succeed, where each is trading on an ambiguity — for instance between a step being rational and a group being reliable, or between independence as a modeling assumption and independence as a fact about the world — and what both are missing. I do not want a verdict on herding in general, only on the moves as they actually made them.
