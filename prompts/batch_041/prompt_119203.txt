The foundation I work for funds bridge-building work — deliberative forums, media-literacy courses, cross-partisan exchanges — and I need help with something that recurs every funding cycle. We receive around thirty proposals a cycle, four cycles a year, and almost all of them arrive with the same diagnosis attached: the applicant has identified a group that is, in their view, trapped in a bubble and cut off from information, and they want money to go and expose it to better sources. I have come to believe this diagnosis is frequently wrong in a way that matters, because two quite different conditions travel under the same name. In the first, people simply lack exposure: their information environment is narrow, they bear outsiders no particular hostility, and new sources can still reach them, because nothing in their situation prevents it. In the second, the narrowness is a symptom rather than the disease, and the disease is a trust structure. The group has been taught, usually by someone with an interest in doing so, that anyone outside the group is compromised, paid, or lying, with the result that contrary evidence does not merely fail to persuade but gets absorbed as further confirmation, and exposure to the outside world makes things worse rather than better. If that distinction is sound, the two conditions call for opposite remedies, and our current practice — treating every proposal as a request for exposure — is not merely wasteful but potentially harmful, since we would be paying for interventions that entrench the people they are meant to help.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my program officers can run on a proposal in about an hour. They will have no access to the target group's members, feeds, or private communications; everything they know comes from the application, a single phone call with the applicant, and public sources. The procedure should say what to ask and in what order, what would count as an answer that settles each question, where it should stop with a rejection, and where it must hand the decision back to a human because the question has turned out to be one of values rather than facts. It should also state plainly what it cannot determine, and it should flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as tests. Assume no one on staff has any philosophical training, that anything requiring more than an hour or an expert we do not have will simply not get done, and that grants above $25,000 need trustee sign-off, so a recommendation has to be defensible to a skeptical board.

Then run the procedure on three applications from this cycle and show your work. (i) A rural congregation that takes its news from one local broadcaster and a handful of social-media accounts, and that has been refusing a routine childhood vaccine, but that seems to hold no theory about the wider world — they have mostly just never encountered the other side. (ii) A dispersed network of former members of a high-demand wellness organization who have carried over from it a diffuse suspicion of all institutional medicine, and who treat any former member who has made peace with the mainstream as a traitor. (iii) A community of parents organized around one charismatic online figure, who circulate a standing list of which doctors and journalists may be trusted and who describe anyone who questions the figure as either stupid or bought. Each applicant is asking for $40,000 for an exposure intervention of the kind we normally fund; tell me for each what the procedure prescribes, including any case where it says to reject the proposal the applicant plainly wants, or to fund something they did not ask for. Finally, I have a worry that cuts the other way: some groups distrust institutions because the institutions have earned it, and I do not want a diagnostic tool that ends up telling us to go and repair the credibility of people who do not deserve repair. Say explicitly whether your procedure can distinguish manufactured distrust from warranted distrust, and if it cannot, say what a program officer should do instead of guessing.
