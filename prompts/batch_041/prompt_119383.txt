Right, I've just got back from the pub where I spent about three hours having an arguement with my mate Jon (he's a microbiologist, so he should know better) about whether viruses are alive. I need to get an exam question out of it before I lose the good bits.

The setting: third-year optional module, philosophy of biology, last week of term is the 'what is life' week. It's a 45-minute essay question in the final, so a good answer is realistically 600-700 words handwritten. 25 marks. I'm calibrating against a 2:1 as the decent-but-not-special answer.

What I don't want is the answer they all give in the seminar: list the criteria (metabolism, reproduction, heredity, evolution, being made of cells), say viruses are borderline, done. That's dead. I want them to have to commit to something.

So I've put together a case file of five things for the question, and I've invented the fourth one myself, because it isn't in any textbook I know of and I want them reasoning rather than remembering:

1. A flu virus: no metabolism of its own, needs a host cell to copy itself.
2. A mule: metabolises fine, sterile.
3. A prion: propagates its own shape, no nucleic acid, no metabolism, not an organism by any normal standard.
4. The invented one: a sealed flow reactor in a lab, containing a set of small molecules that between them catalyse their own production and have kept the thing going for years. No heredity at all, nothing passed on, no lineage, no variation. So it maintains itself indefinitely but could never evolve.
5. A self-replicating creature in a computer simulation: mutates, gets selected, its lineages adapt over thousands of generations, but there's no chemistry in it anywhere.

The question has to force them to say what the definition is actually for, whether we're after a checklist of criteria that sorts cases or a theoretical role ('life' as whatever turns out to play a certain part in some future theory), and whether 'life' being a cluster concept rather than a natural kind is a real problem or just a cop-out. And it has to force a verdict on 4 and 5 specifically, because they pull in opposite directions: 4 keeps itself going but can't evolve, 5 evolves but isn't chemical. The one the astro people like (a chemical system that maintains itself and can undergo Darwinian evolution) handles one of them and not the other, and I want them to feel that squeeze rather than recite the definition at me.

They also need to say something about the 'let's just wait until we have a proper theory of life' line, because Jon used it on me, I couldn't think of a comeback at the time, and it's definately still annoying me.

So: the exam question, a model answer of about 750 words, and a marking scheme. Five must-hit points, marks adding up to 25, and for each point one line on how candidates typically muck it up. Then a short note on what separates a first from a 2:1, i.e. the specific moves the first has that the 2:1 doesn't. No reading lists and no citations please, they can't bring anything in anyway and I don't want them name-dropping.
