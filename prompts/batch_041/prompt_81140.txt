ok, here's one I built for a reading group and I can't stop picking at it. Its meant to be a stripped-down doomsday argument with all the actual extinction-risk stuff taken out, so it's just the skeleton. Every line looks fine to me, and the last one is that we're probably cooked within a few centuries.

(1) I have no special information about where I sit in the history of my species. I'm not entitled to assume I'm early, or late, or typical in any way beyond what I can actually derive.
(2) So I should treat my birth rank as a uniform draw from the ranks of all humans who will ever be born. If there are N of us in the end, the chance of landing on my particular rank is 1/N.
(3) I'm roughly the 100 billionth human.
(4) 1/N is bigger for small N, so my being number 100 billion is far more likely if N is, say, 200 billion than if N is a hundred trillion.
(5) So N probably isn't much past 100 billion, and the end is close.
(6) Notice none of that used biology, or AI, or nuclear war, or anything empirical at all. It's pure armchair reasoning, so it should move my credence whatever I think the real risks are.
(7) Two things nag me. First, I can't see why my reference class should be humans rather than mammals or conscious observers, and the answer moves a lot when I change the label, which feels like a bug rather than a feature. Second, this is the same move as picking up a book, seeing you're 100 pages in, and guessing it's about 200 pages long, which is a heuristic that actually seems to work. So why does it spit out something I can't believe when I point it at us?

Which line do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd rather not loose (2), because if I drop the sampling assumption I don't see how any self-locating reasoning gets off the ground at all. But tell me where the crack is.
