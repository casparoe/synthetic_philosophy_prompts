I would like help with a decision about which I have been given conflicting advice, and I would rather have the underlying issue sorted out than be told what to do.

Three years ago I sold my share of a small firm and put the proceeds, about £400,000, into cash. Since then a former colleague has brought me a series of deals, roughly one every few months. Each has the same shape. I choose an amount; a few weeks later that amount comes back either multiplied by 1.5 or multiplied by 0.6, the two outcomes equally likely, independent of one another and of anything I do. There is no ceiling on what I may put in, and the rest of my money simply sits in cash, which in real terms does nothing.

I have declined every one of them, and my colleague is now openly exasperated. His argument is this: each deal taken on its own is worth taking, because on average I finish 5% ahead; therefore anyone who prefers more money to less should take every deal and stake as much as possible on each. Declining, he says, is leaving money on the table, and doing it over and over is that mistake compounded.

I can follow the arithmetic and I cannot find the error in it, but I cannot bring myself to act on it. When I picture doing what he recommends — putting the whole £400,000 in, every time, for as long as the offers keep coming — I picture a line that rises and falls and eventually reaches zero. He means it literally: he expects these offers to continue for decades, perhaps a hundred of them in all. I must also take roughly £25,000 a year out of the pot to live on, whatever the deals do. And I should admit that I have not been checking his figures against reality: I have watched about a dozen of these deals play out, and while they have been roughly even, a dozen is not many, and the 1.5 and the 0.6 are his numbers rather than something I have measured.

A second friend tells me the difficulty is that I am treating money as though it were worth the same at every level, when in fact the difference between £300,000 and £400,000 matters to me far more than the difference between £1.3 million and £1.4 million; once I take that seriously, he says, the case for staking everything evaporates. That reply leaves me uneasy, because it appears to make the answer a matter of my personal appetite for risk, as though someone with a stronger appetite would be right to stake the lot. I suspect a different reply is available — one that says something is wrong with the plan itself, not merely with my feelings about it — but I cannot state it.

What I would like, in order:

1. A concrete recommendation: what fraction of the pot should I stake on each deal, given the figures above, and how the answer changes once you allow that there are perhaps a hundred deals rather than infinitely many, that I am withdrawing from the pot each year, and that I am betting on an estimate of the odds rather than on the odds themselves.

2. A statement of what, if anything, is wrong with my colleague's principle — and, just as importantly, a fair statement of what is right about it, since I suspect he is not simply confused.

3. Your view on whether the correct stake is a fact about my situation or an expression of my preferences, and what follows for the second friend's argument. If the two replies I have been given are genuinely different, I want to know how they differ in their practical advice and not only in their vocabulary.

4. A clear account of the sense in which a bet can be favourable on average and yet be a bad thing to do repeatedly, and of whether the relevant average is taken over many versions of me or over the years of my one life — including whether that distinction does any real work or is merely rhetorical.

I am not asking for a reading list. I would rather have the reasoning, with the numbers worked through, than a survey of positions.
