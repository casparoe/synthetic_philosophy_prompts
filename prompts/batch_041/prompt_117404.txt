I took up philosophy in retirement — thirty years in soil chemistry before that, mostly trace-metal work, field kits and lab benches — and I've got alot of gut verdicts about knowing things that I can't get to line up. I'd like you to find the principle behind them, or the least bad set of principles. One warning up front: I'd rather throw out one of my own verdicts than accept a principle that needs a special clause for every case. I spent a working life throwing out data that needed an epicycle.

Here's the picture I keep coming back to, from the bench. We never asked "is this reading true," we asked what the instrument would have read if the answer had been different. A method with a silent failure mode — one that reads normal in exactly the cases where the thing isn't there — is worse than useless. So my tentative principle: you know something when your belief is a detector for it with no silent failure. It reads yes when the thing is there, and it would have read no if the thing hadn't been there. A friend who teaches this stuff says I've reinvented a view of Nozick's, and that its fans pay for it by dropping closure. Fine, whatever it's called — I want the best version of it, not the label.

Now my verdicts, and where the picture and I part ways.

1. I know I have two hands. I dont know I'm not a brain in a vat. But I won't give this up: if I know the sample is clean, and I know clean means no lead, then I know there's no lead in it. Deduction has to carry knowledge through, or the lab falls over. I gather 1 and 2 are supposed to be what kills that third one, but I want it shown, not asserted.

2. My field kit never misses contamination — lead above threshold, purple, every time. It does cry wolf now and then on a clean sample, call it one in twenty. So: kit stays clear, I say I know the sample is clean. Kit goes purple, I don't think I know it's contaminated, not without a second method. Is that asymmetry real, or am I fooling myself? And if it's real, where's the line between one in twenty and one in a million?

3. I know my own name and where I was born. The picture seems to say I can't — if I'd been born elsewhere I'd have been told, and I'd believe that instead, so there's a silent failure. Same for the sun coming up tomorrow: if it weren't going to, I'd still be sitting here expecting it. I'm not giving those up. Field work is one long bet that the world keeps behaving, and I'd say I know the assay will work tomorrow, not just that I hope so.

4. My brother-in-law is certain that 7 times 8 is 56 because a fortune teller told him. That couldn't have been otherwise, so the counterfactual is free, and my picture hands him knowledge. Absurd. So maybe I need a clause that the belief has to be driven by the fact and not merely covary with it — but does that clause wreck case 2, or the hands? And doesn't it land me somewhere worse, needing a story about how arithmetic gets into a man's head?

5. A valley I surveyed years ago: another species there mimics my target's song perfectly. Half the records I wrote were wrong, and I'd have written the same thing if the bird hadn't been there. I don't think I knew, on any given song, that it was the target species. I've read about a barn version of this and agree with the verdict there. Does the picture get it right for the right reason — my method had a silent failure — or is the real problem that the valley was rigged against me?

So: the principle, stated precisely, with each clause pinned to the case that forces it. Tell me which verdict of mine it can't save, and why I should drop that one rather than bolt on another clause. Tell me whether closure survives in any restricted form — I'd like it to. And give me two or three cases I haven't mentioned where your principle returns a verdict that will surprise me, and commit to it.
