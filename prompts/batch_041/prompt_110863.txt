I'm in a reading group on formal epistemology and decision theory, and last week we spent a session on how to model disagreement between experts. At one point, after a long back-and-forth, someone cut the discussion short with a line I've now heard a few times: that rational agents cannot agree to disagree. In context it was being used to argue that the persistent, widely reported disagreement between two camps of economists over the employment effects of a minimum wage — both camps well informed, both apparently reasoning carefully, neither budging over decades — shows that at least one camp must be irrational, or must be ignoring evidence the other has. I didn't want to argue on the spot, because I suspect the line is doing several different jobs at once, and that the interesting work lies in separating them.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict on the slogan as a whole. Lay out the distinct precise claims that 'rational agents cannot agree to disagree' could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical: the near-tautologies about agents who share a prior and share their information; the version that is a genuine theorem about what can and cannot be common knowledge between two agents; the version that treats the common prior not as a modelling convenience but as a substantive claim about how actual people's credences are distributed; the normative version on which any surviving disagreement is a defect of rationality; and any further readings you think are in circulation, for instance ones framed around betting, trade, or the difference between agreeing to disagree and agreeing to disagree about the prior. For each reading, I want to know what exactly it asserts, what further assumptions it needs — common knowledge of rationality, common knowledge of priors, a shared information partition, a single well-defined event under dispute — and whether it is true, false, trivial, or substantive but contested. Note also, where it matters, whether the claim is about probabilities or about outright belief, and whether it concerns the fact of disagreement or only the possibility of that disagreement being common knowledge.

Then tell me which of these readings would actually license the move my reading-group colleague made. Does any of them show that the two camps of economists cannot both be rational, or does the most that follows amount to something considerably weaker — say, the impossibility of their disagreement being common knowledge under a particular formal idealization? I'd also like an account of what the slogan leaves out: what the underlying result is simply silent about regarding the real causes of persistent expert disagreement (differing priors, private evidence, differing models of the other's rationality, incentives, and so on), and whether, once that silence is made explicit, the one-liner is a genuine objection or just a conversation-stopper.
