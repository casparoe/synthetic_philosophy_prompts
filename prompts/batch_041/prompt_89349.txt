I'm a novelist — three books, one of which actually sold okay — and through a long boring set of circumstances I've ended up as the "practitioner" member of the thesis board for a small PhD program in creative writing. We've got a case on Thursday that I can't stop chewing on, and I have to circulate a short written opinion before we meet. The candidate's critical dissertation is genuinely strong: it's a long, careful argument that there's no such thing as craft knowledge that isn't really propositional knowledge in disguise — that whenever we say a writer "knows how" to do something (pace a scene, withhold a reveal, make a sentence land), we can in principle always spell that out as a set of rules or facts, so the supposed knowing-how is just knowing-that, compressed. It's smart, it's definately well argued, it might even be right. The problem is the novel that comes with it. Two of my fellow readers describe it as "technically flawless and completely dead" — every rule the dissertation endorses is dutifully followed and the thing is unreadable. And at the viva last week, when we asked the candidate to just fix a paragraph on the fly, she couldn't. Froze completely. She could explain exactly why the paragraph was bad, in three different theoretical vocabularies, but she couldn't write a better one.

Our program's rulebook says a candidate must demonstrate "mastery of the craft," which nobody has ever bothered to define, and there are three possible outcomes: pass, fail, or pass with conditions (conditions meaning a revision period, usually six months, then a second defense; a plain fail ends the candidacy and, for what it's worth, her funding). The panel is split down the middle. One camp says the dissertation is the degree — the novel is a seperate artifact, and you don't flunk a philosopher of music for being a bad pianist. The other camp says the entire point of a doctorate in *writing* is that you can write, and a dissertation arguing that craft is just propositions, submitted by someone who can't actually craft, is practically a reductio of its own thesis. I lean one way, but I want this memo to be fair to the other side rather than a hit piece.

So: take the role of someone who actually knows the philosophical literature here — knowing-how versus knowing-that, skill, tacit knowledge, whatever's genuinely relevant — and think it through carefully before you write anything. Start by working out, for yourself, the strongest version of each camp's position, and be honest that there may be a reading on which the candidate is right and the panel's discomfort is just snobbery about novels. Then draft the written opinion I'll circulate: a clear recommendation of one of the three outcomes, the reasoning behind it, a concrete process (a timeline, and what the conditions would actually require if you go that route), and a serious engagement with the camp you're ruling against — the losing side should recognize its own argument in your summary of it. One more thing: I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and the board will smell it instantly if this reads like a journal article, so keep it in plain prose without dumbing down the argument. And at the end, tell me how confident you actually are in the recommendation and which single step of your reasoning you're least sure about, so I know where to push back in the meeting.
