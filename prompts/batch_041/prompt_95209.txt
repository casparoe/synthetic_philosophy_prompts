I'm a postdoc splitting time between a lab-adjacent AI safety group and a philosophy of science reading group, and in three weeks I have to present a paper-in-progress to a mixed audience and I want to be ready for the Q&A, not just the talk. The thesis, roughly: control-style safety measures — untrusted monitoring, honeypots, capability restrictions, trusted editing, that whole toolkit you reach for when you assume the model might be scheming — get sold as a fallback that keeps you safe when alignment fails, but they quietly rest on two assumptions that pull against each other. First, that misalignment is a stable property of the policy which shows up in behaviour we can elicit and score; second, that we can keep the elicitation conditions out of the training loop. If both hold, the control evidence is diagnostic; if the second one fails — because the model gets trained, or RL'd on the outputs, or just read by people who've absorbed your protocol — the tests become a selection pressure rather than a measurement, so "the monitor never caught anything" gets less informative the more you lean on it. I've been calling the two kinds of measure evidence-generating controls and fault-tolerance controls and claiming the field mostly conflates them (my office mate says that's just validation vs verification wearing a hoodie, which I don't think is right but I don't have a crisp answer yet).
