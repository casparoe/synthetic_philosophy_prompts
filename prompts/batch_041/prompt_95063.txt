I run a weekly AI-safety reading group at my company, and since I am the only member who has worked through any of the technical material, I have been put in charge of writing the problem sets. The group has just covered the universal prior: rank every hypothesis expressible as a program by 2 to the power of minus its length, and update by Bayes. For the next session I need one exam-style essay question that tests whether people can reason about what happens when the agents described by those hypotheses are themselves consequentialists, that is, when the agent must weigh hypotheses in which other agents built it, simulate it, and reward or punish its outputs.

To stop the question from being answerable by reciting the simulation argument, please build it around a modification that the question itself defines: the tilted prior, in which each hypothesis keeps its ordinary Solomonoff weight but is multiplied by (1 + alpha times N), where N is the number of consequentialist sub-agents inside that hypothesis whose utility depends on the simulated agent's behaviour, and alpha is a non-negative knob the student can turn. The essay should ask the student to work out what the posterior over "I am being simulated by consequentialists who will reward or punish me" does as alpha rises, whether the tilt can actually force that posterior to concentrate given that a hypothesis containing many consequentialist sub-agents is longer and so pays the length penalty, whether the resulting pressure is a defect of the prior or of consequentialism as such, and what an agent who is genuinely a consequentialist inside such a prior ought to do about it.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, with a one-line note on how a weaker answer typically goes wrong on each point.
