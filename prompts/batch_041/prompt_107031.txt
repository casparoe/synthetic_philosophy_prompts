I'm putting together a scene for a seminar on memory politics and I need a dialogue, not an essay, because I keep going back and forth on this and it's driving me nuts. Write a dialogue between two members of a city comission deciding what to do with a former detention center that's now a museum. One, call her A, is a memory ethicist in the Margalit line: she thinks communities owe the dead and the disappeared a duty of remembrance, that forgetting is a kind of second death, and that inherited memory is part of what makes a moral community at all. The other, B, is a Rieff-style skeptic: he thinks collective memory is mostly a trap, a mix of kitsch, grievance, and inherited hatred, and that a healthy polity needs the ability to let things go, not because the past didn't happen but because remembering everything is not the same as justice. They're both smart, both well read, neither is a strawman.

The commission has to vote on a specific proposal: install an interactive AI exhibit that lets visitors ask questions of holographic survivors reconstructed from testimony. The catch is that when the archive is silent the system generates plausible answers in the survivors' voices, it confabulates. The vendor calls this generative empathy; the museum's historian calls it lying. A says the exhibit betrays the dead by putting words in their mouths and turning testimony into improv. B says it's actually the perfect monument to forgetting, because it papers over the gaps and gives people a smooth, usable past they can consume without guilt. Let them go back and forth about whether the duty to remember is owed to the dead, to descendants, or to truth; whether forgetting can ever be an act of respect rather than erasure; whether an AI that confabulates in a victim's voice is a new kind of witness or a new kind of desecration; and whether B's position can avoid the charge that it lets perpetrators off the hook. Don't have them just trade slogans, have each respond to the actual point the other just made, and let them find at least one place where they partially agree. End with each giving a one-sentence recommendation for how they'd vote, and a line on what the other's recommendation gets right.
