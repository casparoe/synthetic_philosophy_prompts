John Buridan, the fourteenth-century philosopher, is remembered less for a doctrine than for a picture: an agent — in the popular telling, a donkey — set down exactly between two options that are equal in every respect bearing on the choice, and therefore, it is said, unable to act, because there is no reason to prefer either. Whether Buridan told the story in that form is doubtful, but the problem it encodes, choice without preference, is genuine, and he is generally read as treating it as no deep puzzle for the will: on that reading the will is not a balance that must tip toward the greater apparent good, and it can act even when the reasons on the two sides are exactly equal.

Imagine him confronted with a debate that arose only centuries after his death — how decision theory should treat ties and the absence of preference. The textbook treatment of expected utility says that an agent facing two acts of exactly equal expected utility is indifferent, and that either act is permissible; notice that this says nothing at all about what the agent is actually to do. Against it stands a revisionary thesis: that a rational agent can never be genuinely indifferent between two options she must choose between, and that, absent some tie-breaking consideration, she ought to randomize, on the ground that a coin flip is the only response that gives the tied options the fairness they are owed, whereas an arbitrary pick does not.

What would Buridan have said? Would he have accepted the revisionary thesis, holding that the agent must find a reason or else resort to chance — or would he have held that the agent may simply choose, the choice counting as rational precisely because no reason determines it? Some contemporary writers press a related distinction, between mere picking (selecting among options one has no preference between) and choosing (selecting on the basis of a reason), and argue that only the latter is a full exercise of rational agency; say what Buridan would make of that distinction too.

Take a definite side on each question, reconstruct the strongest Buridanian argument for your verdict out of his commitments about will and intellect, and then name the single most serious objection to it and explain why it does not overturn the verdict. Give me a verdict rather than a survey of the options.
