For thirty years I studied signaling in birds and ungulates, and since retiring I have been attending a graduate seminar in philosophy, where I have become interested in whether the notion of an honest signal can be given a principled analysis rather than a merely ecological one. I would like you to help me extract a principle from my own verdicts on a set of cases. I regard the peacock's tail as an honest signal of general condition, and gazelle stotting as an honest signal of escape ability. I regard a bird's alarm call as honest about the presence of a predator but not as a signal of the caller's quality. I regard a student who passes a notoriously difficult qualifying exam as having sent an honest signal of competence. By contrast, I do not regard a man who burns a large sum of cash in public as having sent an honest signal of wealth, even though the act is costly and difficult to fake; it seems to me to signal something more like indifference or a willingness to be irrational. Yet I do regard an expensive engagement ring as an honest signal of commitment, even though it is merely a purchased object. I regard a firm's Super Bowl advertisement as an honest signal of resources and confidence but not of product quality, and a politician's risky vote as an honest signal of sincerity but not of competence. I also have a verdict that sits awkwardly with the others: I do not regard a startup founder's lavish office as an honest signal of anything except the ability to raise money, though it is as costly and as visible as the engagement ring.

A colleague has proposed that a signal is honest with respect to some quality just in case the cost of sending it is negatively correlated with the sender's possession of that quality, so that low-quality senders cannot profitably imitate it. That principle handles the peacock, the stotting gazelle, and the advertisement about resources, but it seems to make the burnt cash an honest signal of wealth and to leave the engagement ring unexplained, since a wealthy but uncommitted suitor can buy a ring as easily as a committed one. I suspect the missing distinction has something to do with whether the cost is a direct consequence of the quality signaled, whether it is a mere payment, and whether it is a transfer that alters the sender's future incentives rather than a destruction of value. But I cannot get the cases to line up under any one formulation, and I am not sure whether the fault lies in the principle or in my verdicts.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures the pattern, stating each clause precisely and identifying the case that forces it. Then apply your preferred principle to cases I have not listed: a hostage exchange, a non-compete clause signed by a job candidate, a peacock whose tail has been artificially enlarged, a corporation that donates to charity only when its logo is displayed, and a person who publicly vows to donate a kidney. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should abandon that verdict or accept a more complex principle that saves it. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations to the literature; I want the analysis itself, in plain terms, so that I can see where my ecological instincts have quietly smuggled in a moral judgment.
