My friend and I have been arguing about punishment for about two years now and I want to write up the document we should have written after the first month. She's a retributivist: the state may punish because, and only because, someone deserves it, and the amount has to fit the wrongdoing. I'm the deterrence person: hard treatment is a cost we impose to buy something (less crime, denunciation, norm repair), and if it buys nothing we should stop. We agree on basically all the facts about prisons and recidivism and we still can't agree on anything normative, so I want the adversarial-collaboration version — a numbered list of claims we can both sign, then a disagreements table.

What I want in the agreed section:

- Claims about what we're even talking about — hard treatment imposed by an institution for a breach of a rule, plus the fact that there are non-punitive things the state does that hurt just as much (civil commitment, surveillance, deportation) — that neither of us has to give up.
- Everything we both accept about the *object* of justification: the institution of punishment as a whole, a rule or practice, and this particular sentence handed to this particular person. I suspect half our fight is that we keep sliding between these three and answering different questions without noticing.
- Whatever we both accept about deterrence, framed as predictions where that's more honest than as findings: that certainty seems to matter more than severity, that the curve is probably non-linear and maybe has a threshold below which nothing happens, that only marginal deterrence counts for the last year of a long sentence, and that our best evidence is observational and confounded. Neither of us should have to pretend to know more than we do.
- A short section of claims we both accept but use in opposite directions. I think she treats the principle that nobody should be punished more than they deserve as the foundational constraint, whereas I treat it as a rule of thumb that happens to be institutionally handy. If there are others like that, list them.

Then the disagreements table, with a column for the crux, a column for the formulation each of us would sign for our own side, a column for the case or argument or evidence we both agree bears on it, and a column with each side's prediction written down in advance. The cruxes I think are actually there, though add or kill as you see fit:

1. Is desert a reason for the state to act at all, or only a limit on how far it may go? Give me the version she can sign that says reason and the version I can sign that says limit, without either being a strawman.
2. Is the retributive claim about pain or about censure? If hard treatment turned out to add nothing to a public, credible denunciation — if everyone took a purely symbolic sanction as seriously as prison — does she still want the prison time? Her prediction, mine, and the case we'd both accept as bearing on it.
3. The scapegoat case, and its cousin: an offence that can't recur, say the only person who could do it is the one who did it, and it's over. Same sentence as before or not? What does each of us predict the other will say, and what would actually change either of our minds?
4. Timing. She justifies the sentence by what was true when the offence was committed; I justify it by what was expected when we chose it. Can you state the crux so it's clear whether this is substantive or just two different questions wearing one hat?
5. Proportionality: culpability-anchored or harm-anchored, and whether either of us is committed to anything we'd have to abandon if the two came apart.

If the honest landing spot is a hybrid — desert sets a floor and a ceiling, forward-looking stuff picks within the range — say so, and then tell me what's left to disagree about, because I want to know whether that's a real position or the two of us quietly giving up. And if part of this turns out to be a verbal dispute about the words punishment or justification, say which part and why. Also flag any agreed claim that only works because it's vague, since I'd rather have six real agreements than twelve that are doing no work.
