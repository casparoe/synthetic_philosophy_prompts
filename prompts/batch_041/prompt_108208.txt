I have been defending the following argument in a reading group on solution concepts, and it now strikes me that it proves too much. I cannot locate the bad step, and I would like you to find it and tell me what it costs to give it up.

The argument concerns what a rational player may do in a finite game, and it runs as follows.

(1) A rational player maximizes expected payoff relative to her beliefs, so she will never choose a strategy when another of her strategies yields a strictly higher payoff against every combination of the others' choices. Call such a strategy dominated.

(2) The point is not confined to pure alternatives. If a probability mixture over her remaining strategies does at least as well as some strategy against everything the others might do, and does strictly better in at least one case, then playing that strategy is equally indefensible: a lottery that is never worse and sometimes better is simply a better plan, so it dominates the strategy just as a pure alternative would.

(3) A dominated strategy may therefore be struck out. And since the others are rational too, a struck-out strategy should carry no weight in anyone's beliefs; so we may delete it from the game and apply the test again.

(4) Deleting in this way until nothing further can be deleted leaves exactly the strategies a rational player could use.

(5) Hence the survivors constitute the rational prediction for the game.

Now consider the following game. The row player chooses T, M, or B; the column player chooses L, C, or R; entries are (row payoff, column payoff).

|   | L | C | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T** | (1, 1) | (1, 0) | (1, 1) |
| **M** | (2, 1) | (0, 1) | (2, 0) |
| **B** | (0, 1) | (2, 1) | (2, 0) |

The trouble is that steps (4) and (5) behave badly here. If I delete the column player's dominated strategies first, I am left with the profile (M, L). If instead I delete the row player's dominated strategy first and then the column player's, I end with the row player free to play M or B and the column player free to play L or C. So the survivors depend on the order of deletion. Worse, the strategies the procedure throws out are not obviously irrational: in the first order it discards B, even though B is the unique best response to the belief that the column player plays C; and the row strategy it discards in the second order, T, is a best response to the belief that the column player plays L and C with probability one half each, against which T, M, and B all yield the same payoff.

I am confident of (1) and would hate to lose it, and (2) has always seemed to me a plain piece of common sense. Tell me which step is doing the damage, why it looked so plausible, and what I have to give up if I reject it. In particular, say what becomes of the strategies deleted in each order, and whether any version of the intuition that one should not play a never-better strategy can be salvaged.
