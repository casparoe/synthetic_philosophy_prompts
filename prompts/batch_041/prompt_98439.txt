Okay so I'm a novelist — historical fiction, four books in, currently deep in one set around a famine — and I keep bumping into this move that historians make now where they want to treat a volcano, or a plague, or a run of bad weather, as an actor in the story. Not a backdrop. An actor. Some of them go as far as 'agent.' I read it and half of me nods and half of me wants to argue, becuase making non-human things carry narrative weight is literally my job, and I know the tricks, and I know when the tricks are lying.

So here's what I want. Restate this whole historians' debate in my vocabulary — the real working vocabulary, not the watered-down version: character vs. setting, motivation, POV, what a character wants, plot, dramatic necessity, foreshadowing, and the difference between a thing that causes events and a thing that chooses them. Take the strongest version of the 'volcano as actor' position and the strongest version of the objection — that this is just environmental determinism in a nicer coat, that it quietly lets the humans off the hook — and do them both, faithfully, in my terms. Don't just swap the words; tell me what the swap forces you to change.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. What comes through cleanly? What only comes through if you fudge it? What can't be said in my vocabulary at all? And for everything that falls out, tell me whether it fell out because a novelist's way of talking about agency is too thin to hold it, or because the historians were hand-waving and there was nothing solid there to begin with. My hunch, which I'd like you to test: I suspect 'actor' is often just the thing the plot needs, dressed up. Is that right, or am I missing something the historians can see and I can't?

I've got no theory training, so plain language — but don't dumb it down, I can take the real argument.
