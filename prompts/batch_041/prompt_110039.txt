I chair the house council of a four-person cooperative, and I have been asked to arbitrate a dispute between two members. Before I rule, I want to understand what is actually wrong with our rules, because the same rules are about to be copied into a second house and I would rather not enshrine something that cannot be applied.

The dispute is this. Ana eats meat in her own room; Bo has a large collection of violent video games and plays them in his room in the evenings. Neither member's conduct reaches the other in any physical way: the rooms are at opposite ends of the house, the meals are eaten alone, the games are played through headphones, and neither has ever complained of noise, smell, or mess. Each objects to what the other does on principle. Bo thinks it is wrong for anyone to eat meat; Ana thinks it is degrading to spend one's evenings with violent games. Each also insists, whenever their own conduct is the subject, that a member's room is that member's own business.

Our charter contains two clauses we have always treated as obviously correct. The first says that what a member does inside that member's own room is for that member alone to decide, and that the council should not overrule it. The second says that when every member prefers one arrangement of the house to another, the council should adopt the arrangement that everyone prefers.

Now consider four arrangements: both do their thing; only Ana eats meat; only Bo plays; neither does. Ana's order, best to worst, is: only Ana eats meat; neither; both; only Bo plays. Bo's order is the exact mirror: only Bo plays; neither; both; only Ana eats meat. Apply the first clause to Ana, and the arrangement in which she eats meat beats the arrangement in which she abstains, with Bo's games held fixed either way. Apply the first clause to Bo, and the arrangement in which he plays beats the arrangement in which he abstains, with Ana's meals held fixed either way. Apply the second clause, and since both prefer "neither" to "both," neither beats both. Put the three results together and I get: both beats only-Bo-plays, only-Bo-plays beats neither, and neither beats both. That is a loop, and a loop is not a ranking of the arrangements at all.

I have checked this several times and cannot find an arithmetic error. What I want is not a way to settle this particular quarrel — I can manage that with a rotating schedule — but a diagnosis. Which clause, if either, is the one that has to go, and what would the house lose by giving it up? Is there a third way of writing the rules that preserves what is right in both clauses, and if so, what does that third way cost? And does it matter that neither member is harmed by the other's conduct, or would the difficulty be the same if they were? I care most about keeping the first clause — it is the reason most of us joined — so I want to know exactly what I would be buying, and what I would be paying, if it had to go. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning: no preamble, and do not restate my question.
