I am a third-year undergraduate reading Philosophy and History of Science, and I am currently taking a module on the demarcation problem and pseudohistory. My department is hosting a public panel next month on the theme of contested origins, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks. The panel will consist of a historian of ancient Mediterranean archaeology, a philosopher of science, and a popular writer who defends the idea that Atlantis is a distorted memory of a real prehistoric civilisation. The audience will be mixed: roughly half will be academics and students from the department, and the other half will be members of the public, including a local society that is sympathetic to the new chronology thesis—the claim that conventional ancient history is largely a duplicate of mediaeval history—and to ancient-astronaut ideas. I have to welcome everyone, set the tone for a civil discussion, and explain what will count as a serious historical argument, without either debunking the panel's more controversial guests in advance or pretending that all claims are equally well supported.

Please act as a professional philosopher of science with expertise in demarcation and as an experienced speechwriter. You have no web access, so work from your general philosophical understanding and do not rely on specific sources. Think carefully about the rhetorical situation. Start by brainstorming, in note form, the demarcation criteria you will use, how each might be abused, and the particular traps of this occasion. Then write the speech itself, about seven minutes long, in British English.

The philosophical content must do structural work: I do not want a speech that merely mentions the demarcation problem and then says that we should all be nice. I want the speech organised around a small set of questions that any historical claim must answer—questions about testability, consilience with independent lines of evidence, predictive fertility, the handling of anomalies, and the difference between a research programme that grows and an ad hoc rescue that only protects a favoured conclusion. Let those questions determine the shape of the speech and what I can and cannot say. Apply them, lightly and without adjudicating, to two examples: the Atlantis claim and the new chronology claim. The speech should leave the audience with a clear sense of the standards, not with a verdict on the guests.

Do not cite any authors, works, or dates, and do not include any quotations. I cannot verify them, and the speech must not sound like a literature review. Avoid naming scholars or schools of thought; use the ideas themselves. Do not use a reading list, footnotes, or references. If you need an example of a fallacy or a criterion, describe it in plain language.

The speech should be warm enough to work as a welcome, firm enough to be worth hearing, and honest about the fact that some views at the table may not meet the standards being set. It should not sneer, and it should not engage in false balance. End with a sentence that hands the panel over to the moderator.
