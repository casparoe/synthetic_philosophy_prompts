I am a doctoral candidate working on the metaphysics of consciousness, and I would like your help with a fairly local problem rather than the standard zombie-and-qualia fare. Take illusionism to be the view that phenomenal consciousness — the felt, qualitative character of experience — does not exist as such; what exists is a certain kind of introspective representation, and that representation misrepresents our inner life as having phenomenal properties. The view is usually motivated by analogy with familiar perceptual illusions: as a straight stick half-submerged in water looks bent, so introspection delivers a seeming of phenomenality that outruns the facts.

Here is the wrinkle. Perceptual illusions are, at least in principle, correctable: touching the stick reveals that the appearance is misleading. The illusion of phenomenality, if illusionism is true, cannot be corrected in that way — no further introspective or cognitive access could expose the seeming of felt quality as merely a seeming, since any such access would arrive with the same seeming attached. Call an illusion hermetic when no further access could disconfirm it.

Give me one example of a conceptual issue — a single case, developed far enough to bite — that this asymmetry creates for the illusionist. I do not want a survey of the objection literature; I want one worked example.

For that example: (i) say precisely what the conceptual trouble is, and why it is conceptual rather than empirical; (ii) state what the illusionist's best reply would be and whether it succeeds; (iii) say whether the trouble is fatal or can be absorbed by the view; and (iv) identify the feature of the case that is doing the work, and what happens to the example if that feature is removed. Do not cite any literature or appeal to what particular authors have said; argue it from the materials I have given you. Take a definite position at each step and give your reasons — do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me.
