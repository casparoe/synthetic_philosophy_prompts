I'm leading a reading group on medieval political thought next week and I've hit a snag in the sedition question in the Summa (II-II q.42 a.2) that I can't think my way out of. I've stitched together the bits I care about below, all from the same article.

Objection 3: "Further, it is praiseworthy to deliver a multitude from a tyrannical rule. Yet this cannot easily be done without some dissension in the multitude, if one part of the multitude seeks to retain the tyrant, while the rest strive to dethrone him. Therefore there can be sedition without mortal sin."

From the body of the article: "Those, however, who defend the common good, and withstand the seditious party, are not themselves seditious, even as neither is a man to be called quarrelsome because he defends himself..."

Reply to objection 1: "It is lawful to fight, provided it be for the common good, as stated above (II-II:40:1). But sedition runs counter to the common good of the multitude, so that it is always a mortal sin."

Reply to objection 3, as far as my copy goes: "A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10)."

(My paste of that last reply cuts off right there, annoyingly.)

And from earlier in the question, his definition: "sedition, in its proper sense, is between mutually dissentient parts of one people, as when one part of the state rises in tumult against another part."

What I can't get straight: he says sedition is always a mortal sin, and he doesn't seem to be treating the tyrant case as an exception to that rule -- its that the tyrant case simply isn't sedition. But then what work is the argument in the reply actually doing? Is it a definitional dodge (sedition just means disorder aimed against the common good, so anything aimed at the common good can't be sedition by definition, and the always-mortal claim goes through vacuously), or is it a substantive judgement that resistance to a tyrant really does serve the common good? And if it's the first, does the whole thing become circular -- whoever wins gets to say they were defending the common good, since his test for defending the common good seems to be that you're on the side that isn't disturbing the peace?

Second thing, smaller: given that he defines sedition as being between parts of one people, does a unilateral uprising by one part against the ruler even fit the definition, or does it only count as sedition once the rest of the multitude splits over it? The objection seems to assume the latter (it's the split over whether to keep the tyrant that generates the dissension), but the reply doesn't obviously need that assumption.

Could you walk me through this -- what the reply is really claiming, which reading of it the text supports, and what a good objection to it would look like? I don't need a summary of his views on rebellion in general, I know that part; it's this specific bit of machinery I'm stuck on.
