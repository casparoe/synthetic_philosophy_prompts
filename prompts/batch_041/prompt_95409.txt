I became a naturalized citizen this spring and took the oath to support and defend the Constitution. Since then I have not been able to settle what, exactly, I pledged to. The document I swore to uphold was for the first decades of its existence the legal frame within which American slavery operated, and the abolitionists who called it a proslavery compact were not being unreasonable. Frederick Douglass began among them and later broke with them, arguing that the Constitution is in fact antislavery. I have written a short essay on this and would be grateful for your comments, especially on whether the central distinction I draw is a real one.

The essay:

Reading the Constitution Against Its Practice

Douglass's later position is usually summarized as: take the text as written and stop importing the intentions and habits of the men who wrote it. The Constitution never names slavery; it apportions representation by counting some persons as less than whole; it permits the importation of persons to continue until a fixed year; it requires that persons held to service who flee be returned. Read without the gloss of practice, Douglass held, none of this establishes slavery, and several clauses can be turned against it.

The trouble is that this method works too well. A sufficiently charitable reader can find antislavery implications in the guarantee of republican government, in the privileges and immunities of citizens, in the ban on bills of attainder, in the due process clause. If charity is the standard, the Constitution was antislavery all along and the Civil War amendments were largely redundant.

I therefore separate two things, which I call the readable Constitution and the usable Constitution. The readable Constitution is what the text can be made to yield by a reader determined to read it in its best light. The usable Constitution is what a person held in bondage could actually have pleaded in a court of the period, without amendment, with some prospect of prevailing. Judged by the second standard the document looks very different: the clauses that presuppose slavery serve apportionment and rendition, and the clauses that might have helped were read by the courts of the era as protecting property in human beings.

If that is right, then Douglass's argument is not straightforwardly textualist, and my oath is more ambiguous than I would like: I may have sworn to the readable Constitution while living under the usable one.

What I would like from you: is the readable/usable distinction a genuine one, or merely the familiar difference between a rule as stated and a rule as enforced? Does my second standard prove too much, since by it no pre-war clause could count as antislavery? Am I being unfair to Douglass by treating his case as textual when he also appealed to the Declaration and to natural rights? And does the essay's conclusion follow, or have I confused a claim about interpretation with a claim about what I am now obligated to do?
