Last term I sat in on a seminar on logical empiricism, and I have since been going back over Otto Neurath's picture language — the method of pictorial statistics he built up at the social and economic museum in Vienna in the 1920s and which was later renamed Isotype. What keeps pulling me back is the mismatch between the modesty of the technique and the size of the claim made for it. The technique is governed by a handful of austere rules: greater quantities are never shown by enlarging a pictogram but by repeating a single standard-sized one, so that a taller house never means more houses; the stock of signs is fixed and learnable; each chart is meant to carry one comparison rather than a tangle of them; perspective is avoided so that the comparison stays unambiguous. The claim is that this makes social facts visible to people who will not read statistics, across languages and levels of schooling, and that it slips past what Neurath took to be the metaphysical seductions of abstract prose — his whole outlook being that pictures unite where words divide. And yet Neurath himself would not quite call the result a language: when pressed he described it as a language-like technique, a helper that always travels alongside verbal text, which suggests he knew he was not in the business of building a full notation. Now, a friend in that seminar remarked that all of this is just the picture theory of the early Wittgenstein transferred from logic to statistics, and I cannot tell whether that is a deep observation or a pun on the word "picture." On the Tractatus line, a proposition is a picture because its elements are correlated with objects and because it shares logical form with what it depicts — it is a picture in virtue of its structure rather than its resemblance, and it cannot depict its own pictorial form, only display it. Neurath's pictograms are pictures in the ordinary sense: they look like what they stand for, and where they stop resembling — a standard mannikin standing for so many thousand workers — they lean on convention, on a legend, on words.

What I want, then, is a comparison of these two ideas of what a picture language is, and of what each assumes about how a sign gets its grip on the world. Does Neurath's method presuppose anything like pictorial form, or are resemblance plus a fixed convention enough — and if the latter, what makes the finished chart anything more than a decorated sentence? Is the equivocation between the two senses of "picture" harmless, two species of a single genus, or does it conceal a genuine disagreement about representation? I would also like the comparison pushed on the question of expressive power: whether a language of repeated unit symbols can manage negation, disjunction, generality, or counterfactual conditionals, or whether it is confined to reporting magnitudes and proportions, and what Neurath's own concession — that this was a helper rather than a language — concedes to his critics. Finally, tell me whether the two projects diagnose the failings of ordinary language in the same way: is the trouble with prose that it is logically opaque, or that it is abstract and therefore ideologically loaded, and do those two diagnoses recommend the same remedy? Argue it out, and give me a verdict on whether the Tractatus comparison illuminates the picture language or is a category mistake; if you think it is a mistake, say what the right thing to compare it with would be. I do not need references or a reading list — I want the argument, and I want you to be explicit about which features of each project are doing the work.
