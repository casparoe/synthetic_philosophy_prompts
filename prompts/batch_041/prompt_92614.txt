Reading around the units-of-selection literature, I have come to think that the controversy is a pseudo-problem, and I would like to be told what substantive question, if any, the participants could still be arguing about.

Here is the reasoning that convinces me. Write the change in the population mean of a trait as the sum of two terms: a between-group term, the covariance between group trait values and group fitnesses, and a within-group term, the average across groups of the covariance between individual trait values and individual fitnesses. Given a fixed partition of the population into groups, this decomposition is not an empirical hypothesis but an algebraic identity; it holds for any population, any trait, and any assignment of fitnesses. And where two descriptions of the same process are formally inter-translatable in this way, they are empirically equivalent, neither being more correct than the other. It seems to follow that any case of apparent group selection can be rewritten without loss of content as selection on individuals or on genes, and conversely; that the level at which selection is said to act is a bookkeeping convention rather than a fact; and that the relatedness-based condition for the evolution of altruism and the multilevel condition for it are one condition written in two notations, so that altruism cannot be evidence for one level rather than another.

I accept the algebra, and I accept that each notation can be recovered from the other. What I want to know is what remains. Please do not tell me about the sociology of the field; I am not asking why careers, journals, and schools of thought keep the argument alive. I am asking what claim a genic selectionist and a multilevel selectionist who both accept the algebra could still disagree about, and what would count as settling it. If the answer is that the disagreement is entirely verbal, then say which parts of it are verbal, and why the participants do not notice.
