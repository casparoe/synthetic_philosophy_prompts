I am preparing a seminar for a criminology master's programme on the presuppositions of blame and punishment, and I have come to think that the literature on free will is organised around a question it rarely states cleanly: not whether we have free will, but which varieties of it would be worth wanting, and worth wanting for the purposes we actually put the notion to. Before I settle on what my students should read, I want the specification rather than the contenders. Write me the desiderata that any account of the kind of free will worth wanting must satisfy. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it: the phenomena the account has to accommodate (deliberation from the inside and the way it presents its own outcome as open; the difference between the coerced and the merely caused; the intelligibility of regret, of resolution, and of the thought that one ought to have done otherwise), the distinctions it must not collapse (between being caused and being compelled; between an agent's reasons and the causes of those reasons; between the freedom that grounds desert and the freedom that grounds mere attributability; between what excuses an action and what exempts an agent), and the results it cannot contradict, whether from the sciences of decision or from our inability to have chosen our own constitutions. Mark which of these are genuine requirements and which are the prejudices of one camp, and say whether some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable; if the list itself turns out to be a result of that kind, that is what I most want from you. Note also, if it matters, that the requirements may be indexed: what is worth wanting from the standpoint of the deliberator need not be what is worth wanting from the standpoint of the blamer, and I would like to know whether that gap is a nuisance or the whole story.

One case of my own that any account will have to handle, and which I have not seen discussed in quite this form: two agents, Ravi and Nia, are identical in every respect at the moment before they act, and the histories of their worlds up to that moment are identical as well. In Ravi's world the decision that follows is fully determined by that history; in Nia's, a genuinely indeterministic event at the moment of choice tips it one way rather than the other. Everything either of them could deliberate about, everything their responsiveness to reasons tracks, and everything that figures in any explanation of what they did is the same. I cannot see what Nia has that Ravi lacks, and I cannot see why the difference, if there is one, would be worth wanting; if your list implies that there is one, tell me which requirement puts it there, and whether that requirement survives contact with the case. Feel free to add requirements I have not thought of, including ones that make trouble for the framing of the question itself.
