I spent 35 years as a field ecologist, mostly on grassland birds and small mammals, and since I retired I've been reading alot of philosophy. I'm now on the board of a land trust that owns a 1,200-acre former dairy farm. Our mission statement says we protect native ecosystems and reduce wild animal suffering. Those two goals may not fit together, and I have to write the memo for next month's vote on what to do with the farm.

The options are: (A) restore it to native prairie and wetland, which would bring back huge numbers of insects, rodents, and songbirds, most of whom die young or get eaten; (B) keep it as low-intensity pasture, which supports fewer wild animals but they tend to live longer; (C) sell it to a solar developer and use the money to protect an existing old-growth forest elsewhere, which would keep the farm from becoming habitat at all. The farm is mostly tall fescue right now, so it supports very little. The board's scientific committee has proposed scoring each option by something they call welfare-years per hectare: you add up every animal's positive and negative experiences over its life and sum across all the animals on the parcel. A rival group wants to use a seperate metric, deaths per hectare, or the number of animals who die in ways that seem bad for them. I think the metric choice might decide the vote, and I'm not sure which metric is even the right kind of thing to use.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend one option, with a concrete timeline and any conditions, and it should give the strongest argument for the option I end up rejecting. It also needs to say whether the mission statement is coherent: if habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering by reducing the number of animals born into lives that are mostly suffering, then protecting habitat and reducing suffering might pull in opposite directions. I don't want a literature review or citations; just the reasoning. Also, please don't assume that more animals is automatically better, or that fewer animals is automatically better. I'm a scientist, not a philosopher, so tell me plainly where the value judgments are doing the work.
