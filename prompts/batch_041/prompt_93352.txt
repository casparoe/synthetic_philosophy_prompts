I teach an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on AI and society, and next week's ninety-minute session is on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems. My students come from computer science, law, and philosophy, and most arrive with the phrase half-memorized: they assume a responsibility gap is simply a liability shortfall that better explainability or a human-in-the-loop will close. I don't want an explanation of the topic; I want a plan for teaching it.

First, the order. Tell me what has to be in place before the concept even applies — which of the distinct things that can go missing when an autonomous system causes harm must be separated first, and in what sequence, so that "gap" does not collapse into "diffusion" (responsibility real but spread thin) or "displacement" (responsibility relocated onto a deployer or operator).

Second, the carrying example. I want the session built on this case rather than the overused autonomous-vehicle trolley: a fulfillment center where a learning-enabled robot fleet, through nothing any engineer specified, settles into a congestion pattern that no single robot's policy encodes; one afternoon that pattern pins a picker against a conveyor and breaks her arm. No robot chose the pattern, no one foresaw it, yet the harm is real and traceable to the fleet's behavior. Say whether this case can bear the weight, and what it would need to.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will surface — the zero-sum intuition, the human-in-the-loop fix, the belief that opacity is the whole story — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off.

Finally, one closing question that reveals whether they can distinguish a genuine gap from its look-alikes, and what I should openly set aside at this level.
