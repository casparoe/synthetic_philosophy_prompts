I'm assembling a two-page proposal for a small summer grant in philosophy of AI — roughly three months of work — and I have a half-formed idea I want turned into something defensible. The tool-AI versus agent-AI dispute is almost always run as a claim about what gets built: systems that hold objectives of their own versus systems that are merely instruments. I think that framing keeps stalling because "agent" is being treated as an architectural or behavioral property, when the more promising reading is historical — a device counts as pursuing an objective partly because of how it came to be selected for that role, the way a biological trait counts as a function because of its selection history. If that's right, whether a system is an agent can come apart from anything you can observe in it: a tool that has been iterated on by an outer optimizer for its usefulness may already be an agent in the sense that matters, and a system with internal objectives may be a tool if nothing selected it for that.

What I want the proposal to do: state one question with a determinate answer — something like whether the tool/agent distinction can bear the weight the safety argument puts on it, given that the distinction lives in causal history rather than in behavior. Say why the existing positions on both sides leave this open rather than settling it. Set out what I would actually do across three months, and note that this is conceptual work, so the method has to be something other than reading more and thinking harder: what I would need to show about whether "tool" is a stable regime under competitive pressure, which cases I would test the reframing against, and what a counterexample would look like. Say why this reframing rather than the obvious alternatives, and what would count as the project failing. Be honest about whether three months is enough, and don't build the proposal around a literature review — I'll add the citations myself.
