Ok so I'm working through an intro metaphysics book (it's a perfectly respectable one, I just don't want to hold it up as the target) and I've hit the chapter on Leibniz, and I want to check the gloss before I take notes on it, because something about it feels off and I can't put my finger on what.

Here's roughly what it says, from memory. Leibniz's law — the principle of the identity of indiscernibles — is the claim that no two things can have all the same properties; if two things are exactly alike then they aren't really two things at all. It's the philosopher's version of "no two snowflakes are alike." Max Black's counterexample: imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, alike in every respect — same size, shape, colour, mass, temperature, the same distance from each other, empty space all around. Nothing whatever distinguishes them, and yet there are plainly two of them, not one. So the principle is false. You might try to rescue it by saying the spheres differ in their relational properties — one is to the left of the other — but the setup is perfectly symmetric, so every relational feature you can point at in one sphere is matched by the corresponding feature in the other; relations don't get you out. That leaves only haecceities, bare thisness, and hardly anyone wants to believe in those. Leibniz's own escape was to say that God would have had no reason to arrange things one way round rather than the other, so the situation can't arise — but that's his theology doing the work, not an argument. Bottom line: most philosophers now think Leibniz's principle is simply false.

What I want is an audit, claim by claim: what a Leibniz person would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong. Two things naggs me in particular. First, the symmetry move against relational properties. Fine, each sphere is the same distance from the other — but "being two miles from sphere B" and "being two miles from sphere A" don't look like the same feature to me, if you're allowed features that mention particular objects by name. Is that a legitimate move in this debate or a cheat, and does it matter whether the principle is supposed to range over all properties or only over intrinsic ones? Second, "the principle is false." I thought Black's example, if it works, shows at best that the principle isn't a necessary truth, and that it might still be true of the actual world — is that distinction doing any real work here, and does the "most philosophers" line survive? Also, dismissing Leibniz's reply as "just theology" seems cheap: the principle of sufficient reason is a whole thing, isn't it? If some of the gloss turns out to be wrong, tell me whether its bottom line still stands or whether it collapses with the errors. No reading lists, just the audit.
