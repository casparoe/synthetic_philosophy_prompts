I should be grateful if you would draft a question-and-answer brief for a paper I am presenting at our departmental work-in-progress seminar in three weeks' time. Please write in British English, and write it as a briefing document for me rather than as an essay. I know the literature, so do not supply references, reading lists, or suggestions for further reading; do not attribute positions to named authors; and do not put words into the mouths of real historical figures — keep to the unnamed interlocutors described below and paraphrase wherever paraphrase will do.

The paper defends a version of Moore's proof of an external world — the argument that, since here is one hand and here is another, at least two external things exist. I make no claim that it refutes the sceptic. My claims are three. First, that it is a proof: its premises are known, the inference is valid, and the sceptic has not shown either premise to be unknown, since the demand that I first prove the premises is a demand I may refuse without absurdity — proofs must start somewhere. Second, that the proof's real philosophical content lies in what I call the ledger asymmetry. Any exchange of reasons runs on two ledgers: what stands fast without argument, and what must be argued for. Both Moore and the sceptic can move items from the first ledger to the second; but a legitimate move is always locally motivated, by some particular thing's having gone wrong, whereas the sceptic's move is total and motivated by nothing in particular. A doubt generated by a global hypothesis that makes no differential prediction about the course of experience — the dreaming hypothesis is my example — is therefore not a doubt but a change of subject. Third, that this yields a constraint on challenges, which I call the same-currency requirement: a challenge counts as a challenge to a given item of knowledge only if it could in principle be answered in the currency of the warrant at issue. The sceptic's challenge, if granted, rules out every candidate answer in advance, and so challenges nothing in particular.

I know where I am soft. My position must permit legitimate global doubts — about my memory as a whole, or about my competence in reasoning — while forbidding the sceptic's, and I am not confident that my locality condition draws that line in the right place.

The audience matters more than usual. The seminar will consist of: a colleague of Wittgensteinian sympathies who thinks the proof is a category mistake, because Moore has misidentified the role that claims to know play in our language, and who will want to know why anyone thought a proof was needed at all; a formal epistemologist working on closure and on the transmission of warrant, who will press me on whether my premises can be known while the sceptical hypothesis remains a live possibility, and who suspects my manoeuvre is either a denial of closure or the familiar no-self-undermining move in new dress; a transcendental idealist who holds that the only respectable version of this argument is transcendental, and that my ledger is a psychological notion with philosophical pretensions; a historian of analytic philosophy who will object that I have sanitised the original into something it was never meant to be; a predictive-processing person from the mind group who will say my differential-prediction criterion begs the question; and a handful of graduate students, one of whom will ask what any of this has to do with conspiracy theories, testimony, or simulation talk.

What I need from you:

- Eight to ten objections, ranked by the product of how likely each is and how much damage it would do, with a line for each saying which of those two factors drives its ranking.
- Each objection phrased the way that particular interlocutor would phrase it — the Wittgensteinian's version, the formal epistemologist's version, and so on — as a question or challenge I could actually be asked from the floor, not as a topic heading.
- For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes (roughly 250 to 300 words), followed by a blunt note on the weakest joint in that reply.
- Clear flags on the two or three where I should concede something rather than dig in, stating the exact concession I should make.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks, and what I should say if it comes.
- A short closing section for the last five minutes: if the chair cuts me off and I get only three questions, which three must I handle well, and in what order.

Aim for a document I can read in twenty minutes — useful rather than exhaustive — and assume I am competent but tired.
