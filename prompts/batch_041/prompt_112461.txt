I chair the medical policy committee of a mid-sized nonprofit health plan, and I need a written opinion to circulate before Thursday's vote. The matter before us is an appeal from the parents of a twelve-year-old boy whose height sits at roughly the first percentile. His endocrinologist's working diagnosis is idiopathic short stature: no syndrome, no identified endocrine pathology, just a persistent failure to grow along the expected curve. Our plan covers growth hormone for children with documented growth hormone deficiency, and our medical-necessity definition, which the hearing officer has already flagged as ambiguous, runs in two prongs. The first requires that the intervention address a defect or abnormality of bodily structure or function. The second requires that it be expected to restore the patient to the normal range for his age and sex rather than to push him beyond it. The family's physician says that a boy at the first percentile plainly has an abnormal growth axis and that treatment aims only at the ordinary range. Our utilization-review nurse answers that we routinely cover eyeglasses for mild refractive error and orthodontia for mild dental crowding, neither of which is a defect in any biological sense, so the first prong cannot be doing the work we pretend it is; and that the second prong is a rationing rule dressed up as a definition. There is a further wrinkle: our policy stops paying once the patient reaches the twenty-fifth percentile, and the parents want treatment continued to the fiftieth, on the ground that if the point is to relieve a disadvantage, stopping short of average is indefensible.

I recognize this as an instance of the therapy/enhancement distinction, but the textbook version is no help, since the case sits directly on the line. I would like a memo of roughly 1,200 words that gives the committee a clear recommendation and a rule we can defend publicly. It should say plainly whether to grant or deny the appeal, whether to pay to the twenty-fifth percentile or to the fiftieth, and what general policy the plan should adopt going forward, including an operational criterion specific enough that our claims staff could apply it to the next case without calling a philosopher. In reaching that bottom line it should engage honestly with the strongest position against it: our consumer advisory panel's disability-studies representative argues that covering treatment for short stature entrenches the very stigma that makes short stature a disadvantage, and that the plan should therefore decline to cover any of it. I want that argument stated at its best and then answered rather than waved away, and I want an explicit treatment of the alternative that the plan drop the language of defect and normal range altogether and adopt a straightforward test of whether the intervention is expected to improve the patient's own welfare.

Please also address, in whatever detail the argument requires, whether our plan's notions of a defect and of a normal range are descriptive findings or evaluative choices, and whether the second prong can survive the nurse's observation about eyeglasses. Write for a board that includes two physicians, a nurse, a benefits lawyer, an employer representative, and a patient advocate; the advocate and the employer representative have no philosophical training and should still be able to follow the reasoning. Do not include citations or a reading list — the board will not consult them, and the argument needs to stand on its own.
