Two colleagues of mine — Iris, a literary translator, and Theo, a contracts lawyer — spent a lunch break arguing about an incident a mutual friend had described, and neither would give ground. I stayed out of it, but I would like a proper adjudication.

The incident: a supervisor was phoned for a reference by a hiring manager, who asked whether the candidate was good with clients. The supervisor answered, "She's extremely well organized, and she never misses a deadline." The manager thanked her, then told HR that the reference had effectively confirmed the candidate was weak with clients, and the offer was downgraded. When the supervisor heard, she was indignant: she had said nothing false and nothing negative, had praised what she could honestly praise, and the manager had over-read a polite answer.

Theo: the manager read it correctly. Asked a direct question about client skills, the supervisor answered on a different topic, and under any cooperative reading of the exchange that non-answer implicates that she could not bring herself to praise client skills. Iris: then how can the supervisor sincerely say she meant no such thing? She proposed what she called a replay test — ask the supervisor to write out everything she meant by her sentence; the negative claim will not appear on the list, and what is not meant cannot be implicated. Theo: the supervisor's denial is a retraction, and the very fact that an implication can be retracted is the mark of an implicature — were it part of what her sentence said, retracting it would be contradicting herself rather than clarifying. He added that the insinuation survives paraphrase, since any similarly evasive bit of praise would have done the same work, so it is not a trick of her particular wording.

Iris then shifted ground: what a speaker implicates ought to be fixed by what a reasonable hearer, entitled to assume cooperation, must infer — not by the speaker's private intentions. On that view the supervisor implicated it whether she likes it or not, and her denial is backpedaling. Theo replied that this turns implicature into a fact about hearers, so that any reasonable but unintended inference becomes something the speaker meant; he would rather say the manager's conclusion was warranted and yet the supervisor did not mean it. Iris countered that if sincere denial could defeat an implicature, implicature would collapse precisely where it matters, into plausible deniability.

Please adjudicate: who is right about which claim, move by move — the supervisor's culpability, the replay test, the retraction and paraphrase points, and the final disagreement over whether hearers or speakers fix the implicature — and what, if anything, both are missing. No citations are needed; I want the argument sorted out.
