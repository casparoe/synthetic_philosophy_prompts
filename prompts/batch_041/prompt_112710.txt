I am assembling a methodological brief for a graduate seminar on multi-causal explanation in history, and what I want from you is a specification rather than a survey. The subject is overdetermination: outcomes that appear to have more than one cause, where each of the causes on its own would have been enough. Before I look at any account of what causation is, I need the list of what such an account must deliver if a working historian is to use it — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the verdicts it must be able to return, and the constraints it must respect.

Treat the following as stipulated. They are constructed for the exercise, nothing in them depends on the historical record, and I want the world assumed deterministic so that talk of a factor being sufficient for an outcome is unambiguous.

1. Redundant sufficiency. Two factors, each sufficient on its own for the outcome, both present and both operating; either alone would have produced it. (Two independent detonators, each wired to fire the charge by itself; both fire.)

2. Preemption. One sufficient factor operates and, in the course of operating, removes the second before the second can operate — though until then the second was equally sufficient for the outcome.

3. Joint sufficiency. Two factors, neither sufficient alone, jointly sufficient, each making a difference to the outcome. (Two conditions, each necessary given the other.)

4. Necessary but insufficient. A factor without which the outcome could not have occurred, which was nevertheless not enough for it.

5. Screening off. A remote factor brings about a proximate factor; the proximate factor is sufficient for the outcome; and every route by which the remote factor bears on the outcome runs through the proximate factor. The historian still wants to say that the remote factor is why the proximate one was there, and so why the outcome occurred.

6. Description relativity. One and the same process under two descriptions: under the first, the factor is a cause; under the second, it looks like a standing background condition. (The striking of a match under one description, the presence of oxygen under another.)

Now the requirements. For each, give me the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as something only one camp in the debate needs. I want at least the following settled by the list, though you should add requirements I have not thought of:

- Must the account return a verdict about the world, or is it permitted to return a verdict about what the historian can know or compute? I need the former; tell me what it costs.
- Can it deliver its verdicts without appealing to laws of nature or other covering generalizations?
- Does it explain why the redundant factor in case 1 counts as a cause while the preempted factor in case 2 is normally demoted — or does it deny the asymmetry and accept the consequence?
- Does it treat causation as relative to how the outcome is described? If so, what keeps historical explanation from collapsing into an artefact of our vocabulary? If not, what does it say about case 6 and about the standing practice of distinguishing causes from conditions?
- Can it underwrite unequal assignments of weight, that is, degrees of causal contribution, or must it declare such talk incoherent? If it can, what in the cases fixes the weights?
- What does it say about case 5: is the remote factor a cause of the outcome, or is causation cut off where the proximate sufficient factor begins? And does its answer to case 5 commit it to anything about case 2?
- Does it survive the point that if outcomes are individuated partly by their causes, the two sufficient factors in case 1 do not overdetermine a single outcome but produce two distinct ones, so that overdetermination is not a phenomenon at all? Say whether the account can accommodate both fine and coarse individuations, and what follows either way.

Finally, flag any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument rather than the assertion, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling accounts out. I do not need a survey of named positions or a reading list; if you mention a position, mention it only to illustrate what a requirement excludes.
