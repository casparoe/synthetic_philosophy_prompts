Ok, so I've been rereading LOTR and it struck me that Tolkien's ring is a much stranger answer to Glaucon than people give it credit for. Glaucon's ring in Republic II is a clean instrument: it strips away the punishment and the reputation cost of being unjust and leaves the person inside untouched, so the test is whether anyone would still be just when there's nothing in it for them. Tolkien's ring doesn't do that. It doesn't just hide you, it works on what you want, and the plot is basically one long series of characters being made an offer that gets harder to refuse the longer they sit with it. I want a reading of the book as an actual reply to Glaucon's challenge, taken seriously as philosophy and not as "the ring symbolizes power."

So: does the novel answer the question, or does it quietly swap in a different one? Frodo takes the ring on purpose, uses it, and at the end can't let it go. Does that vindicate Glaucon or dodge him, given Frodo isn't choosing injustice for its rewards so much as being eaten from the inside by the thing? Same question for Boromir (wants it for decent reasons, gets wrecked), Faramir (turns it down flat), Galadriel (passes her test and pays for it), Sam (carries it for a bit and hands it back), Bombadil (just doesn't care). Are those data about human nature under impunity, or about what the ring does to a person, which is a different claim entirely?

The part I actually care about: Gyges' ring removes the outside audience but leaves you as your own audience, whereas Tolkien's ring goes after the audience. So if the agent's preferences can shift while they're holding the thing, is Glaucon's challenge even well-posed? And what does that do to Socrates' answer that the just man wouldn't use the ring — is the book more Socratic than it looks, since the characters who come out clean are the ones who don't want it, or less, since the one who does the job right gets destroyed by it?
