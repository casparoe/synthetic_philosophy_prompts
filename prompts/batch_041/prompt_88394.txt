I am preparing a short note on the epistemic credentials of majority rule for a workshop, and I want my own position audited before anyone else gets to it. I would rather find the cracks myself than have them found for me. Here is what I hold.

(1) A decision procedure is legitimate only if it is at least as likely as any available alternative to get the answer right. Procedural fairness is not a substitute for this; at most it governs what we do when the epistemic case is a tie.

(2) Condorcet's theorem is the engine of the epistemic case for large electorates, and it is more robust than its critics allow. What the theorem needs is not well-informed voters but voters whose individual probability of getting the matter right sits on the correct side of one half; the individual shortfall is averaged out. Since a modern electorate is enormous, an average competence barely above one half is enough for practical certainty, so I do not lose sleep over voter ignorance.

(3) Critics of the theorem make too much of the independence assumption. Votes need not be independent of one another as they come in; it suffices that they be independent once the state of the world is fixed. What the theorem cannot survive is a shared cause of error — one source, one framing, one bias that many voters take up together — and that is a failure no increase in the number of voters repairs.

(4) On much of what actually appears on a ballot, I doubt the competence condition invoked in (2) is met. Voters are not merely ill-informed; on many questions I would expect a substantial share of them to be worse than chance.

(5) Deliberation probably raises average competence and also raises the correlation of errors. I have no settled view about which effect dominates, and I do not think the theorem can settle it either.

(6) Majority rule is nonetheless the right procedure for a polity, for reasons that have nothing to do with truth-tracking — something in the neighbourhood of equal respect.

(7) Some questions put to a vote — how much of a budget should go here rather than there — have no answer independent of the votes, and the theorem has no business assessing them.

Audit this. Which commitments are actually in tension with which others, and how badly? Rank the tensions rather than treating them as so many separate alarms; say where a tension is only apparent and why it dissolves; and identify the single revision that resolves the most while costing the least, together with what it costs. I am not antecedently attached to any one item on the list, so do not assume I would sooner give up any of them than any other. Where the mathematics of the theorem bears on whether two items can be held together, be explicit about which direction the result cuts.
