so at my local rationalist meetup last week somebody gave a talk about Moloch, and afterward two people in the back — Dana and Marcus — got into it, and I've been chewing on it ever since because I honestly can't tell who's right.

Dana went first. She said Moloch is basically just a fancy name for a bad Nash equilibrium — tragedy of the commons, arms races, prisoner's dilemmas, that whole family. If you can't point to the actual incentive structure that makes defecting individually rational for each party, she said, then you're not talking about Moloch, you're just moralizing about outcomes you personally dislike. She called it a thought-stopper.

Marcus said that's too narrow. He said alot of the cases people call Moloch aren't clean prisoner's dilemmas at all — sometimes nobody is really better off defecting, and everyone would be fine if everyone stopped, but the thing is held up by common knowledge and the fear of being the one sucker who quit. So the trap is partly epistemic, a self-fulfilling belief, not just a payoff matrix. He said if you only count cases with a strictly dominant defection you'll miss the ones that actually grind people down.

Dana came back: if literally no one gains by defecting and everybody wants out, then they can just stop — the trap dissolves. What Marcus is describing is a coordination problem, and coordination problems get solved by communication, focal points, credible commitments, that sort of thing. She said slapping the name Moloch on a coordination problem and throwing up your hands is the exact opposite of useful, it's fatalism dressed up as insight.

Marcus said coordination isn't free and isn't always credible, that's kind of the whole point. Assurance contracts are themselves costly, and even a coalition that could win has each member scheming to be the last one to join so they get the best terms. Then he introduced a distinction he called open traps vs. closed traps — open traps are ones where the option of not playing still exists (you could in principle delete the app), closed traps are ones where the option is basically gone (you need the smartphone, the credential, the weapon, to participate in the economy at all). His claim was that for a latecomer, "just stop" isn't on the table, so Dana's fix only works for open traps, and the closed ones are the scary ones.

Dana said "closed trap" is just a way of saying the costs of exit are high — which is still an incentive structure, which is still a game, so it's still her point, not a counterexample to it. And if the exit costs are high *because* everyone else is already in, that's a network effect, i.e. coordination again, not some mystical third thing. She accused him of smuggling in "there is no game here" when what he actually means is "the game has bad payoffs," which she never denied.
