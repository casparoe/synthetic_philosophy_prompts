Please write a comparative essay in American English on endurantism and perdurantism as competing accounts of how material objects persist through time. I am compiling a casebook for an advanced metaphysics seminar, and I want a piece that works through the arguments rather than surveying the slogans, so please treat the essay as self-contained: no citations, no reading list, no appeals to what a particular author is supposed to have said. Argue with the positions directly, and recognise that the elementary formulations are already familiar to the intended reader.

Address the following in order, and make the transitions between the sections explicit.

1. State the disagreement with more precision than the usual formula (an object is wholly present at each moment versus an object has distinct temporal parts). Say what each view is committed to about (a) what it is for an object to have a property at a time, whether the qualification attaches to the object's possession of the property, to the property itself, or to the manner in which the property is had, and (b) what change consists in. Indicate which commitment carries the explanatory weight in each view, and where each view risks circularity or an unexplained primitive.

2. Consider the following argument. Take a disc that is perfectly homogeneous, uniform in colour, density, and every other intrinsic feature, rotating about its axis. At any single instant its instantaneous state is said to be indistinguishable from that of an exactly similar disc at rest, so that nothing in any instantaneous state reveals the motion; yet the disc rotates. Assess this argument before you rely on it. Is the premise true? If an object's instantaneous state includes its velocity distribution, or if homogeneity cannot be specified without reference to parts and their positions, the argument may collapse; if it survives, the endurantist owes an account of how a wholly present disc can be moving at an instant at which its shape and material arrangement are exactly as they would be were it at rest. Set out what each view says about the case, and be candid about the price each pays: whether the endurantist must treat shape and mass as relations to times, and whether the perdurantist's appeal to distinct temporal parts genuinely explains the rotation or merely restates it in a longer idiom.

3. Analyse two objections in turn. The first is that the perdurantist never explains how temporal parts compose a single persisting thing: if the parts are held together by a further relation, the same question can be asked of that relation, and if they are not, the unity of the object is simply assumed. The second is that the endurantist's temporally qualified properties are not intrinsic properties at all, so that on that view nothing ever simply has a shape or a mass. Consider whether the second objection is a reductio or merely a revision the endurantist can absorb, and whether the perdurantist faces a parallel difficulty about the properties of temporal parts.

4. Then test both views against two stipulations, introduced here only to push the comparison past its standard exchanges. The parity principle: differences between the temporal and the spatial dimensions are to be treated as structural facts to be explained rather than as brute metaphysical asymmetries, so that a theory which treats time as formally on a par with space is, to that extent, to be preferred. The process-first constraint: what is fundamental are processes and activities rather than things that have histories, and continuants are at best stable patterns abstracted from those processes. Which view fares better under each stipulation? In answering, distinguish the version of perdurantism on which the persisting object is the four-dimensional whole, with the momentary stages as its parts, from the version on which the persisting object is the momentary stage itself and persistence consists in a relation of succession between stages. Say whether the second version escapes the objections raised in section 3, and whether either version genuinely approaches the process-first picture or merely extends a substance ontology into a fourth dimension.

5. Close with a verdict: say which account you take to be better supported, on what grounds, and identify one consideration that would lead you to abandon that verdict.

Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, in continuous prose rather than note form, written in American English. Define any term you introduce, and avoid rhetorical questions in place of argument.
