I do people ops at a company of about 600 and part of my job is vetting the incentive schemes that come down from leadership before they go to the exec team — comp-linked stuff, awards, the quarterly engagement thing. It's maybe four or five a quarter and there's alot riding on each one. I'm the one who signs off, so if a scheme quietly blows up in our faces it's my name on it.

The one keeping me up is the values bonus. Leadership wants to tie a chunk of everyone's bonus to 'living our values', and the wording they want includes genuinely believing in the mission and caring about the work, not just hitting the numbers. There's a culture champion award too. Seperate proposal: tie each manager's bonus to their team's psychological safety survey scores. And a third one: pay people a small bonus for reporting near misses on the factory floor, on the theory that we want them to actually believe the risk is real and not just comply. There's also a boring one where we pay people to sit through a compliance course, which I assume is fine.

A friend of mine has a philosophy PhD and when I described the values bonus she said this is a known problem — she gave me a case where a demon says he'll torture you unless you admire him, and the point is that you now have a reason to admire him, but it isn't the kind of reason that makes admiring him right, it just makes it useful. She said something about reasons that attach to the state you're in versus reasons that attach to the thing itself, and that an incentive is at best a reason to want the attitude or to bring it about, never a reason for it. Then she said it's contested, and that there's a real fight about whether it even matters how you came to have the attitude if you end up genuinely having it, and went to pick up her kid. That's as far as I got and I need something I can actually run.

So: write me a procedure my team can run on a one-page proposal, in twenty minutes, without any philosophy. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it hands the decision back to me or the exec team because it's a values call and not a facts call. Build in the hard cases: attitudes that are partly about why you have them, incentives that can be satisfied by the appearance of the attitude, incentives that work by changing what people know or what their job is actually like, and schemes that never touch the attitude at all but just hire for it or fix the conditions. It should come out yes sometimes — I don't want a machine that says no to everything.

Then run it, properly, on the values bonus, the psych safety one, and the near miss one. At least one should come out no in a way leadership will hate, and you should tell me straight which one that is and how I should sell it.

Two more things. Keep the theory out of the checklist itself — my team won't read philosophy and shouldn't have to — but give me a short note at the end on where the checklist is a simplification of something that's actually contested, and what the procedure can't do. And no reading lists or citations please; I'm not going to read them and neither is anyone on the exec team.
