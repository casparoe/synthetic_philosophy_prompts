I spent thirty-odd years in a physiology lab before retiring, and I have since been making my way through philosophy with the habits of someone who spent a career measuring things and remains suspicious of categories that do no work. I have been sitting in on a reading group devoted to weakness of will, and it has left me genuinely uncertain how the philosophical discussion relates to the behavioural-economics literature I thought I already understood from the outside. On the philosophical side the question appears to be whether an agent can act against her own best judgement at all, with the interesting positions differing over where the failure lies — in arriving at the judgement or in acting on it — and over whether the conflict is confined to a single moment of choice or runs between a person at one time and that same person later on. On the economics side the whole matter is handled as the shape of a discount function: people are described as present-biased, as reversing their preferences as a reward approaches, and the difficulty is then treated as solved rather than dissolved. I cannot tell whether these are two names for one phenomenon, two distinct phenomena, or one phenomenon and one redescription of it, and what I want is an instrument that sorts the available answers rather than an essay advocating one of them.

Please construct for me a decision tree — a taxonomy in branching form — that begins from something I take to be uncontroversial, namely that people very often do, knowingly and under no external compulsion, precisely what they sincerely said they would not do, and that ends at named positions on what is actually going on in such cases. Every branch node should be a question a reader can answer about a concrete case without already knowing which position it leads to, and it should be phrased in ordinary language rather than in the vocabulary of any single camp: whether the agent's evaluation genuinely changes between the two moments or remains the same while her conduct diverges from it; whether a momentary time-slice or a temporally extended person is the proper unit of agency; whether the later person is bound by the earlier person's judgement or is better pictured as renegotiating terms with her; whether the failure occurs in the adoption of a plan or in its execution; and whether the observed pattern of preference reversal is a fact about preferences themselves or an artefact of the way the delays were described and carved into intervals. I would like the leaves to include at least the view that clear-eyed weakness of will is impossible; the view that it is possible but always amounts to a failure of practical judgement rather than of will; the view that it is a genuine conflict of motives with judgement left intact; the deflationary economic view on which the phenomenon is redescribed as impatience and ceases to count as a failure of rationality; the view that treats the agent as a coalition of successive selves bargaining over one body; and the view that the real failure is diachronic — a lapse in executing a plan one still endorses — rather than synchronic. Six branch nodes and eight leaves should be about right.

Three further things, since I am new enough at this to be taken in by a tidy diagram. Mark any pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate, and say why it cannot; list the combinations of answers that go unoccupied, with a sentence for each on whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended; and give each leaf one line naming the commitment that an unsympathetic reader would find hardest to swallow. Finally, tell me which single branch you have least confidence in, and what argument, case, or empirical finding would make you redraw it — whether that would move a leaf to a different branch or split one leaf into two.
