Here's a case I want run through the truthlikeness framework rather than just summarized: the phlogiston theory of combustion getting replaced by Lavoisier's oxygen chemistry in the 1770s-80s. Take the usual facts as given -- metals were calxes plus phlogiston, burning was phlogiston escaping, and the awkward datum was that metals gain weight when calcined, which the phlogistonists handled by making phlogiston weightless or negatively heavy; Lavoisier said combustion is combination with a component of the air instead. Question: was Lavoisier's theory closer to the truth than Stahl's, and by which account of truthlikeness? Do it twice, once on a content/consequence account and once on a likeness/similarity account, and tell me whether the two agree. Then the part I actually care about: the 1974 result (Tichý, Miller, and Harris all hit it at once) that Popper's own definition of verisimilitude has the consequence that no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other false theory, plus the separate worry that you can reformulate the same body of claims in a different but empirically equivalent vocabulary and get the ranking to flip. If the verdict depends on which vocabulary you count consequences in, does the phlogiston case show that progress really happened, that truthlikeness just can't capture it, or that the comparison was never well posed to begin with? Be honest about which parts of the case are neutral, and don't make the framework speak where it has nothing to say.
