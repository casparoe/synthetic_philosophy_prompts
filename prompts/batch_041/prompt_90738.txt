For a novel I'm writing, I need to solve a problem of technique, and I suspect the technique has a name I don't know. Here is the pattern. Twice now I have read a piece of writing that never argues against a usage. Instead it invents a parallel vocabulary and then speaks it fluently for pages — not with a wink, not as a one-line joke, but in the calm register of an editorial or a style manual. In one of them, the modifier that in ordinary English attaches to a person's race only when the person is not white was applied to everyone alike, so that white readers met their own category as the one requiring a suffix; the compounds and coinages arrived so thickly and so unremarkably that the page read like an ethnography of the reader's own mouth. In the other, a feminine form served throughout as the ordinary word for a person, with the masculine reserved for the special, exception-carrying case, and this was sustained for several pages without a single break in frame. In both, what did the work was not the argument — there was scarcely any argument — but the fluency: the reader's own habits of speech, the words that go unlabelled because they name the assumed standard, were suddenly the strange ones.

Is there an established name for this device? I don't mean satire in general, and I don't mean allegory, where one figure stands for another; here nothing stands for anything, the lexicon itself is the argument. Nor is it quite a thought experiment, since those are usually conducted in the third person while this is performed in the first. I am confident linguists have a term for words that carry a label only when they depart from an assumed standard, and I want to know whether the rhetorical use of that fact has a name of its own or is merely a trick with no literature behind it. A name alone would not help me; what I need is the carving. Must the inverted word be a grammatical default rather than merely a socially dominant one? Must the piece be sustained rather than a single paragraph? Must the author never break frame? And is there a name for the characteristic failure, in which a reader who misses the frame takes the mirror for the thing mirrored and the piece ends up amplifying precisely what it meant to expose — the maxim, familiar from online argument, that a sufficiently earnest parody is indistinguishable from its target? Please also say what would change your verdict: which consideration would push you to conclude that the pattern is better captured by one of these neighbouring ideas than by a distinct concept of its own, or that it does not warrant a name at all.
