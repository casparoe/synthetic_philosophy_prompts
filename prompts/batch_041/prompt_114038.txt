I have been persuaded by Turchin's structural-demographic account of secular cycles — the idea that states oscillate between integrative and disintegrative phases under the combined pressure of popular immiseration, elite overproduction and fiscal strain — but I want to defend a sharper and, I think, more exposed version of it. On my version the proximate driver is the rate at which aspirant elites are produced relative to the rate at which elite positions are created: a growing stock of credentialed but unplaced elites is what generates counter-elite mobilisation, and immiseration matters chiefly because it supplies recruits rather than as an independent cause. The commitment I take to be risky is that a glut of aspirant elites should be able to precipitate a crisis even where living standards are stable or rising. Treat this as the position to be attacked. What would count against it? I want the observations, historical cases, formal results and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and sorted into (i) evidence that would sink the specific rate-based mechanism, (ii) evidence that would sink only the general claim that such cycles exist, and (iii) apparent counter-evidence that a defender of the view would rightly dismiss. Two things in particular I want you to be honest about: whether elite position can be defined non-arbitrarily enough across very different societies for the ratio to have content, or whether the index merely absorbs the analyst's degrees of freedom; and whether the freedom to fit lags, thresholds and composite indices case by case leaves the theory unfalsifiable — and if it does, whether that is fatal or simply the normal condition of macro-historical work. Finally, tell me what the theory predicts that its rivals (Malthusian limits, climate shocks, external conquest, institutional drift) do not, and what I personally should find most persuasive as grounds for abandoning it. Please answer in British English.
