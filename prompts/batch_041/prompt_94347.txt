I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been working through a well-regarded popular book on ethics. I would like an audit of how the book handles the procreation asymmetry. Here is its account, in my paraphrase: The asymmetry is the thesis that coming into existence can be bad for the person who exists but never good for them, while never existing can be good for them but never bad. Four judgments do the work: the presence of pain is bad; the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of pain is good even though there is no one to enjoy that good; and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived by it. Because every life contains some suffering, and because the goods of a life are only ever good for someone who exists, coming into existence is always a net harm, and since no one is deprived by never being born, it is always wrong to have children. The book concedes that critics call the whole thing inconsistent: if the absence of pain counts as a good, then by parity the absence of pleasure should count as a bad, so the asymmetry is unmotivated special pleading. It closes by describing the thesis as a version of negative utilitarianism, in which the point of morality is to reduce suffering and pleasure never enters into the calculation.

I cannot tell whether the problems I sense are in the thesis or in the book's rendering of it. Please sort the account clause by clause into four piles: what a careful defender of the asymmetry would accept as stated, what is a common simplification, what is a misrepresentation, and what is a different position that has been quietly folded into this one. My own inclination is that each of the four judgments is hard to deny on its own, and that the conclusion is one I nonetheless resist, so I would like to know whether that resistance marks a real gap in the argument or is merely discomfort.

Beyond the sorting, I have four specific questions. First, does the asymmetry by itself license the conclusion that having children is always wrong, or does that conclusion need further premises the book never states? If the latter, what are they, and are they themselves contested? Second, is the alleged inconsistency a refutation or a live objection, and what is a defender's most natural reply? Third, are the four judgments all claims about the same kind of value? My inarticulate suspicion is that some of them concern what is good or bad for a particular person while at least one does not, and that this bears on whether the asymmetry is even coherent. Fourth, if the argument really did establish that coming into existence is a net harm, would it also establish something about continuing to live? The book never raises this, and I suspect the omission is doing work.

I do not need further reading or the names of articles. I want the audit, and then a one-paragraph verdict on whether the book's account is fair, unfair, or fair but misleading.
