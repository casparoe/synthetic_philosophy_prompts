Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether insect suffering should be one of the great moral priorities of our lives, and I went to bed unsure whether I had lost. I would like you to write the dialogue we should have had. It need not be a transcript — compress, sharpen, rearrange — but both speakers must be at their strongest, and neither may be a straw man.

My friend is a consequentialist with a serious interest in philosophy of mind. Her case, which she calls the scale argument, runs roughly as follows. However many insects there are, the number is so large — she puts it at no fewer than a hundred million for every living human — that no plausible discount on their moral status neutralizes it. She offered a deliberately stingy toy model: suppose the probability that any given insect is sentient at all is one in a thousand, and suppose further that even if it is, its suffering counts one thousandth as much as a mouse's. She claims the product still dwarfs everything we currently take seriously, and that the arithmetic is not the weak point. She also proposed a test: we may discount a being's moral weight only by a factor we would be willing to apply, on the same kind of evidence, to a human being — since our evidence about other human minds is equally indirect, our inability to get inside a bee cannot by itself license the discount. And she insisted that treating the probability as effectively zero is itself an empirical bet, and a reckless one. Do not let the exact figures carry weight; the argument needs them only to be enormous.

The other speaker is me, and I am the skeptic. My best objections — which she answered without convincing me — were these. First, the correlation objection: the considerations that lower the probability of insect sentience also lower the weight a sentient insect's experience should carry. If sentience requires integration over time, some representation of one's own states, or the affective machinery vertebrates share, then whatever makes me doubt the probability also makes me doubt the weight, and the expected-value product does not simply inherit the enormity of the headcount. I want her to meet this at its strongest, not to wave it away. Second, the unit problem: what exactly is the subject? A single bee, a hive, a nervous system distributed through a body that can lose its head and keep walking? If the unit is not the organism, the multipliers shift by orders of magnitude, and it is not clear we even know how to count. Third, the proves-too-much objection: the same arithmetic covers mites, nematodes, and the uncountable insects that die in ordinary natural processes, and it would seem to demand a wholesale reorganization of how we live. That she does not so reorganize her life is not a refutation, but I want her to tell me why it is not an indictment.

Requirements for the dialogue. Both speakers should argue in the register of people who already know the terrain, without exposition dumps for a bystander. There should be genuine back-and-forth: she should answer each objection, I should answer her answers, and she should have available the strongest replies a competent proponent would give, including any that would leave me without a response. If you use her numbers, actually work them rather than gesturing at them, and keep them consistent. Do not cite studies, name researchers, or quote anyone; I want the arguments themselves, not the literature. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, and let the exchange end in a sharper disagreement rather than a resolution — I do not want a tidy synthesis.

After the dialogue, add a brief note, clearly set off from it, saying how confident you are that each side is a fair reconstruction of what a competent proponent of that side would actually say, and identify the single point in the dialogue you are least sure of — whether that is an empirical claim, a step in the arithmetic, or a characterization of one of the positions.
