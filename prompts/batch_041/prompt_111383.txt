I teach a third-year seminar on action and intention — we do Anscombe's Intention in the first half of term and the moral philosophy in the second — and I've got one 90-minute slot for the Truman business: her campaign against Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree, where she held that ordering the killing of civilians as a means to ending the war made him a murderer, and that the standard defence (it saved lives, it shortened the war) wasn't outweighed but beside the point, since she thought the whole thing wasn't a matter of opinion at all. I have 18 students and I do not want to stand up and lecture them about it. What I want is for them to end up in the middle of it themselves, under a rule that quietly makes her position feel like the only one they can actually state out loud. The shape I'm imagining, though throw it out if you've got something better: a staged debate in which the House is deciding whether to confer the honour, with a Standing Order that no argument may rest on what would have happened otherwise — no counterfactuals, no lives saved, no comparison with any alternative course of action, only what was done, by whom, to whom, and with what intention. I want them to find out that under that rule one side has a complete case and the other has nothing, and to find it out by running out of things to say rather than by being told. Don't let the words consequentialism, or her name, or the phrase 'doctrine of double effect' get spoken until the debrief.

So: rules, roles (I'm thinking a chair who enforces the order and rules on challenges, two counsel teams, a registrar who keeps a seperate written record of every consideration that gets used, and a House that votes), a timeline for the 90 minutes that includes at least one moment where the order is suspended and then reimposed, cheap printable materials, and a secret ballot we can do something with afterwards — a colleague suggested running the whole thing a second time with the historical facts flipped so we can see which arguments survive the flip, but I'm not sure that fits in 90 minutes, so tell me if it earns its time. I'll make a one-page sheet of the facts of the case (projected invasion casualties, the state of the war, that sort of thing): tell me what should be on it and, more importantly, what I should deliberately leave off. The debrief should map what actually happened in the room onto her claims — including the part I care about most, that her real target was the honour and the moral climate that made the question look open, not the bombing as such. Then be straight with me about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm expecting smuggled counterfactuals, and I'm expecting someone to say the act-description just begs the question — what do I do when that happens?), and about which feature of her view this set-up can't reproduce, so I know what I have to say out loud myself at the end.
