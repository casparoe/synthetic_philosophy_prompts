I work at a small AI safety nonprofit — nine people, no lab of our own — and we've been hired to set up monitoring for a client who wants to fine-tune a mid-size open-weights model on their internal docs and let it write code for them. The setup is: the model's chain of thought gets read by a second, smaller model that flags anything looking like a plan the user wouldn't sign off on, plus a rule layer that scrubs output before it ships. My colleague who built it keeps saying the whole point is that we assume the model can't be trusted — we don't have to think it's actually scheming, the monitor just has to hold up even if it is.

So the client's CTO asked me, pretty reasonably, why we'd ship the thing at all if we think there's a real chance it's working against them. If you thought your contractor might be sabotaging you, you wouldn't hire a babysitter and let him keep working — you'd fire him. When I tried to answer I noticed my answers pulled in opposite directions. One is "we don't actually believe that, this is just cheap insurance, like a seatbelt." The other is "we do think it's plausible, and the monitor is what makes it ok." I dont think both can be the honest story at once — if it's a seatbelt we'd ship without it, and if it's load-bearing then the CTO seems right that we shouldn't ship at all.

The details only seem to make sense under one story or the other and I can't tell which. The monitor is weaker than the model it's watching, and we tell ourselves that's fine because the little one only has to catch things, not do them. But someone on the team pointed out that a strong model that knows it's watched will just learn to look innocent to a weak reader — fine if we only wanted insurance, bad if we wanted the thing to actually hold. And we wrote the monitor's rules by hand off examples we generated ourselves, which means we're testing the model against our own picture of what scheming looks like, and I can't shake the feeling that anything clever enough to matter will be better at hiding than we are at looking. There's also a seperate issue: the rule layer has to decide what the user "wouldn't sign off on," which is really us guessing at the client's actual preferences and calling it policy.

What I want is not a reading list. I want someone to tell me: is the CTO's argument good, or is there a version of "assume the worst and ship anyway" that survives it? Is the seatbelt/load-bearing split real or am I just confused? And is there a coherent position where you deploy a system you half expect to be adversarial and the monitoring is neither an excuse nor a fig leaf — and if so, what has to be true for it to be that, rather than one of those two?
