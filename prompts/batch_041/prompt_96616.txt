I've argued myself into a position I've been calling "no free lifts," and I want to know what it's actually exposed to.

Here's the position. Every case of apparent novelty -- a new level of organization, a new kind of normativity, minds, conventions, the whole lot -- is the product of a chain of dumb, incremental, mechanism-sized steps that do the real hoisting. There are no skyhooks. It's basically Dennett's cranes-and-skyhooks picture from Darwin's Dangerous Idea, with two additions of my own. First, "skyhook" is never the right answer even as a placeholder: when we can't find the crane, the honest verdict is "we haven't found it," not "something lifted this in from outside." Second, the greedy-reductionism diagnosis is a real diagnosis -- there genuinely are intermediate levels, and the right fix for someone who skips them is to go find the missing cranes, not to declare the level irreducible and walk away.

What I want is what would count against this. Not the objections that are merely annoying -- the ones that should actually move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be. Some things I'd like you to press on specifically:

- Is there any case where the honest verdict is "this looks like a skyhook, and we have positive reason to think no crane exists"? If so, what would the shape of such a case even be? My worry is that "we haven't found the crane yet" fits every possible datum, which makes the view look less like a claim about the world than a research policy.
- Is it a claim or a resolution? If someone says "fine, but you'd say the same thing no matter what we found," is that a refutation or just a description of what a regulative principle is? I'm definately worried this thing has been insulated into unfalsifiability. Be honest about whether that's fatal here or just the price of a stance like this.
- What should a defender of the view concede as counting against them? Not what critics say -- what someone who actually holds it ought to admit is a real cost.
- Does the greedy-reductionism half ever cut the other way? I mean a case where the demand for intermediate cranes is itself the mistake, and the right account is flat, with no levels left to lift between.
- What does the view predict that a rival doesn't? If the answer is nothing, just say so.

The case that worries me most is the origin of biological function -- not the origin of life so much as the first time something was *for* something, where the crane story seems to need a norm that the physics doesn't hand you. Is that a skyhook-shaped hole, or am I just failing to imagine the crane? And tell me what would change your mind, not just what would change mine. Also, if this is really two or three views stapled together, seperate them for me and tell me which part is doing the work.
