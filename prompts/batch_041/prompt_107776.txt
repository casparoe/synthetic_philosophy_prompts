I hold that the absurdity of sentences of the form p-but-I-do-not-believe-that-p is a special case of a broader phenomenon, and that this broader framing does the explanatory work better than the usual appeal to a norm of assertion. The broader class is the propositions that can be true yet cannot be believed by anyone — for instance, "no one believes this very proposition" — where the content is self-defeating for a would-be believer. On my view the Moorean cases are absurd for that same reason, and the absurdity should therefore surface in silent thought, in memory, and in partial belief, not only in public assertion. What would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: whether some Moorean sentence can, after all, be believed by a sufficiently self-deceived or confused agent; whether there are non-Moorean members of the wider class whose absurdity has a different flavor, and whether the class is too coarse to do any explanatory work; why the absurdity should be anchored to the first person at all, given that the second- and third-person variants are perfectly fine; and whether the account predicts absurdity in cases where our intuitions are simply silent. Say which of these the view's defenders would themselves concede and which they would dismiss, and whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind.
