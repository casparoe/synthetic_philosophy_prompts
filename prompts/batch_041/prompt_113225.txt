Act as a philosopher of science specializing in the epistemology of archaeology — the problem of what, if anything, material remains can tell us about past minds — and think carefully before committing to a verdict. Begin by brainstorming the strongest and weakest steps in the argument below; then give a definite answer rather than a survey of options.

A colleague has been defending the following argument, and I cannot tell whether it is airtight or whether something slips between the steps.

(1) Every inference from an artefact to a past belief or institution requires a bridging assumption: a claim that these particular objects stood in the relevant relation to that belief — that they were made, used, deposited, or destroyed in a way that tracks it.
(2) Nothing about the past is directly observable. So a bridging assumption can be supported in only two ways: either (a) we can observe the same relation between such objects and such beliefs among living people, or (b) it follows from a general regularity about human behaviour.
(3) (b) reduces to (a), because any general regularity about human behaviour is known only from cases we have observed, and every case we have observed is present-day.
(4) (a) is unavailable for the deep past. The ethnographic present is not a random sample of the human past, so using it to interpret the past assumes a uniformity across times and cultures that is precisely what is in dispute; to lean on it is to argue in a circle.
(5) So no bridging assumption can be justified, and no inference from objects to past beliefs or institutions can be justified.
(6) Archaeology can describe objects; it cannot reconstruct beliefs or institutions.

He takes (6) to be a reductio of the demand for justification rather than a truth about his field, but the steps look airtight to me. I would rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (6), and I want to know what that preference would cost me. Name the step you reject, explain why it looked compelling in the first place, and price the alternatives: what does the discipline gain or give up if you cut the step you cut? Be committed — choose one fault line and defend it rather than hedging across several.
