I have no academic training in philosophy or medicine—I drive a delivery van and read on my lunch breaks—but I have been stuck on a condition I came across called pain asymbolia. As I understand it, people with this condition can still feel pain, and can still say where it is and how intense it is, but they do not seem to suffer from it in the ordinary way: they may not pull away, may not look distressed, and in some accounts may even smile or seem indifferent. What interests me is not the medical curiosity but the philosophical question it opens up. We usually treat pain as the clearest case of something bad in itself—bad because of how it feels. But if the sensation can be present while the awfulness is missing, then maybe the badness of pain is not in the sensation at all. Maybe it is in the way pain grabs you, commands you, makes you care. I have been calling this, in my notebook, the difference between a signal and a summons. A signal can be true or false, loud or quiet; a summons is something you cannot ignore. If pain asymbolia is real, then perhaps what we hate about pain is not the signal but the summons—and a pain that no longer summons anything might be a different kind of event, not just a milder one.

I want to write a poem about this for a small zine my reading group is putting together. The group meets in the back room of a laundromat, and we have been working through a semester on pain and pleasure; the last session is on whether pain is intrinsically bad. I would like the poem to be in the form of a pantoum: four-line stanzas where the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third of the next, and the first line returns at the very end. I am drawn to the pantoum because its repeated lines keep coming back in new contexts, which seems to fit a sensation that keeps reporting itself while the reaction around it changes.

Please write the poem in the voice of someone who has pain asymbolia, speaking to a neurologist or a philosopher who is examining them. Let the repeated lines do the structural work: one line should report the bare sensory fact (it hurts, or something like it) and should stay nearly unchanged each time it returns, while the lines around it should gradually lose the language of aversion, fear, or complaint. I do not want the poem to resolve the question of whether the person is still in pain in the morally important sense; I would rather it leave the reader unsure whether the badness has been removed, hidden, or simply relocated. The form should make the reader feel the difference between a signal and a summons, not just hear the terms. If you can, let the ending be a line that returns from the beginning but now sounds different, the way the same words might sound different if the fear behind them had gone quiet.
