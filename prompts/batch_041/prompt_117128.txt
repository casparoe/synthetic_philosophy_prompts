My game theory reading group has a freind who argues that randomizing is never rational, and I can't find the hole in it. Here's his argument, cleaned up a bit:

(1) Say I'm choosing between some pure options, and I could also mix over them.
(2) Fix whatever the other player ends up doing. My payoff from the mix is just the weighted average of what each pure option in it would have gotten me against that same thing.
(3) An average can't beat the best thing it's averaging.
(4) So against whatever the other player does, at least one of my pure options does at least as well as my mix does.
(5) So a mix is never better than its best ingredient. It's a way of hedging, not a way of improving.
(6) Now suppose a mix is supposed to strictly dominate one of my pure options, i.e. beat it no matter what the other player does. Since the mix is just an average of its ingredients, and an average can't beat its best term, one of those ingredients must already be beating that pure option no matter what the other player does. So nothing is ever dominated only by a mixture.
(7) Which means that when you run the iterated elimination thing, letting mixtures do the eliminating is a mistake — you'd be deleting options that no pure option beats.
(8) And in general a rational player never needs to mix: for any fixed guess about what the opponent will do, some pure option is at least as good, so mixing is at best a fancy tie-break.

(8) has to be wrong, because there are games — the ones you teach with pennies, or with rock-paper-scissors — where every pure option can be punished and the only thing that can't be punished is a mix. And I'm fairly sure (4) and (6) are seperate claims, but I can't say what the difference is.

I also tried to build a case against (6) myself and I don't trust it. Here's one player's payoffs, three options down and two across (the other player's payoffs don't matter for this):

        L    R
  T     3    0
  M     0    3
  B     1    1

T doesn't beat B across the board, and neither does M, but the 50-50 mix of T and M gets 1.5 against L and 1.5 against R, so it beats B in both columns. Is that really a counterexample to (6), or have I got 'dominated' wrong — do I have to worry about the other player mixing rather than just picking L or R?

So: which step do I give up, and why did it look fine before? Then price it for me. In particular, if I keep letting mixtures do the eliminating, does the order in which I eliminate still not matter, and what am I supposed to say about the games where the only unexploitable choice is a mix? I'd rather keep (1)-(3) than (8) if something has to go.
