I teach a 300-level seminar called Games, Proofs, and Paradoxes -- it's basically an excuse to spend a term on arguments that establish that something exists without ever handing you the thing -- and we close the term with a unit on strategy stealing. My students can all do the Hex routine on command: suppose the second player had a winning strategy; have the first player make some throwaway opening stone and then play the second player's strategy; notice that an extra stone of your own never hurts you; get a contradiction; conclude the first player wins. What they can't do when I press them is say which step of that is actually doing the work, or explain why "the second player has no winning strategy" and "the first player has a winning strategy" are not the same claim and what extra ingredient bridges them, or tell me what happens to the argument when an extra move *can* hurt you. And it has honestly never occured to a single one of them that the proof never produces the strategy -- they write "so the first player wins" as if we'd just handed them a game plan, which is a bit rich in a course whose entire theme is that gap.

So I need one essay question for the final, and I want to build it around a variant a colleague of mine in the CS department and I were messing with all summer: take the ordinary Hex board and rules, except that you *lose* if you connect your two sides. We've been calling it Giveaway Hex; I'm sure somebody has looked at this before under some other name, but I haven't chased it down and I don't want the question to depend on that. What I want the question to force is: (i) precisely which premise of the strategy-stealing argument breaks in this game, plus a concrete position where one of your own extra stones is a liability rather than just the assertion that stones can be bad; (ii) exactly what the argument establishes in ordinary Hex and what it leaves open, including the ingredient you have to add to get from "the second player can't win" to "the first player wins"; and (iii) a short closing coda on whether "there's a winning strategy but nobody can exhibit it" is really a case of knowing something, which is where I want them to connect it to the non-constructive-existence stuff we did in week two. Closed book, fifty minutes for this question, so it has to be answerable in maybe 700-800 words of actual writing.

Please give me the question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific moves that should cost marks. Two answers I am sick of and want punished: the one that says "strategy stealing fails here because the game isn't monotone" and stops, and the one that claims the argument tells you the winning first move in Hex. And don't let the model answer hinge on who actually wins Giveaway Hex -- I don't want them guessing at a result, and I'd rather the marks go to the diagnosis of the argument than to the outcome; if there's a known result about the winner they can drop it in a footnote, but nothing should turn on it.
