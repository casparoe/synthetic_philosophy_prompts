My friend and I were up til 2am arguing about this and I still cant let it go. It started with the whole Newton-Leibniz thing — one of them clearly had the method years earlier and just sat on it, the other got into print first, and people fought about it for a century — and then we got onto Darwin and Wallace, where one guy sits on a theory for 20 years, the other sends a letter from the other side of the world with basically the same theory in it, and they end up doing a joint announcement. My friend's line is: credit belongs to whoever first made the idea a real, usable part of what everybody knows — not whoever first thought of it privately. I said fine, but then I went through my actual gut reactions and I'm not sure they fit that, and now I want to know whether there's a principle behind them or whether I'm just making stuff up as I go.

Here's how I actually react to cases:

- Two mathematicians, neither knows the other exists, publish the same theorem three weeks apart. Both discovered it. I dont think the second one discovered it any less. My friend agrees, which is part of why I'm confused about the next one.

- Same setup, but one of them had the whole thing written in a notebook for 12 years and never showed anyone, and the other worked it out cold and published. I still feel the notebook guy discovered it. But if you ask me who gets the statue, I'd say the publisher, and I hate that answer.

- The idea was in the air: four groups publish within a month and honestly anyone competent in that field would have gotten there that year. I feel the discovery was inevitable. But I dont feel that makes any of them less of a discoverer. Except the fourth one, scooped by a week, gets nothing — and that feels like a real injustice, even though I just said inevitability doesn't matter.

- A grad student gets the key idea because her advisor asked one pointed question at a seminar. The student did all the work. I give the student full credit. But if the advisor had just stood up and said the idea out loud as a hypothesis, I'd flip and give it to the advisor. I'm not sure that's consistent.

- Someone guesses the right answer from a wrong argument, or stumbles onto it by accident. I dont want to call that a discovery at all, even though they published first and everyone cites them.

- Someone re-derives a result that was known and then forgotten 400 years ago. That feels like a real discovery to me, maybe full credit. But someone re-deriving a result a colleague explained to them last week — zero credit. Those two feel very different to me and I cant say why.

What I want out of this: is there one principle, or a couple of competing ones, that gets all of these or close to it? Which of my calls does it force me to overturn, and why? Tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't thought about — say an AI system spits out a proof nobody has, and the humans only ran it and checked it. I genuinely have no idea what I think there.

And then the real point of the argument: does any of this matter? If the thing was going to be found that decade regardless, does the identity of the discoverer matter morally, or only for the sociology of science? My friend says only the latter. I think that's obviously wrong but I cant articulate why.

Please dont hand me a tidy principle that only fits because it's vague. If my gut is actually just a mess and there's no single account, say so and tell me which calls have to go. And if the question itself is confused — like if 'credit' is quietly doing two different jobs across my list and that's the whole problem — say that straight out instead of humoring me.
