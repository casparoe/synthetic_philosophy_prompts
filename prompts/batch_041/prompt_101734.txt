For some years I have kept a private ledger of my verdicts about animals, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they amount to. Rearing and slaughtering cattle for beef I regard as seriously wrong. Rearing and slaughtering chickens for meat I also regard as wrong, but I notice I feel it far less keenly, even though I am told that a diet of chicken costs more animal lives per calorie than a diet of beef. Milk and eggs trouble me only mildly, and I cannot decide whether that is because the hen and the dairy cow suffer less or because I have simply ceased to attend to them. Arable farming, which certainly kills field mice and countless insects beneath the plough and the combine, I regard as entirely innocent. Shooting a deer that would otherwise starve through a hard winter I take to be permissible; culling elephants for the same reason I find monstrous, and a friend calls this inconsistent. Pulling a mouse from my cat's jaws feels obligatory, whereas pulling the same mouse from an owl feels like an interference I have no business in. Poisoning insects seems to me trivial; poisoning rats, faintly distasteful. I suspect the common thread is something about whether I am the author of a harm rather than its bystander, but that cannot be the whole of it, since the arable farmer is as much an author as the stockman. Is there a single principle that captures these verdicts, or close; what precisely does it claim; which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon; and what does it predict for cases I have not listed, such as meat cultured from cells, or a vaccine baited in the wild to spare animals a slow disease? Please answer in British English.
