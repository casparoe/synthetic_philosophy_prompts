I spent most of last night in the middle of a long argument between two friends of mine, Marion and Theo, about a vote taken last month by a hospital research committee in their field, and I came away unable to say which of them had the better of it. I would like their exchange adjudicated rather than discussed in general terms, and I would like it done move by move, because every time I tried to intervene I ended up repeating each of their points back to the other and settling nowhere.

The vote was this. Five committee members had to decide whether to approve a trial protocol, under a rule they had all accepted in advance: approve if and only if both (P) the preclinical evidence is strong and (Q) the safety profile is acceptable. Each member voted on P, on Q, and on the conclusion R, which was to approve. Two members voted for P, for Q, and for R; one voted for P, against Q, and against R; one voted against P, for Q, and against R; one voted against all three. So a majority affirmed P, a majority affirmed Q, and a majority rejected R, even though R was supposed to follow from P and Q. Each individual ballot is consistent with the rule; only the aggregate is not.

Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Marion said the committee's outcome is a contradiction in its own mouth: the body simultaneously affirmed both conditions for approval and refused approval. Institutions cannot run on contradictions, so a procedure has to be adopted that guarantees a coherent result.

(2) Theo said there is no contradiction anywhere. A contradiction would require a single set of beliefs, and the committee has none; it has a tally. Nobody believes P and Q and not R. Treating the majorities as "what the committee believes" is an interpretation one adds to the vote, not something the vote contains.

(3) Marion said that only makes matters worse. If the committee declines approval and gives as its reason that the safety profile was unacceptable, it asserts something a majority of its own members denies; and a body whose official reasons are not its own reasons is not deciding by reasons at all. The repair, she said, is to put only the conditions to a vote and let the accepted rule deliver the conclusion — then at least the announced reasons are the body's actual majorities.

(4) Theo said that repair decides the case by the rule rather than by the members. On these numbers the majority that wanted to reject the protocol would lose, and would lose precisely because the rule was drafted so that the conclusion follows from the conditions. The conclusion would then be an artifact of the agenda: whoever writes the list of conditions determines the outcome. Voting on the case itself at least leaves the decision answerable to the people who must live with it.

(5) Marion said that answerability to a majority holding no coherent view is not answerability to anything, and that if the votes on the conclusion are what matter, the votes on the conditions are noise — the committee might as well skip deliberation and poll five verdicts.

(6) Theo said that is more or less what juries do, and we accept it. He added that a third friend, Ines, regards the whole dispute as a small-numbers curiosity: by the Condorcet jury theorem, if each member is more likely than not to answer each question correctly, larger and better-informed committees converge on the truth issue by issue, so the difficulty dissolves at scale, and the practical remedy is simply to enlarge the committee and raise its competence. Marion replied that the numbers in the example were the least of her worries.

Please go through the six moves and rate each of them from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for every rating — separating, wherever it matters, what is true as a matter of logic from what is a further normative claim that the logic does not settle. Then say who had the better of the exchange overall, and what both of them, and Ines, are missing.
