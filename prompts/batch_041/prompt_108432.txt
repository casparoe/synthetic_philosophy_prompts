Write the joint statement that could be signed by two philosophers of history who have been arguing past each other for years: one who holds that to explain an event historically just is to show that it was to be expected given antecedent conditions and general laws (the covering-law or subsumption view, in whatever hedged, ceteris paribus form its defender now prefers), and one who holds that historical explanation is essentially narrative -- that the historian explains by telling a story that traces how one thing led to another, and that no laws, not even loose ones, are doing the real work. I want the document a genuine adversarial collaboration would produce: first a numbered section of agreed claims, phrased so that neither side has to give up anything it cares about; then a disagreements table, with each row stating one residual disagreement in wording that the side holding that view would accept as fair to its own position; and for each row the crux -- the sub-question that, if settled, would move at least one side -- together with the case, argument, or historical episode that both sides agree would actually bear on that crux, and each side's prediction about how it would come out. To keep it concrete rather than programmatic, I want both parties to work through one shared example: a small, self-contained historical episode I will sketch below, about a provincial grain-trading town whose merchants, over roughly a decade, first profited from a speculative boom in stored grain and then, when a hard winter and a rumor of requisition coincided, abruptly abandoned the trade and dispersed. Both parties should say, for this episode, what would count as explaining it, and where their accounts of the explanation diverge; if you think part of their disagreement here is merely verbal -- e.g., about whether 'understanding' and 'explaining' name the same thing -- say which part and why. Finally, I want you to include, as a separate short section, the question of whether any of the disagreement survives if we stipulate that history contains no strict universal laws at all; each side should say what it would concede and what it would still insist on under that stipulation. Do not include reading lists, citations, or references to specific historians' books -- the document should stand on its own argumentative legs.
