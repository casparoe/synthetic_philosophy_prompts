I cover science and ideas for a small magazine, which mostly means I spend my week asking researchers to explain things to me and then writing them down badly. Last month I was working on a piece about wetland restoration and I got talking to an ecologist who's spent twenty years putting marshes back, and he handed me an argument that he says has been sitting on his chest for years. Now it's sitting on mine. He half-joked that he ought to quit and take a job paving over the marshes instead, because his life's work might be the most harmful thing he does. He wasn't smiling much when he said it.

Here it is, roughly as he gave it to me:

1. What matters morally is the total amount of welfare in the world — pleasure minus pain, summed over everyone it happens to.
2. In the wild, nearly every animal dies young and dies badly. A frog lays a few thousand eggs; the overwhelming majority of the tadpoles starve, dry out, or get eaten while they're still alive, and the ones that make it to adulthood are a rounding error. For the great mass of wild animals, life is a short stretch of fear and hunger with a bad ending.
3. So the net welfare of a functioning ecosystem is negative: the suffering in it outweighs whatever good there is.
4. Habitat destruction kills wild animals — mostly by killing them young, and by preventing a great many more from being born at all.
5. Killing an animal that would otherwise live a net-negative life prevents a net amount of suffering, so it's good for that animal.
6. Therefore clearing habitat, all else being equal, reduces the total suffering in the world and is good. The kindest thing anyone could do for wild animals is to get rid of them.

He can't accept 6 and neither can I, and he's definately not wrong that the frog thing is just biology. If anything I'd rather keep 2 and 3 than throw them out — I've seen the footage. He even conceded that clearing a forest kills horribly in the short run, all those displaced animals starving on the edges of what's left, but he said that's a one-off pulse and the decades of lives that never get lived afterwards are what you have to weigh against it. That part made me feel like I was being conned but I couldn't say where.

So which step do I cut, and what do I have to give up to cut it? If the answer is that the whole summing-welfare framing in 1 is the rotten board, fine, but then tell me what to put in its place and what the replacement can't do. I want to know what I'm paying either way, and I'd rather pay in a way that lets me still say something honest to a room full of conservationists.

One request: write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, nothing that looks like a slide deck. Just talk me through it the way you'd talk me through it on the phone. I'm a journalist, I'll probably steal your phrasing.
