A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about underdetermination, and it took us an hour to realise we were defending two different things. I was defending the broad, holist version: no body of evidence can ever force a theory on us, because any hypothesis can be retained by revising our auxiliaries elsewhere. She was defending the narrow version: underdetermination is only worth the name when rival theories are genuinely empirically equivalent, fitting every possible observation and differing only in what they say about the unobservable. Please compare the two carefully. What does each one claim, exactly? Does either entail the other, and is the holist version doing any work that the equivalence version cannot do on its own? Then take a case we never got to. A physicist and a historian each confront a body of evidence that leaves two rival accounts standing, but the physicist's rivals diverge only over unobservables, whereas the historian's diverge over what actually happened — and are even couched in different vocabularies, one in terms of agents' intentions, the other in terms of economic pressures. Is that one phenomenon or two, and does the historian's case expose something the narrow version keeps hidden? Finally, tell me which version, if either, is a genuine threat to the thought that evidence can make one theory rationally preferable to another. My friend thinks the narrow version is toothless, and I am half-convinced; what I suspect she is wrong about is her further conclusion that the broad version is therefore the serious problem.
