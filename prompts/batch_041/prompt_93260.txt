What would Kant have made of the responsibility-to-protect era? Assume he is presented with the post-Cold War record of interventions justified on humanitarian grounds: the 1999 Kosovo campaign, waged without Security Council authorization; the 2003 Iraq war, justified partly on humanitarian grounds after the stated WMD rationale collapsed; and the 2011 Libya intervention, authorized by Resolution 1973 but widely read as having exceeded its mandate by pursuing regime change. He is also given the main features of the responsibility-to-protect doctrine as it was formulated in the early 2000s -- the shift from a right of intervention to a responsibility of the international community, the threshold criteria of just cause, right intention, last resort, proportional means and reasonable prospects, and the requirement of proper authority. Where the doctrine's criteria pull apart from his own articles on the law of nations -- in particular the article forbidding forcible interference with another state's constitution or government, and the cosmopolitan right of hospitality and the idea of a violation of right in one place being felt everywhere -- reconstruct how he would adjudicate the three cases. Attribute to him a determinate verdict on each, state which of the R2P criteria he would treat as load-bearing and which as mere prudence, and say whether he would regard the doctrine as a genuine development of his own position or as a betrayal of it. Do not hedge into "Kant could be read either way"; commit to the reconstruction you think the texts and the architecture of his practical philosophy best support, and defend it.
