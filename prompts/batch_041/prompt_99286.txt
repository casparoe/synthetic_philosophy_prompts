I'm a backend dev at a small startup — fifteen of us, and we all get a vote on big calls. The rule is that a motion passes if a majority of the whole team says yes, which means abstaining or being away on holiday effectively counts as a no (that already feels off to me but nobody else seems bothered). Last week we voted on three things that turned out to be tangled together. One: should we move onto the paid tier of that inference API so the product stops being so painfully slow. Two: if we move onto the paid tier, we have to put our prices up. Three: we should not put our prices up. All three passed — the first two 8–7, the third 9–6. Nobody clocked anything wrong until Priya pointed out you literally cannot have all three at once: we'd be on the paid tier, we'd have raised prices, and we wouldn't have raised prices. There's definately something fishy here and I can't put my finger on it.

Now our CTO is saying we should stop voting on the individual questions and just vote once on the bottom line — "do we move onto the paid tier and raise prices, yes or no" — and whatever that says, that's the decision, no contradictions possible. But when Priya ran that version in the meeting, the bottom line actually got voted down, because most people were against the price rise. So the two ways of doing it give opposite answers from the exact same set of individual opinions, which feels like it can't be right. Tom then suggested just letting the three people who care most about pricing decide, which somehow sounds even worse. I'm the one who's been asked to write up what we should actually do for the next all-hands, and I'm out of my depth. Which method is fairer, is there a principled way to choose between them, and does it matter that whoever picks which questions we put to the vote basically gets to steer the outcome in advance? I did a bit of logic at uni but I've never really done any philosophy, so go easy on me.
