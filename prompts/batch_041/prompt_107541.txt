I'm a postdoc in a philosophy department that shares a building (and a seminar series) with the biology folks, and I'm applying for a small workshop grant — three days, maybe twenty people, half philosophers of biology, half people who actually do quantitative genetics on plants or livestock — so I need a two-to-three page research proposal. Here's my half-formed idea, and I'd like you to build the proposal around it, or say so plainly if it doesn't hold up. Almost all the philosophical work on heritability that I've read is in the deflationary register: h² is a population statistic, it's relative to a range of environments, a high value doesn't mean the trait can't be changed, within-group heritability doesn't license between-group inferences, and so on. Fine, I beleive all of that. What I'm curious about is that in plant and animal breeding, essentially the same quantity — narrow-sense heritability, roughly the additive part of the genetic variance divided by the total phenotypic variance — is used to make actual predictions, via the breeder's equation: you predict how far the population mean will move in one generation from how hard you selected, and it works well enough that people run breeding programs on it. So the concept isn't empty; it's just doing a different job. My question, which I want the proposal to sharpen into something with a determinate answer: is the fight in human behavioral genetics a disagreement about what heritability means, or a disagreement about whether the human case satisfies the assumptions that make the breeding use legitimate? And the more general version of it: what is the weakest set of assumptions under which a heritability number licenses a prediction about what would happen under an intervention, which of those assumptions fail in the human case, and what does each failure do to the inference?

The proposal needs all the usual parts, done properly: the question stated precisely (not "is heritability useful" — that's mush and I don't want it); a quick map of the existing positions and why they leave this open, keeping in mind that the deflationary literature is about interpretation while the breeding literature is about prediction, and I don't think anyone has said cleanly what bridges the two; the method, which I think has to be conceptual with a bit of formal work — lay out the model behind the breeder's equation, go through its assumptions one at a time, and show with small counterexamples what breaks when each fails — plus a word on why that's the right approach rather than the obvious alternative of just collecting more data (we're not funding data collection, and I don't want this to read like a twin study in disguise); what a result would look like, ideally a short list of conditions each paired with the failure mode that shows why it's needed; and what would count as the project failing — if it turns out the two uses of the word are simply equivocal and the bridge is purely verbal, that's a legitimate finding, so say that honestly instead of pretending the project can't fail.
