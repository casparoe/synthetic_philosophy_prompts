A close friend of mine has run a large online community — a general-interest forum with a few hundred thousand members — for the past six years, and he is the person who makes the final call on what stays up and what comes down. He has no philosophical training and would be baffled to hear that he is supposed to have a theory of free speech, but his decisions have been consistent enough that I have kept a list of them.

He has taken down a thread that published a member's full name, her employer, and the school her children attend; a post telling a named member that she should kill herself and that everyone would be relieved if she did; a post that copied a member's private messages into the public forum in order to humiliate him; and a post urging members to keep messaging a particular member until she quit.

He has left up a long, carefully written argument that men are on average better than women at the forum's main subject, with links to studies he considers junk; a post mocking a religion's dietary rules and its founder; a post calling a well-known public figure an idiot and a fraud; and a post mocking the moderators themselves.

The case in front of him now is unlike anything on that list. A member has written a long, calm, well-organized post arguing that a common prescription medicine is a fraud — that the trials were faked, that doctors are paid to prescribe it, and that anyone taking it should stop at once and switch to a cheap herbal alternative. He names no one, insults no one, threatens no one, and does not tell anyone to do anything; he simply makes a case. In the three weeks since it went up, several members have written in to say that they stopped taking the medicine and got sick, and one says that her father was hospitalized. My friend has to decide by tomorrow.

He would tell you that he is simply against people getting hurt, and I have always assumed he was applying the harm principle — the rule, usually credited to John Stuart Mill, that the only justification for restricting someone's speech is to prevent harm to others. What I cannot work out is which version of that rule he is actually running. So: what does he do with the medicine post; what are the competing rules that fit everything he has already done; what does each of those rules predict here; which one do you think he is really running, and why; and if two of them survive the evidence equally well, what case should I be watching for that would separate them?
