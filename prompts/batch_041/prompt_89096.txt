ok so I've somehow volunteered to run a 90 minute session for our reading group next month — mixed crowd, a handful of philosophers, some psych grad students, and one economist who is going to make everything harder in a useful way — and the topic I picked is the preference/bias distinction in morality. Not the empirical question of whether implicit bias is real. The conceptual one: when someone says they just prefer X and someone else says no, thats a bias, what is actually being claimed, and is there any principled way to sort the two?

Don't explain it to me, I roughly know what I think. Give me the plan for the session. Specifically:

What has to land before anything else, and what depends on what — the actual order, and why that order rather than the obvious one, which I assume is define preference, define bias, then compare. I have a suspicion the definitions-first order is the trap and that the session has to get the audience into a case before it gets them into a taxonomy. Tell me if I'm wrong about that.

The one example that carries the whole thing. What it has to have (I think it needs an agent who sincerely avows the attitude, and reasonable people in the room who disagree about whether it's a preference) and what it has to lack. Then tell me what to do with the cases the students will throw in that break it — I'm expecting at least three.

Here's the device I've been circling and I want you to either kill it or fix it, because it's the thing I'd build the session on. I've been calling it the drop test, or maybe the repair asymmetry. Rough version: a preference is something you can drop without argument and without felt loss; a bias is something that resists dropping and pulls a justificatory story along behind it. My worry is that this sorts for how much is at stake for the person rather than for preference vs bias — my strongest moral commitments also resist dropping and also come trailing arguments. So if it collapses, tell me exactly where, and then either repair it or hand me a replacement that still does real work in the room. A deflationary replacement is fine if you can show me what it buys us.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. My guess for the first: that the difference is just conscious versus unconscious, endorsed versus unendorsed, and someone will wave implicit bias testing at me at that exact moment — I want to know what I do with that. Others I'm expecting: bias is irrational and preference is rational; preferences are subjective so they're immune to criticism; if you feel it strongly and would defend it, it's a preference; and the cynical one, that the whole distinction is political labelling with no content. Which of these should I concede something to rather than squash, and what do I concede?

The economist. He is going to say that bias is just deviation from the agent's own ranking under full information, that the distinction is therefore technical and easy, and that the moral case is the same thing with worse notation. What's the fastest honest way to show him that the moral case isn't that, without me doing an hour of decision theory in front of everyone.

The closing check. One question I can ask in the last five minutes that tells me whether they've got the structure of the problem rather than memorized a definition.

And what I should openly refuse to settle in the room, plus how to say so without it looking like I'm dodging.

No readings, please — it's a non specialist room, they won't do them, and I don't want the session to turn into a bibliography.
