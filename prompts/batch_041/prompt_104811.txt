You are an expert in the ethics and law of armed intervention — just war theory, the responsibility to protect, and the running quarrel between legalists who treat Security Council authorization as necessary and cosmopolitans who treat humanitarian necessity as decisive. Reason carefully from the record I give you rather than from first principles, and start by listing the candidate rules before you commit to any of them.

I am a member of a standing ethics commission that vets proposed military interventions for a coalition of parliamentary democracies. Our recorded verdicts, with the features we cited at the time:

- Kosovo, 1999: endorsed. No Security Council authorization (a veto was certain), but an ongoing ethnic-cleansing campaign, a broad and cohesive coalition, and aims publicly confined to civilian protection.
- Iraq, 2003: opposed. No authorization, a threat we judged speculative, an avowed aim of removing the government, and a coalition we judged divided and self-interested.
- Libya, 2011: intervention endorsed on the strength of the resolution and an imminent massacre; the commission later recorded that the interveners exceeded the mandate by working for regime change.
- Syria, 2013: opposed a punitive strike over chemical weapons, on the grounds that authorization was absent, the legal basis contested, and no limited plan with a credible end-state was on offer.

The new file: in the state of Marava, the government has begun the mass killing of a minority; the Security Council is deadlocked by a veto; the regional organization is split, with roughly half the neighboring states backing a mission and half opposing it; a coalition of willing states offers a protection-only mission — no arming of rebels, no stated aim of removing the government, and a fixed exit condition tied to the killings stopping. The killings are ongoing and sanctions and mediation have failed.

Predict our verdict. State the candidate rules our record is consistent with, what each predicts about Marava, your best guess and the reasons for preferring it, and the single further case that would best tell those rules apart. If our record is in fact incoherent — if no single principle fits it and we have simply been improvising — say so plainly rather than constructing a rule that flatters us; I would rather hear that than a tidy rationalization.
