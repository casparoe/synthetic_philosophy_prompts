I host a monthly philosophy night at a comunity center — usually about 15 people, no one with a philosophy degree (a retired nurse, two software folks, a high school teacher, a guy who does carpentry), ages 25 to 70, and I get 90 minutes with a break in the middle. Here's the thing I've been chewing on, and I want the group to run into it with their hands instead of hearing me explain it. Take a sentence like my mug is on the table. It seems to be made of parts: the mug, the table, and the on-relation between them. And yet the whole thing says something, it can be true or false, whereas just listing the parts — mug, table, on — doesn't say anything at all, it just sits there. So something has to glue the parts into a claim. But every answer I come up with says: fine, there's one more thing, the gluing thing, that hooks the relation onto the parts. And then the gluing thing is itself a part, so what hooks it onto the rest? And off it goes forever. I want people to actually feel that "and off it goes forever" in their legs before anyone says the word regress.

What I'm imagining is something physical — I've got a big stack of blank index cards, a roll of masking tape, a ball of string, marker pens, name badges, a big whiteboard, and a bag of fun-size choclate bars as a prize. Maybe the people are the parts, or maybe the cards are, I don't care which, as long as the rule is that nothing counts as connected unless there's a separate card or person doing the connecting, and that connector is itself the kind of thing that needs connecting, so the group has to keep adding. The aim I want to announce is: build one arrangement that actually says something true using only the allowed moves; first team to do it wins the chocolate. Which of course they can't, and that's the point — I want the frustration itself to be the lesson. But it has to be a game and not just a puzzle that stalls, so give me rules, teams or roles, the materials, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes including the break, and the payoff scheme. Then give me the debrief, mapping each moment of what just happened onto the actual steps of the argument, and a second short round I can run after the debrief where I tell them the move a friend of mine who studies language made when I described this to her — she said maybe a proposition isn't a thing with parts at all, maybe it's just the act of putting one thing together with another, the doing rather than the done — and the group has to decide whether that escapes the regress or just relabels it. Don't name any philosophers up front; if there's a name for what she said, save it for the very end.

Two things I definately need. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time. I tried a half-baked version of this last year and within ten minutes everyone was arguing about whether "on" is even a word or a relation, and someone kept insisting you could just tape the mug to the table and be done, which isn't exactly wrong but derails everything. Second, be honest with me about what the activity can't do — I assume there's something about the real version of this problem that a room full of people holding cards simply cannot reproduce, and I'd rather know in advance than have someone in the group point it out to me.
