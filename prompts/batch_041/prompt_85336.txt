I sit on the grant committee of a small foundation with a fixed annual budget, and we must choose among three proposals: mass deworming in a high-burden region; a campaign against confined animal farming; and a pilot study on the welfare of farmed invertebrates, where it is genuinely unsettled whether the animals are sentient at all, though on one of the moral frameworks represented on the committee that last proposal would dwarf the other two. Our disagreement is not mainly empirical. We differ over which framework should govern the choice, and the frameworks do not merely weight the options differently; they rank them differently once numbers of beneficiaries are traded against intensity of benefit. A colleague wants us to adopt the machinery of decision-making under moral uncertainty wholesale. Please set out the most important implications of moral uncertainty for a decision of this kind: what it changes about how we should choose, which parts of ordinary expected-value reasoning survive the shift from empirical to moral uncertainty, and where the approach is most likely to mislead.
