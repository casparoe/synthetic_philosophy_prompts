I keep noticing my gut has strong opinions about when it was actually worth finding something out, and I'd like to know if there's one principle behind them. I know there's a theorem — Good's, I.J. Good I think — that the expected value of information is never negative, on the grounds that you can always ignore what you learn. That sounds like it says you're never worse off in expectation for knowing, and my gut doesn't obviously agree with that, so I want my gut turned into a principle and then told where it's wrong.

My verdicts:

- There's a free test for a condition with no treatment and no prevention that wouldn't hit till I'm about 70. I don't want it. Not worth knowing.
- Same test, but there's something I could actually do about it. I want it, obviously, even if the answer is bad.
- I paid for a pre-purchase inspection on a used car, it came back with three things wrong, it killed the deal, and I'm out the fee and glad. Best money I spent all year.
- A friend told me the score of a match I'd recorded and was about to watch. I'd have paid real money not to be told. I watched it anyway, I just enjoyed it less. Nothing about what I did changed — the thing itself got worse.
- Found out after my dad died that he'd had a whole other family for years. Nobody to confront, nothing to do about it, it just hurt, and I'm glad I know. I wouldn't give it back.
- A friend got a scan for something unrelated, they found a shadow, three weeks of hell, then benign. She says she should of skipped the scan. I think she's wrong about that and also right that she came out worse off than if she'd never gone, and I can't make those two fit.
- A small one: my doctor listed the side effects before I took the tablets and I'm fairly sure that's why I got them. I'd rather not have been told, and I also think she has to tell everyone.

The best principle I've got is this: information is only worth having if it could change what I do. Where there's no decision to make, wanting the truth is just a taste, like olives, and having the taste doesn't make it good for me.

Is there a better principle that gets all of these, or close? Say what it predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on — my mum's four boxes of diaries sitting in the spare room, nothing to be done about anything in them now, might be lovely, might be awful, and I keep not opening them. Give me one more case of your own that the principle settles in a way I wouldn't have guessed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't keep.
