I am a novelist at work on a book about a city that decides to govern itself by markets, and last week I sat through two hours of argument between two friends that I now need refereed. The scheme under dispute: the city must choose between a rent-stabilization ordinance and a direct housing subsidy, and instead of debating it, it runs a pair of prediction markets — traders bet on whether the poorest quintile's real income will be higher two years out if the ordinance passes, and likewise if the subsidy passes; whichever market prices the better outcome becomes law.

Iris opened: the council cannot know which works, but a market can, because thousands of traders with money at stake will out-forecast eleven politicians and a stack of staff reports. Hugh said fine — but look at what the market is being asked to price. Only one of the two policies will ever be adopted, so only one of the two markets will ever resolve; the road not taken is never graded. Iris said that is true of every decision, votes included. Hugh said no: a vote at least asks people to commit to the alternative they would have preferred, whereas here the losing market is simply void, so no one is ever held to account for having been wrong about the world they did not get. Iris said the accountability is ex ante — a trader who pushes the ordinance and is wrong loses his stake when it underperforms. Hugh said that is accountability for the branch that was chosen, not evidence that the choice was right; you can only learn the counterfactual by living in both worlds, and the mechanism guarantees you never do.

Iris granted the point but said it cuts against all policy, then turned to manipulation: a developer who owns half the district can buy the ordinance market cheap, drive the price, and profit from the rezoning that follows, so the market is corruptible in a way a ballot box is not — one person, one dollar, rather than one person, one vote. Hugh said money buys politics under any system, and at least here the manipulation leaves a visible trace in the price. Iris said visible only to whoever is watching the tape, which is no one.

Then the metric. Hugh said the deeper trouble is upstream: the market can only answer a question someone has already posed, and choosing the welfare metric — real income of the poorest quintile, over two years, adjusted how — is itself the whole political dispute, smuggled in as a technical setting. Iris said that is the design, not a bug: we vote on values, we bet on beliefs; the council fixes the metric, the market picks the policy. Hugh said that only works if the values can be fixed independently of the beliefs, and they cannot — move the horizon from two years to ten, or the quintile from the poorest fifth to the poorest tenth, and the market's answer flips. The measurement is not neutral with respect to the question. Iris said that is an argument for choosing the metric carefully, not against markets. Hugh said it is an argument that the mechanism has no authority once the metric is contested, and the metric is always contested, because that is what politics is.

Iris came back that he was holding futarchy to a standard no institution meets — courts, legislatures, cost-benefit analyses all fix a metric and answer a question — and that his complaint reduces to "decisions are hard." Hugh said her defense reduces to "markets are no worse," which is not the claim being made; the claim is that markets are better, and better specifically because they aggregate information, and aggregation requires that traders be right or wrong about something that actually gets settled. If half of what they are pricing never settles, the aggregation is only half an aggregation.

I need a point-by-point ruling: for each exchange above, who has the better of it, and why. Then take a definite overall position rather than handing me a menu — is Hugh's never-graded-counterfactual objection fatal to the scheme as described, or does Iris's reply answer it? And tell me what each of them is missing. No reading list or citations needed; I want your own judgment, argued out.
