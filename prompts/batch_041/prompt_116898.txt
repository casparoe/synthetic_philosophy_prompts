Next month I present a paper to the weekly vision-science seminar of the lab I'm affiliated with, and what I want from you is a prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the paper. I'm a philosopher of mind, so this is not my home crowd. The room is mostly psychophysicists who study apparent motion, masking, and metacontrast; two people who work on neural timing, one running EEG studies of the Libet/readiness-potential kind and one doing TMS chronometry; one Bayesian modeler of perception; and one philosopher of mind who is a committed Dennettian. Nobody there has read the philosophical literature on the timing of experience, and several have firm opinions about it anyway. They care about effect sizes, replication, and whether a measure is confounded, and they are quick to call a question verbal.

The paper is this. Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque readings of the color phi phenomenon is supposed to be a difference that makes no difference. In color phi, subjects report seeing a dot change color midway along its apparent path, i.e., before the second dot was ever displayed; and since the retrospective report is the only evidence, you can always say either that the experience was staged with a delay, so that the change really did occur mid-path (Stalinesque), or that the subject first experienced the whole path in the first color and the memory was later rewritten (Orwellian). Same data, two stories, no fact of the matter about when the change was experienced. My claim is that this equivalence is an artifact of there being exactly one window onto the episode — the retrospective report — which is precisely the channel that cannot distinguish the two. Add a second window that runs online, during the trial, and the readings come apart. My design: the standard two-dot display, plus, on a random subset of trials, a brief probe (a tone, an irrelevant flash elsewhere in the field, or a single TMS pulse over the relevant retinotopic region) delivered at one of several delays after the first dot; and, crucially, a continuous measure sampled every few milliseconds, either tracking the moving dot's position or continuously setting its apparent color on a dial. Stalinesque staging predicts that probes inside the staging window catch the un-staged content and that the online track shows the interpolation at a latency that includes the staging delay. Orwellian revision predicts no staging latency in the online track, a short-delay probe that catches the pre-revision content, and a report that matches the later, overwritten content rather than what was online. The two models therefore predict different orderings of probe and track latencies — a signature, not a re-description. I concede in advance that all of this depends on the online track counting as experience, which is the contested premise; my line is that the premise should be made explicit rather than left doing covert work.
