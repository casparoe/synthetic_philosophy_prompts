I am trying to decide whether predictive processing deserves to be treated as a substantive scientific theory, and I want the requirements list in hand before I assess any particular model. Set out what any adequate predictive-processing account of perception, action, and psychopathology has to deliver: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it must respect from the rest of the science, with the consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are genuine demands and which are merely the commitments of one camp. Four points I want handled with care. For any given system, the theory must say what its generative model is a model of, and why that model rather than another would be selected. It must explain how a system that minimizes prediction error can nonetheless seek out the situations that maximize it — novelty, pain, deliberately broken routines. It must say what observation would count against it, and I suspect that on the standard formulation nothing could, so tell me whether that charge lands and what the theory would have to add to escape it. And it must do all this for a bacterium as readily as for a person, if it is meant to be general. Take seriously throughout the possibility that the claim that the brain minimizes prediction error is a redescription of any system's behavior rather than a claim about one. If some of your requirements cannot be met together, say so plainly; that is the result I most want. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, lists, bold text, or tables.
