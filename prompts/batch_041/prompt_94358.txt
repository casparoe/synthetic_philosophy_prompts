Please draft the joint statement that two parties to a long-running dispute about moral luck could both sign. Party A is a control-principle hardliner: an agent is morally assessable only for what was within her control, and every appearance of luck infecting blame is an epistemic artifact — what varies with the outcome is our warrant for blaming, not the agent's desert. Party B is a moral-luck realist: responsibility itself, and not merely our knowledge of it, varies with factors the agent never controlled, and this is a constitutive feature of the practice rather than a defect to be engineered away. Assume both are competent and neither is a caricature: A can feel the full force of the cases that move B, and B can say precisely what the control principle gets right.

I have read enough of these exchanges to know the standard moves, so I do not want a survey of them. I want the artifact the two camps never actually wrote:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign — including, for each of B's strongest cases, what A concedes it shows, and, for the control principle, what B concedes it captures.
2. The residual disagreements, each stated in wording that the side holding it would accept as fair, and each sorted into (i) the psychological/descriptive question of what our blaming practices in fact track, and (ii) the normative/metaphysical question of what they ought to track or what desert really is.
3. The cruxes. I suspect one is whether blame is answerable to a truth-like standard, such that an uncontrollable fact could make a blame judgment incorrect rather than merely unkind; and another is whether responsibility is a single relation or a family of distinct ones, some luck-permeable and some not. For each crux, name a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, record each side's prediction in advance, and state the double crux — what each side would have to see in order to move.
4. A verdict on which part of the dispute, if any, is merely verbal, with the reason.

Two constraints, to keep this from collapsing into recitation. First, adopt the following shared test case, invented for this exercise, and have both sides commit to predictions about it before invoking any theory: two drivers, identical in every controllable respect — same blood-alcohol level, same speed, same attention, same skill, same route — round the same blind corner on the same night; in one world a child is in the road, in the other the child was called inside a minute earlier; and in one world the driver learns of the child while in the other the driver never does. Second, the two sides must jointly propose a single operational criterion — something a third party could in principle check — for when outcome-luck has changed an agent's degree of blameworthiness rather than merely our grounds for believing in it, and each side must state what it would take to falsify its own preferred criterion.

Write it as a document both of them would actually put their names to. No citations and no reading list; argue from the concepts.
