Here's the question from the exam (Q3, 10 marks, students were told to spend about 20 minutes on it):

Fomenko's New Chronology and the ancient-astronaut reading of the Egyptian monuments are both standardly classed as pseudohistory rather than as false history or as bold speculation. (a) Explain why falsity by itself cannot be what justifies that classification. (b) Give one claim from each case that is clearly testable, and say why that testability is awkward for the usual charge that pseudoscience is just "unfalsifiable." (c) A defender of the New Chronology replies to the radiocarbon and dendrochronology evidence by saying that those methods were themselves calibrated against the conventional chronology. Say what that reply is doing, and what it shows about the limits of the unfalsifiability charge.

And here's the answer I'm sitting with:

Falsity can't be the mark of pseudoscience. A theory can be fully scientific and turn out false — phlogiston, the ether, the steady-state universe — and a claim can be plainly false without being pseudoscientific at all ("the moon is made of cheese"). So the mark has to be something about how a claim behaves in inquiry rather than whether it wins in the end.

Popper's answer was that a scientific claim has to forbid something: there must be some possible observation that would refute it, and pseudoscience is the discourse that forbids nothing. But both of our cases forbid plenty. Fomenko's chronology makes hard empirical claims: that the star catalogue in the Almagest was observed much later than Ptolemy's date, that whole dynasties in the conventional timeline are duplicates of one another, that the radiocarbon dates have to be shifted by centuries. Those are checkable, and they've been checked against eclipse records, coinage and tree rings. The ancient-astronaut case is the same: the star shafts in the Great Pyramid, the "lightbulb" relief at Dendera, the Nazca lines. You can go and measure. So the charge of unfalsifiability is simply misapplied here. These claims aren't unfalsifiable; they're unfalsified by their defenders, which is a fact about the people rather than about the sentences.

But if falsifiability can't do the work then nothing can, and the honest conclusion is that the demarcation problem is a dead end: there is no principled line between science and pseudoscience, only the line between what the relevant community accepts and what it doesn't. That's why "pseudoscience" is a word people use to end arguments rather than to describe anything.

The recalibration reply is an immunizing move: whatever evidence arrives, some peripheral assumption gets revised and the central claim survives untouched. Radiocarbon and dendrochronology? Their calibration was fixed against the conventional chronology, so they're circular. Written sources? Renaissance forgeries. This pattern — never giving up the core, always paying somewhere else — is what actually makes the position pseudoscientific rather than merely mistaken. Which is also why Popper's later distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes doesn't rescue the criterion; it just gives the same problem a new name.
