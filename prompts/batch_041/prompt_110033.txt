I'm taking a seminar on aesthetics and politics, and the sessions on Du Bois have left me with a problem I can't get past. Everyone in the room is happy to evaluate his position — his insistence that art is always propaganda, that the purist who claims to stand above politics has not thereby escaped it, and his related claim that beauty and political purpose need not be enemies — but nobody has said what a theory of that kind would actually have to deliver before it can be judged. I'd like the specification first. So: write the desiderata for an adequate theory of politically engaged art of the sort Du Bois was reaching for. What must such a theory be able to do — which cases must it get right, which distinctions must it not collapse, which facts about how art and audiences actually behave must it not contradict? For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely the prejudice of one camp.

I have a rough list of candidates and I want you to pressure-test, correct, and extend it rather than simply accept it. First, the theory has to make sense of the asymmetry: a novel about lynching is propaganda while a novel about suburban ennui is not, and the theory must explain why the accusation attaches to some work and not other work without quietly conceding that apolitical art is a neutral baseline. Second, it has to handle what I've been calling the double-audience problem. Work made for the group — think of the four conditions Du Bois laid down in the mid-1920s for a real Black theatre, that its plays be about Black life, written by Black authors, aimed primarily at Black audiences, and staged in Black neighborhoods — and work made to change the minds of the dominant group pull in different directions, and a theory has to say which is prior, or how a single work can serve both. Third, it has to survive the recurring fight over honest depiction versus uplift: whether a novel that shows crime, sexual frankness, or self-doubt inside the community is truthful art or ammunition for racists. Fourth, it has to keep being good as art distinct from being effective as propaganda, or the theory dissolves into a theory of messaging. Fifth, it has to say who the propagandist answers to — the party, the patron, the editor of a movement's own magazine who is at once its chief publicist and its chief literary critic — and whether the theory can avoid resting on a vanguard's authority. And sixth, it has to say something about scope: is this a theory of Black American art, of political art generally, or of all art whatsoever?

Two things I want most from you. Flag any pair of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect the honesty requirement and the effectiveness requirement pull against each other, and that the claim that all art is propaganda threatens to make the whole position self-undermining, since it removes the ground for criticizing political art as such; if that is right, I want it stated as a result about the specification rather than smoothed over. And tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate views. Then, briefly, say where Du Bois's own position meets its own specification and where it fails it. Keep the weight on the criteria and the reasoning behind them; I don't want a survey of who said what, and please don't give me a reading list — I can find the texts, and a model without a library should be able to answer this from the requirements themselves.
