I'm taking a historiogrophy course and we've just done the cyclical crowd -- Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, Toynbee. I keep noticing something I don't have a word for. They all say history goes in cycles, but the cycle never actually closes. It always has a direction: the return is to a lower point, or the whole thing is a spiral, or the cycle is for other civilizations while the theorist's own is placed at a specific stage on a one-way track. Polybius' constitutional cycle is supposed to repeat, but Rome gets treated as the end of it. Ibn Khaldun's dynasties cycle, yet the arc from solidarity to luxury is a decline. Spengler's cultures are cyclical but each is unique and unrepeatable, so the big picture is a series of one-way declines. Toynbee's challenge-and-response repeats, but it's also a story about whether a civilization can avoid breakdown. And in each case the theorist can point to their own present as the late phase, which seems unfalsifyable -- any evidence can be read as either late-stage decay or early-stage vigor. Is there a name for this specific move: a cyclical theory that smuggles in a linear, usually declinist, trajectory and uses it to diagnose the present? I know the obvious labels -- cyclical history, declinism, progress, Spenglerian -- but none of them seem to carve it exactly. If there's no established name, coin one and define it so I can tell it apart from plain cyclical history and from linear decline narratives. I don't just want a label; I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. Please take a definite position on whether it's a real distinct phenomenon or just a confusion, and give your reasons. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullets, or bold.
