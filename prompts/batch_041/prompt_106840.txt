I spent the better part of forty years in the natural sciences before turning, in retirement, to moral philosophy, and I have arrived at a puzzle that I suspect is not a peripheral curiosity but the load-bearing fault line of the deontological tradition. Stated baldly: a deontological constraint forbids me to do a certain kind of thing even when doing it would prevent a greater number of instances of that very kind of thing — I may not kill one to save five, though the five killings I decline to prevent will then occur. Taken at face value the view appears to command me to bring about more of what it forbids, and so to be at war with itself. I do not want a verdict on whether deontology survives this; I want the specification first. Set out the desiderata that any theory of deontological constraints must satisfy in order to count as adequate.

For each requirement, give me the consideration or case that motivates it, and be explicit about which requirements are genuine constraints on any theory and which are merely the commitments of one camp. I have in mind at least the following, but I expect you to tell me which of them are real desiderata and which are prejudices of a faction:

- It must explain the practical authority of a constraint — why it gives a reason that is not simply the good it promotes — without collapsing into a disguised form of consequentialism.
- It must dissolve or absorb the paradox above: it must show why refusing the minimizing violation is not irrational, or concede that it is and say what follows.
- It must keep constraints and agent-relative prerogatives distinct, and explain how the two relate, rather than trading one for the other.
- It must get the stock cases right and for the right reasons: the transplant case, the bystander and footbridge variants, and the contrast between killing and letting die, including whether the intention/foresight distinction is doing honest work or merely patching.
- It must say whether constraints are absolute or admit thresholds, and if thresholds, on what principle — since a threshold that can be set by consequences threatens to reinstate the very reasoning constraints were meant to block.
- It must respect the thought that each of us is specially answerable for his own deeds in a way he is not answerable for the deeds of others.

I am also curious how the list behaves under two cases of my own devising, which I offer because the standard examples are too tidy. The first is a nested or iterated dilemma: I may commit one violation now to prevent two violations later, and those two, if they occur, will each present someone with a choice between one violation and four. The second is reflexive: the only way to preserve the institution of constraint-respecting is to violate a constraint — to break the rule in order to save the rule. A theory that handles the single-step transplant case may say nothing useful about either.

Two things I most want from you. First, whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable; if it is, that impossibility is the result I am after, and I would rather have it stated plainly than have a theory finesse it. Second, which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories — where the field is thinnest. A brief scorecard of the main families (straight absolutism, threshold views, agent-relative reconstructions, rule-consequentialist renderings, and views that build the exception into the specification of the rule) is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring is secondary and I will work through it myself. No citations or reading lists, please — I cannot consult them and would not trust a reconstruction from memory.
