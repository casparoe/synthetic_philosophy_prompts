I help run a small housing cooperative, and for some months now I've been trying to put my finger on a problem we keep having, because I want to be sure I'm describing it correctly before we try to fix it. The pattern is this. We hold a number of things in common — a workshop, a good set of kitchen knives, a van, a common room. Individually we are a conscientious bunch, perhaps excessively so, and precisely for that reason the shared things go unused and, over time, quietly decay. The van's battery keeps dying because no one wants to be the one to take it out for a frivolous errand. The good knives go dull because each of us assumes someone else has a stronger claim on them. The common room sits empty because no one wants to impose on the others by scheduling something there. The workshop tools rust because each of us feels that whoever has the more pressing project should get first call, and each assumes that person is somebody else. Nobody is overusing anything; if anything the failure is one of over-restraint. I know the standard story about shared resources, in which they are depleted because each person takes more than their share, and I have also come across the mirror-image story in which a resource is wasted because too many separate people each hold a right to keep others out, so that no one can actually get in. Our case seems to be neither: there are no fences and no competing owners, only a group of people who each defer to the others and thereby, collectively, let the thing rot.

Is there an established name for this — a commons ruined by mutual deference rather than by overuse or by fragmented ownership? And if there is, I would like to know exactly what it is supposed to cover, because I can think of cases where the deference is the whole mechanism and cases where it looks more like a symptom of something else entirely. If no name exists, please coin one and draw its boundaries for me.

To be clear about what I'm after: I do not want a bare label. I want the phenomenon carved at the joints — what makes it genuinely distinct from the overuse story on one side and the too-many-owners story on the other; which of my four examples really belong to it and which are just more ordinary things in costume (plain procrastination, the mirror image of free-riding, an everyday coordination failure); and whether the active ingredient is deference itself or merely uncertainty about who is expected to act. Where people who study this area would disagree with one another, please keep the disagreement visible rather than smoothing it into a single agreed account — mark off clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
