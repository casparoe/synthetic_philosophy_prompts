The coherence argument for expected-utility maximization is usually presented as a theorem about individual agents; I want a piece that follows it into a courtroom. Write the full text of an appellate opinion — a mock judgment, roughly 750 words — from a jurisdiction whose common law has adopted the thesis that rationality consists in immunity to being pumped, and which now applies that thesis with the confidence of settled doctrine.

The facts are these. A client holds cyclic preferences over three assets: he prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. His broker, who discloses every fee, charges him for each transaction and moves him round the cycle — A to B, B to C, C to A — as many times as he is willing to go, returning him to his starting position several thousand dollars lighter. The client sues for breach of fiduciary duty. The court holds for the broker. The broker is not a villain: he misrepresented nothing, concealed nothing, and broke no rule. The plaintiff is not a fool: he is a competent adult with an intransitive preference ordering. The horror of the piece should come from the doctrine itself, working flawlessly, and not from any stupidity or corruption in the court.

The opinion's reasoning must be an accurate deployment of the machinery, so that a decision theorist reading it recognises every move and winces only at the conclusion. The following touches are required:

- The economics of the pump: it is the per-trade cost that converts a cycle into a loss; an agent who can cycle at no cost loses nothing. The court should acknowledge this and dispose of it in a clause.
- Naivety and sophistication: the plaintiff did not see the whole sequence in advance, and had he foreseen it he could have refused the first trade. The court should use this to locate the defect in the plaintiff's foresight rather than in the broker's conduct.
- Resolute choice: the plaintiff's expert testifies that an agent who adopts a plan and holds to it cannot be led round a cycle, since at every node he declines to depart from the plan. The court should reply that the law assesses each transaction as it is made, and has no unit of account for a plan.
- Extended preferences: the expert adds that an agent who ranks whole trajectories rather than single options has no cycle to be led round. The court should reply that preferences are taken as the law finds them, option by option.
- The gap between acyclicity and expected-utility representation: this is the expert's strongest point — that at most the pump shows strict preference must be acyclic, and establishes nothing about completeness, the sure-thing principle, or the existence of a utility function. The court should concede the point in a single sentence and then hold that the distinction is finer than the law requires, the law's standard being the pump and nothing else.
- Intransitivity and incompleteness: the opinion should note that a different disorder, incompleteness, would need further premises before it yielded a pump, and should decline to decide whether the statute reaches it.
- The self-torturer: the court draws an aside comparing the plaintiff to a device he described in evidence, which increased a discomfort in increments too small to be noticed, each increment judged worth its price, the sequence ending in agony. The court treats this as the same disorder under another name.

Form and voice: a syllabus of one or two sentences, numbered paragraphs, and a formal order at the close — an order that should include the plaintiff's remedy, which lies in having his preferences completed, at his own expense. The register is dry, authoritative, and unhesitating: the voice of a court that has never had occasion to doubt itself. The opinion must never state the objections to its own doctrine, never concede that coherence is one virtue among several, and never let any character speak for the author. Any authorities cited must be invented — fictional statutes, fictional counsel, a fictional reporter — and no real judge, scholar, or decision should be named.
