a friend in my decision theory reading group has spent all week telling everyone he has a proof that nobody can actually weigh reasons for acting. i think its a trick but i cant find it. he writes it out like this:

(1) When you choose between options (jobs, say), every consideration you care about, pay, the work itself, the city, the people, ranks the options. Those rankings are all you have to go on.
(2) Any decent way of turning those rankings into your overall preference has to satisfy five things: unanimity (if every consideration ranks A above B, you prefer A to B); independence (whether you prefer A to B depends only on how the considerations rank A against B, never on how they rank some third option C); no restrictions on the input (the considerations may rank the options in any way at all); a complete and transitive output; and no single consideration gets to settle everything no matter what the others say.
(3) Arrow's theorem says you cant have all five. Give it unanimity, unrestricted inputs, independence, and a complete transitive output, and one consideration comes out a dictator: its ranking settles every pair, whatever the others say.
(4) Any real chooser has a complete transitive overall preference and does respect unanimity.
(5) So any real chooser has one consideration that settles everything, and someone who says shes torn between the money and the meaning has either already let one of them win or isnt making sense.
(6) Which generalizes. Its not really a theorem about voting, its a theorem about any agent with more than one thing she cares about. Practical reason is impossible.
