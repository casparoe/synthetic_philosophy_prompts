I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on blame and moral responsibility, and I need one essay question worth 20 marks, answerable in 45 minutes, closed book. The course reading keeps running three questions together — whether a piece of blame is warranted, whether it is sincerely meant, and whether the person doing the blaming has the standing to direct it at this particular person — and I want a question that forces them apart. It should hinge on a case I've written for the exam, so that no one can answer it out of a textbook summary. Here is the case. A senior colleague, M, reprimands a junior colleague, J, for passing off someone else's analysis as his own in a report. Variant A: M has been doing exactly the same thing for years and is doing it now. Variant B: M did it once, ten years ago, was found out, apologised, and has since campaigned against the practice. Variant C: M has never done it, but M's whole position rests on a mentor who did, M knows this, and M has never said anything about it. Candidates must give a verdict on each variant along all three axes — warranted, sincere, standing — and defend a general account of standing on which it can come apart from warrant in just these ways. The sting is the self-undermining worry: if standing is lost through the blamer's own fault, almost all blame is disqualified, since most blamers have some relevant fault; if standing is explained by something thinner, it is no longer clear why hypocrisy matters at all. They must choose a horn and pay for it.

Please give me: (1) "The question", as it will be printed, with the case and the three variants, 120–150 words; (2) "Model answer", about 700–800 words, arguing rather than summarising, and without naming authors, titles, or page numbers — candidates have no library in the exam, and I want the argument, not a reading list; (3) "Marking scheme", five numbered must-hit points with the marks each is worth (totalling 20) and one line each on how candidates typically lose those marks; (4) "A versus B", about 100 words, on what an A answer has that a solid B answer lacks.
