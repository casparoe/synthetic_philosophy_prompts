I'm in the third year of a philosophy degree, and this term I'm taking a metaphysics seminar on modality and essence that has spent the last three weeks circling a single question: whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones I actually came from. I can follow the argument perfectly well in the seminar room — the thought that the particular pairing that produced me is not incidental to who I am but partly constitutive of it, so that a child made from a different pairing, even from the same two parents on the same night, would have been someone else and not me — but I cannot make the intuition survive contact with ordinary conversation, and I have started to suspect that the position rests on a picture of identity I can only feel while I am inside the argument and lose the moment I step outside it. Our final assessment can be either an essay or a short story, and I want to write the story, on the condition that the story does the philosophical work rather than illustrating it. I would like something a reader could finish without knowing the debate at all and still come away with the problem lodged in them.

Here is the seed I want you to build the story from, and I would rather you take it seriously than treat it as a puzzle to be solved with a twist. Set it a few decades from now, in a cold coastal city where in-vitro fertilisation has become unremarkable and a service has grown up alongside it: before implantation, parents may request a preview of the child each available embryo would become. Not a photograph — a session, an hour with a rendered person who has your family's face, your mother's way of holding a cup, a plausible life. The effect is that choosing between embryos stops feeling like choosing between medical options and starts feeling like choosing between people. Your protagonist is a woman in her late thirties, midway through her own cycle with her partner, sitting with the clinic's consent forms and a list of previews she has been offered and has not yet opened. She is also an only child, conceived this way herself. In the middle of all of it her mother dies, and the clinic's archive hands her something she did not know existed: her mother's own preview sessions, recorded in the year the service was still experimental, from when she was choosing among the embryos that would produce her daughter. So the protagonist can watch her mother, decades ago, spend an afternoon with the two or three people who would have been her mother's children instead of her — and then choose her.

What I want from the story is the collision of two things I cannot reconcile. Watching the recordings, she wants to find that there was never really a choice, that she was always the one; instead she watches her mother hesitate, weigh things, be kind to the others, and at one point settle the matter on something close to a whim. Meanwhile she has to make the same choice herself and discovers that she cannot bring herself to open her own previews at all — or that she opens them and it changes nothing, or that it changes everything. Please let both intuitions breathe: the one on which the child she might have been is a stranger who merely happens to share her parents, and the one on which the difference between her and that stranger is a coin landing, nothing more. Do not let the story settle which is right, and do not let anyone in it explain the metaphysics — no one in this story has read any philosophy, and no one should announce the thesis out loud. Keep it under about 2,500 words, third person limited on the protagonist, and end on an image or a gesture rather than a moral. Finally, please do not make the mother a sage. She should be a person who made a decision she mostly did not think about, the way most people make them, and the story should let that be both unbearable and entirely ordinary.
