I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar that straddles social choice theory and political philosophy, and I want a single decision tree that maps the live responses to the liberal paradox — the result that no social decision procedure can be at once weakly Paretian, minimally liberal, and defined over every profile of individual preference orderings. The tree should carry a reader from a bare starting commitment to a named position, where every internal node is a concrete question about a case rather than a question like "which school do you belong to?" and no leaf is reachable only by already knowing its label.

Use the following case throughout as the running illustration, and make each branch node a question that a reader can answer by taking a stand on this case alone:

Two housemates share a flat. Ada can read, or decline to read, a controversial book; Ben can read it, or decline. Call the outcomes x (Ada reads it), y (Ben reads it), and z (neither reads it). Ada's ranking is z over x over y: she would rather nobody read it, but if someone must, she would rather it be herself than Ben. Ben's ranking is x over y over z: he would rather the book be read than not, and would rather Ada read it than himself. Now add two wrinkles the textbook version leaves out. First, treat the boundary of the "personal sphere" as contested: a third housemate, Cleo, insists that whether Ben reads the book is not purely Ben's business, since a flat is a shared moral environment, and that the same goes for Ada. Second, allow that a person may waive or delegate the prerogative over their own matter — Ada can hand her decision about reading to Ben, or to a coin.

Build the instrument as follows:

1. Work from the logical space first. List the branch questions and the combinations of answers they allow, and only then place the actual positions inside that space. The questions should be ordered so that earlier ones prune the space as much as possible.
2. The branch nodes should be diagnostic of the real fault lines: whether the paradox is read as a genuine conflict among the three conditions or as evidence that "minimal liberty" was mis-specified; whether the personal sphere is taken to be fixed by the alternatives themselves, by the preferences, or by the participants' say-so; whether a right is modelled as a restriction on the social ordering, as a game form, or as a power that can be exercised or waived; whether individual preferences may be conditioned on others' preferences or must be taken as given; whether the domain is restricted to self-regarding preferences; whether cycles, intransitivity, or incompleteness in the social relation are tolerated; and whether the whole framework is rejected as the wrong tool.
3. Each leaf should carry the name of the position — or "unnamed" if you judge that nobody defends it — plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, phrased so a reader can see the cost of standing there.
4. Note every combination of answers the tree permits but that no position occupies, and diagnose each gap in a clause: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
5. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what extra question would be needed to separate them, if any exists.
6. Aim for seven to eleven leaves and no more than eight internal nodes.

Assume the reader knows the bare statement of the paradox but not the secondary literature, so the nodes must be self-contained. Do not cite authors, works, or dates anywhere in the tree; identify each position by what it claims, not by who held it.
