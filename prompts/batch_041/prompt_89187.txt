I'm in a graduate seminar on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and I've ended up with an argument of my own that I can't decide whether to be pleased with or worried by. It began as an attempt to give the stakes-sensitivity of knowledge some rationale, rather than just asserting it and defending it case by case, and it ends somewhere I don't think anyone in the debate wants to go — which is either a sign that I've found the weak point in the whole programme or a sign that I've made a mess. Here it is.

(1) The point of the practice of attributing knowledge is to license reliance: when I tell you that S knows that p, I give you permission to treat p as settled — to stop checking and act as though p. (2) What may be treated as settled for a given purpose depends on what is at risk if p is false: the same evidence licenses reliance when the cost of error is a wasted afternoon, and does not license it when the cost of error is a life. (3) So a knowledge attribution is correct only relative to a level of stakes; there is no such thing as its being correct simpliciter. (4) But truth is not relative — a sentence is true or false, period — so an utterance whose correctness is relative to something is not stating a fact, it is doing something else: licensing, recommending, warning. (5) Hence 'S knows that p' is not a factual claim, and the vocabulary of knowledge is an instrument for managing reliance rather than a description of anything; there is no fact of the matter about who knows what. (6) That I cannot accept. I use 'knows' to explain and predict: the inspector who knows the crack is stable and the one who merely hopes so behave differently, and that difference is the difference between an inspection regime that works and one that kills people, and no amount of talk about managing reliance makes it go away. It also looks self-undermining: (5) is offered as a fact about knowledge attributions, and if there are no facts of the matter about what anyone knows, I don't see how there is one about what 'knows' means either.

A friend who works on contextualism says (1) is the culprit and that I should just drop the idea that knowledge talk has a point and stick to its semantics. But (1) strikes me as the most solid item on the list — it's just the observation that we don't hand out 'knowledge' the way we hand out gold stars — and I'd rather keep (1) and (6) and lose something else. What I can't tell is where the argument actually slips: in the move from the point of the practice to its correctness conditions, in the pivotal phrase 'correct only relative to a level of stakes', or in (4)'s assumption about what relativity costs. So: which step do I give up, why did it look so plausible, and what am I paying if I pay it — in particular, does the repair leave me with a position that is still recognisably either pragmatic encroachment or contextualism, or does it quietly turn into one of the others?
