Write a satirical piece in the form of a formal written arbitration award: a single arbitrator, after a hearing, resolves a two-party dispute by applying the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution to it. The register should be the deadpan, numbered-paragraph register of a real tribunal decision, and the arbitrator should be entirely sincere, competent, and unflappable.

The mechanics the piece must honor. A two-person bargaining problem consists of a convex set of feasible agreements plus a disagreement point, which is what each party receives if no agreement is reached. The ideal point is the pair consisting of each party's best feasible payoff, taken separately, whether or not those two best outcomes can be achieved together. The Kalai–Smorodinsky rule selects the Pareto-optimal point lying on the straight line from the disagreement point to the ideal point; equivalently, it gives the parties equal proportional gains, so each captures the same fraction of the distance from its own disagreement payoff to its own ideal payoff. The rule is characterized by Pareto optimality, symmetry, invariance to positive affine transformations of the parties' utility scales, and a monotonicity axiom usually called individual monotonicity. It is the last of these, not Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives, that the rule is built to satisfy, and the Nash solution is its natural foil: it satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives and violates individual monotonicity, and the two rules can deliver different awards to the same problem.

Form and content. Around 650 to 800 words, in American English, with a title in the style of a decision and the parties and the tribunal invented — no real people, firms, or institutions. Keep the underlying dispute ordinary enough that a reader who knows no bargaining theory can follow it. The award must do all of the following.

- State the disagreement point and the ideal point explicitly and compute the award as the point on the line joining them, so that a reader who knows the rule can check the arithmetic and confirm the equal-proportional-gains property.
- Treat the four axioms as though they were provisions of a governing arbitration code, and use them as the stated grounds for procedural rulings that exclude evidence. The arbitrator should refuse testimony about what the disputed goods mean to each party or what they are worth in real terms, on the ground that the utility scales are arbitrary. The arbitrator should refuse testimony about who contributed more, who needs more, or who behaved badly, on the ground that the award depends on nothing but two points. And the arbitrator should rule, correctly and without any joke at the rule's expense, on the consequence of individual monotonicity: if one party's best possible outcome improves while the other's does not, the second party's award cannot shrink. Let at least one party try to turn this to its advantage.
- Include a short dissent or a clerk's memorandum urging the Nash solution instead, together with the arbitrator's rebuttal. The disagreement between them must be a disagreement about which axiom is normatively compelling — independence of irrelevant alternatives versus monotonicity — and not about the arithmetic. Neither side may be a caricature, and neither may be made to concede the central point.
- Close without a moral and without any sentence that tells the reader what the joke was.

The piece must not explain the theory to the reader in the manner of a tutorial; if the arbitrator cites the axioms, he should cite them as authority rather than define them for a novice. Nothing in the ruling should signal that the author knows it is funny. The target is the practice of axiomatic fairness reasoning — the habit of treating a division as settled because it follows from a short list of axioms — and not any named individual.
