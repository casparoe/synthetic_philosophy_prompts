Alright, philosophy question, want your real working-out on this one.

There's a notion in philosophy of biology — I'm fairly sure Dennett coined the phrase — called the "free-floating rationale." The idea: you can genuinely explain a biological trait by citing what it's for. The wing is shaped that way in order to generate lift; the bee's dance is performed in order to point the hive at the nectar; the fever is there in order to cook the pathogen. And those "in order to"s are real explanations that do real explanatory work even though nobody ever intended anything. No designer, no plan, no bee sitting there thinking "right, time to give directions." Natural selection can lock onto a reason that no one ever had, and the reason just sits there in the world, doing work, with no reasoner attached to it. Reasons without reasoners.

So: what would Kant have made of that? And I don't want the lazy one-liner — "he'd say it's regulative rather than constitutive, case closed." I want the actual argument. In the Critique of Judgment he treats living things as natural purposes, things we're obliged to judge as if they'd been put together for ends, while insisting the "as if" comes from us and not from the thing. Does the free-floating rationale drop neatly into that slot, or does it blow the slot apart? Where does he plant his feet and where does he have to give ground? Is the whole idea, in his eyes, a category mistake, or a genuine discovery he'd have to swallow and rework his machinery around? Run it on one concrete case too — say, whether we're allowed to say a fever is *for* burning off an infection, or whether that's just a byproduct we're dressing up — and tell me what his verdict would be. Argue it, don't label it. I've had alot of people hand me the regulative/constitutive thing and then definately not be able to say what follows from it.
