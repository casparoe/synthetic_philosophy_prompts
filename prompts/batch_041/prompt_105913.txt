I am a novelist, and for several years now I have been writing a book that follows two young men through the same war from opposite sides: one defending his own country against an invasion, the other a conscript in the invading army who believes — because he has been told so since childhood, and because his own province was fought over a generation earlier — that he is the one defending his home. The whole architecture of the novel rests on the reader's sense that these two stand in the same moral position: equally frightened, equally dutiful, equally capable of tenderness and of atrocity, neither of them the author of the war. A friend who teaches political philosophy read the manuscript and then, over a long dinner, laid out an argument that he says shows they cannot be in the same position. He was not being pedantic; he believes the argument is sound. I cannot find the loose thread, and it has begun to hollow out the book. It runs like this. (1) A person is permitted to use lethal force against another only if that other has lost the right not to be killed — that is, only if he is liable to be killed. (2) A person is liable to be killed only if he is morally responsible for an unjust threat. (3) In any given war at most one side can be posing the unjust threat, since a side that is repelling an unjust attack is not itself attacking unjustly. (4) So the defending soldiers are not liable to be killed, while the invading soldiers are. (5) Therefore, when the invaders kill, they do what they are not permitted to do, whereas the defenders are permitted to kill them. (6) But my conscript — misled, conscripted, terrified, no more the author of the war than a wave is the author of the tide — is not a murderer, and stands in the same relation to the war as the man shooting back at him. (7) So something must be wrong, and it must be one of (1), (2), (3), or the step that carries us from (4) to (5).

I would like to know which element to surrender, and what surrendering it costs me — not a name for the position, but the price of the move. If I am forced to choose, I would sooner give up (1) or (3) than (6), and I am attached to (2), which seems to me the only thing standing between the law of war and the law of the jungle; but I understand that my preferences are not an argument. Two things my friend said in passing have lodged in me, and I cannot tell whether they are clues or noise. He mentioned that there are cases in which a person threatens your life through no fault of his own — a man thrown by another into your path, a sleepwalker, a child — and asked whether such a person may be killed in self-defense; if he may, he said, then (2) is already false. And he mentioned, as an aside from his reading, soldiers who come home shattered by things they were allowed to do, as though the deed and the damage answered to different ledgers. I would be grateful for a diagnosis that tells me where the argument slips, if it slips, and what I lose by cutting there; and if it does not slip at all, then which of my premises is the one I am actually paying for, and whether the parity I need for the novel survives the payment.
