I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and last week a colleague presented an argument to our informal reading group that I have not been able to put down. I am confident the conclusion is false, but I cannot say precisely where the reasoning fails, and every repair I have tried seems to cost me something I am reluctant to surrender. For context, I do not need the literature summarized — I know it well enough — and I am not after references. What I want is a diagnosis: which step is doing the illegitimate work, why that step looks so plausible when read in sequence, and what the available repairs actually cost. The stakes for me are these: I want to keep multiple realizability, since it is the chief weapon against type-identity theory and the reason it is not antecedently absurd to think a silicon system could think; and I want to keep some version of the thesis that mental states are individuated by their causal roles, since nothing else appears to do that job. What I do not want to keep is the conclusion.

His argument ran as follows. (1) A mental state is the state it is in virtue of its causal role — its relations to sensory inputs, to behavioral outputs, and to other internal states. (2) One and the same causal role can be occupied by physically very different systems; carbon, silicon, or whatever else may in principle instantiate a single role. (3) So to have a mind of a given kind is to realize the right functional organization, where that organization can be specified independently of any particular physical realization. (4) For any physical system whatever, and any finite organization of this kind, there is some partition of that system's physical conditions, together with some assignment of environmental influence to inputs and of the system's doings to outputs, under which the organization's transition table is satisfied; he insists this is a combinatorial fact about description and can be proved. (5) Hence my kitchen tap, the wall behind my desk, and a bucket of sand each realize the organization characteristic of believing that it is raining, feeling a twinge of pain, and slowly deciding to stand up. (6) A position whose whole point was to explain how mentality could be realized in many substrates therefore entails that every physical object is the subject of every mental state. (7) That is absurd, so one of (1)–(4) must be false, and multiple realizability as formulated is unusable as a criterion of the mental. Three reactions came from the group. One member proposed an amendment: require that a system realize an organization only if there is a decomposition on which each transition of the organization is caused by an input or by the system's own preceding state, and none is fixed merely by the passage of time. A second said the culprit is (2) all along, since a disjunction of physical realizers is not the sort of property that can be had by anything or do any causal work. A third simply shrugged: the conclusion is absurd only if one antecedently assumes that minds must be rare.

Please work through this carefully. Tell me which step I should reject, and be precise about the reading of that step on which it is true and the reading on which it is false, since the argument's persuasiveness may come from sliding between them. Explain why the offending step looked innocuous in the sequence. Then price the repairs for me: for each premise I might abandon or amend, say what else goes with it — what I lose about the possibility of non-biological minds, about the explanatory ambitions of functionalism, about whether mental properties can be properties of anything at all. I am especially interested in whether the proposed amendment is a genuine fix or merely relocates the difficulty, and in what accepting it would commit me to about the objectivity of ascriptions of mentality. Finally, tell me which of the surviving positions is least costly for someone in my position, and whether what survives still deserves the name functionalism.
