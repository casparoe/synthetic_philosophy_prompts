I'm a third-year PhD student working on the epistemology of machine learning — mostly on whether testimony from opaque systems can justify belief — and I keep getting dragged onto teaching committees, which is eating into the time I should be spending on the thesis. Here's what's been nagging at me. My department runs three-hour handwritten unseen exams for the final year of the philosophy degree, and the official line, when you get the exams office to say it out loud, is that this is the only way to be sure a student 'really understands' the material rather than just being able to produce the right text. Fair enough. But the same university cheerfully lets those same students take calculators and statistical software into maths and psychology papers, allows a formula sheet in the physics exam, and hands out citation managers as standard. Last term someone in my tutorial submitted a lab report that had obviously been drafted with an LLM, and the committee treated it as straightforward fraud; that same week the psychology people were teaching students to push their data through a package whose internals nobody in the room could explain. And one colleague keeps floating what he calls a 'reconstruction test': any tool is fine, however opaque, so long as the student can, on demand and without the tool, rebuild the key steps of the output and say why each step is warranted. Everyone nods, and then nobody can agree what counts as a key step, or whether the test is even coherent when the output is a fitted model.

So what I actually want is conceptual, not a policy recommendation. Reconstruct the view of understanding that this whole tangle is implicitly running. Does it presuppose that understanding is unaided performance, or the ability to warrant and verify an output however it was produced, or the possession of the internal machinery that would generate the answer, or something about opacity as opposed to the offloading of cognition? Which rival views does the practice genuinely rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably alongside? Is it even internally consistent — is the calculator/LLM line a principled distinction, or just a line about how recently the tool showed up? And if we swapped the implicit view for something distributed across person and tools, or for a view on which understanding is the capacity to evaluate and deploy outputs rather than to produce them, what would actually have to change in how we examine people? I'm definately not asking you to settle the committee's argument for them; I want the presuppositions dragged into the light, including the bits where the practice is underdetermined and several theories would license exactly what we do. Please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email to a colleague — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just argue it through.
