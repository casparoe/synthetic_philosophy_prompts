I teach a seminar on technology and education at a small liberal-arts college, and this term my students kept returning to a question I had not expected: if a machine can learn, what would it mean to educate it? That question has become personally urgent for me because I have been asked to advise our school's new AI-literacy curriculum, and I keep finding that the standard engineering vocabulary of training and alignment does not capture what teachers actually do. I am going to host a public conversation with a philosopher of education who has spent several years developing a reading of Turing's postwar writing on learning machines. Her central claim is that Turing's child-machine proposal is not merely an early sketch of machine learning but a genuine educational theory: on her reading, Turing holds that a machine must acquire its concepts through something like a curriculum, and that the teacher's role is not to program rules but to create the conditions under which the machine can form its own distinctions. I want the interview to press her on that reading rather than let her restate it. Please draft eight to ten questions, with at least one follow-up for each, keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The questions should cover: (1) what work the analogy between educating a child and training a machine is doing, and whether it is meant to be literal or heuristic; (2) the difference between Turing's child machine and an adult machine, and why the former is supposed to require education rather than programming; (3) the role of reward and punishment in Turing's account, and whether that commits him to a behaviorist theory of learning; (4) the tension between discipline and initiative in education, and how a machine could be trained to follow rules while also being capable of original work; (5) whether the child machine needs a social or embodied environment, or whether a disembodied symbol-manipulator could be educated in the relevant sense; (6) how Turing's educational analogy relates to his better-known test for machine intelligence, and whether the test distorts the pedagogical point; (7) what the analogy between education and evolution is supposed to accomplish, and whether it makes the machine's learning a matter of natural selection rather than teaching; (8) what Turing's view implies about the ethics and politics of machine education -- who the teacher is, what authority the teacher has, and whether the machine can be said to have a childhood; (9) whether the child-machine proposal is committed to an empiricist or blank-slate view of mind, and how it handles innate structure; and (10) what a Turing-inspired curriculum for a machine would look like today, and how we would know when its education is complete. Please make the questions concrete enough that she cannot answer them with generalities about the importance of learning, and include a final question that asks her to name the strongest objection to her reading and to say what would have to be true for her to abandon it. Do not ask for bibliographic recommendations or reading lists; I want the questions to stand on their own for an audience that has not read Turing.
