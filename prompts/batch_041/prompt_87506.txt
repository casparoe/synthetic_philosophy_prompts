I am a patient advocate who was recently diagnosed with a chronic pain condition, and I have been appointed to my regional health authority's priority-setting committee, where we are being asked to compare the urgency of very different kinds of suffering; next month I am hosting a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a distinctive account of interpersonal utility comparisons, and I need help preparing questions. Her account treats interpersonal comparisons not as measurements of a common mental currency but as constructions from extended preferences: each person is asked to rank not only their own life states but also the prospect of living the other person's life from the inside, and the comparison is licensed only when the two people's extended rankings converge on the same ordering of the two lives; where they diverge, she says the comparison is indeterminate rather than merely uncertain. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the view. I want at least one question each on (a) how the convergence requirement handles extended preferences that have been distorted by adaptation to deprivation, by projection bias, or by the fact that people often mispredict how they would feel in another's circumstances; (b) whether the account can compare lives when one party cannot take up the extended perspective at all, such as a person with a severe cognitive disability or a non-human animal, and whether that limitation is a defect in the account or a discovery about the limits of comparison; (c) what the account implies for the committee's actual choice between a treatment that reduces moderate chronic pain for many people by a small amount and a treatment that prevents one person's episodes of excruciating pain, including whether the account can yield a verdict at all when the extended rankings do not converge; and (d) whether the account collapses into an ideal-observer theory or merely relocates the value judgment into the selection of whose extended preferences are to count and how convergence is to be measured. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and end with one question that asks what would count as evidence against her account rather than merely a difficulty for it.
