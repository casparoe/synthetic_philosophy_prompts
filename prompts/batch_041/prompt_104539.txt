The room is working reporters plus two editors, so nobody needs convincing that lying is bad and nobody has opened a philosophy book since uni. First, the order. My instinct is that the question "is there a real moral difference?" can't even be asked properly until the room has a grip on the gap between what a sentence says and what it gets across, and until they've seen that lying isn't the same as saying something false -- you can lie with words that happen to come out true, as long as you thought they were false when you said them. Tell me if that ordering is wrong, what has to come before what, and where the piece about whether lying even requires an intention to deceive belongs. Second, the example that carries the whole thing. Bless it or replace it. I also specifically want them to hit the case where the speaker exploits a false presupposition instead of a false implicature -- answering a question that assumes something untrue as though the assumption were fine, the "have you stopped..." shape -- because that's where "but I never actually said anything false" gets genuinely hard to defend.
