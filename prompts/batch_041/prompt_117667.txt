I am a high school student reading about decision theory on my own, and I would like an explanation of a distinction I cannot pin down. Two problems seem to pull in opposite directions. In the first, a predictor has already decided to give me a large reward if a coin came up tails, conditional on whether I would pay a small cost if it came up heads; I see that it came up heads, and one style of reasoning says I should pay the cost anyway, even though the reward is already settled and my payment cannot causally affect it. In the second, a predictor offers to sell me insurance against termites and sends me a letter that is present if and only if exactly one of two things is true: my house has termites, or I decide to buy the insurance; here the same evidential style of reasoning seems to say I should buy the insurance, which is a mistake, because the letter is already evidence about the house and my decision cannot change the house. I want to understand what really separates the two cases. Is it that in the first case the correlation between my action and the predictor's setup runs through my decision procedure, while in the second case it is manufactured by a condition on the payoff? Can that distinction be made precise without circularity, and does it tell us what a decision theory would have to look like to get both cases right?
