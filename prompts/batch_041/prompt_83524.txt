I chair the scientific advisory panel of a national environment agency, and next week we must vote on an application to run a five-year outdoor trial of a fast-growing genetically modified poplar for biomass. Our statute instructs us to apply the precautionary principle and says we need not wait for full scientific certainty before acting, but it neither defines the principle nor settles whether it merely permits action on incomplete evidence or instead shifts the burden of proof onto the applicant to show the trial is safe. The company accepts that gene flow to wild relatives cannot be quantified with present methods; the trial's defenders reply that it is the first stage of a programme meant to displace coal generation, and that refusing it would forgo an uncertain benefit and simply move the work to countries with weaker oversight. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, commit to a definite reading of the precautionary principle and defend that reading, and give the strongest objection to your recommendation a fair hearing — in particular the objection that a strong precautionary rule proves too much, since refusing the trial carries unquantified risks of its own. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
