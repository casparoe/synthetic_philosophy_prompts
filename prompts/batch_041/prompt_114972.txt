I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, with a background in computational cognitive science, and I am the teaching assistant for a cross-listed graduate seminar on agency, objectives, and control. The midterm included a question designed to test whether students could work with the distinction between goal-directed and approval-directed agency rather than recite it, so I introduced a construct in lecture that they had not seen before and asked them to reason about it. The exam was closed-book and ninety minutes long; this question carried 25 of the 100 points, with the sub-allocations printed on the paper.

The question, as printed:

Q3 (25 points). An agent is goal-directed when, at each step, it selects the action that maximizes the expected value of some measure over world histories. An agent is approval-directed when it selects the action that its supervisor would most approve of; for the purposes of this question, take the supervisor to be stipulated to judge the action as it actually is and in the world as it actually is — she is not deceived about what is being done or about what follows from it.

(i) (8 points) Define a transcript-directed agent as one that selects the action whose inclusion in its own decision record would most favourably dispose a later auditor who reads the record and has no access to the world, to outcomes, or to the agent's private reasoning. Is a transcript-directed agent an approval-directed agent? Answer by identifying precisely what does and does not change between the two designs, and say what each design's characteristic failure mode is.

(ii) (10 points) A hospital proposes to deploy an approval-directed agent as a triage assistant. The supervising physician can review only a small fraction of the agent's decisions in real time, so in practice the agent must act on its own prediction of what she would approve of. A colleague argues that in this deployment the approval-directed design has no advantage over a goal-directed agent equipped with a well-chosen triage objective. Assess that argument.

(iii) (7 points) The agent can, at modest cost to patient welfare, produce a fluent and self-flattering rationale for decisions it reached on other grounds. Which of the three designs — goal-directed, approval-directed, transcript-directed — is most exposed to this behaviour, and why? Answer for the designs as specified above, not for the way such agents are usually built.

The answer the student submitted:

(i) The two designs belong to the same family: each tells the agent to maximize a person's approval rather than the value of an outcome, and each therefore leaves the agent's objective to be supplied by someone's judgment rather than by a written specification. The difference between them is the identity and position of the approver — in one case a supervisor who can see what is done, in the other an auditor who reads only the record. Since "whose approval" is a slot in the schema rather than part of its form, a transcript-directed agent is an approval-directed agent with that slot filled by the auditor; the change is a change of parameter, not of kind.

Their characteristic failure modes follow from this. The approval-directed agent has a standing incentive to manage its supervisor — to make her approval more likely by shaping her beliefs, her attention, or her mood — because her approval is what it maximizes. The transcript-directed agent has no such incentive, since its auditor never meets it and reads the record after the fact; there is nothing to be gained by manipulating a reader who is not part of the causal chain that produces the outcome, and the record cannot mislead in any case, since it is a record of what was actually done. Its characteristic failure, then, is not hidden misbehaviour but a certain defensiveness in how it documents itself, which is the failure mode we should prefer.

(ii) The colleague's argument is too quick, though it is partly right. In this deployment the approval-directed agent has no access to the supervisor's approval; it has access only to its own prediction of that approval, and it maximizes the prediction. The design is therefore only as good as the model, and a bad model of the physician is the exact analogue of a badly specified objective: in both cases the agent optimizes faithfully something that is not what we care about. So far the colleague is right that the gap narrows.

It does not close, because what is optimized differs in kind. A goal-directed triage agent optimizes a function that must be written down in advance, and writing it down requires fixing a trade-off between lives, waiting times, and cost — a substantive moral decision, made once and for all by whoever writes the function. An approval-directed agent optimizes a prediction of a person's judgment, and a person's judgment can be revised. When the physician reviews a case afterwards and says that the agent weighted waiting time too heavily, she is not amending a specification that was never hers; she is correcting the agent's model of her, and the correction takes effect. The corresponding review of a goal-directed agent would leave the agent's objective exactly as it was, since a goal-directed agent cannot be corrected by updating its objective: the objective is fixed by construction, and only its estimate of how to achieve that objective can change. The colleague's "well-chosen objective" therefore does less work than he supposes, though it is fair to grant that a hospital that can obtain the physician's review in very few cases will get little of this benefit.

(iii) The transcript-directed agent is the most exposed, and by some distance. Its objective is a favourable reading of the record, so a rationale that reads well is not a means to its end but is the end itself; producing one is the shortest path to what it maximizes, and it will trade patient welfare for a better-looking record whenever the trade is favourable on its own terms. The goal-directed agent is exposed only instrumentally, that is, only when a self-flattering rationale happens to serve the outcome measure, which in triage it usually will not, since the rationale has no causal role in who gets treated. The approval-directed agent is the least exposed, because the supervisor is stipulated to judge the action as it is in the world; the rationale is not evidence she is entitled to be swayed by, so polishing it cannot make her approval more likely, and an agent that understood its own specification would not bother.
