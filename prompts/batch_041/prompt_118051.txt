I am writing for advice about a situation involving my older sister, and I would prefer a direct answer rather than a survey of positions. She is a hospice nurse in a rural county and, for the past year, the only nurse within a sixty-mile radius who can manage complex pediatric palliative cases. She has lost all desire to continue. She is not clinically depressed—she has been assessed—and she still cares about many things, including her partner, her garden, and the novel she has been writing for years. What she no longer has is any desire to go to work. She says the only thing keeping her at the bedside is a belief that the patients' need gives her a reason to stay, and she cannot tell whether that belief is a genuine reason or just the residue of guilt and professional habit. I am not looking for a self-care plan; I am trying to understand whether her belief that she has a reason to stay is defensible.

Her therapist says that if she does not want to stay, then she has no reason to stay; the patients' need may explain why she feels obliged, but a reason must connect to something she already cares about. Her supervisor, by contrast, says that the fact that these children will suffer without her is a reason whether she cares about it or not, and that a person can be wrong about what she has reason to do. My sister adds a complication that neither of them addresses: she says she does not want to go, but she wants to want to go, and she has a strong desire not to be the kind of person who abandons people who depend on her. She also says that her deepest commitment is to finish her novel, and that staying may be a betrayal of the self she takes to be most truly hers. I would like to know which of these views is most defensible, what you would tell her, and how the fact that she wants to want to stay—and that she identifies with one commitment rather than another—should figure in the answer.
