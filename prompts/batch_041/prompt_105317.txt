I am in a theory-and-methods seminar on historiography, and we have spent three weeks circling the same fight about periodization — whether "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" name anything in the past or only something in the discipline. I have to hand in a short response paper next week, and I would like your comments on the draft below before I commit to it. Please be critical rather than encouraging; if the whole framing is wrong, I would rather hear that now than in the seminar.

The draft runs as follows. My thesis is that periodization is neither discovery nor fiction but infrastructure. I distinguish three positions. The first is realism: a period name like "the Renaissance" picks out a real kind of historical entity, in roughly the sense that a Boyd-style homeostatic property cluster is a real kind — a bundle of features (urban wealth, patronage, print, the recovery of classical texts, new techniques in painting) that reliably co-occur because there are mechanisms holding them together, so the question whether the Renaissance was real becomes an empirical question about whether the cluster is causally glued. The second is conventionalism: periods are organizing fictions whose only warrant is pragmatic, and we should stop pretending otherwise. Everyone in my seminar thinks this is the sophisticated position. The third, mine, is that periods are real but their reality is produced by the practices that use them — a looping effect of the kind Hacking describes for human kinds. Calling a span "the Renaissance" reorganizes archives, chairs, journals, degree requirements, museum wings, and funding lines, and that reorganization makes the span progressively more causally efficacious; the label does not describe a joint in nature so much as install it. I concede two things here, and this is where I most expect to be attacked: the looping story seems to require that the labeled people be able to hear the label, which the dead cannot, so at best the loop runs through us and not through them; and if any label that survives counts as real by construction, the view looks unfalsifiable.

The rest of the paper applies a distinction I find useful between thin and thick period claims. A thin claim says something like: in Italian cities between roughly 1300 and 1550, the rate of production of texts and images that self-consciously imitate classical models was markedly higher than in the preceding two centuries. That is checkable, and it is the kind of thing I think we can keep. A thick claim says the age was animated by a new spirit of individualism or worldliness or man-centeredness. That is where the heat comes from, and I argue it is a nineteenth-century inheritance — the label as a bounded period was consolidated in European scholarship then, most influentially by Burckhardt. I also note that the standard boundaries are not self-evident: the Italian Renaissance is variously said to end in 1453, 1492, 1517, or 1527, and the Scientific Revolution is bracketed by 1543 and 1687 essentially because two famous books sit at the ends, while the participants did not call it that — Butterfield's Origins of Modern Science, which did so much to popularize the phrase, announces that it "outshines everything since the rise of Christianity." And I point out that there were earlier renaissances, Carolingian and twelfth-century, which suggests the pattern recurs whenever conditions allow and so is not a unique rupture. The conclusion I want to draw is that the question "are periods real?" is a category error; the real question is which of several incompatible periodizations best serves a stated purpose, and best is purpose-relative, so the labels function like a convention about which side of the road to drive on — their value is that we all draw the line in the same place, not that the line is in the right place. "Early modern" is my exhibit: it is a compromise label that swallows the Renaissance without settling anything.

What I would like from you is a set of margin notes rather than a rewrite. Third, tell me whether the thin/thick distinction does the work I need or collapses under pressure — in particular whether thick claims can be rescued by being restated as claims about what people at the time believed about themselves. Fourth, identify the weakest joint in the argument as a whole and say what the strongest objection to it would be. Please do not give me reading lists or citations; I am asking about the argument, not the bibliography.
