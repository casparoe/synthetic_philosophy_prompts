I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy of science and, more recently, the literature on extracting what large language models know. I have written down the position I currently hold, and before I commit it to a seminar paper I would like an audit rather than a summary. Here it is.

1. A trained network is a physical system with a determinate internal organisation, and questions about what it does or does not know have answers that do not depend on whether anyone has yet managed to extract them. I am a realist about unobservable entities in physics; I do not regard electrons as useful fictions, and I see no reason to be a fictionalist about the internal states of a network.

2. Nevertheless, I hold that a claim to the effect that a model knows some particular thing is empty unless someone can specify a procedure — a family of prompts together with a scoring rule — that would count as testing it. A proposition about a model's knowledge that no procedure could ever address is not false; it is not yet a proposition.

3. At present the only technique I am prepared to treat as reliable evidence is black-box behavioural testing: prompt the model, sample it many times, and characterise the distribution of outputs. Probing classifiers, feature-attribution methods and other attempts to read the internals are interesting, but I do not yet treat them as evidence about what the model knows.

4. A finding about a model's knowledge counts as scientific only if another group, given the same weights and the same prompt distribution, can reproduce it. Single-prompt anecdotes are not evidence, and neither is anything that depends on the particular phrasing that happened to work in my hands.

5. In my old field I would not trust a reading from an instrument whose error characteristics I did not understand; trusting a measurement meant possessing a causal account of how the instrument produced the number. For a language model, however, the instrument is also the object under study, and the standard we calibrate against is usually another model or a panel of human raters.

6. When a model narrates its reasoning, I treat the narration as further behaviour to be explained, not as testimony about its internal causes — much as a patient's account of why they acted is data about the patient rather than a readout of the mechanism.

7. All the same, the technique I find most productive is patient interrogation: asking the model to explain itself, to give reasons, to consider counterarguments, and to answer the same question in several different ways.

What I want from you is a consistency audit of this set. Tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to conflict, and for each genuine tension trace it to the specific commitments that generate it. I expect some of these to be independent of one another, so please do not flag every pair indiscriminately; where an apparent conflict dissolves, state the distinction that dissolves it. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than cataloguing them as equally bad, and for each one say what the least costly repair would be — weakening a commitment, reinterpreting it, or abandoning it outright — and what that repair would cost me in explanatory or evidential terms. Please conclude by naming the single revision that would resolve the most tension at the least cost, and say what I would be giving up by making it.
