I am trying to understand precisely what Malthus commits himself to in the preface to the first edition of the Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), and I would like help reading the following passage closely. I am not asking for a summary of his doctrine, which I know well enough; I want to understand the structure of the claim he takes himself to be making. He writes:

"The view which he has given of human life has a melancholy hue, but he feels conscious that he has drawn these dark tints from a conviction that they are really in the picture, and not from a jaundiced eye or an inherent spleen of disposition."

And a little earlier in the same preface:

"If he should succeed in drawing the attention of more able men to what he conceives to be the principal difficulty in the way to the improvement of society and should, in consequence, see this difficulty removed, even in theory, he will gladly retract his present opinions and rejoice in a conviction of his error."

What I want help with is this. Malthus distinguishes between the empirical facts he adduces and a "difficulty" that might be "removed, even in theory." Does that distinction commit him to a falsifiable prediction, or to something weaker — a claim about the tendency of population to press against the means of subsistence that could survive even if his specific historical forecasts turn out badly? Suppose we grant that the two centuries since 1798 brought falling fertility, the demographic transition, and agricultural productivity growth on a scale he did not anticipate. What exactly would count as "removing the difficulty in theory," as opposed to merely refuting his forecasts? And how should I weigh his stated willingness to retract, and to retract gladly, against the fact that the Malthusian tradition — including later editions of the Essay and later writers in his wake — has so often preserved the theory by adding qualifications rather than abandoning it? Is there a principled line between a legitimate rescue of a long-run prediction and an ad hoc one, and if so, where does Malthus's own case fall?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
