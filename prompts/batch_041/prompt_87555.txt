Having spent a career in experimental physics, I have lately taken up philosophy, and I find myself drawn to Ayer's handling of survival after death as I understand it: the hypothesis is not unintelligible, since one can say what it would be for a person to persist without a body, but nothing in our evidence gives it positive support, and the identity conditions it would require — continuity of memory and character sufficient to reidentify the same person — are precisely the ones we ordinarily anchor to a living organism. My own credence that some form of personal survival occurs is about 0.05; a former colleague of a religious temperament puts it near 0.6, and we cannot locate where our disagreement actually lives. Please give me your own credence in the proposition that a human person's psychological continuity survives the destruction of their body, and decompose it: the probability that the survival hypothesis is even coherent given our best account of personal identity; the probability that the identity conditions it requires could be met without the organism; the probability that the available empirical material (near-death reports, mediumistic claims, and the like) lifts the likelihood of survival above its base rate; and any further terms you think belong. Give each term a number and a one-line justification, show how they combine, and name the term your final figure is most sensitive to, along with the observation or argument that would move it most. End with \answer{...} enclosing your final credence.
