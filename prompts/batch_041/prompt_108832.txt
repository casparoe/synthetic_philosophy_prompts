I am a novelist, and I have been trying for some time to make sense of the Death in Damascus case — the man whom Death informs of an appointment, who flees Damascus for Aleppo and dies there, and who learns that Aleppo is where the appointment had been all along. I have read enough to know the story and the shape of the quarrel, not the machinery: philosophers use it against both the causal and the evidential ways of deciding what to do, and the story seems to leave the man with no move that helps him. My real vocabulary, though, is the novelist's: foreshadowing, dramatic irony, the gap between what a character knows and what the reader knows, motive, the author's plan standing behind a character's choice, the inevitability that makes an ending feel earned. Restate the disagreement in those terms, as faithfully as you can — what the causalist says about the man, what the evidentialist says, and where the two part company — and then report what does not come across. Which parts survive the translation intact, which I can carry over only in a distorted form, and which have no counterpart in how I think about plots at all? In particular, is the fatalism the story seems to force on us the same fatalism I feel when I have already decided how a novel ends, or is that resemblance an illusion? If the loss is a defect of my vocabulary, say so; if it is because the philosophers are trading on a distinction that is not really there, say that instead. Please write continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables; I would rather read it as a letter.
