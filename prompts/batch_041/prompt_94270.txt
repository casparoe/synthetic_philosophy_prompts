I run the interpretation team at a natural history museum — three writers, none of us trained in biology — and we're currently churning out about 60 labels for a new gallery on insects, fungi and odd animal behaviour, each label capped at 80 words and pitched at a grade-6 reading level, with sign-off from a curator who is very picky about wording. We keep having the same fight: when is it OK to write that the wasp paralyses the spider so that its larva has fresh food, vs. that the wasp wants the spider to stay alive, vs. just describing the mechanics? Last month a visiting scientist complained that one of our labels smuggled a little mind in through the back door, and honestly he wasn't wrong, and I dont have any principled way to draw the line — I've been going on vibes and on what sounds nice in a vitrine.

Then a colleague mentioned a philosophy idea I'd never come across: free-floating rationales (Dennett, I think — dont quote me on the attribution). The gist, as she explained it: a rationale is a reason for something, and a rationale can be free-floating when there really is a reason why a trait or behaviour is the way it is — normally because of what it did for the organism's ancestors — but nothing anywhere actually has or entertains that reason. The bee's dance really is for telling the others where the food is, in the sense that this is why the dance exists and why it works; but the bee isn't doing arithmetic on sun angles and thinking, if I do this they'll find the flowers. And sometimes there isn't even a free-floating rationale — the thing is just a byproduct, a constraint, or an accident, and then the label should say so.

What I want from you is a procedure my writers can actually run, label by label, with no philosophy and not much biology: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what each answer licenses them to write (which register of language — so that, in order to, serves to, because it wants, or bare mechanism), where the procedure stops and forces neutral wording, and where it hands the decision back to a human because the question is one of judgment rather than fact. Then run it on three cases from the gallery, one of which I expect to be annoyed by: (1) the waggle dance; (2) a fungus that makes an ant climb up and bite into a leaf so the fungus can drop its spores on new ground — the ant is obviously just a vehicle, so whose rationale is it, if anyone's, and how does a label even begin to phrase that; and (3) our beloved diorama story that the jays plant the oak forests by forgetting where they buried the acorns — the visitors adore it, the curator hates it, and I suspect the procedure will tell me to gut it. For each case, show me the label text we'd end up with and say which question in the procedure did the real work. And be straight with me about the two things I'm nervous about: which steps are just judgment calls wearing a lab coat, and what would change your mind about the procedure itself — which case or consideration would make you draw the lines somewhere else, and where the reasoning is most likely to fail.
