Last Thursday two of my colleagues tore into each other after our colloquium and I sat in the back row taking notes like a coward instead of refereeing. I've typed the exchange up below as faithfully as I can. I want you to actually adjudicate it — move by move, not vibe by vibe.

The setup, briefly, so you don't have to guess at it: Dana is running the eliminativist line. Belief, desire, intending and friends aren't loose-but-serviceable descriptions of something real; they're the posits of a stagnant theory of behavior that a mature neuroscience will eventually replace rather than reduce, the way phlogiston got replaced. Ravi thinks that's either false or vacuously true, and he isn't being cute about it. They're both serious people and neither is a strawman, which is exactly why I couldn't just call it.

Here's how it went.

1. Dana: Folk psychology is a theory, and a bad one. Two and a half thousand years and it hasn't gotten any better at predicting what a person will do. When our ancestors said a falling rock moved because it wanted to get back to its place, we didn't reduce that, we dropped it. Belief-talk is in the same spot; we just don't have the replacement yet.

2. Ravi: The analogy does alot of work and none of it is argued. Phlogiston went because a successor explained the same data better and predicted new things. Nobody has a successor that predicts what my colleague will do at the next faculty meeting without belief-desire vocabulary. And heat wasn't eliminated by kinetic theory, it was identified with it. So "replace or reduce" is a false choice, and belief could be the heat case, not the phlogiston case.

3. Dana: The reason heat-talk got vindicated is that it made impersonal, quantitative, converging predictions. Belief-talk "succeeds" because we score it ourselves — we predict loosely, then redescribe what happened in the same vocabulary, then declare the prediction vindicated. A theory that grades its own homework: its successes aren't evidence.

4. Ravi: That's a general skepticism about observation dressed up as an argument against one vocabulary. If self-scoring disqualifies a theory, it disqualifies every theory, including the neuroscience you're betting on. And it cuts the other way: your prediction that belief-talk will be eliminated is being scored by your own account of what elimination is.

5. Ravi, second wind: Grant the whole causal story. Suppose neuroscience one day gives a complete circuit-level account of why I stood up and left the room, with no beliefs anywhere in it. Belief still does a job that no causal story does: it's the thing we blame, forgive, promise, testify and sue over. A posit can be indispensable to a normative practice without being a natural kind, and that's not a consolation prize.

6. Dana: That's a prediction about what people will keep doing, not an argument about what there is. We also built courts on witchcraft and possession, and those didn't survive contact with the causal story. Ravi: witchcraft had no predictive grip at all, belief-talk does. Dana: we're back to the self-scoring. Anyway, I'm not telling anyone to stop saying I believe it'll rain — my claim is about what exists, not about what to say. Ravi: then there's nothing left to disagree about except a prediction about the future of science, and if that's all it is, stop calling it a revolution in our self-conception. Dana: no — if nothing in the world answers to belief, the practice is in the same position phlogiston-talk was in, minus the timetable.
