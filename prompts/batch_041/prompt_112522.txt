I run a ninety-minute session at a weeklong AI policy workshop for about two dozen graduate students drawn from philosophy, computer science, economics, and public policy. It comes at the end of a day on what it means for a deployed system to serve human values, a day that leaves most of them with a comfortable division of labor: the normative part is settled by saying that people should decide, and the hard part is technical. I want an activity that makes them feel why that answer stalls — the moment when "let people decide" runs out because there is no single decider, and every procedure for manufacturing one decider out of many does something to somebody — and then a second, subtler moment, when they see that neutrality among conceptions of the good is a constraint on how you aggregate rather than a way of escaping aggregation. I know the literature on value aggregation and liberal neutrality well enough that you can be technical with me in the design notes, but the participants should not hear either phrase, or any lecture, until the debrief; the idea has to land through the rules.

Please design it as a runnable simulation with roles, materials, a written timeline, and a debrief. The frame I have in mind: the participants sit as a values council convened to fix the objective function of a municipal AI assistant that allocates a fixed pool of public resources across a mid-sized city — an aging native-born population, a fast-growing immigrant community, a university, a shrinking manufacturing base, and a housing shortage. Each participant receives a character card giving them a coherent conception of the good life, a short first-person statement of why it matters, and a preference profile over five named goods (say: transit subsidies, elder care hours, school places, library and public space hours, and direct cash transfers). The council's job is to set the weights in the objective function. Build in two things that I think are essential. First, an exchange-rate problem they cannot dodge: give each participant a small number of tokens that can be spent only to increase the weight on the good their character ranks highest, and make the tokens scarce enough that the group has to decide whether intensities across people are comparable at all, and who gets to say. Second, a nonlinearity downstream of the weights — a threshold, a winner-take-all allocation, or a scoring rule with a cliff in it — so that a small difference in weights produces a large and visible change in who gets what, and averaging two positions to be fair turns out to have victims. Then add a round with two designated auditors whose job is to object that the resulting weights could not be justified to some reasonable conception of the good, and who hold a veto that costs them something to use, and a final round in which the machine is released and the group watches its allocations, before being asked whether they would want to run the exercise again with the weights they now wish they had set.

For the deliverable: the exact rules, the cards, the token and veto mechanics, the timeline for ninety minutes, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the underlying issues — the difference between aggregating preferences about outcomes and aggregating whole conceptions of the good; the possibility that a procedure can be neutral while its output is not, and the regress that opens when the council must also choose the procedure for choosing the weights; whether the weights should track what people say they value or what they would value under fuller information and greater mutual concern, and why those two answers diverge here; and the thought that a machine maximizing a weighted sum is not a neutral instrument but a policy with a constituency. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect at least one participant to refuse to put numbers on anything, at least one to treat the whole thing as pure horse-trading for their own card, and the auditors to be either ignored or captured — and how to handle each without rescuing the exercise by explaining it. Finally, be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself, and tell me what to say if the group converges on a comfortable procedural answer and wants to go home satisfied. Please write in American English, and do not include a reading list or citations.
