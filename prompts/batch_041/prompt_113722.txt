I'm a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy training, and I've somehow ended up running a small lunchtime Stoicism thing with two people from my team — we read a bit each week and argue about it, mostly badly. Last session is next Thursday and I promised to close it with a poem, partly because I said I would and partly because I want something that isn't just another poster on the wall. So put on your poet hat and your classics hat at once — I need someone who knows this material cold, not someone who's read the top ten quotes. Here's the thing I'm actually stuck on and want the poem to hold. The version of Stoicism I got into first was the airport kind: sort everything into what you can control and what you can't, stop sweating the second pile, move on. Then we read the actual stuff and it's stranger than that. The older doctrine doesn't say the other pile doesn't matter — health, reputation, your kid are things you're still supposed to go after, just not things you'd call good, and every plan is supposed to come with a silent rider attached, something like "if nothing gets in the way." And once you ask whether my *judgments* are really up to me — I've got a short fuse because of how I was raised, I lose a week to a bad nights sleep, I can't will myself into a different reaction when a deploy goes sideways at 3am — the clean two-way split starts to look like it's got a big smeared middle, which is exactly where the modern books either ignore the problem or quietly bolt on a third category and never deal with the consequences. I want a poem that lives in that middle and refuses to resolve it.

Form: a sestina. I like it for this because the permutation is basically a fixed index shuffle you didn't choose — you only get to decide what happens in between the end-words — and then the three-line envoi drags all six of them back together at once and something has to give. Six stanzas of six, same six end-words, the next stanza taking them in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3 of the last, then the envoi. Use these six end-words unless one of them is genuinely going to fight you, in which case swap one: back (I herniated a disc last year and it definately owns me), weather, temper, opinion (my dad's, mostly), deploy, past. Two hard rules: don't quote any ancient text and don't put words in Epictetus's mouth — I can't check a quote and I don't want a fake one — and don't let it slide into a motivational poster or into the cheap move of just sneering at the poster either. It has to work for someone who's never heard of any of this; my two friends will get the in-jokes, but my partner will read it and I want her to like it without a footnote. Before you start writing, think through two or three different ways the envoi could land and pick the one that actually earns the turn instead of the obvious one.

Last thing: when it's done, tell me straight how confident you are that you've got the Stoic ideas right, and name the single point you're least sure about — and if the poem leans on a contested reading of the philosophy or takes a liberty to make a line work, say where, don't bury it.
