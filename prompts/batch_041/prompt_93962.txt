Right, I've got a standing bet with a collegue in the politics department and I need an actual number out of you, not a survey of the literature.

The question is whether Sweden is more liberal than Norway — and my side of the bet is that this isn't the sort of thing that's true or false at all. Here's the line I run in my seminars. 'Liberal' in ordinary political speech is doing at least three jobs, and they come apart: (a) how much the state leaves you alone — negative liberty, deregulation, low tax, easy to run your own life; (b) how much the state provides and redistributes — social liberalism, the welfare state; (c) how permissive the surrounding society is, i.e. whether your neighbours, your employer or the person at the till actually care what you do in private. (In the American sense 'liberal' mostly means (b) and (c); in the European sense it often means (a); that's half the trouble.) I've been calling (c) the 'neighbour variable' in lectures, which is a rubbish name, but never mind. My suspicion is that the two countries sort one way on (a), another way on (b), and that (c) is where the whole thing falls apart — partly because we've got no decent measure for it, and partly because the indices we do have can't seperate two countries this similar.

So:

1. Give me your credence that there's a fact of the matter — that exactly one of 'Sweden is more liberal than Norway' and 'Norway is more liberal than Sweden' is true, without relativising to any conception of liberalism. Mine's about 0.25, but I'm arguing the other side of the bet, so discount accordingly.

2. Decompose that number. How much of it turns on whether 'liberal' is a determinate property at all, as opposed to a family-resemblance cluster or an essentially contested concept? How much on whether the (a), (b) and (c) orderings actually converge once you fix a conception? How much on whether convergence would even be evidence of the real thing rather than of a shared metric built by people with an agenda? Add whatever other terms belong, give each a rough probabilty and a line of justification, show me how they combine, and tell me which one you're least sure about.

3. Separately: your credence that Norway is the more liberal of the two, under whichever single conception you'd defend if someone made you pick one. If you think even that is ill-posed, say so and give me the number for the nearest well-posed neighbour of the question instead.

4. What would move your number in (1)? A new survey item, a legal change, some conceptual analysis that dissolves one of the three readings, or nothing at all? And if the honest answer is 'nothing', tell me whether that's a defect in the question or just the normal condition of comparative political philosophy.

One more thing, and it's the bit my collegue keeps ducking: if you come back and say it's underdetermined, what does that do to the thing people actually say in the pub — that Sweden's the liberal one? Is that false, meaningless, or true enough for the pub? Straight answer on that, please.

British English, and please don't use 'it depends what you mean by liberal' as a substitute for the decomposition. The decomposition is the answer to that worry, not a way round it.
