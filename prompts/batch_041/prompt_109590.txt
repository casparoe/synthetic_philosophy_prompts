I'm the senior fiscal analyst in the nonpartisan research office of a state legislature, and the revenue committee has handed us an unusual request. The chair has introduced a bill to phase out our estate tax entirely; other members have floated counter-proposals — sharply raising the exemption, converting the estate tax into an inheritance tax on what each heir actually receives rather than on the estate as a whole, capping tax-free lifetime gifts, and closing the generation-skipping loophole that lets a trust shelter a fortune from the tax for a century. The committee wants our office to produce, for any such provision, a reasoned statement of whether it makes the distribution of opportunity in this state more or less equal, written so that it can be read aloud by a member of either party without being dismissed as advocacy. We have about a week, no budget to commission new microsimulations, and a charter that forbids us from taking a position on the members' ultimate goals; we may analyze a provision against a stated standard, but the choice of standard and the final vote belong to the committee. The office director signs off on everything that goes into the packet, so the procedure has to be something a competent analyst can run the same way twice and defend line by line.

I want you to build that procedure. Begin from the most defensible account you can give of what equality of opportunity actually requires, then translate it into a decision procedure my analysts can run on a tax provision without having read any of the underlying philosophy. A few things I've been reading that strike me as relevant but that I cannot yet operationalize: the thought that egalitarian justice should be sensitive to people's ambitions and choices while remaining insensitive to their endowments, so that inherited wealth and inherited capacity are candidates for neutralization but differences traceable to a person's own effort are not; the hypothetical-insurance idea, that we ask what protection against being born without wealth or earning power people would buy before knowing where in the lottery they would land; and the distinction between taxing an estate and taxing what each heir receives, which changes who is treated as bearing the burden and therefore what the provision is even doing. I want the procedure to specify, in order, what to look for in the text of a provision, what fact or standard assumption makes each step answerable, what answer at each step disqualifies the provision from passing the screen, and — importantly — which steps are really value judgments dressed up as analysis, which my office must flag for the committee rather than resolve on its own. It has to survive at least two hard cases that I expect to come up: an illiquid family farm or closely held business that could only pay the tax by being sold, and a provision whose equalizing effect on opportunity is real but whose revenue cost falls on programs that themselves equalize opportunity.

Then run the procedure, step by step, on three provisions — a straight repeal, a large per-heir exemption with a graduated rate above it, and a lifetime accessions tax on the cumulative total of gifts and inheritances a person receives — and show me where the procedure lands in each case. I expect it to give an answer on at least one of these that the committee, and perhaps I, will not like, and I would rather see that spelled out than smoothed over. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: the questions it will always hand back to the committee, the assumptions it smuggles in, and the circumstances under which an analyst applying it honestly should refuse to give a clean verdict.
