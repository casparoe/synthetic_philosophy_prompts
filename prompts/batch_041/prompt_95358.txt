Ok so I've talked myself into a position and I want you to try to talk me back out of it. Here it is. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, if both players know they're going to reason through the situation in the exact same way — literally the same program run twice in separate sandboxes, or two people who've both committed in advance to following the same decision procedure — then mutual cooperation is the rational play and defecting is a mistake. Not a moral mistake, a mistake in the decision-theoretic sense. The argument is embarrassingly simple: if you and I are running the same procedure on the same problem, then whatever I decide, you decide too. So my choice isn't really a choice between C and D, it's a choice between the pair (C,C) and the pair (D,D), and (C,C) pays better. Defecting only looks tempting if you smuggle in the assumption that you're the one doing the deciding while the other guy is just a fact about the world. I'll stipulate numbers if it helps: mutual cooperation 3 each, mutual defection 1 each, unilateral defection 5 for the defector and 0 for the sucker. Dominance says defect. I say dominance is reasoning about the wrong thing.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed this actually is. Some specific things:

What would count against it? Not the usual objection that people don't cooperate in lab experiments, I already know that and I don't think it's very interesting. I mean the arguments, cases, and formal results that should actually move me.

Rank them. Fatal, then serious, then merely awkward. I want to know which ones a defender can shrug off and which ones they can't.

Is this just evidential decision theory wearing a costume? If it is, does it inherit the standard counterexamples, the ones where treating an action as evidence for an outcome it obviously can't cause gives you insane advice? And if it isn't the same thing, what's the actual difference, and does the difference survive pressure or is it verbal?

How much of the view survives when the two agents are only similar rather than identical? Same source code but a different random seed. Two humans who both took the same econ class and both read the same blog. Is there a principled place to draw the line, or does the whole thing just go vague at the edges as soon as you leave the perfect-correlation case? I suspect this is where it gets shaky and I want to know if I'm right.

What about games where the joint optimum is asymmetric — where the two of you doing the same thing is the problem, not the solution? Like one has to go left and the other right, and there's no symmetric point that's best. Does the view have any recommendation there at all, or is it just silent? And if it's silent on a whole class of games, does that count against it or is that just outside its scope?

What do the people who actually hold this kind of view concede? I don't want only the critics' list. If someone sympathetic would say yeah, X is a real cost of the position, I want to know what X is.

And the uncomfortable one: is there anything that could happen that would make me give this up? If the view turns out to be a conceptual claim about what the word rational means rather than a claim about how anyone should bet, is that a dodge or is that just honestly what the view is? I genuinely can't tell and it bothers me.

For what it's worth, the case that does all the work for me is the two-identical-programs version. I'd probalby abandon the whole thing before I gave up that case. So push hardest there — I want to know whether that case is carrying more weight than it can bear, and what it would take for me to have to give up on it specifically.
