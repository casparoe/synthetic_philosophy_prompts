I'm trying to seperate two rival explanations of the Allais reversal: the one that says it's regret (roughly Loomes and Sugden's idea — when you pick an option you compare your payoff in each state with what the other option would have paid you in that same state, and regret over a bad comparison bites harder than rejoicing over a good one), and the one that says it's just probability weighting (rank-dependent utility à la Quiggin, or the weighting part of cumulative prospect theory, where you transform the cumulative chances so a lottery's value depends only on its own marginal distribution).

Build me a pair of choice problems that pulls those two apart. Brainstorm a couple of candidates first, then commit.

Constraints: each problem is a straight choice between two lotteries, A and B. Any theory that looks only at marginals must therefore give the same verdict in both problems, while regret theory, which looks at the joint distribution, should flip and prefer a different lottery in problem 2 than in problem 1. Keep it realistic: no sure-thing lotteries, no zero probabilities, prizes a normal person could actually face (tens or hundreds of pounds), no more than four states, and no dusting off the textbook Allais numbers.
