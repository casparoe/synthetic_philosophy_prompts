I'm a science and ideas journalist, and in a few weeks I've been asked to run a seventy-five-minute session for the data desk of a metropolitan daily: a dozen reporters who can read a regression table and took one college statistics course, ten years ago. The subject is why the uncertainty we publish is almost always a single number, and what we give up by pretending it is one. I don't want the session to be a lecture on imprecise probability, and I emphatically don't want you to explain the topic to me; I want you to plan how I should teach it, with a whiteboard, no slides, and no notation beyond what I can put up in a minute. So: what has to come first, and what depends on what? My own instinct is that a room cannot see the point of a range of credences until it has first felt the full force of the argument that a rational agent's degrees of belief must be precise (the betting argument, the one that says that if you will buy and sell at odds that don't correspond to any single probability, someone can arrange to take money off you no matter how the world turns out), and that the session only works if the audience accepts that argument and then watches it come apart. If that ordering is right, tell me exactly where the pivot belongs and what has to be in place before it can land; if it's wrong, tell me what the real dependency structure is. One specific question: does the formalism have to be on the board before or after the intuitive case that motivates it? I mean the move from "my credence is 0.4" to "my credences are the set of all probability functions that satisfy certain constraints," together with the fact that the lower and upper bounds of such a set need not themselves be probabilities of anything.

For the case that carries the whole session, I had planned the two-urn setup: one urn whose composition is stated, a second urn where I know only that it holds a hundred balls, red or black in some unstated proportion, and the usual pull toward betting on the known urn. Is that the right carrier, and if so, what must it be made to do that it doesn't do on its own; if not, what should replace it? I'm suspicious of it on two grounds. Everyone has seen it, so it arrives pre-loaded with sixty years of commentary and the audience may simply recognise it rather than think about it; and it invites the reading that the second urn's composition is merely unknown, when the point I want them to leave with is that some questions about my own state of belief have no answer, and that this is not the same as ignorance, and not the same as a second-order probability either. It isn't that I have a distribution over distributions; it's that my credence is genuinely a range, and the range is not a summary of what I don't know. Whatever carries the session also has to be able to do something later on: show that gathering evidence can make a range wider rather than narrower, which is the result that breaks most people's grip on the picture, and which I'd like them to reach as a conclusion rather than be told. If a different example can carry both jobs, I'd rather use it.

Then the misconceptions, which are really what I'm paying for. I'd like a list of the ones this audience will predictably bring in or form, each with the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and a one-line way of heading it off that doesn't require the machinery I've said I won't put on the board. My own guesses, to be corrected: that a range just means "we don't know"; that anything a range can do a prior over priors can do, so the whole topic is a detour a good Bayesian can skip; that imprecision is a licence for wishful thinking, since if your credences are a set you can always find a member that likes the conclusion you wanted; that the bounds are probabilities of something in the world; that imprecision is a temporary defect that more evidence will always cure; and that this is a topic about groups disagreeing rather than about one person's state of mind. I expect the most serious objection belongs near the end and that I should present it in its strongest form myself: that a set of credences is just a committee of precise agents, so nothing is gained and the real work is merely displaced into an unprincipled choice of decision rule. Tell me whether that is the right objection to foreground, where it should go, and how much of the standard battery of decision rules (act only on options that are optimal under some member of the set; rank options by their worst case; some weighted compromise between best and worst case) a room like this needs in order to see the problem, versus how much I should openly refuse to explain. End with the single question I should ask in the last five minutes to find out whether the distinction actually landed rather than whether they enjoyed the hour, and with what I should say out loud that I am leaving out at this level. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this for a living, and don't hand me a contested position as though it were the consensus; if some part of what I've assumed above is itself disputed, say so plainly, because I will be repeating it to reporters.
