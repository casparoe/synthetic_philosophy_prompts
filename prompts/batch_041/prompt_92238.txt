I teach a grad seminar on modality and I'm bored to death of the same four thought experiments, so this term I want to run a session on the Narnia books as a stress test for David Lewis's modal realism. I want your take before I inflict it on the students.

Here's the puzzle. Lewis's whole picture is that a possible world is a maximal, concrete, spatiotemporally connected whole, and that worlds are causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another — nothing in one world bears any spatiotemporal relation to anything in another. Fine. But the Pevensies walk into a wardrobe and come out in Narnia, and later they get there through a wood full of pools (in The Magician's Nephew) and through paintings and rings and whatnot. Whatever the mechanism, the traffic is two-way and the people are the same people, which seems to break isolation twice over: causally, because events in one world are causing events in the other, and by identity, because the same Lucy who is a schoolgirl in England is a queen in Narnia. On Lewis's own view there's no trans-world identity — you get counterparts, not the same individual in two worlds — so if the wardrobe really delivered you to another possible world, the books would be quietly assuming something Lewis denies.

My instinct is that the honest move is to say the wardrobe and the Wood Between the Worlds don't deliver you to a possible world at all: Narnia is just a far-off region of our world, connected by some weird causal channel, and the isolation thesis survives. But that feels like it saves the theory by demoting the fiction, and I don't like it. There's also the time thing — years pass in Narnia while an afternoon passes here, and in one book Narnia's whole history runs down while the children barely age — which is a real causal coupling, so it cuts against my demotion too. And then there's Aslan creating Narnia, which is odd for a Lewisian world: worlds on his picture aren't made, they just are the maximal sums they are, and a world with a creator looks like it's got an outside.

The part I most want your help with is the ending of the series, where Narnia is destroyed and the characters end up somewhere that's presented as the real Narnia, with the one they knew being only a copy or a shadow of it. That's not Lewis's flat ontology of worlds at all — it's a hierarchy, with some worlds more real than others and ours somewhere near the bottom. So I think the books are actually running a Platonist picture, not a modal-realist one, and the wardrobe is a device for moving up the hierarchy rather than across to a peer world.

So: read the Narnia books through modal realism and tell me whether any of it survives. Where does Lewis's apparatus genuinely illuminate something in the books — the counterpart relation, say, when the children come back from Narnia and have to be children again, or the question of what makes a world a world? Where does the fiction just refuse the theory, and is that refusal interesting or just a category error on my part? And does C.S. Lewis have a picture of worlds that's actually doing work here, one that a modal realist should feel the pull of — because if he does, I'd rather teach it that way than as a cautionary tale. Don't bother with page references or what the critics have said, I can't look anything up from here, I just want the argument. Also flag if I'm cheating somewhere, I have a habit of that.
