I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a module on normativity — specifically the half of it concerned with reasons for attitudes rather than reasons for action. The final assignment permits one creative piece in place of an essay, and I want to spend it on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, transplanted into a genre where nobody expects it.

Write the employee handbook excerpt for a fictional financial firm, Calloway & Frame, whose Values Programme requires not merely compliant behaviour but the attitudes themselves: staff must admire their line managers and be grateful for the opportunity to work there, and the annual review scores those attitudes. The piece should be deadpan throughout — a header, a short preamble, a run of numbered clauses, and a closing note signed by a Chief Values Officer — with no narrator, no winking, and no footnotes explaining the joke. Nobody should be able to tell from inside the document that it is a joke.

The humour has to come from the philosophy rather than from generic corporate-bashing, and I want the seminar's machinery visible in the clauses. Without naming any of it as theory, I want the document to dramatise:

- the distinction between a reason for a state and a reason for an action — the penalty, if it gives me anything, gives me a reason to *be* grateful or to *be* admiring, while what it plainly gives me is a reason to do things (attend the workshop, sign the form) that might bring the attitude about;
- the thought that the first sort of reason leaves the attitude unfitting — nothing about my manager has altered, so admiration procured by threat is admiration with the wrong grounding — which the handbook should half-concede in the flat register of a compliance document;
- the self-defeat built into the requirement: a clause stating that instrumental admiration is reclassified as compliance and does not count toward the score, so that the requirement becomes unsatisfiable by the very reason the document supplies for meeting it;
- the regress, in a clause obliging me to value the fact that I am being asked to value the firm's values;
- and the "just fake it" escape route, closed off by a sincerity audit whose methods the handbook describes only vaguely and menacingly.

Keep the document internally consistent — no clause should accidentally release the reader from the obligation — and let the piece read as fair to anyone who knows the object-given/state-given literature, while someone who doesn't should still be able to take it as a straight office document gone quietly mad. Invent everything; use no real firm and no quotation from any real text.

Structure the response as follows:

1. "The document" — the handbook excerpt described above, roughly 700 words.
2. "Commentary: the machinery" — roughly 350 words, mapping each clause to the philosophical move it dramatises and telling me which of the jokes is the weakest and why.
3. "Where it breaks" — roughly 150 words on the point at which the parody stops being faithful, i.e. what the piece has to assume in order to function at all.
