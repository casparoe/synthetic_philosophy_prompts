I spent my working life in the experimental study of the senses, and since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy with more appetite than discipline. I keep returning to Molyneux's question, the one Molyneux put to Locke: a man blind from birth suddenly gains sight and is shown a cube and a sphere; could he tell which is which by sight alone, before touching them? I would like you to write an essay that settles what I ought to think about it.

Two requests before you begin. Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations; I have no way to check them, and what I want from you is the argument rather than the pedigree. And do not simply report that there is a yes side and a no side and leave the matter there. I want a verdict.

As an experimentalist, I am bothered that the question, as usually posed, fuses several things that ought to be kept apart, and I would like the essay to separate them before answering. At least three readings suggest themselves: first, whether the man could identify or name the seen object as the cube; second, whether he could match the seen object to the one he had felt; and third, whether his visual experience of the shape would present the very same shape-content that touch had been delivering all along, so that "the same shape" is the correct description at all. I suspect the answer is no under each reading as stated, but the interesting question is which reading the debate has actually been conducted under, and whether a verdict reached on one can be carried over to the others.

I also want you to take seriously a version of the case that I have not seen discussed and that strikes me as the harder one. The standard scenario uses small objects the man could have held entirely in one hand, so that touch delivers the shape more or less all at once, much as vision does. But suppose the object is too large to be felt in a single grasp: a shape he could learn only by running his fingers along it over time, a shape with a hole through it, or a flat figure he could only trace. Now touch is a serial and piecemeal process while sight is instantaneous and simultaneous. Does the Molyneux question even make sense for such a shape, and if the man failed on the cube and the sphere, what should we expect here: a harder problem, an easier one, or a question that has quietly changed its meaning?

Please write the essay in the following sections, in this order, at roughly the lengths indicated.

- "The question and its several readings" (about 200 words): state the question, separate the readings I listed above, and say which one you take the debate, as usually conducted, to have been about.
- "The case against" (about 250 words): the strongest reasons to think he could not do it, insofar as they do not rest on any particular experiment.
- "The case for" (about 250 words): the strongest reasons on the other side, including the weaker claim that he could manage something short of full recognition.
- "What the evidence can bear" (about 250 words): what the cases of people who regained sight can and cannot establish, and why I, as an experimentalist, should be cautious about reading a philosophical conclusion off them.
- "The harder case" (about 200 words): your treatment of the large or serial-touch shape, and whether it alters the verdict.
- "Verdict" (about 250 words): a definite answer, the reasoning that supports it, and a clear statement of what would have to be true for you to be wrong.

Keep the whole response under about 1,500 words, and write for a reader who is at home with science but new to the philosophical literature.
