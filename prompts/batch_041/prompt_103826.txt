I sit on the bylaws committee of a housing cooperative I joined about eighteen months ago, and I need a philosopher's help with something that began as an ordinary procedural fight and has turned into a puzzle I cannot put down.

Here is the fight. Our bylaws contain an amendment clause allowing any bylaw to be changed by a two-thirds vote of the membership, and the clause applies to itself just as it applies to everything else. One faction wants to entrench a rent-stabilization provision by amending the amendment clause so that the clause and the rent provision can thereafter be changed only by unanimous consent. Another member objects that this is illegitimate in a way deeper than ordinary politics: she says you cannot use a rule to destroy the very authority you are relying on in using it. I assumed that was rhetoric until a friend in law school wrote out the argument below, and now I cannot find the exit.

(1) A rule is legally valid only if some already-valid rule authorizes its enactment.
(2) The amendment clause is itself a rule of the bylaws.
(3) So the amendment clause is valid only if some valid rule authorizes it.
(4) If the authorizing rule is the amendment clause itself, then the clause confers its own validity, which is not authorization but bootstrapping.
(5) If the authorizing rule is a different rule, that rule in turn needs authorization, and so on.
(6) An actual legal order cannot run an infinite chain of authorizations.
(7) So the chain must stop at some rule that no other rule authorizes.
(8) By (1), a rule that no other rule authorizes is not valid.
(9) Hence no rule of the bylaws is valid, and by the same reasoning no rule of any legal order is valid.

The conclusion is that our amendment clause — and every law — is void. That is absurd; we amend bylaws constantly and the world does not end. So something in the argument is wrong. I would rather the error were not in (1), because (1) is the only thing standing between me and the view that a bare majority can declare itself authorized to do anything it likes. My friend is confident the mistake is at (6). I think (6) is obviously true of any real institution, which makes me worry the problem is elsewhere. Where does the argument actually go wrong, and what do I have to pay to fix it?

Please act as a philosopher of law specializing in the theory of legal validity and self-referential norms. Before answering, brainstorm: lay out the candidate diagnoses — a false premise, an equivocation between steps, a scope or temporal shift, a step valid only under a reading the premises do not support — and rank them by how much each would cost someone determined to keep (1). Then commit to one diagnosis. Explain why the step you reject looked fine on a first pass, and price the alternatives: in particular, say what the view that there can be a final rule that is valid without being authorized by any further rule commits you to, whether that differs from saying the final rule's standing is a matter of social fact rather than validity, and how either option bears on whether the entrenchment amendment my faction wants is something we can actually do. Write it as advice I could bring back to the committee, in plain prose. Keep it self-contained and do not give me citations or a reading list; I will track those down myself.
