I'm giving a paper at a small interdisciplinary ethics workshop next month. My thesis is that the everyday conventions people get sanctimonious about -- queueing, tipping, not jaywalking -- aren't moral obligations at all. They're things you have reason to do when the reasons are local (someone's watching, the queue would collapse if everyone did it, you're somewhere the tip is really a wage), but nobody wrongs anybody by cutting a line at 2am, strolling across an empty street against the light, or tapping "no tip" in a country where tipping is optional. And the visitor point follows: a tourist who doesn't tip hasn't done anything morally wrong, just something socially expensive. Being a guest obliges you to be civil, not to adopt the locals' whole normative world.

I want this to sound more rigorous than the paragraph above, so I've built a little taxonomy I'll lean on all through the Q&A: coordination conventions (they only work because we all do the same thing -- which side of the escalator to stand on), consideration conventions (they encode a real claim about someone's interests -- holding a door, giving up a seat), and complicity conventions (following them keeps an unjust arrangement running -- tipping where the wage already assumes it, or "behave, the cops are watching" norms in an over-policed neighbourhood). My line is that only the middle kind can generate anything like an obligation, and even then only when the underlying interest is genuine.

The room: four or five Kantians who'll want to know what makes something a duty and whether I've just relabelled inclination. A couple people who work on social norms and coordination equilibria, who I expect to think I've reinvented their wheel badly. One political philosopher who'll head straight for the complicity cases and say that if my theory can't say the under-tipper is doing wrong, it's a theory that lets people off the hook. And a sociologist of consumption who'll insist tipping is gift-exchange and I'm flattening it into a transaction.

Build me the Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, in their vocabulary, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And tell me the one question I'm dreading.

Be blunt with me: if the taxonomy is doing less work than I think, or if the whole thing collapses into "conventions aren't duties, duh," say so instead of writing me a polite prep sheet anyway. I'd rather find out now.
