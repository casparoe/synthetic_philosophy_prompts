I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory working on the ethics of migration, and I have a recurring arguement with my supervisor that I'd like you to help me settle. Before she came to philosophy she spent the better part of a decade doing refugee status determination, and she has a pretty consistent track record on the hypotheticals we run through in supervision. A dissident journalist who'd be imprisoned at home: we owe him admission. A woman whose ex-husband has threatened to kill her and whose local police are on his payroll: admission. A young man from a country in the middle of a civil war, nobody in particular after him, who mostly just wants away from the shelling: no obligation, and when I push she says things like who is actually doing this to him. A family that came because the wages here are five times what they'd earn at home: no obligation. A religious minority that a militia is hunting and that the army can't reach in time: admission. A man from a country whose government has simply collapsed, no persecutor, no working police, dangerous roads for everyone: she says no, and her reason here is a different one, something like everyone there is having a terrible time, that doesn't make it a claim on us. And a guy a criminal gang has singled out for extortion and beatings, with the police taking the gang's money: admission. I've noticed her refusals always come with one of two justifications and I genuinely can't tell which one is doing the real work. Either there's no agent persecuting you (call it the persecutor rule), or else your predicament isn't specific to you and your own state can't or won't protect you (call it the protection-gap rule). Both seem to fit every case above, which is annoying.

Here are the new ones. First, a coastal community in a low-lying country where the wells have gone salty and the land floods several times a year now; nobody is doing this to them, but there's no relocatoin plan and staying means destitution, and the harm is pretty clearly specific to coastal communities like theirs. Second, a valley community about to be flooded out by a big dam the government is building — no one intends them any harm, it's a development project, but they lose everything and the state is the one doing it.

What does she say about each, and which rule is she really running? I'd rather have your best guess with the reasons for it than a fence-sit. If the two rules come apart on these cases, tell me which single further case would separate them most cleanly — I keep thinking it has something to do with whether a state that is willing and able to protect you defeats the claim even when there is a real persecutor, but I'm not sure that's the sharpest test, and I'd also like to know which of the two new cases is the closer analog to which of her earlier rulings. And please be honest about whether her track record actually underdetermines this, or whether there's a third rule I'm missing that fits her verdicts at least as well as the two I've named.
