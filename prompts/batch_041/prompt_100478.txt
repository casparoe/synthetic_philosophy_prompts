Right, so I've retired from chemistry and I've been doing a lot of reading in philosophy — mostly decision theory and political philosophy — and I've got myself stuck on the paradox of voting. You know the one: it's supposedly irrational to vote, because the chance of your single ballot actually deciding the election is one in millions, so the expected benefit is basically nil — and yet alot of people vote, and we mostly don't think they're being idiots. I'd like you to turn my half-formed idea about this into a proper short research proposal: two pages, for a small grant from a philosophy department's visiting-scholar fund, six months part-time.

My suspicion is that the whole paradox comes from asking the wrong question. Everyone asks, what's the probability that my vote is decisive? But that already bakes in a very individualistic, one-at-a-time picture of what voting is. Coming from the sciences, I keep thinking about how we handle large ensembles: no single molecule matters, yet the ensemble has a perfectly definite behaviour, and the sensible question is about the marginal shift in a distribution rather than about some magic atom being the one that tips it. So I want to try reframing the voter's decision as a small perturbation to the probability distribution over outcomes — and to take seriously that people who vote the way I do are correlated with me: if I'm the sort of person who turns out to vote, that tells you something about how the others like me will vote.

The question I want the proposal to make precise is roughly this: does the instrumental case for voting look different, and maybe a good deal less hopeless, once you replace the am-I-pivotal question with a distributional, marginal one, and once you let my decision be correlated with the decisions of people like me? Is the paradox real, or is it an artefact of the model?

The proposal should cover: why the standard answers (plain instrumental rationality, the expressive-voting story, the ethical or altruistic voter line) don't settle this; what method I'd actually use — I'm happy to do some simple formal modelling and spreadsheet or Python simulations, I'm not a mathematician but I can muddle through; what a genuine result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a realistic month-by-month scope for six months part-time. Don't bother with references or a literature review, I'll dig those up myself — I just want the argument and the plan.

One more thing: be honest if the reframing turns out to be a distinction without a difference, or if I'm just reinventing something that already has a name. I'd definately rather know that up front.
