I am a third-year undergraduate in the final weeks of a course on normative ethics, and the paper I owe at the end of term asks me to state and defend a position of my own rather than reconstruct someone else's. I have drafted that position below and would like an audit of it before I commit to it. I am not asking for a literature summary or for reading suggestions — those would not help me at this stage — and I would rather have a precise diagnosis of where the pressure falls than a general verdict that the position is unstable. I am willing to revise any part of it; please do not assume in advance which part I am attached to.

Here is what I hold.

(1) I treat it as a fixed point rather than a rule of thumb that it is wrong for me to kill one innocent person in order to save five, when the one is not threatening anyone and the five cannot be saved otherwise.

(2) The ground of that verdict is the inviolability of the person who would be killed. In my notes I have been calling this a standing objection, and I define it as follows: the person who would be killed has an objection to being used for someone else's purposes, and the force of that objection is a matter of their standing rather than of the size of the stakes. It does not weaken as the number of beneficiaries grows, the way an annoyance weakens as it is spread more thinly.

(3) I do not think the constraint is absolute. Past some scale of harm — I will not pretend I can say where — I think killing the one becomes permissible. Even then, I think the person who refuses to kill is admirable rather than merely eccentric, and that their refusal still carries weight.

(4) I think the reason is addressed to me as the one who would act. I may not kill in order to prevent other people's killings, and my reason to prevent killings by others is weaker than my reason not to kill at all.

(5) I think the right test for a moral requirement is whether it could be justified to each person who has to live under it, with each person's complaint considered on its own. I do not think a number of smaller complaints can be added up to outweigh one larger one.

(6) I think moral requirements are universal: they apply to anyone relevantly situated, and no one is exempt because of where they happen to stand.

What I want from you is an audit. Which of these are genuinely in tension with which, and how severe is each tension, ranked rather than listed? Which pairs look like tensions but are not, and what makes the difference? Are any of the tensions I have written down really the same tension in different clothing? For each genuine tension, say what would have to be given up, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs elsewhere in the position — including whether fixing one tension reopens another. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the position that results is still recognizably deontological or has quietly become something else. Where a judgment is not forced by the commitments themselves but depends on a further choice I have not made, say so explicitly rather than picking for me.
