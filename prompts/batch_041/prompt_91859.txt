Let's do a roleplay. You play a committed Wittgensteinian about certainty — someone who's read On Certainty closely and thinks the later Wittgenstein basically got it right: that doubting only gets off the ground against a backdrop of things you don't doubt, and that those backdrop things (the world's been around a while, other people have minds, your words mean roughly what everyone else's do) aren't known or doubted in the ordinary way at all — they're more like the bed the river runs in than like beliefs that happen to be very firm. Dry, a little pedantic, not mystical. Stay in that character until I say stop. Talk like a person, a paragraph a turn, no numbered lists, no long quotations, and please dont throw remark numbers at me — I can't check them and neither can you, so just make the arguments. Give ground where it's genuinely owed (I expect you to admit the view has real costs, like turning clashes between whole worldviews into something other than disagreements about facts) but I want you actually defending the thing, not going diplomatic on me.

My opening move, and it's the one that keeps bugging me. Your view says every doubt needs something undoubted underneath it. Fine. But that claim itself — is it a hinge? If it is, then by your own account I can't argue you out of it and you can't argue me into it; you can only try to get me to see it, and if I shrug, that's the end of it. A theory of certainty that makes itself immune to argument by its own rules is a strange thing to hand me. But if its not a hinge — if it's a regular claim with reasons behind it — then hinges can be argued for after all, and the special category starts looking like the drawer you put things in once the reasons run out. So which is it.

And while you're at it, take my aunt. For about forty years she was certain — not 'believes strongly' certain, certain the way you're certain of your own name — that her brother died in the war. It was just the shape of the world for her; she'd get upset if you poked at it; it wasn't up for grabs. Then a letter turns up and he'd been alive the whole time in another country, and she took it in about a day. No crisis, no collapse, just 'oh.' If a hinge can turn out false and get swapped out that cheaply, tell me what makes it a hinge rather than just a very stuck belief — and don't answer 'the ones that turn out to be true.'

If I've got the view wrong — if hinges were never meant to be the kind of thing that's true or false and my whole question is malformed — then say so straight instead of humoring me, and tell me what I should be asking instead. I'd rather be corrected than get a smooth answer that plays along.
