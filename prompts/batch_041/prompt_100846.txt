I spent most of last night arguing with a friend from my logic reading group, and I still can't tell whether I lost the argument or whether he cheated. He's a dialetheist: he thinks some contradictions are true, and he treats the Liar sentence as the standard example of one that is both true and false. I ran what I took to be the decisive objection against him, and I want you to tell me which step I should give up — or which step is only valid under a reading I haven't actually earned — and what giving it up costs.

The argument I gave him went like this. (1) If dialetheism is true, then at least one contradiction is true: there is some sentence P such that P and its negation are both true. (2) If P and not-P are both true, then everything follows. Take any sentence Q whatsoever; since P is true, the disjunction "P or Q" is true; since not-P is true, the first disjunct is ruled out, so the only disjunct left standing is Q, and Q is true. (3) So if even one contradiction is true, then every sentence whatever is true — trivialism. (4) Trivialism is absurd: it makes assertion, denial, and reasoning pointless, and it would mean my friend's own theory is no better than its negation, which is not a position anyone can hold. (5) Therefore no contradiction is true, and dialetheism is false. He said the whole thing begs the question, that I had smuggled classical logic into step (2) twice over — once in assuming "or" behaves the way I think it does and once in assuming what "not" means — and that I was attacking a view he doesn't hold. I think he's waving his hands: surely "or" and "not" mean what they mean, and if he gets to redefine them mid-argument then he isn't defending the position I'm objecting to. But I also can't shake the feeling that step (2) is doing something under-described, that "not-P is true" and "P is true" are being pulled in two different directions within the same breath.

So: which step do I reject, or which step is valid only under a reading the premises don't support? And if the answer is (2), price it for me properly. What exactly does the dialetheist have to surrender — disjunctive syllogism, the classical sense of negation, the very idea that a disjunction with a true disjunct is true, or something else entirely — and is that a genuine cost or just a bookkeeping trick that lets him keep saying "contradictions are true" while quietly changing what he means? I'd rather keep (4) than (1), but I want to know what keeping (4) actually commits me to, and whether the price falls on me or on him.
