I have been reading Schrödinger's later philosophical essays, where he argues — under the influence of Vedanta — that the apparent plurality of conscious subjects is an illusion and that there is, at bottom, only one consciousness appearing as many, a claim he treats as continuous with the way the observing subject is always left out of the world-picture that physics constructs. I find the view attractive and would like to hold it, but I cannot tell how much of it is a substantive metaphysical thesis and how much is a redescription of experience dressed up as a discovery. Play it straight: what would count against it? I want the real defeaters, ranked by how decisive they would be, not the standard objections recycled. In particular, I want to know which empirical or clinical findings — say, from the split-brain literature, from dissociative cases, or from the ways unity of consciousness can be partial and graded rather than all-or-nothing — would tell against numerical oneness specifically, as opposed to merely against psychological unity; which arguments from philosophy of mind bite hardest, including the combination problem for a single cosmic subject and the worry that unity is a feature of the contents of experience rather than of the subjects who have them; and which of these a committed defender would simply concede versus dismiss as changing the subject.

I would also like you to sort out a distinction that I suspect does most of the work here and that I keep conflating: whether the claim is about empirical minds (in which case it looks empirically exposed, and the split-brain data seem to matter) or about a transcendental subject that could never be one object among others (in which case it may be unfalsifiable by construction). If it is the latter, say so plainly and tell me whether that insulation is a fatal defect or just the price of the kind of claim it is, and how it differs from a reading on which the one consciousness is more like the relative-state or many-minds picture from quantum mechanics than like a single Cartesian ego. Finally, tell me what I would have to give up believing if I adopted the view, and what I would have to observe to abandon it — and if there is nothing I could observe, be honest that the position risks nothing, and say whether that makes it empty or merely not the sort of thing evidence adjudicates.
