So I just finshed a pop-science book on consciousness — the kind written by a working neuroscientist rather than a philosopher — and I want to know how much of its big chapter to trust before I go around repeating it. The gist is this. Global workspace theory, the leading scientific theory of consciousness, says a mental state is conscious exactly when its contents get broadcast to a global workspace, a network spanning prefrontal and parietal cortex. On this view, that broadcast just is what it is to be phenomenally conscious — there's nothing left over, no extra 'felt' ingredient for a theory to account for, which is how the framework dissolves the hard problem. The author says this makes Block's old distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness obsolete. Block thought phenomenal experience 'overflows' what we can access and report, but the no-report paradigms have now refuted that: once you take report out of the picture, brain activity lines up with what the workspace theory predicts, and the cases where it doesn't line up turn out to be an attention effect rather than a consciousness effect. So the empirical evidence, he says, settles the question — access consciousness is all the consciousness there is.

I buy the neuroscience but I'm suspiscious of the philosophy being done here, and I honestly can't seperate which is which. Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a careful researcher in this area would sign off on, what they'd call a harmless simplification, and what's actually wrong? I'm especially interested in whether the author has quietly swapped a claim about access for a claim about experience, whether the no-report work really cuts against Block the way he says it does or maybe the other way, and whether the "the evidence settles it" conclusion follows from anything that's actually been established. And what would someone like Block say in reply, if they got to write the response?
