I've just started teaching 9th grade bio (first year, already regretting the "characteristics of living things" unit) and something keeps happening that I can't put a name to. I put the list on the board — made of cells, grows, uses energy, responds to its surroundings, reproduces — and within a week the kids have wrecked every item on it. A mule doesn't reproduce. A seed just sits there. A crystal grows, and so does a fire. The thermostat on the wall responds to the room. A virus has no cells and no metabolism, but it's obviously up to something. The thing I actually want to ask about is how I patch the list every time: I quietly change what level the word is working at, or what kind of claim I'm making. "Reproduces" stops meaning "this thing reproduces" and starts meaning "this thing belongs to a line of things that reproduce." "Grows" stops meaning "gets bigger" and starts meaning "gets bigger in a self-directed way." "Uses energy" becomes "can use energy, given the right conditions" — so the seed and the spore pass, not because of anything they're doing now but because of what they'd do if you woke them up. Nobody flags the swap, including me, and the definition comes out the other end still sounding like the one I wrote on the board.

What I want to know is whether this pattern has a name. I don't mean "moving the goalposts," because that sounds like bad faith, and I don't think this is — the repairs feel forced on me by the subject, not dodged. And I don't think it's just the usual thing about borderline cases, because the borderline case isn't really the problem; the problem is that one word ends up meaning two different things (one about the individual, one about the group it belongs to; one about what's happening right now, one about what's possible for it) and the argument slides back and forth between them without anyone noticing. If there's an established concept for this, in philosophy of biology or logic or wherever it lives, I'd like to know what it is — but honestly I care more about where its edges are. When is shifting a criterion from the part to the whole, or from the actual to the possible, a legitimate fix, and when is it a fudge? Is there any principled way to say which level a definition of life should be pinned to, or does this slide doom the whole genre of definition?

I'd also like you to test whatever you come up with against two cases I genuinely can't sort out. One is a prion — just a misfolded protein that makes other proteins misfold, no cells, no metabolism, but it definately spreads. The other is my sourdough starter, a jar of goo I feed every morning that clearly does stuff, but if you ask whether the goo is alive or whether it's the yeast and bacteria in it that are alive, I don't know how to answer without doing exactly the slide I'm complaining about. I'm not just after a label to drop in class — if the honest answer is that there's no single established term for this, say so, and then tell me what you'd call it and precisely what it does and doesn't cover.
