I am writing an essay on fatalism and practical reasoning, and I want to read Shakespeare's Macbeth through the decision-theoretic puzzle known as Death in Damascus. Please give me that reading, and tell me honestly where the lens distorts the play.

The puzzle, as I have it: a traveller in Damascus meets Death in the market and Death starts back in surprise. The traveller reads the surprise as information about where Death's appointment with him lies — not here — so that fleeing to Aleppo would be a way of running towards the appointment rather than away from it. But Death has foreseen his decision; so whichever way he resolves the matter, the news about his own fate is bad, and the reading of his own act as good or bad news cannot be exploited. The puzzle is standardly used to bring out a contrast between two ways of assessing an action: as evidence about how things will go, and as something that makes things go as they do.

My initial thoughts, which I would like you to test rather than accept:

1. In the first act Macbeth reasons rather as the traveller does: because the prophecy has been given, he treats the crown as a settled fact that will arrive whether or not he stirs himself, and he is content to let chance do the work. If the prophecy is evidence of what will happen independently of him, then murder is unnecessary — and this is the fatalism Lady Macbeth has to break down. Is that genuinely an instance of the evidential reading of one's own future, or is the analogy doing more work than the play can bear?

2. Once he acts, he reasons the other way, as though the prophecy were something his deeds could secure or defeat: the murder of Duncan to bring the crown about, the murder of Banquo to keep it, and finally the promise that no man born of woman can harm him, which he treats as armour. Is the play showing that neither reading is available, because the prophecy was never a fact independent of what he would do, but a prediction of his own choices — so that the witches are less like fate than like Death in Damascus, who has foreseen the decision and cannot be got behind?

3. The play is full of equivocation, promises that appear to guarantee one thing and deliver another, and I take this to be Shakespeare noticing what the puzzle notices: a prediction of what you will do cannot be used as evidence in the way that a prediction of the weather can. Is that defensible, or is the equivocation doing other work?

4. The ancient argument against fatalism — that if what happens is fated, no action of mine makes a difference, so deliberation is pointless — seems both refuted and confirmed by the play: Macbeth's deliberation is what brings the prophecy about, and yet deliberation never gives him a way out. Which is it?

5. Where does the reading fail? I do not want the witches reduced to a decision-theoretic puzzle, or Lady Macbeth turned into a merely causal agent who spoils her husband's fatalism. Point me to the scene, speech, or character that the lens cannot accommodate, and say what the play knows that the puzzle does not.

I know the play well enough that you need not quote it. What I want is the reading, and a verdict on whether the play adds anything to the puzzle that argument alone does not.
