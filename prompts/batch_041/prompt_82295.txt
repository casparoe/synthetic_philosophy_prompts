Draft: The status of the Church–Turing thesis

The Church–Turing thesis is standardly introduced as one claim, and then immediately hedged with the remark that it is not a theorem. I think the hedging is a symptom of having bundled three separable claims into one, and that the bundle can be taken apart in a way that both explains the hedge and removes the need for it.

The first claim is extensional: the functions computable by an ideal human calculator who proceeds by a finite, exact, surveyable set of rules are exactly the Turing-computable functions. The second is constitutive: the phrase 'effective procedure' does not name a kind that was already there, waiting to be discovered, which the Turing machine then turned out to match; the machine model fixes what we are henceforth to mean by the phrase. The third is physical: no physical process computes a function outside the Turing-computable class.

Most discussion runs the first and third together under the heading 'the thesis', and treats the second as a deflationary fallback for people who have given up on proving the first. I want to keep all three apart, because the evidence available for each is different in kind.

Consider the first. The evidence usually offered is that every candidate for a mechanical procedure that anyone has proposed has either proved equivalent to a Turing machine or proved to be ill-defined. I want to press on the second disjunct. The standard against which 'ill-defined' is judged is the machine model itself, and the candidate procedures we test are described in a vocabulary we learned from that model. Our inability to imagine a counterexample is therefore not independent testimony about where the mechanical ends; it is in part a fact about what our imagination has been trained on. Call this the calibration objection. I take it to be the reason the thesis feels at once undeniable and unproved.

There is a second and more local difficulty. The evidential base is finite: finitely many proposed procedures, finitely many cases of agreement. Any finite body of agreement is consistent with a deviant reading of 'rule' that coincides with the intended reading on every case a human could actually survey and diverges on inputs too long to write down. If that is right, no amount of checking can establish the extensional claim, and whatever establishes it must be a decision about what to count as a rule — which is the constitutive claim.

I do not want the constitutive claim read stipulatively. We did not have the freedom of choice that stipulation suggests: rival precisifications were on the table — one might have identified computability with the primitive recursive functions — and they were abandoned not because a convention was adopted but because they could not do the work. So the thesis is best described as a precisification under constraint: the concept is remade, and the remaking is vindicated by a track record rather than by a proof.

The consequence I want to draw is that asking whether the Church–Turing thesis is true is the wrong question, and not because it is deep but because it is a category mistake: truth is the wrong assessment for a proposal about how a term is to be used. The right question is whether the precisification earns its keep. The uncomfortable corollary is that the thesis remains revisable in principle, and that a community which has come to treat a precisification as a discovery will not notice that the option is open.
