I have been attending a reading group on truth and knowledge, and I should say up front that I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though I am comfortable reading formal derivations and can usually follow an argument once it is laid out. We have spent several sessions on the paradox of knowability, and I can follow the proof well enough: assume there is some truth that is not in fact known, apply the principle that every truth is knowable to it, observe that knowing a conjunction entails knowing each of its conjuncts and that knowledge entails truth, and the assumption that the truth was unknown turns out to be contradictory; so the principle seems to force the conclusion that there are no unknown truths at all. What genuinely puzzles me is that the discussion never resolves and, so far as I can tell, has not resolved in the literature either. Everyone grants that the conclusion is absurd and, once the assumptions are conceded, that the derivation goes through — as I understand it, the proof is valid even on intuitionistic logic, so one cannot wriggle out of it merely by refusing classical reasoning. Yet nobody agrees on which assumption to abandon, and every repair on offer looks tailored to save the principle rather than independently motivated. One line holds that the knowability principle must be restricted to propositions whose being known is not already contradictory; another holds that "knowable" has been misread and should mean something like "there is, in principle, a way by which it could come to be known" rather than "some possible knower knows it"; a third locates the trouble in the proof's quantifier over knowers, which supposedly ranges only over knowers who actually exist, so that the anti-realist never gets the merely possible knower the principle requires; and a fourth insists the whole affair is a distraction, because knowability was never really what anti-realism about truth was committed to in the first place. My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than accept, is that the disagreement is not formal at all but verbal — that the parties are running different readings of the single phrase "in principle knowable," something like the difference between "there exists a program that would compute it" and "there exists a machine that actually runs the program," and are then surprised that their conclusions diverge.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a survey: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a substantive disagreement about which premise is false, a dispute about the meaning of one phrase, two groups answering different questions under a single label (one about the conditions under which our statements are assertible, one about what some possible knower could know), or a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work? Please engage what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction between the readings, a formal result showing that one restriction is independently motivated rather than ad hoc, a decision about which reading of the principle anti-realism actually needs, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the parties simply want different things from the word "knowable." If you think my hypothesis is wrong, say what the disagreement really is instead, and explain why the diagnosis you reject is tempting.
