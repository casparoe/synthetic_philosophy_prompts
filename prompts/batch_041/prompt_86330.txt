A colleague of mine — a physicist who writes popular science on the side — has sent me the draft of a chapter on the philosophy of science and asked me to flag anything I think is wrong before he sends it to his editor. The section on falsificationism is the part I am least equipped to check: I read some philosophy of science as an undergraduate, and I have the nagging sense that a few of his sentences do something illegitimate, but not the confidence to say which. The passage runs as follows:

"Popper's great contribution was to solve the problem of distinguishing science from pseudoscience by a single criterion: a statement is scientific if and only if it is falsifiable, and meaningless otherwise. This is why he dismissed metaphysics as gibberish, and why he classed Freudian psychoanalysis and astrology as pseudoscience — their claims cannot be refuted by any observation, because their defenders can always explain the evidence away. Einstein's relativity, by contrast, was science because it stuck its neck out and predicted something that could have failed. The catch is that falsification is a one-way street. No amount of successful prediction can ever confirm a theory, so for Popper science is conjecture and refutation with no accumulation of knowledge, and a theory that has survived every test is no more likely to be true than a wild guess. And since any theory can be rescued by a sufficiently clever auxiliary hypothesis, what makes a theory falsifiable is really a matter of the scientist's intellectual honesty rather than the theory's logical form. In practice the criterion is unusable in any case, because observation is theory-laden: what counts as a refuting observation depends on the theory you already accept, so there is no neutral tribunal before which two theories can be tried, and Popper's programme collapses into the sociology of scientific communities."

He draws two further conclusions from all this. The first is that on Popper's own terms evolutionary theory fails to qualify as a science while creationism qualifies. The second is that a working scientist has no rational grounds for preferring a well-tested theory to a fresh conjecture.
