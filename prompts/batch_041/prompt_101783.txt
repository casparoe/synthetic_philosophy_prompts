I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm doing a piece on whether the concept of "belief" is quietly on its way out now that we're all building chatbots. The angle I want to try is this: take Gilbert Ryle — the Oxford guy who thought the Cartesian picture leaves you with a ghost haunting a machine, and who argued that talk of beliefs and desires isn't talk about inner states at all but about how someone is disposed to behave — and ask what he'd make of the modern eliminativists, the Churchland-ish crowd who say beliefs and desires aren't just not inner states, they're not real, they're a bad folk theory we should scrap. Then stir in the new twist: LLMs give us a working system that talks about its own beliefs and desires fluently while having no obvious inner life to point to.

My suspicion is the lazy answer ("Ryle would be thrilled, he was halfway there already") is wrong, and I want you to work out why, or whether it's actually right after all. The thing I keep circling is the gap between Ryle's move (belief-talk is perfectly fine, it's just about dispositions, not inner states) and the eliminativist move (belief-talk is false and should be dropped) — those are different claims and I don't think they sit together as neatly as people assume.

So: be a philosopher of mind who also knows the history of mid-century British philosophy cold. Think it through carefully, and maybe start by jotting the two or three readings of Ryle that are actually on the table before you commit. Then give me the piece in under 400 words, leading with the bottom line — one sentence on whether Ryle signs up with the eliminativists or digs in against them — then the reasoning, then what the LLM twist does to the verdict. No citations or reading lists, please; neither of us can check them.
