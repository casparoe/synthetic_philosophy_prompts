I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, writing a chapter on the anachronistic reach of Kierkegaard's social criticism: his polemic against the Danish press, the account of levelling and of the public that he develops in his review of Thomasine Gyllembourg's novel Two Ages, and the distinction he draws there between the public (a phantom abstraction) and the crowd (a concrete many, which he held to be untruth). I am not after a summary of those texts. I want you to attempt something harder: to reason your way into what Kierkegaard would have made of the media environment of the 2020s, and to say where his categories would have to be stretched and where they would break.

Treat the salient novelties as these: (i) the ordering of what a reader sees is set by a model of their attention rather than by an editor's judgement; (ii) engagement is quantified and the number is public, so that the success of an utterance is a statistic; (iii) participation is anonymous and aggregate, each contribution one among millions and leaving no trace on the contributor; (iv) text is generated at negligible cost, so the volume of discourse is no longer bounded by the number of people willing to write; (v) the bylined polemic is displaced by an aggregated consensus that no one owns and no one must defend; (vi) a writer's works circulate as summaries, excerpts and training data, detached from the occasion of their writing.

Then work through the following. Would the algorithmic feed count as a further intensification of levelling, or as something closer to the crowd than to the public, given that he separated the two? Is machine-generated text the culmination of the levelling he diagnosed, or a different thing altogether — chatter without a chatterer, in which the public is no longer even a phantom of anyone? If the readers of such text are indifferent to what they read, does his account of the public still apply, or does it presuppose a public composed of persons who at least take themselves to be forming an opinion? And what becomes of his insistence on the single individual, and of indirect communication, once a reader's access to an author is mediated by a summarising model?

Be explicit about where you are extrapolating and where you take yourself to be on firm textual ground; a hedged and precise answer is worth more to me than a confident one. Where the modern phenomena cut against his diagnosis rather than confirming it — for instance, if genuinely committed communities can now form online — say so, rather than forcing everything into his framework.
