I would like a genuinely explanatory account of the Löbian obstacle — the reason a sufficiently reflective reasoner cannot, on pain of collapse, adopt a blanket policy of trusting its own proofs. Build it up rather than citing it. Begin with a system strong enough to encode and prove facts about what is provable within it, and an agent whose considered beliefs simply are the theorems it can derive; then consider the natural proposal that the agent augment its own rules with the schema licensing the inference from "A is provable in this system" to "A". Explain, as concretely as the material allows, the mechanism by which that schema is self-defeating rather than self-endorsing — including the special case where A is an absurdity, so that the schema effectively asserts the system's own consistency — and why the phenomenon is not merely a restatement of Gödelian incompleteness but a distinct failure with a different moral. Say where the derivability conditions actually do their work, and why an agent cannot simply sidestep the difficulty by confining itself to reasoning about things other than its own proofs.

Then disentangle the distinctions that are usually run together: trusting a particular conclusion one has already reached versus trusting the general soundness of one's reasoning; self-trust installed as a single axiom versus an ascending hierarchy of trust principles of increasing strength; and what the system can prove about its own reliability versus what an external observer can establish about the system. A useful test case to work through: an agent that can read and reason about its own source code, and can therefore prove things about what it will in fact output — ask whether that capacity gives it any genuine purchase on the trust question or merely moves the problem.

Finally, survey the escape routes and assess them honestly rather than cataloguing them. Does appealing to a stronger system, to an oracle, to a bounded or resource-limited prover, to probabilistic credences in place of outright proof, or to an infinite tower of nested trust assumptions actually secure self-trust, or does each merely relocate the obstacle one level up? I want a clear verdict on whether the Löbian obstacle is a deep fact about rationality as such or an artifact of the idealizations — formal system, deductive closure, whole-system trust — and, if the latter, exactly which idealization has to give.
