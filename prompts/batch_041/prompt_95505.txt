A truth commission grants amnesty to individual perpetrators on a single condition: that they give a full and public account of what they did, while those who keep silent remain liable to prosecution. The same state's ordinary criminal law never works this way -- it does not let a confessed murderer go free for narrating the crime in public, and even plea bargaining trades only a reduced sentence for cooperation, not the erasure of liability. What does the amnesty-for-truth practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that public acknowledgment is a good of a different kind from punishment, something victims can be owed that a conviction cannot supply? Does it presuppose that a legitimate state may alienate its own authority to punish -- waive a deserved penalty for a political good -- and, if so, why only for this class of wrong and only in this currency? Reconstruct the implicit theory of what the practice is for, separate what it strictly requires from what it merely fits, say where it is underdetermined enough that retributive and restorative theories would both license it, and tell me what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced.
