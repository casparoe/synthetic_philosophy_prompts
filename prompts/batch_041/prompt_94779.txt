I work in a team that's half Munich, half Austin, and in our retro last week I complained that we have to fill in a twenty-minute form before every deploy, even for a one-line fix. My team lead shrugged and said "Befehl ist Befehl" — an order is an order — and everyone nodded like that settled it. Then someone else said this is exactly the difference between the two countries: Germans follow the rule, Americans follow their own conscience and would just say "rules are made to be broken", and then the room split on which of those was meant as the compliment and which as the insult. The only other place I've come across the phrase is in documentaries about the Nuremberg trials, where it quite obviously wasn't working as an excuse.

Can you seperate out all the different precise claims "an order is an order" could actually be making — the ones that are just descriptions of how people happen to behave, the ones that are claims about what you have reason to do, and the ones about who cops the blame when it goes wrong — and for each tell me whether it's a platitude, genuinely contentious, or just false? Then say which of those readings would actually back up using it to shut down my complaint about the form, and which wouldn't. And is it even the same kind of claim as the American one, or are the two of them just talking past each other? I want a menu I can take back to the next retro, not a lecture on Kant.
