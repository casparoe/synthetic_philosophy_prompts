For the past several weeks my seminar on the philosophy of language has been working through the cluster of theses usually gathered under the heading of linguistic relativity, and I have found myself in need of a teaching artifact that my students can genuinely argue with — something that dramatizes how the doctrine tends to be sold rather than how it is defended. I have accordingly set the following exercise for the group, and I would like you to attempt it as well.

Please compose a mock customer-support document — an FAQ, roughly 600 to 700 words, laid out as a numbered list of question-and-answer entries — for a plausible start-up that sells "cognitive upgrades" by installing a new language in its customers. The company is to take the strong reading of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis entirely at face value, treating grammar as a set of cognitive rails from which thought cannot escape, and its marketing copy, its tiered pricing, its compatibility notes, and its refund policy should all follow quietly from that commitment. Write it deadpan: no narrator should ever wink at the reader, and the document should never concede that the strong reading is contested, even as its own fine print exposes the difficulties. Let the comedy come from the doctrine's real features rather than invented absurdities — the gap between the modest claim that vocabulary and grammar nudge habitual attention and the immodest claim that they fix the bounds of the thinkable; the fact that a customer who genuinely could not think a thought her native language lacked could never have requested the upgrade in the first place; what a refund policy must say to someone who reports no change in worldview; whether a customer who buys two languages is now two minds, or one; how the firm could possibly test its own efficacy without administering the test in some language; and the awkward standing of the well-worn claim that a certain northern people possesses an unusually rich vocabulary for snow, given how that claim is usually circulated. Somewhere in the document, let a support agent field a complaint from a customer who insists she now perceives a distinction her original language supposedly forbade, and let the company's reply be bureaucratically evasive rather than philosophically candid.

The humor should reward a reader who knows the actual literature while remaining legible to one who does not; a specialist should recognize that the author understands the difference between the empirical and the metaphysical versions of the thesis and finds the satire fair rather than merely derisive. I care less about punchlines than about a document every clause of which is a consequence of the position it pretends to hold.
