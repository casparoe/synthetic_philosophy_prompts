My reading group has spent three weeks on a friend's argument that hedonism cannot survive the sheer variety of pleasures, and I am now fairly sure it proves too much without being able to say where it breaks. He reconstructs it as follows. (1) Hedonism is the thesis that pleasure alone is good in itself and pain alone bad in itself. (2) For that thesis to be substantive, "pleasure" must name a single kind of thing — some one feature that all and only the things we call pleasures share, and in virtue of which they are good. (3) But consider what we call pleasures: the taste of water when you are parched, the moment a proof finally closes, a friend's deadpan joke, a long bath, hearing that a rival has failed, the hum of a song you half-remember. These share no one feature; some are sensations, some are attitudes toward a proposition, some are objectless moods, and some seem to consist in the activity itself rather than in any feeling that accompanies it. (4) So there is no such kind as pleasure. (5) So hedonism is either false, since there is no single good to maximize, or empty, since it collapses into the advice to get whatever you happen to want. (6) Either way hedonism fails.

I am content to grant (1) and (3) — the variety is real and I would not want to deny any of his examples — but (4) and (5) strike me as proving far too much. Run the same reasoning on pain, desire, belief, memory, or emotion and it abolishes those kinds too, and a line of argument that dissolves every psychological kind, and with them every substantive claim in ethics and the philosophy of mind, has surely gone wrong somewhere. Worse, it seems to cut against every rival theory of welfare at once: desires are heterogeneous and so are the items on any objective list, so the argument, if good, would leave no theory of welfare standing rather than refuting hedonism in particular. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs. If the repair is to weaken (2), say what would then unify pleasures — whether a higher-order property such as being liked or being what one is disposed to seek, a determinable with many determinates, or a mere cluster — and what fresh trouble that unifying story invites; if instead the fault lies in the move from (3) to (4), say exactly what that move assumes and whether any hedonist needs it. I would rather not surrender (1) or (3), but I want to know the price of whatever I keep. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the step you reject and the fix — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
