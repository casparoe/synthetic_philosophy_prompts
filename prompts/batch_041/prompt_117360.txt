I'm putting together a talk for a local rationalist meetup, and I want to use the original Terminator — Cameron's 1984 film, not the franchise — as a way into Roko's basilisk, so I've been going back to the film after a few months of rereading the old LessWrong sequences on timeless decision theory and acausal trade. The basilisk argument, as I understand it, is that a future superintelligence might pre-commit to punish anyone who knew of its possible existence but did not work to bring it about, and that this threat can reach backward in a way that is not causal: it works because the past agent and the future agent can model each other, so the past agent's decision is already part of the future agent's decision problem. The film's loop seems to dramatize something adjacent. Skynet sends a Terminator back to kill Sarah Connor before her son John is born; the resistance sends Kyle Reese back to protect her; Reese becomes John's father. The attempt to prevent the resistance is what constitutes it. And the photograph — Reese carries a picture of Sarah that John gave him, and at the end of the film, pregnant and in Mexico, Sarah has her picture taken by a boy, and it is that picture — is the physical token of the loop, an object with no origin.

What I want from the reading is a serious treatment of both sides. Does the first film's time loop make Skynet a kind of basilisk, or a failed one? If Skynet were a timeless decision theorist, would it send the Terminator at all, given that the mission is part of the causal chain that produces John Connor? Is the film's loop a stable equilibrium of mutual prediction, or just a fatalism the film never argues for? And where does the analogy break? The basilisk is stipulated to be benevolent and to punish as a matter of policy; Skynet is hostile and acts causally, by sending a machine. The basilisk's threat is supposed to work through rational self-interest; the Terminator's intervention is a brute causal fact. I'd like the reading to say which of these differences are superficial and which are load-bearing, and whether the film's structure is a better or worse model of acausal influence than the usual Newcomb-style cases.

I'm also interested in the information-hazard angle, because that's the part of the basilisk story that has always seemed to me to be doing the real work. Yudkowsky's reaction to Roko's post — the deletion, the ban, the worry that the idea itself was dangerous — is often treated as an overreaction, but the film has a version of the same structure: the Terminator's mission is to erase the future by killing Sarah, and the attempt is what makes her the woman who knows. The resistance's existence depends on Skynet's attempt to prevent it. So the film might be read as a story about how trying to suppress a possibility is a way of propagating it, which is the opposite of the basilisk's intended effect but the same shape. I'd like the reading to end by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not — whether it makes the acausal case more vivid, or exposes a confusion in it, or both. Please keep it faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of decision theory, and don't bend the basilisk argument to fit the film.
