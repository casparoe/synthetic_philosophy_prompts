Since I retired from the lab I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and for the last few months we've been chewing on the difference between lying and merely misleading — saying something you know is false versus saying something true that you know will steer the listener to a false conclusion. Same examples every week (the murderer at the door and whether you can mislead him without lying, the politician whose technically-true answer leaves entirely the wrong impression), and nobody has moved an inch in months.

Here's my hunch, tell me if its right. I think there are two or three different quarrels tangled up under one heading. One is a question in the philosophy of language: where exactly does the line between what a sentence literally says and what it merely suggests fall, and is that line even stable — because the people who think the distinction is bogus keep producing cases where the context seems to make a true sentence say something false, while the people who defend it keep producing cases where the suggestion is obviously cancellable. The other quarrel is a straight-up ethical one: suppose we can draw the line, does it mark anything morally important, or are lying and misleading equally bad because both just use someone's trust against them? So some of us are doing semantics and some are doing ethics and we keep talking past each other.

Is that the real structure of the impasse, or am I missing a fourth thing hiding in there? Which part is actually stuck and which could be cleared up just by getting the semantics straight? And be honest with me: is there anything that would actually move this — a cleaner example, a distinction, some bit of formal apparatus — or is the staleness a sign the whole question is ill-posed? I'd rather have a diagnosis with reasons than your verdict on whether misleading is really as bad as lying.
