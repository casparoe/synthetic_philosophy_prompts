ok so I'm writing the framing chapter of my thesis and I keep hitting the same wall. The whole chapter rests on one sentence: science makes progress toward the truth even though its theories keep turning out to be false. Everybody nods at that sentence. I want to know what it would actually take to earn it, so before I read one more thing on the subject: write me the desiderata. The full requirements list for an adequate account of truthlikeness (closeness to truth, verisimilitude, whatever you want to call it) -- what any such account has to get right, the case or consideration behind each requirement, and which ones are non-negotiable versus which are just the taste of one camp.

A few I'm already sold on, so you know the register. It has to rank some false theories above other false theories; if every false theory comes out equally far from the truth, my sentence is empty. It can't flip the ranking when I redescribe the same theory in a different but intertranslatable vocabulary, because otherwise closeness to truth depends on which words I happened to use. And it can't let me inflate a theory by bolting on true-but-irrelevant claims.

Three more I've been argueing about with my office mate, and I suspect these are the real ones:

(i) Should the account be answerable to practice? I want a false theory that's closer to the truth to also be the one that predicts and controls better. He says that's just confirmation theory sneaking back in; I say if the two can come completely apart, then 'closer to the truth' is doing no work and I should delete the sentence.

(ii) The relabeling case. A theory that gets every prediction right and has the right structure but is wrong about which entity is which -- intuitively that's the same theory, or maximally close. Any account that just counts true sentences will call it badly wrong, because the sentences that identify the entities come out false. Is that a requirement, or am I smuggling in a whole seperate structuralist picture of what a theory is?

(iii) Idealization. What does the account say about a theory that's false only because it idealizes -- no friction, point masses, perfect gases? Close to the truth, or not even in the running for truth and falsity? Because that's most of what my science actually uses, and an account that's silent there is silent where it matters.

Then the part I most want: which of these requirements are in tension with each other, and whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable -- if so, say so, that's a result, not a failure. And tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the popular families of account: the ones that define closeness as having more true and fewer false consequences, and the ones that define it as similarity to the truth among models or possible worlds. You know the families. Skip the scorecard, I'll do that part myself.
