My view, as plainly as I can put it: superrationality is the correct theory of what it's rational to do in one-shot symmetric games between reasoners who know they're the same kind of reasoner. Not a heuristic, not altruism smuggled in through the back door — the actually correct answer. Hofstadter's case, back in the eighties: a trillionaire gets twenty strangers in a room and says that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram by noon the next day, that one person gets a billion dollars; if two or more send, or nobody does, nobody gets anything; and they're not allowed to communicate with each other. The right answer, I take it, is that each of them sends with probability 1/20. The real-world version is the one Scientific American actually ran: you post a card with a number on it, the number is how many entries you're buying, the prize is a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, awarded to the holder of one randomly chosen entry. Superrationally you're supposed to roll a die with as many sides as there are expected entrants and only post a card with a 1 on it if you roll a 1. What actually happened is that readers posted numbers like a googolplex, the prize collapsed to a fraction of a cent, and nobody got anything. My view is that those readers weren't just greedy — they were wrong about what reason required of them.

So, what would count against this? I'd like these worked through:

- Something I noticed that might be embarassing. In the twenty-person telegram game, the 1/20 answer isn't only what superrationality recommends — it's also exactly the symmetric mixed-strategy equilibrium a completely orthodox game theorist would find. If the other nineteen each send with probability p, sending wins me the billion only when none of them send, and not sending wins it only when exactly one of them sends, and those two quantities are equal when p = 1/20. So on this game, the radical theory and the boring one give the same prescription, which makes me wonder whether the Platonia dilemma is a test case for superrationality at all or just a bad poster child. And if the theory only comes apart from orthodoxy in games where the symmetric joint optimum isn't an equilibrium — the prisoner's dilemma being the obvious one — doesn't that make it a theory about how to play the prisoner's dilemma dressed up as a general theory of rationality?

- There's a line of work in the multi-agent literature where you don't choose moves, you submit a program, and the referee runs everyone's programs against each other. In that setting a program that cooperates if the other party's program is the same as it and defects otherwise gets mutual cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and equilibria of that flavour exist. This cuts both ways for me. It shows the apparently magical cooperation isn't magic — but it also suggests what's doing the work is transparency and commitment, not rationality as such. In the Platonia setup the twenty people are opaque to each other, so has the engine been removed and the argument is now running purely on the assumption that everyone reasons identically, which is the thing in dispute?

- A variant I keep chewing on: before the game the trillionaire takes me aside privately and says he's going to offer one of the other nineteen ten thousand dollars for sending a telegram, and he won't tell me which one. Now the symmetry is broken in a way I can't resolve, and 1/20 clearly isn't right any more. Does that show the prescription was never a deliverance of reason but an artefact of an assumption that almost never holds? Related: what happens to the 1/20 advice if I think there's even a small chance that one of the nineteen is a crank who always sends?

- The standard causalist complaint, which I want you to state as strongly as it can be stated: my sending a telegram doesn't cause anyone else to send one, so the whole if-we-all-did-it calculation is magical thinking, the epistemic cousin of winning the lottery by visualising the balls. Plus the companion jab: look at who actually walks away with money in these situations. In the Scientific American game the googolplex crowd did exactly as well as the die-rollers, which is to say nothing — but in general the defector against cooperators leaves with $150. What's the honest reply? Is there one, or does the superrationalist just have to say the objector is using the word rational differently?

- My own toy data, for whatever it's worth. I run a stripped-down version with my seminar: twenty-five students, a £100 prize, email me if you want in, exactly one entrant takes it, nobody gets anything if two or more enter. First time round, four people entered. Second time, after I spent twenty minutes explaining superrationality, six entered. My own students defect more the better they understand the theory. I assume you'll tell me this is evidence about psychology and not about rationality, but I'd like that distinction made carefully rather than waved.

- And the big one. Does this position risk anything at all? If every counterexample gets answered with "that's not a symmetric problem between identical reasoners", the theory is true by construction and tells nobody anything. I want an honest verdict on that. If it does risk something, name the game or the experiment that would settle it. And if superrationality can be pressed into service for any coordinated act whatsoever — if the trillionaire had offered the billion to whichever of the twenty personally did the worst thing imaginable, would the dice come out then too? — what does that tell us about whether this is a theory of rationality or a coordination principle that's silent about ends?
