I'm a software engineer with no philosophical training, and next month I'm the best man at the wedding of one of my oldest friends. He's Dutch, from a large Rotterdam family; his fiancée is Japanese, from Osaka; both families will be at the dinner, along with a fair number of friends who belong to neither culture and will just be watching the two sides react to each other. I've been asked for a toast of about four minutes, and I've backed myself into a corner. Over the past year I've read and argued my way into a position on white lies that I actually hold: the moral cost of a lie is not mainly in the false belief it plants but in what it draws down from the speaker's standing as someone whose word can be relied on, so that a small false compliment and a large false reassurance are the same kind of act differing only in dose, while a graceful non-answer, a changed subject, or a plainly signalled refusal to comment costs the listener nothing because it borrows against nothing. I don't want to deliver a lecture at a wedding, but I also don't want to stand up and say things I believe to be false merely because the occasion forgives it, and the two rooms have genuinely different expectations of what a toast is for. My friend's side will hear anything short of blunt as evasion and will conclude I'm being a coward; his fiancée's side, as best I can tell, reads public bluntness about the couple's flaws or their families as a form of aggression, and treats tact not as dishonesty but as the shape respect takes — the distinction they draw between the sincere feeling and the presentable surface seems to them like ordinary social competence rather than concealment. The toast has to be warm in both directions, and I want it to enact my view rather than announce it: nothing in it should be false, but it shouldn't be so proudly blunt that half the room goes cold, and it shouldn't be so smooth that my friend's family hears me saying nothing at all.

The one true story I want at the centre of it: on their second date he told her, flatly, that her master's thesis topic was boring, and she has said more than once that this was the moment she decided to trust him. I'd like you to write the toast, roughly five hundred to six hundred words, letting that story carry the weight and letting my position on lying shape what the toast is allowed to do — where it must simply omit, where it can say something true that both rooms will hear differently, and where honesty would be self-indulgence dressed up as principle. Then, in a short note kept clearly separate from the toast itself, tell me which details you invented and which claims about Dutch and Japanese norms you're actually confident in versus merely repeating a stereotype; how confident you are in the position I sketched, in particular whether the line between lying and declining to answer survives contact with cases where the omission is obviously designed to deceive and the listener is meant to be deceived; and which single claim in the whole response you're least sure of and why.
