You are a philosopher and curriculum designer who builds experiential learning for advanced undergraduates, and you are known for activities in which students run into a problem by acting it out rather than by being told about it. Before you commit to a design, brainstorm several candidate mechanics and reason carefully about which one will actually produce the target experience rather than merely illustrate it; then develop the strongest candidate in full.

I teach an upper-level seminar on death and the value of life: 16 students, one 90-minute session, seated at four tables of four. I want to stage the Epicurean challenge so that students feel it before they can name it — the thought that death is nothing to us, since while we are, death is not, and when death is, we are not — together with the deprivation response (death is bad because it removes the good life you would otherwise have had) and the two difficulties that response generates. First, if death is a harm, it seems there must be some time at which it is a harm, yet there appears to be no time at which it is a harm, and no subject who is worse off at that time. Second, your own pre-birth nonexistence is structurally just like your post-death nonexistence, yet almost no one thinks it was bad for them never to have existed sooner.

Design the activity. Give me rules, materials, roles, a scoring or payoff scheme that requires no real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Arrange the rules so that the students themselves produce the deprivation judgment, then are forced to date the harm, then are forced to score the pre-birth and post-death cases on the same scale — ideally with the mirror between them becoming visible only at the reveal. Include at least one case in which the counterfactual comparison is blocked, so that the group has to notice how much the deprivation account depends on it. Nothing in the activity should name Epicurus or any of the positions; the labels arrive in the debrief.

The debrief should map specific moments in what happened onto the argument, the deprivation account, the timing problem, the symmetry problem, and the objection that a harm needs a subject who is worse off. Tell me where the activity stacks the deck, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure. Finally, be candid about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce — what I will have to supply myself, and where in the session I should do it. Assume nothing beyond paper, index cards, and a whiteboard, and no student preparation beyond the assigned reading.
