Two camps in the philosophy of causation have been arguing past each other for years about omissions, and I want the document they should have written together. The first camp holds that causation is a relation between things that happen or exist -- events, states, processes -- and that a sentence such as "her failure to water the plant killed it" is, strictly speaking, either false or true only derivatively, in virtue of some positive fact about her (a duty she had, a norm she breached); on this view the impression that omissions are causes is an artefact of how we select and describe causes, not a feature of the causal structure of the world. The second camp holds that omissions are causes in the same sense in which actions are: if the plant's death counterfactually depended on the watering that never happened, and the absence of watering is a difference-maker for the death, then the omission is a cause of the death, and any theory that cannot say this has misdescribed the phenomenon.

Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps: a document each could put its name to, not a commentary on the debate and not a verdict for either side.

It should contain four parts.

1. A numbered list of claims both sides accept. These should cover the ordinary-language data, the counterfactual structure of omission cases, what a theory of causation is answerable to, and which parts of the dispute are merely verbal. Both sides must accept at least the following: that in the standard pairs of cases -- the neighbour who forgot to water versus the stranger who was never going to, the inspector who overslept versus the firm whose policy is never to inspect -- the two omissions are on a par in their counterfactual relation to the outcome while differing in their normative status; and that each side owes an account of why our causal talk tracks that normative difference. Nothing on the list may be a claim only one side would regard as a concession, and nothing may be so hedged that accepting it costs nothing.

2. A disagreements table, one row per issue, each row stating the residual disagreement in wording its own side would accept for its own position. At minimum: whether "omission" names a kind of causal relatum or is a device for describing the causal role of surrounding positive facts; whether norms enter the truth conditions of causal claims or only their pragmatics; and whether the dispute over omissions is a special case of the dispute over the metaphysics of absences generally.

3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side -- and for each crux a case or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about the outcome written down in advance. Where the crux turns on how people would judge a case, each side should stake out its prediction rather than cite a study. Both sides must engage with these two cases, which I take to be under-discussed:

(a) The norm-free omission. Two adjacent hillside plots. On the first, the owner has mowed a firebreak every August for years, with everyone's knowledge; this year he is in hospital and does not, and a spark from a passing train burns the plot. On the second, no one has ever mowed a firebreak, no duty attaches to it, and no one expects it; the same spark burns it in the same year. Both omissions have the same counterfactual relation to the burning. Is the first a cause and the second not, and if so, on exactly what does the difference rest? Say what each side predicts about ordinary speakers' judgments here before any norm is introduced, and whether those judgments should count as evidence at all.

(b) The designed omission. A dam's spillway gate is controlled by a sensor specified to open the gate above a certain river level. It does not open; the valley floods. Is the gate's not opening a cause of the flood, over and above the rainfall? Does it matter that the omission was the realisation of a specification -- the gate is meant to stay shut -- rather than a departure from one? Does it matter whether the sensor failed because it was struck by lightning, because it was mis-calibrated in the factory, or because a programmer set the threshold too high? Which of these variations, if any, should change the causal verdict, and why?

4. A short section on which residual disagreements both sides agree are verbal, with a translation manual: for each side, a sentence in its own vocabulary and the sentence in the other's vocabulary it should regard as saying the same thing. If a disagreement survives translation, say which one and say what is left of it.

Prefer, wherever possible, a formulation both sides can sign to one that favours a side. Format the result as a document with two signatories.
