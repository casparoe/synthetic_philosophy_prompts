Write a poem of roughly twenty to twenty-four lines in two alternating voices, and make the poem's form carry the disagreement between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness.

The two voices walk the same sorites: a single borderline predicate advanced one step at a time (a heap gaining a grain, a colour shading from red toward orange, a person ageing a day at a time, a shoreline crossing a tideline — pick one and hold to it). They share one refrain, which is the borderline sentence itself (a line such as "this is still a heap" or "that is not yet red", or whatever your predicate yields). Each stanza closes on that refrain, but the two voices speak it in different keys.

One voice is the epistemicist: the predicate has a sharp boundary, there is a fact of the matter at every step, and the boundary is nonetheless unknowable, because knowing where it falls would require a margin for error that the sorites never grants. Its refrains should sound like a line that exists but that no one is ever standing on.

The other voice is the supervaluationist: in the borderline zone there is no fact of the matter, since every admissible way of making the predicate precise draws some line, no one of those lines is the right one, and the sentence there is neither true nor false. Its refrains should sound like a line drawn everywhere at once and so nowhere in particular.

Let the refrain recur as the sorites advances, so that the earliest appearances are plainly true, the latest plainly false, and the middle ones are what the poem is about. At the middle, both voices must be able to say the refrain, and neither should be refuted by what the poem has shown; do not let the poem settle the dispute. Include, somewhere in the supervaluationist's part, one line that is determinately true even though neither of its two halves is — a disjunction that survives while its disjuncts do not — because that is where the view earns its keep. In the closing lines, expose exactly what separates the voices: not whether the boundary can be known (both agree it cannot) but whether there is anything there to be known — a fact that outruns our access, or only our language failing to reach a fact that was never fixed. Do not name the theories in the poem; let the voices show them.

It should be accurate enough that a philosopher working on vagueness recognises both positions and does not wince, and clear enough that a reader who has never heard of either comes away with the shape of the problem.
