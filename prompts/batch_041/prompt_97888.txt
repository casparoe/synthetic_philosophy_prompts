I retired a few years ago from a career in experimental physics and have been working my way through philosophy on my own. Free will is the topic I keep circling back to, partly because a lifetime of thinking in terms of causes and laws makes the hard-determinist position feel almost like a default for me, and I have learned to distrust how comfortable that default is. I recently read a trade book on the subject, and its treatment of P. F. Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment struck me as too tidy to be trustworthy. I would like you to audit it.

Here is the book's summary, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it:

"Strawson's essay argues that whether determinism is true makes no difference to whether we are justified in holding one another morally responsible. His key observation is that our practices of blame and praise rest on the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, guilt — which are natural human responses that we simply cannot choose to abandon. Because these attitudes belong to our emotional constitution rather than to any beliefs we hold about causation, the discovery that our actions were causally determined all along would leave them exactly as they are. Strawson contrasts the participant stance, in which we engage with others as responsible agents, with the objective stance, the detached, scientific view of a person as a thing to be managed; the latter is the view we take of the insane or the very young, and it is the one the determinist is forced to try to occupy all the time. Strawson's verdict is therefore a compatibilist one: determinism and moral responsibility stand or fall together, and the libertarian's demand for a special, contra-causal freedom is an incoherent requirement that we can happily discard."

I would be grateful if you would go through this claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher who knows the essay well would accept as accurate, what would make them wince as an oversimplification, and what is simply a misreading. Where the book has blurred two things Strawson kept apart, or has drawn a conclusion he would refuse, I would like that stated plainly, along with what he actually does say at that point. Finally, please tell me whether the compatibilist verdict the book extracts survives your corrections, or whether the essay's real conclusion is something the book's framing has hidden from me.
