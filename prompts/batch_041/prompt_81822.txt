I am a senior at my high school and the student representative on the school's Curriculum and Assessment Council. The council votes next Tuesday on the end-of-year task for the eleventh-grade world history course, and I have to circulate a written opinion to the members beforehand.

The history department has proposed a document-based essay in which students explain one outcome: why, in the same decade, a revolution succeeded in one country and collapsed in its neighbor. The council is deadlocked between two grading standards.

The first, submitted by the department chair, says an explanation earns full credit only if the student states a general regularity (say, about the conditions under which a regime loses the loyalty of its armed forces) together with the particular circumstances of each country, so that a reader who granted the regularity and the circumstances could have foreseen the outcome. On this standard, a student who merely recounts what happened, in order, earns partial credit at best, because a story is not an explanation.

The second, submitted by two teachers, says an explanation earns full credit when it reconstructs the actual path by which the outcome was produced, including the choices, accidents, and near-misses, and identifies the points at which events could have gone otherwise. On this standard, a student who offers a covering regularity without showing how this case actually traveled that path is doing something else: describing, or predicting, but not explaining.

Please draft the memo the council can act on. It should: recommend one standard, or a specific amended wording of one, with a clear bottom line the chair can put to a vote; make the reasoning followable by the non-historians on the council, which includes a math teacher, two parents, and the principal; give the strongest case for the standard I end up rejecting, rather than a straw man; show concretely how the standard I recommend would treat a student answer that satisfies the other standard but not this one; and be candid about what the council cannot settle merely by choosing a rubric, for instance whether an explanation must license prediction at all, or whether a question of why something happened and a question of how it happened are really the same question. Please argue it out from the case rather than giving me citations or a reading list.
