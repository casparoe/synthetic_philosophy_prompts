Writing a section on whether satisficing is a genuinely distinct normative category and I can't shake a friends argument about it. He lays it out like this:

(1) If you rationally stop searching and take an option that merely clears your threshold, you must have a reason to stop there rather than look further.
(2) The only reason to stop is that the expected payoff of more search wouldn't justify its cost.
(3) So even the satisficer has weighed stopping against continuing in expected-value terms — i.e., has optimized.
(4) So "satisficing" picks out no distinct rational strategy; it's just optimizing with a bad search-cost model.
(5) The same reasoning governs the threshold itself: choosing T is just another optimization.

Conclusion: bounded rationality is a redescription of full rationality, and Simon was chasing a distinction that isn't there.

I want to keep (1), since denying it feels like saying you can just stop for no reason at all. But (4) can't be right, and I suspect the thing proves too much, since by its own lights the optimizer would also have to have optimized the decision to optimize. Where does it actually break, and what do I pay if I bail at (2) versus at (3) versus at (5)? I'd rather not lose the ability to say the satisficer is doing something the optimizer isn't.
