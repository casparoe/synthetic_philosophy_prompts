A friend of mine has been reading about the idea that an ant colony could itself be a mind — that the thing doing the thinking is the colony, not the ants. He and another friend got into an argument about it and asked me to referee. He argued that the colony does everything a mind does: it forages, allocates workers, chooses a new nest, defends territory, and adjusts when conditions change; and since behavior is the only evidence we ever have for any mind, denying the colony one while granting one to a dog is arbitrary. She replied that behavior cannot settle it — a thermostat tracks and acts too — and that what the colony lacks is a subject, a single point of view: its "decisions" are just the sum of a million ants each doing its own local thing, and nobody is anywhere experiencing the result. He countered that this proves too much, since her neurons are individually mindless while she has a mind, so "made of mindless parts" cannot be disqualifying. She said the disanalogy is integration: in a brain the parts pool information into a state available to guide the whole system, whereas the ants' information stays in local pockets, making the colony many tiny agents sharing a few signals rather than one mind composed of many parts. He then offered a test — wire the colony to a scanner that reads out each ant's state; you would get a million separate reports and no single voice, hence no colony mind. She answered that the same scanner on her brain would yield a billion neuron reports and no voice either: the voice is the pattern, not a location. Neither will move, and I genuinely cannot say who has the better of it.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point, saying which moves are sound, which equivocate, and what each of them is missing. In particular, tell me whether the question "does the colony have a mind?" is even well-posed as they are posing it, or whether it has to be split into a functional question and a question about experience that their arguments keep sliding between. Also state what would change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
