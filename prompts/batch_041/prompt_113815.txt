I've been persuaded by a version of the performance-legitimacy thesis, and I want to know what would count against it. As I hold it, a regime's right to rule is not conferred by authorization — elections, acclamation, constitutional pedigree — but earned by output: if it durably secures material prosperity and civil order for the people under it, at a cost those people would not repudiate if they could speak at no risk, then it rules legitimately, and the absence of a mandate is beside the point. On my version the warrant is not a one-time grant but a standing condition. It is continuously re-earned, it lapses when the performance lapses, and it attaches to the regime's ongoing relation to its subjects rather than to its origin. The usual examples — the city-state that made itself rich under one party, the large state that lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty without competitive elections — are what drew me to it, but I don't want the answer to rest on contestable numbers about them.

What I want is a map of the position's exposure. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or cases would count against it — not the standard objection that it licenses any effective tyrant, which I already accept as a bullet I'm willing to bite, but the ones that actually threaten the structure of the view? Sort them by how decisive they would be, and separate what a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against the view from what only critics say.

I'd like the answer to press especially on the following, since they seem to me where the view is most exposed. First, the counterfactual baseline: the thesis compares a regime against nothing in particular, so what would it take to show that the prosperity and order it claims are not attributable to it — or that a rival arrangement would have produced them without the costs? Second, self-certification: if the regime defines and reports the very metrics (growth, crime, stability, satisfaction) on which its warrant depends, does the view have any way to distinguish performance from the regime's account of its own performance? Third, the epistemology of consent: when speaking against the state is costly, order and contentment are exactly what one would expect to observe whether or not they are real, so what could count as evidence that the performance is genuinely endorsed rather than merely unprotested? Fourth, the transition problem: a warrant that is re-earned moment by moment and is a property of the relation rather than the office seems to say nothing about how rule may legitimately change hands, which is where regimes of this kind have historically failed — is that an incidental gap or a symptom? Fifth, the half-life question: if performance can lapse, can the view specify how much failure, over how long, extinguishes the warrant, or is any such threshold arbitrary?

And then the structural question I care most about: does this position, once stated carefully, amount to anything more than the claim that effective and tolerable rule is effective and tolerable — that is, does it collapse into a realist or Hobbesian account in which legitimacy is just the fact of stable power, dressed up? If it does, say so plainly. If it doesn't, tell me what it predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualification into unfalsifiability — and whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or simply what any view about legitimacy must look like. I'm most persuaded by defeaters that come from cases rather than from intuitions about desert, but I'd rather you tell me where the view is actually vulnerable than flatter the way I framed it.
