Then write the analysis. I don't need a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I care about the reasoning.

Here is the case, stated from my own knowledge so we are working from the same facts. The Therac-25 was a computer-controlled radiation therapy machine built by a Canadian manufacturer in the early 1980s. Its predecessors had hardware interlocks and independent hardware dosimetry; on the 25, some of those interlocks were replaced by software checks running on the same minicomputer that ran the treatment control logic, and the machine was sold with less hardware redundancy. Between 1985 and 1987 at least six patients received massive overdoses, several fatal. At least one was triggered by a race condition that a fast operator could hit by typing ahead at the console. The console displayed fault codes that misdescribed what was happening, and the operator could press a key to clear the fault and proceed, after which the machine delivered the high-current beam while the display reported little or no dose. When early injuries were reported, the manufacturer's position was that the machine could not have produced them, supported by an argument that a recent round of software modifications had improved the hazard rate by orders of magnitude; a hospital physicist was told the damage could not have come from machine malfunction or operator error. The control software had largely been written by one person, was derived from an earlier model's code, and the safety argument leaned on that reuse. Regulators required the machines shut down in 1987.

Now the analysis I want, running in both directions.

Note that the monitor was not independent: the same processor and largely the same code path did both the control decision and the safety check, so a fault in the shared substrate silently voids the check. Third, the manufacturer's safety case was a probabilistic argument from a claimed hazard-rate improvement, and it functioned as epistemic closure: because the machine could not be at fault, anomalous reports were reclassified as something else.
