I have been rereading the Hart–Devlin exchange — Devlin's claim that a shared morality is the cement of society, that its erosion threatens social survival, and that the ordinary person's moral revulsion is a legitimate guide to when the law may step in; Hart's reply that the disintegration thesis must be demonstrated rather than assumed, together with his distinction between preventing harm to others and enforcing morality as such, within which Devlin appears as a legal moralist — and I am struck that the dispute has not really moved in sixty years. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about what 'morality' means (positive versus critical), a concealed empirical dispute about whether a shared morality is genuinely load-bearing for social survival, a disagreement about who bears the burden of proof, or a clash between two pictures of what a society is — an association of individuals whose common business is avoiding harm, versus a community constituted by a shared way of life that the law may protect as such? My own hypothesis is the last, which would make the disagreement bedrock and unreachable by argument; but I want to know whether that is right, which parts of the impasse are genuinely empirical or conceptual and therefore resolvable in principle, and what would count as progress — a distinction, some evidence, a reformulated question — or whether none should be expected. Please answer in British English.
