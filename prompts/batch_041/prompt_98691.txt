I'm taking a seminar on perception, and I've grown frustrated with how the disjunctivism debate is usually staged: everyone settles on the same few stock cases — the straight stick that looks bent in water, the hallucination that seems indistinguishable from a veridical perception, the brain in a vat — and then scores the theories against them, so that the entire argument turns on intuitions I neither share nor get to inspect. Before I read any more of it, I want the specification on the table first. So set out the desiderata that any theory of perceptual experience must meet if it is to give an adequate account of illusion and hallucination: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse (say, between an experience's phenomenal character and its epistemic status, or between being caused by an object and being about one), and the background commitments it must stay consistent with — each requirement paired with the consideration that motivates it, so I can see why it is a genuine requirement rather than a preference of one camp. I care especially about which requirements are truly non-negotiable and which are merely articles of faith of the disjunctivist, the sense-datum theorist, or the intentionalist, and about whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable; if it is, that is the result I most want, since it would show that no theory in this area can have everything at once.

To keep this from becoming a recital of textbook cases, please test the list against three cases I've been trying to construct myself, and treat them as constraints the theory has to handle rather than curiosities to be explained away. First, the split case: a subject has a single, seamless experience as of a red cube on a table, but in fact the cube is present and grey while the redness is contributed entirely by a malfunctioning implant wired into his visual pathway — is this one experience that is partly veridical and partly hallucinatory, two experiences, or a third kind, and does the theory's account of phenomenal character survive whatever answer it gives? Second, the prosthetic case: a subject with his eyes closed and a camera feeding signals directly into his visual cortex has a vivid, world-guided experience of the room in front of him — the signal is entirely accurate, but the ordinary perceptual route is bypassed, so does the theory classify this as perception, as hallucination, or as something its taxonomy was never built to hold? Third, the matching case: a hallucination whose content happens to be exactly right about the scene before the subject's eyes, so that nothing behavioral or epistemic distinguishes it from a perception, only its causal history. For each case, tell me which desideratum it bears on and whether it forces a choice among the contending theories or merely constrains them. Then, briefly, score the main families — naive and conjunctive disjunctivism, the sense-datum view, and intentionalism or representationalism — against the full list, though keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard. Tell me how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one requirement or case where you are least sure you have it right.
