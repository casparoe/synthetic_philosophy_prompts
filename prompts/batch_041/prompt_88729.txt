I'm guest-hosting an episode of a small aesthetics podcast next month (the regular host is on paternity leave, so it's me, a Zoom link, and whoever shows up) and the guest is a Rousseau scholar with a new book on the Letter to d'Alembert — the long open letter where he answers d'Alembert's article on Geneva in the Encyclopédie and argues that the city shouldn't build a theatre. Her line, which I find genuinely interesting and also slightly wierd, is that everyone reads that text as a moralistic anti-theatre tract and that this is basically a misreading: on her view the target isn't the content of plays or even the pleasure they give, it's the structure of theatrical representation as such — a play interposes a fiction between people, so the audience's feelings get used up on imaginary characters instead of being spent on the people actually in the room. She thinks this is the only reading that explains why Rousseau can be so rude about Molière (he complains that the Misanthrope makes the honest, awkward Alceste look ridiculous and lets the smooth cynic win, which I've checked and he does seem to say) while also insisting that you could stage the most improving play imaginable and nobody would be made better by it. My co-host, who isn't on the episode but has opinions, keeps muttering that this is really just Rousseau on amour-propre — that the real target is the desire to be admired rather than the theatre, and that the actor and the spectator are both in the business of being seen. I half think he's right, and if he is then the whole thing collapses into 'society is bad' and there's no interview.

So what I want from you is eight to ten interview questions that force her to defend the mediation reading instead of just restating it, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. No citations or reading lists please, I don't need them, I just want the questions. Things that need to be in there somewhere: (1) the biographical embarrassment — the man had a hit opera a few years before this and kept writing music, and later stage pieces, after it — so is that a loophole, a counterexample, or a distinction she can state cleanly in one sentence? (2) the asymmetry: if theatre can't make anyone better, what's the actual mechanism by which it makes them worse, and can she give it without quietly sliding back into a story about moral content? (3) Molière and Alceste — is her claim about what the play means or about what audiences do with it, and what would decide between those? (4) the actors, where he says the trade itself inclines them toward lying rather than virtue, and where d'Alembert had already anticipated him by arguing that it's the contempt we dump on actors, not the art, that ruins them — who's right, and does her reading even need the actor to be corrupted? (5) d'Alembert's actual proposal, that Geneva could have plays and morals both, Sparta's discipline plus Athens's polish — I want her to say why that's supposed to be impossible rather than just unlikely. (6) I have a vague memory that he sketches what Geneva has instead of a playhouse and thinks it's obviously better; press her on whether that alternative escapes the criticism or just relocates the spectators. (7) the amour-propre worry, put to her as a dilemma: if the enemy is wanting to be admired then the theatre is incidental, and if the enemy is representation then she owes us an account of why a festival isn't representation with better PR. (8) modern participation — karaoke, immersive theatre, reality TV, livestreaming where the audience is the show — because if the objection is to being a spectator then those should be improvements and I suspect they aren't, while if the objection is to mediation then participation is just a different mediation and I want to watch her admit it. (9) a question about whether the argument survives dropping whatever assumptions about women and vanity it leans on, if it does lean on any. And end on something that lets her either concede ground or double down: whether her reading makes him less of a censor, which is good for her, at the price of being a defence of the small, homogeneous, face-to-face republic, which is bad for her, especially since he's careful not to ask for Paris's theatres to be shut. Order them so the thing builds, please — I don't want to open with the amour-propre thing and have her defensive for an hour.
