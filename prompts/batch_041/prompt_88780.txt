Im writing the final exam for an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I want one long essay question on counterpart theory — the Lewis view where nobody exists in more than one world, so a claim like 'I could have been a doctor' is true because a counterpart of me in some other world is a doctor. The trouble is every student can recite the Humphrey-style objection (if it's only a counterpart who's a doctor, then I'm not the one who could have been a doctor) and Lewis's reply (that the objection begs the question by assuming de re modal claims about me have to be about me). I dont want that recitation. Build the question around this instead: a chain of worlds, each a small step from the last — in w1 my counterpart made a slightly different career choice, in w2 that counterpart's counterpart has a slightly different personality, in w3 a slightly different body, and so on down the line, until the occupant of the final world is a maple tree. So: does counterpart theory say I could have been the maple tree? I want students to have to work out whether possibility means 'there's a counterpart' or 'there's a chain of counterparts,' what that implies about whether the counterpart relation is transitive, and whether the view is in trouble if the answer comes out that I could have been a tree — including whether it's a real cost to give up the principle that if it's possible that it's possible that P, then it's possible that P (S4, if you want the label).

Write the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20. Have the model answer land on a verdict — is the chain a genuine problem for the view or not? — and defend it, no fence-sitting. In the marking scheme, spell out what separates an A from a B: the B answer recites the standard objection and reply and stops, while the A answer has to see that the non-transitivity isn't a bug waiting to be patched but is doing real explanatory work (it's what lets the theory deny I could have been a tree while still granting every small step), and that the actual cost is the loss of the closure intuition, which the objector has to argue for rather than assume. For each marking point, add a one-line note on how students typically blow it.
