I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a book set in the Warring States period, and my main character is one of those wandering advisors who argued for a living — a court persuader, a man who gets paid to win. The scene I keep circling is the one where he tells a duke that a white horse is not a horse. I know the gist of the arguement well enough to put it in his mouth: if you want a horse, a yellow or a black one will do; if you want a white horse, they won't; so the two names don't take the same thing, and since white names the colour and horse names the shape, what names the colour can't be what names the shape. Fine. What's driving me up the wall is that nobody agrees about what he's actually doing when he says it, and four readings keep coming up. One: he's making a claim about words — white horse is a narrower term than horse, so the two aren't the same term, and the puzzle is a bit of scope or a point about how a modifier restricts. Two: he's making a claim about things — that the particular white horse isn't the horse, or that horse stands for something no particular horse is, so it's really metaphysics about universals, or about wholes and parts. Three: the word he's using for the negation is doing all the work, and it's ambiguous between not being the same as and not being a member of, so the whole thing is a disguised equivocation and half the commentators are talking past each other on that word alone. Four: it isn't philosophy at all, it's a performance — a showpiece in a culture of competitive disputation at court, closer to a lawyer's trick or a party piece than to a thesis, which fits with the fact that the label we use for these people looks like a later filing-category invented by archivists rather than a school they'd have recognised. The people who take reading one and the people who take reading two or four have been going at it for decades and, as far as I can tell, definately nobody has moved an inch — and they seem to be using completely different standards for what would even count as having got the text right. So my question is diagnostic rather than exegetical: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a real disagreement about the facts — what the text says, what the historical man could plausibly have meant — where more scholarship ought in principle to settle it? Is it two groups answering different questions under one heading, the historians wanting the actual man and the philosophers wanting the most defensible argument, in which case the impasse is structural and neither side is simply wrong? Or is it bedrock, a clash between one side's principle of charity and the other's, about how much sense a text from that far away is even allowed to make? I have a hunch and I'd rather you pushed on it than agreed with it: because I read the scene as a scene, I keep thinking the text isn't a thesis at all but an action — the man is doing something to a room — and that if you read it that way, the question of whether the claim is true never arises, so most of the fight dissolves on its own. Is that a genuine resolution, or am I just changing the subject and calling it a solution? And whatever you decide, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, some piece of evidence, or a decision to stop asking one of the questions and start asking another. Please don't hand me a reading list or a survey of who said what; I can't use either and I don't care about the roll call. I want the shape of the impasse. British English, please.
