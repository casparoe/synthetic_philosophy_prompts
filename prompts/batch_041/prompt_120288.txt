Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words about a town that is dismayed to learn that one of its customs is authentic. The custom is a midwinter procession that the town has described for a century as the deliberate invention of a reforming mayor: a rite assembled from scratch, open to anyone, and the town has always taken its manufactured character as its chief virtue, on the reasoning that a ceremony one has chosen is one to which strangers can be admitted, while a ceremony one has merely inherited belongs to the families who have always held it. A graduate student cataloguing a solicitor's papers then finds unambiguous evidence that the procession is a genuine survival — older than the town, older than the church that claims to have baptized it. The council asks the student to sit on the discovery; the old families, never quite included, want it published. Tell the story from the student's point of view, let the dispute between chosenness and inheritance carry the theme rather than any speech about it, and make the townspeople's dismay intelligible rather than ridiculous. End on what the evidence about the custom's age does and does not change. Do not write the familiar debunking plot in which a modern rite is exposed as a fabrication; here the discovery runs the other way, and that is the point.
