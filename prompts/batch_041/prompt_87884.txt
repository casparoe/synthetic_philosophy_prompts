'Epistemic contextualism is the view that knowledge is relative — whether you know something depends on how much is at stake for you. If you're just chatting with a friend, you need almost no evidence to count as knowing where you parked the car, but if you're about to bet your savings on it you need much more, so "knows" means different things in different contexts. Contextualists call this pragmatic encroachment, because practical interests are always creeping into the purely epistemic. The upshot is that you can never just say whether someone knows something, full stop; the answer is always relative to a context, and since contexts shift, so does the truth. This is basically how contextualism keeps skepticism at bay: in ordinary conversation the bar is low and we know tons of things, but once the philosopher trots out the brain-in-a-vat scenario the bar shoots up and we know nothing. Critics say this makes knowledge arbitrary, and that two people who seem to disagree about whether someone knows are really just talking past each other — which the contextualist is happy to accept.'
