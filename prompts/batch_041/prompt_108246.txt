I chair the advisory board of a foundation that runs a former detention site as a museum, and at our next meeting we must decide whether to make the local association of former guards' families a formal partner in the permanent exhibition: they want their own losses acknowledged alongside those of the prisoners, and they bring funding and archival access. Several of the guards came from a minority community that the state turned on a decade later, so the victim/perpetrator line runs through families rather than between them. The board has been arguing by analogy — Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung on one side, the reconciliatory memory culture of the American South on the other, with Japan or Turkey invoked whenever someone wants to warn that official memory is easily captured by whoever holds office. Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate beforehand: a concrete recommendation — accept, reject, or accept with conditions — with the conditions and a timetable attached, the reasoning for it, and the strongest version of the case for whichever option I end up rejecting. Two things I would rather you settled than assumed: whether an institution like ours can do two jobs at once, namely fixing a record of what was done to a standard of proof and sustaining a live relationship between communities that must go on living together, or whether one of them always ends up eating the other; and whether any of those analogies actually fits our case, since some look like they are doing rhetorical rather than descriptive work. Where an analogy fails, say so plainly.
