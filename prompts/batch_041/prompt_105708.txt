Spec: two respectable models of how an archive forms have to give opposite verdicts about the same silence in the same body of records. Model A: documents survive roughly by chance — time is a sieve, whats left is a thin random sample of what once existed. Model B: a document survives because some institution had an ongoing reason to keep it — it was needed for monitoring, taxing, policing, or accounting — so what's preserved tracks what power bothered to care about. The twist I want: Model B, the cynical one, licenses the stronger factual claim, and Model A, the trusting one, licenses almost nothing. Same surviving documents, same claim, same volume of evidence; only the story about why these particular documents survived may differ.

Constraints: realistic setting — a parish, a court, a colonial office, a merchant firm, your call; no invented technology, no omniscient narrator (dont let the scenario cheat), and the scenario itself is one paragraph. Then walk it: state the claim, state both models, show what each says about the silence and why, and name the one feature of the case that makes the verdicts peel apart — plus what happens if you remove it. End with the honest note: is this a real asymmetry in how evidence works, or have I smuggled the conclusion into one of the models? And how does a defender of Model A attack the case?
