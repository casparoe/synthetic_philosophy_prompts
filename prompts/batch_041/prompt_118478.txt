Setup, so we're on the same page: a sorites series of colour chips running from unmistakably red to unmistakably orange, each adjacent pair indistinguishable to a normal observer. Epistemicism says the predicate 'red' has a sharp extension — some chip is the last red one — and we simply cannot know which; bivalence and classical logic survive, and the price is unknowable facts. Supervaluationism says truth is truth on all admissible precisifications: 'that chip is red' is true when it's red on every precisification, false when false on every one, and neither when the precisifications disagree; the sorites premise gets rejected because it's false on every precisification, even though no single step is the false one.

Both of those I can recite. What I want explained, in the weeds, is the following four things, and I'd rather you reason about them than hand me the canned contrast:

(a) Why is it usually said that supervaluationism doesn't solve higher-order vagueness but just relocates it? I want the actual mechanism, not the slogan. If 'admissible precisification' is itself a sharp notion, haven't we just re-introduced the sharp line one level up? And if it's a vague notion, then what — infinite regress, or something worse?

(b) The no-sharp-boundaries worry, run on the sorites itself: if there's no sharp boundary between red and orange, doesn't the same style of reasoning show there's no sharp boundary between 'has a sharp boundary' and 'doesn't', and so on up? Which of the two views can absorb that and which one can't, and what exactly does the one that can't have to give up?

(c) Both views owe us an account of how the boundary, or the set of precisifications, is fixed by what speakers actually do — our use, our judgments, our training. Epistemicism seems to owe an account of how a sharp cutoff gets determined by a practice that nowhere draws it; supervaluationism owes an account of how the admissible precisifications get picked out by that same practice. Which debt is worse, and is either of them payable at all?

(d) The technical costs that never make it into the intro lectures. Supervaluationism keeps excluded middle as a logical truth while dropping bivalence, so you get 'it's either red or not red' true while neither disjunct is true; and 'definitely' stops behaving like a normal operator — I think you lose the inference from 'definitely (p or q)' plus 'not definitely p' to 'definitely q'. Explain why that isn't a cheap price, and how the epistemicist's version of the same terrain looks by comparison, including whether the epistemicist gets to keep all the inference rules at the cost of something else.

One twist I want you to take seriously, because I think it sharpens (c): suppose the community's judgments aren't all-or-nothing but come out as a smooth profile — 99% of speakers call chip 100 red, 50% call chip 300 red, 1% call chip 700 red, and so on. Does that smooth statistical structure help either view, hurt it, or is it just noise from the perspective of both?
