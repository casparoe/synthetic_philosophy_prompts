ok so i've been trying to write a short thing about owning ideas and it keeps coming out either as a boring retread of the usual should-IP-exist debate or as a rant, so im sending you the gist before I try a fourth time. comments please, and be harsh.

Here's the argument. The usual debate about intellectual property asks a normative question first: should people be able to own ideas, and if so how much — incentives, fairness, autonomy, the public domain, all that. I want to say there's a prior problem that isn't normative at all. It's about individuation. Property in a material thing is possible because material things come pre-individuated: there's a fact of the matter about whether this lump of gold is one object or two, where it ends, whether the ring and the nugget it was made from are the same object. Ideas dont obviously come that way. Nothing in the world tells you whether the idea of the wheel and the idea of the axle are one idea or two, whether a given tune is one idea or a hundred, or whether a plot structure is a single idea or an instance of a more general one. So a property right in an idea would be a right over an object whose boundaries are stipulated rather than discovered, which is a weird kind of object to own.

I think this explains why real IP law keeps retreating from ideas toward their embodiments. Copyright gives you a right over a particular text or recording, not over the idea of a talking dog. Patent gives you a right over a particular claimed device or process, and the claims are written so as to draw boundaries by fiat. The idea/expression dichotomy and the requirement that patent claims be definite are usually taught as balancing tests between incentives and access. I want to say they're symptoms of what I've been calling the grain problem: you can only own what you can individuate, and expressions and embodiments are individuable in a way that bare content isn't.

Then the twist, which is the part I actually care about and am least sure of. Some ideas do seem individuable — because they're solutions rather than contents. A solution is individuated by the problem it solves. "How do you keep a door shut without a latch" picks out a fairly determinate target, and a magnet-and-plate is a determinate answer to it; same for an algorithm relative to a spec, or a proof relative to a theorem. So maybe the real line isn't idea vs expression. It's content-shaped vs solution-shaped. Where an idea is a solution to a well-defined problem, ownership is at least coherent; where it's a content with no privileged grain, ownership claims are confused, and what looks like a moral objection to owning them is really a metaphysical one.

Which is why the interesting cases are the in-between ones. A chord progression: content or solution? A magic trick: you can own the prop and the patter, but the method — is it a solution, because it does something determinate, or a content, because it's just an idea about cards? A game mechanic, a scent, a plot beat, a dance move, a business method. My hunch is that when people try to own these they're quietly smuggling in a problem statement to individuate the thing, and the fight is really over whose problem statement counts.

1. Is content vs solution doing real work, or is it just a relabeling of idea vs expression with fancier vocabulary?
2. Does it collapse? Every solution has some content-y description under which it's fuzzy, and every content has a solution-y description under which it's crisp, so maybe the distinction is just about how you choose to describe the thing, not about the thing.
3. If it survives, does it prove too much? It seems to license owning solutions, and I wanted to end up arguing that owning ideas is usually a bad idea. Do I have to give that up, or can I get the badness back from somewhere else?
4. Steelman the other side for me: is there a case of someone owning something my line says they cant, where the ownership doesn't feel like an injustice at all?
