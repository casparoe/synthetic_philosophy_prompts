ok so I lost most of last night to an argument with my friend Tomas about who started the First World War, and I want to get it written out while it's still fresh, because I think I lost on stamina rather than on substance. His picture — and the thing that got under my skin is that he thinks it's just the obvious one, not even a controversial one — is dominoes. The alliances were a row of dominoes standing close enough to knock each other over, Sarajevo toppled the first one, and the mobilisation timetables meant that once a few had gone over nobody had the power to stop the rest; so singling out Germany, or anyone else, as the guilty party is a category mistake, the system did it, and "who started it" is a question for people who haven't understood how the thing was wired. He got to this through the Fischer fight — he read that Fischer's line was that Germany deliberately willed the war, and he says that's just the war-guilt clause of the treaty wearing a historians hat, i.e. pinning on a whole nation what was really a mechanical cascade that caught everyone up. (He also, confusingly, kept insisting that the whole "the trains can't be stopped" story is a myth invented after the fact by some military historian, and I honestly couldn't tell whether that was meant to help his case or mine.) I don't buy it, but every time I said but someone laid the dominoes out and someone flicked the first one, he said that's just me smuggling a culprit back in, and that blaming a nation is nonsense anyway since only people act.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy itself, not a verdict on 1914. Lay out the correspondences first — what's playing the role of the dominoes, the table they're standing on, the wiring, the hand, the momentum, the gaps where the run could have stopped — then the places where the mapping actually breaks, and be explicit about which breaks matter for the work he's making the picture do versus which are just trivia about dominoes. Then tell me what the picture quietly imports that the July crisis doesn't have, especially on the agency side: whether a nation can be an agent at all, whether the analogy needs there to have been a flicker, and whether the guilt-clause point he's making is actually carried by the dominoes or is a separate argument he's parked on top of them.

And then the payoff: does his conclusion — nobody's really to blame, or at most everyone equally — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? If the domino picture is the wrong one, is there a better analogy for the escalation that doesn't do the exculpating work for free, or is the honest answer that no single picture can carry the whole thing and you need one for how it started and a different one for how it spread? He's not an idiot and I don't want to be told he's wrong about everything, so please flag the parts of his picture I should actually concede.
