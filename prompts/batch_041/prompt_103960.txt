Write a poem for a graduate seminar on early modern metaphysics that stages Berkeley's master argument without ever naming it or quoting his slogans. Use six four-line stanzas: the first describes a scene held in being by four perceivers, and each following stanza drops one, so that the fifth stanza has no finite perceiver left and the description ought, by the argument's own lights, to be impossible to write. The sixth stanza is the crux: it must either break off mid-line or carry on, and if it carries on, the reader should see that the only thing still sustaining the scene is an infinite perceiver rather than the poet's words or the reader's own act of reading. Somewhere before that, let the poem give the standard objection its due weight -- that all this establishes is the impossibility of conceiving an unperceived object, not the impossibility of an unperceived object -- and let it feel the objection rather than batting it aside. Meter and rhyme are yours, but the shrinking stanzas must carry the argument; a specialist should find nothing to wince at, and a reader who has never met the argument should still find it a good poem.
