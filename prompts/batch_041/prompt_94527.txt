I'm doing an MA module on fundamentality and I keep getting stuck on a puzzle about grounding that our reading doesnt really address. Here's the setup as I understand it. Say some fact A is grounded in some fact B — the table's being coloured is grounded in the arrangement of its molecules, or whatever. Now consider the further fact that A is grounded in B. Call that a linking fact. The linking fact looks like a fact too, so it's fair to ask what grounds it. If you say nothing grounds it, it's fundamental — but then the fundamental level includes facts about the grounding relations among non-fundamental facts, and that feels off, because fundamentality starts to look like it's partly about structure rather than about what's simply basic. If you say something grounds it — maybe A and B together, maybe just B — you seem to get either a regress, with each linking fact needing its own linking fact, or a kind of circularity, or you have to say that linking facts are grounded in their relata in a way that makes grounding oddly self-effacing. Some people I've talked to think there's a clean third option: linking facts aren't extra facts at all, they're just a façon de parler, and the regress never gets going. Others think that's cheating, because grounding was supposed to be a real explanatory relation rather than a projection of our own explanatory habits.
