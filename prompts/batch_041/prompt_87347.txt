A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about manipulation, and we ended exactly where we began, so I would like to put my position in front of someone who will tell me what should move me.

My view is that manipulation and legitimate persuasion differ in kind, and that the difference lies neither in the truth of what is conveyed nor in the speaker's intentions, but in how the influence does its work. Influence is manipulative to the degree that its hold on the audience does not run through the audience's own assessment of the considerations offered — that is, to the degree that it would succeed just as well on an audience that could not, or would not, scrutinize the grounds. Call it the scrutiny-survival test. I take it to be scalar rather than a line: the more of an influence's efficacy that survives the target's critical attention, the less manipulative it is. On this account a manipulator may be perfectly transparent about what he is doing, and may say only true things, so long as the effect does not depend on the target's evaluation of reasons.

I am confident this is better than the alternatives I know — that manipulation consists in concealing one's intent, or in overriding autonomy, or in bypassing rationality by triggering a bias. My friend's parting shot was that on my test every honest teacher is a manipulator, since almost nothing a student comes to believe would survive that student's scrutiny at the moment of acquiring it.

So: what counts against my view? I want the cases and arguments that should move me, ranked by how far they should. Which influences would the test misclassify — the ones that hurt, not the obvious hard cases? Which rival handles those better, and what does it pay for the advantage? Tell me also whether the test collapses into some broader category that was never about manipulation at all, and what its defenders would concede rather than deny. I have not worked out how it handles influence that is truthful and selective at once: an adviser who presents only the considerations favouring one course, each of them genuinely good, so that the target's scrutiny is fully satisfied. I suspect that is where the account is weakest. Finally, I want an honest verdict on whether the scalar framing has quietly insulated the view from refutation; if it has, say whether that is a defect or merely the price of a graded account. I would give up the mechanism-based picture before I gave up the thought that transparency alone never converts manipulation into persuasion.
