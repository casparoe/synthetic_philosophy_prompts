I teach an undergraduate seminar on the relation between the person and the community, and I want a diagnostic instrument rather than a lecture. Please build a taxonomy of the positions on the question of which gets the relation between person and community right, presented as a decision tree whose internal nodes are the questions that separate positions and whose leaves are the named positions.

Design requirements:

1. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a roster of famous names. Fix the axes you take to be genuinely independent first, then locate positions at combinations of answers. I want at least three axes, and I care especially about separating (a) the constitutive question — is the individual constituted by social relations, merely causally shaped by them, or prior to and independent of them? — from (b) the explanatory question — are social facts explained by facts about individuals, or individual properties by social facts? — from (c) the normative question — is the locus of value the individual, the group, or the relation itself?

2. Phrase every node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Concrete cases and first-person commitments work better than labels: whether a person stripped of every social role and tie is still the same morally relevant person; whether a norm's existence is fully explained by individual beliefs and dispositions; whether a right may be overridden for the sake of communal harmony; whether one can choose the relations that constitute one.

3. Respect a distinction I care about: constitutive dependence of the self on relations versus mere causal influence, and, on the constitutive branch, whether the constituting relations are given and unchosen or revisable and chosen. A position holding that the self is relationally constituted but that its relations are freely revisable should be its own leaf, not folded into atomism or into collectivism.

4. Leaves must include at least: atomistic individualism; the embedded or encumbered self; role-based relationalism, in which the self is the sum of its roles and harmony is the governing good; methodological holism about social explanation; group-priority collectivism, in which the group's interests are irreducible and lexically prior; and a position locating value in relations rather than in either relatum. Add any further leaf you think the space requires.

5. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so and diagnose why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two positions your tree cannot separate and say what additional question would be needed.

6. Treat the East Asia versus the West framing as a hypothesis to be tested by the tree, not assumed: do not allocate positions by geography, and note any leaf that both traditions supply.

Format: an indented tree, each node a one-line question with its branching answers, each leaf named and followed by one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Maximum 900 words. End with a single-sentence bottom line naming the one distinction you think does the most sorting work.
