I teach a grad-level epistemology seminar and I'm writing the final. I need one essay question on the regress of justification, and it has to be one students can't pass by reciting the three horns -- foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism -- and then saying which one they like. I want them to actually have to think.

Here's the setup I want the question built around, so use it. Imagine someone defends a view they call "default foundationalism." On this view, a belief counts as properly foundational for an agent not when it's certain, or self-evident, or incorrigible, but simply when the agent has no actual belief that gives them a reason to doubt it. Foundations are just whatever survives unchallenged in your web of belief. The pitch is that this dodges the regress, because the regress only bites when you demand that the stopping point be justified *in a way that could itself be questioned* -- and unchallenged beliefs aren't like that.

The essay question should force students to say whether that's a genuine escape from the regress or just a relabling of where the chain stops, and whether "unchallenged" can do the epistemic work "foundational" was supposed to do. I want the crux to be whether the view quietly helps itself to a coherence constraint while still calling itself foundationalist, or whether it really does collapse into something closer to a default-and-challenge picture of entitlement. A top answer should be able to tell those two apart.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around maybe five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong.

One more thing: in the model answer, and especially in the marking scheme, be clear about which parts are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who actually work on this -- don't hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus. If a point is one reasonable epistemologists really do disagree about, say so, and mark it as a place where a good student could land on either side as long as they argue for it.
