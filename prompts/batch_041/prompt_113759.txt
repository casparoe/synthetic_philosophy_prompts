I host a long-form interview podcast on science and ideas, and next month my guest is a philosopher of science who has spent much of her career defending a scaled-down version of Kuhn's incommensurability thesis. Her position, as she states it, has two parts. First, incommensurability is local rather than global: it arises when two research traditions' sorting vocabularies overlap only partially, so that a predicate central to one tradition has an extension cutting across several predicates of the other, and no translation can preserve both extension and inferential role at once. Second, and this is the part I want to press hardest, partial overlap does not restore comparability, because comparing two claims requires a shared standard of what would count as an answer, not merely a shared stock of objects to be described. She calls that standard a question-frame, and holds that rival traditions typically do not answer the same question differently; they answer different questions, and a common question can be reconstructed only from outside, by someone whose reconstruction is itself theory-laden.

Draft eight to ten questions that make her work for it rather than restate the thesis, with at least one each on: the objection that partial overlap is precisely what makes translation possible, so that her thesis collapses into a claim about hard translation rather than about meaning; how she separates relativism about translation from relativism about truth, and why she takes the first to be defensible and the second not; and what the notion of a question-frame adds beyond the older notion of a paradigm, including what would count as evidence that two traditions are genuinely incommensurable rather than merely difficult to translate. Include one question on whether incommensurability could be asymmetric -- one side unable to state the other's claims while the reverse remains possible -- and one asking what she would have to give up if she is wrong. For every question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, since her habitual move is to reply that her critics are assuming a shared question-frame rather than showing that one exists.
