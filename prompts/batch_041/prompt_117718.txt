I am a fourth-year PhD student working at the intersection of Kierkegaard and contemporary media theory, and I have reached the point in my dissertation where I need to stop surveying accounts of the public and start specifying what any account of it would have to deliver. Please write out the desiderata: the requirements that an adequate theory of the public — of what a public is, how one comes into being, what keeps it in being, and what it can bring about — must satisfy. I want the specification, not the survey. Do not rehearse who has said what, and do not supply citations or reading lists; I will handle the literature myself.

For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a commitment of one camp in particular. Where you suspect that some pair or subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and say why. An over-specified list that I can trim is more useful to me than a tidy synthesis that conceals a tension. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most work in ruling out candidate theories.

The purpose the theory has to serve is this: it must underwrite a normative critique of the press that does not beg the question — that is, one that can criticise what the press does to public discourse without first assuming either that the public is a fiction or that it is a collective agent with a will of its own.

The phenomena that have to be covered, at a minimum:

1. The public is nobody in particular, and yet claims about what the public wants, or about what people are saying, function as reasons in practical deliberation and carry a force that the assertion of any individual does not. The theory has to say what kind of thing is doing that work.
2. A public forms around something — an event, a document, a person who may be dead or fictional — and its members are unknown to one another. Joining requires nothing but attention; no interaction, organisation, or decision to join is needed. The theory must decide whether membership is a matter of fact or of self-ascription, and must survive the cases in which the two come apart.
3. A public dissolves without anyone leaving. The same individuals are still there, still attentive, and yet the public is gone.
4. Reflexivity: what members take the public's opinion to be is partly constitutive of that opinion. Willingness to express a view varies with perceived prevailing opinion, so a theory that treats public opinion as the sum of individual opinions fails on the very phenomenon it was introduced to explain.
5. Levelling: a downward pressure on distinctions that no one intends, no one performs, and no one can be identified as having caused.
6. The relation between public and press runs both ways. On some accounts — I take this to be Kierkegaard's, though tell me if the reading is contested — the press is what makes a public possible at all, and yet the press is also answerable to the public it addresses. The theory must allow for this without circularity.
7. Cases I want handled without ad hoc amendment: a public constituted around a television finale; a public constituted around a person who does not exist; a public defined less by a shared text than by an algorithmic feed; a public that is entirely real for its members and a fiction for everyone outside it; and overlapping publics, where the same person belongs to several at once.

Constraints the theory must respect rather than explain away: it may not posit a collective mind or a group agent, but neither may it reduce the public to a fiction; it must be consistent with empirical work on opinion expression, including the dependence of expressed opinion on perceived opinion and the effect of asking people what others think on what they then say; and it must be able to explain why the public is treated as a source of authority despite having no will — enough to make the normative question pressing, even if it does not settle it.
