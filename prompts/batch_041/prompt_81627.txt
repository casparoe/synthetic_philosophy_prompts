I'm preparing a seminar session on lost causes and the myths that form around them, and I want to anchor it in a passage from Josephus's *The Wars of the Jews* (Whiston translation, Book VII, chapter 1). Here is the passage:

> Now as soon as the army had no more people to slay or to plunder, because there remained none to be the objects of their fury, (for they would not have spared any, had there remained any other work to be done,) Caesar gave orders that they should now demolish the entire city and temple, but should leave as many of the towers standing as were of the greatest eminency; that is, Phasaelus, and Hippicus, and Mariamne; and so much of the wall as enclosed the city on the west side. This wall was spared, in order to afford a camp for such as were to lie in garrison, as were the towers also spared, in order to demonstrate to posterity what kind of city it was, and how well fortified, which the Roman valor had subdued; but for all the rest of the wall, it was so thoroughly laid even with the ground by those that dug it up to the foundation, that there was left nothing to make those that came thither believe it had ever been inhabited. This was the end which Jerusalem came to by the madness of those that were for innovations; a city otherwise of great magnificence, and of mighty fame among all mankind.

Help me understand how the last two sentences of that passage fit together, and what the answer implies for the structure of a lost-cause myth. The conqueror leaves three named towers and a strip of wall standing so that posterity can see how formidable the city had been — a memorial whose purpose is to keep the scale of the conquest legible — and then the narrator, himself one of the defeated and writing under Roman patronage, closes the account of the destruction by attributing it to "the madness of those that were for innovations." Is the memorial sentence doing the same work as the blame sentence, or do the two pull against each other? Whose posterity is addressed by the spared towers, and what follows from the fact that one and the same structure is at once a garrison camp, a trophy for the victor, and the only surviving evidence of the city's greatness? Then tell me how this bears on the Masada episode Josephus narrates later in the same book, where the defeated are given speeches explaining why they choose death over capture: does that myth rest on the same self-inflicted-catastrophe move, or does it require a different one? I'm interested in the interpretive structure, not in a survey of scholarship.
