I am a novelist by trade, and for the past two years I have sat on the Commemoration Review Commission of the city where I live: a seven-member body, mostly non-specialists, that meets quarterly to decide what our public memorial landscape should keep, move, alter, or retire. I am the chair, and I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's special session. I would like you to draft it with me.

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on public commemoration and collective memory, and who is equally at home in the philosophy of language and in normative ethics, writing for six lay commissioners and for the public minutes. Before you draft anything, think the case through at length and in the open: set out the positions available, test each against the facts, and only then commit. I want to see the reasoning, not just its conclusion. Do not cite scholarship, supply a reading list, or give me footnotes; I cannot check anything, and neither can my colleagues. Argue from the case itself.

The case. On the east edge of the old market square stands a granite and bronze drinking fountain, erected in 1911 and given to the city by Horatio Marston, a textile manufacturer. The basin still runs in summer, and it is the only free water point on the square; people fill bottles there. Its inscription dedicates the fountain to the refreshment and improvement of the working men of the district and identifies Marston as the donor. It is, in other words, both a working object and a monument, and the plaque is a small part of a thing that is used.

Last year a researcher at the county historical society published a dossier drawn from the firm's own ledgers and from the archive of the assize court. It shows that during the strike of 1894 Marston's firm paid for the transport and lodging of imported strike-breakers and for a private guard company, and that its solicitors helped prosecute local men for intimidation. Marston's defenders answer, accurately, that he later endowed the public library, that he sat on the board of the infirmary, and that the fountain was his gift. He died in 1919.

Three proposals are on the table. The first would remove the fountain entirely, salvage the bronze, and commission on the same spot a counter-monument recording the strike and recording the removal itself. The second would keep the object but take Marston's name off it, adding an inscription naming the strikers and the neighborhood. The third would leave the stone as it is and add an interpretive panel. The commission is split three to three; my opinion will decide it. The Old Mill neighborhood association, whose members' families worked the mill, wants the fountain kept where it stands. They say it is the only public object in the city that marks their district at all, and that for three generations they have read it as a monument to their own people rather than to the man who paid for it. The Marston family trust has offered to fund whatever we decide, which some read as an attempt to buy a favorable outcome and others as an honest offer to pay for the consequences of their ancestor's conduct. I should add that my grandmother worked in the Marston mill; the city solicitor advises that this does not require recusal, and I do not intend to recuse, but I want an opinion that would survive a critic who knew it.

Here is a test I have been turning over, and I would like you to use it, and to tell me where it breaks. Divide what a monument does into two ledgers. On the first, it makes claims about what happened, and those claims are answerable to evidence. On the second, it pledges something on the present city's behalf: it says whom we hold up, what we promise to keep honoring, what a child walking past is meant to see. My suspicion is that remedies do not transfer between the ledgers. Re-inscribing can correct the first and leave the second untouched; removal can settle the second and do nothing for the first, or even destroy the physical record of the error we are trying to mark. And there is a further wrinkle that interests me as a writer of fiction: the inscription speaks in the first-person plural of a previous generation, so the question arises whether re-inscribing is an honest change of mind or a kind of forgery, putting our words into the mouths of the dead, and whether leaving the stone untouched means we are still saying, every day, something we no longer mean.

What I need from you is a memo of roughly a thousand to thirteen hundred words, with the following in it. First, a clear recommendation at the top: which proposal, or some fourth disposition of your own, and a concrete course of action with a date attached to each step, working back from the council's budget vote in the spring. Second, the strongest case for the outcome you are not recommending, stated so fairly that its advocates would recognize it, followed by your answer to it. Third, your application of the two-ledger test, including at least one point where you think the test misleads or needs amending. Fourth, a serious treatment of the neighborhood association's claim that the fountain's meaning is made by the people who use it rather than fixed by the donor's intention, and of the suspicion that a counter-monument is less a reckoning than an act of self-congratulation. Fifth, a closing motion of a single sentence that I can put to the vote. Where the facts I have given you are too thin to support a step in your argument, flag the assumption you had to make rather than sliding over it. Write in plain prose for people who read novels and committee papers, not journals, and do not end with interpretive signage unless you can show me why it is not the coward's answer.
