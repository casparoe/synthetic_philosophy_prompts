Write a recruitment brochure — roughly 600 words, in numbered sections, in the flat, formal register of an official call for applications — issued by the secretariat of a fictional international criminal tribunal convened by the winning powers after a war, inviting applications for judicial and prosecutorial posts. Do not wink: the document must never acknowledge that it is satire, and a reader should be able to infer what is being satirized from the content alone. Keep the tribunal, its convening states, and all persons fictional; the word "victor," and any euphemism that names the problem outright, should not appear.

The target is the cluster of difficulties that made the real postwar tribunals legally contested: criminal liability for conduct that was lawful when committed; judges and prosecutors drawn from the winning side passing judgment on the losing one; the charge of waging aggressive war brought by states that had themselves waged it; the tu quoque defense; superior orders; and the gap between a tribunal's legality and its legitimacy. The humor must come from taking these difficulties seriously and letting the brochure's own rules embody them, not from inventing absurdities — a specialist should find the satire fair rather than lazy.

So that the piece cannot become a recitation of a familiar critique, build the following machinery into the brochure and let it do the comedic work: (a) a "counterfactual legality" standard, under which a defendant may be convicted only of acts that the tribunal's convening states would equally have prosecuted had their own officials committed them, together with the internal procedure by which the secretariat certifies that this condition has been satisfied; (b) a graduated-retroactivity schedule, under which the law applied to each defendant is keyed to the date of a designated "anchor act," the method of designation being explained in a footnote; (c) a section on "prior service," in which experience in the defendant's own government, army, or party is treated as a qualification for the bench; and (d) an appeals chamber whose composition is described in a way that quietly disposes of the tu quoque problem without ever mentioning it.

State the eligibility criteria — nationality, language, "standing," "impartiality," security clearance — in the bloodless idiom of a human-resources notice, and let the criteria themselves carry the irony. The result should be legible and, ideally, funny to a reader who has never heard of Nuremberg, and disquieting to one who has: the point is to let the reader feel why a tribunal of this kind would need rules of exactly this shape, rather than simply to mock it. Close with the application deadline, the required forms, and a contact address.
