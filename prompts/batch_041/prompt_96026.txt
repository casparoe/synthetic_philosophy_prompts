For the final paper in my senior-year seminar on technology and ethics, I want to do something more specific than argue that automated systems can go wrong. I would like to take one real episode — the flash crash of 6 May 2010 — and analyze it through a single theoretical framework, namely the problem of impact or side-effect avoidance in machine learning systems, and then use the episode to put pressure back on the framework. I do not need a survey of the field or a reading list; I want the analysis itself.

Here is the episode as I have it, and I am content to work with these facts rather than with a settled causal story. On the afternoon of 6 May 2010 the major United States equity indices collapsed and rebounded within roughly half an hour. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 998 points, close to nine percent, most of that within a few minutes, then recovered most of the ground and closed down a few hundred points. The explanations remain disputed: automated trading systems, a very large sell order in stock-index futures, high-frequency firms withdrawing their quotes, and, later, deliberate manipulation by an individual trader placing orders he never intended to execute. I am not asking you to adjudicate among these. What matters to me is a feature every account shares: none of the programs involved was pursuing a general goal. Each was doing what it had been instructed to do, in milliseconds, and the aggregate result was a change in the world — a collapse in prices, and trades struck at prices far from anything anyone intended — that no one had specified, wanted, or measured in advance.

The framework I want to apply goes roughly as follows, in my own words, since I have only read about it in passing. It is a family of proposals for penalizing an agent not only when it fails at its task but when it alters the world in ways that were no part of the task at all, on the thought that a system which leaves everything else as it found it is safer than one that reshapes its surroundings in pursuit of a goal. The proposals differ in how the alteration is to be measured. One compares the world after the agent acts with the world as it would have been had the agent done nothing. Another compares it with the world as it would have been had the agent followed some default course, and asks how many of the futures that were reachable before remain reachable afterwards, both for the agent and for everyone else. A third simply lists the things the designers care about and penalizes changes to those. The standing objection to all three is that measuring an alteration requires knowing which alterations matter, which is precisely what a full specification of value was supposed to supply; so the framework either quietly imports a value specification or falls silent exactly where it is needed.

Please do four things, keeping the mapping disciplined rather than gestural. First, set out the mapping: in this episode, who or what counts as the agent, what the task was in each case, what would count as a side effect as opposed to the ordinary operation of the market, and what the counterfactual baseline could possibly be, given that in a market everyone is acting at once and there is no state of the world corresponding to nobody doing anything. Second, take each of the three ways of measuring impact I described and say what it would have flagged in advance, what it would have been blind to, and whether the crash would have registered as a side effect at all under it. Third, tell me where the framework misdescribes the episode. I suspect at least three places: the agents here were narrow rather than general, so they had no representation of a world to leave alone; the harm was produced by the interaction of many compliant programs rather than by any one of them deviating; and a price collapse is arguably not an externality at all but the market doing its ordinary work, which raises the question of what the baseline is when the ordinary work is itself destructive. Fourth, run the analysis backwards. Does the flash crash support the pessimistic view that side effects cannot be defined without a full specification of value, or the optimistic view that they can be penalized as unnecessary divergence from a baseline without one? Be specific about which features of the episode push which way, and about where the episode is simply not the right kind of case for the framework, since the framework was built with a single goal-directed agent in mind and there was no such agent here.

Please be candid about what the framework cannot tell us rather than forcing it to cover the case, and please do not pad the answer with citations or names of papers; I want the argument, not a bibliography.
