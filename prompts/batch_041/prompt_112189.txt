For the past three years I have been reporting on how families, clinicians and hospitals handle advance directives, and next month I am giving the keynote at a one-day symposium on decision-making at the end of life, hosted by a large teaching hospital trust. What I want from you is not a summary of my argument but a Q&A prep sheet.

The argument I will be putting forward is this. An advance directive is better read as testimony than as an instruction: it tells us what a particular person feared and valued, and it earns whatever authority it has from the quality of that testimony, not from the bare fact that the person who wrote it once had standing to speak for the person now in the bed. The earlier self gets a vote, not a veto. Three consequences follow, and I mean to defend all three. First, a directive should create a strong but defeasible presumption, rebuttable by evidence about how the patient is actually faring now. Second, we should separate what I call destination preferences — 'I would not want to live like that' — from pathway preferences — 'I do not want to die choking, in pain, or in a corridor'; the first are unreliable when formed in good health about a state the author has never inhabited, the second are robust and should carry most of the weight. Third, directives should carry sunset clauses: absent renewal, a refusal written a decade ago should lapse, because a form signed by a frightened well person is thin evidence about a life that person has not yet met. I will open with a case from my own reporting: a man in his sixties who signed a refusal of ventilation and artificial nutrition after a stroke, whose survival turned on a disputed reading of his document, and who now plays correspondence chess, follows the cricket, and describes the man who signed the form as a stranger.

The room will not be a philosophy seminar, and I need the objections this room will actually raise. It will contain: palliative care consultants and geriatricians who have watched patients say they are glad to be alive and who are quietly sceptical of paperwork; two clinical ethicists who hold the precedent-autonomy line and will press me on whether my view leaves the patient without capacity with no standing at all; a disability studies scholar who will hear in my talk the oldest argument there is — that a life like that is not worth living — and will say that my evidence of flourishing is simply a licence for the rest of us to judge whose life counts; the trust's legal officer, whose lodestar is the Mental Capacity Act and who will want to know what a clinician is supposed to do at three in the morning; and a hospice chaplain who has sat with more dying people than anyone else in the room and distrusts all of us who theorise.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from these people, ordered by how much damage they would do to the argument as sketched above, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is thin. Tell me what to concede at the outset to buy goodwill, and which of the three consequences I should be prepared to give up if I have to. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks. No reading list, please — I cannot look anything up from the podium.
