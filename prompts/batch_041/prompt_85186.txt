I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, though I read a fair amount of it in my own time, and a friend has landed me with a puzzle I cannot shake. Over dinner he asked how it is that I can be genuinely distressed by the fate of a character I know perfectly well never existed, and he told me the question has a name — the paradox of fiction — and a founding paper by Colin Radford and Michael Weston about being moved by Anna Karenina. As I understand it the claims are three: we are moved by fictional characters and events; being moved by something requires believing it real; and we do not believe it real. Radford's own verdict, apparently, is that we are simply being irrational. I have since read that Kendall Walton answers that what we feel is not real pity or fear but a quasi-emotion experienced inside a game of make-believe, that others reply that we are moved by the thought rather than by any belief, and that there is a notion called alief, which I gather Tamar Gendler introduced, for the belief-like state that runs alongside what we actually believe — the one that makes me flinch at a horror film whose monster I know to be a man in make-up. The phrase everyone reaches for, a willing suspension of disbelief, has never explained anything to me; if I have suspended it I still do not believe, so the problem looks untouched. In my own line of work I am tempted to say that the emotional response simply fires on the representation without checking its provenance, the way a cached response comes back before anyone has troubled the server, but I suspect that is a slogan rather than a solution.

What I would actually like is a reading of a film through this problem. I am introducing The Purple Rose of Cairo at a small film club next month — Woody Allen, 1985 — and it seems to me to be a film about precisely this rather than one that merely illustrates it. Cecilia is a waitress in Depression-era New Jersey with an abusive husband named Monk, and she escapes into the cinema; one afternoon the archaeologist Tom Baxter, a side character in the picture she has come back to see yet again, notices her in the audience, speaks to her, and walks out of the screen to be with her. The other characters are stranded in a film that cannot proceed without him; the actor who plays him, Gil Shepherd, arrives from Hollywood to coax him back and seduces Cecilia in order to do it. She chooses the actor over the character, is abandoned for her trouble, and the film closes with her sitting alone in the auditorium watching Top Hat, where the sight of Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers dancing to Cheek to Cheek brings her round to a smile.

So: read the film through the paradox. I want the literalising of the mechanism taken seriously — a character crossing from the fiction into the world, and a fiction frozen for want of its object — and I want to know which of the three families of answer the film's own machinery supports. Does Tom Baxter's love count as evidence for the thought theory or for Walton's quasi-emotions, given that his entire past is what the picture has shown us and nothing more? What does Cecilia's preference for the actor teach us, given that the real man is the one who lies to her and the invented one is the one who keeps faith? And what are we to make of that closing smile: is Allen conceding Radford's point, that the consolation is a substitute and the emotion therefore irrational, or is he claiming that being moved by an acknowledged fiction is not a failure of rationality but the most dependable source of value in her life? Please also tell me where the reading strains and where the film resists it — I have a nagging feeling that Allen stacks the deck by making her real life so uniformly bleak that no theory of fiction is really being tested, only two worlds compared, and I would rather have that said than glossed over. Finish with what the film adds to the argument that argument alone cannot supply. I do not want a reading list or references; I would rather you argued it out, and please write in British English.
