I am a novelist in the middle of a book about a small circle of friends who once studied mathematics together and have since scattered into very different lives, and I have reached a scene that has defeated three drafts. Two of them—in the manuscript they are Ines and Voss—meet for the first time in years on the terrace of a house above a river, and the whole scene is one long argument about the infinite future. Ines has walked away from a promising career to work on what she calls the long-term flourishing of intelligent life; Voss, who stayed in mathematics, is convinced the whole project rests on a mistake about infinity. I want the scene to be a genuine contest rather than a showcase for whichever of them I privately agree with: each must be the equal of the other, each must answer the other's actual words, and neither may win by caricature. What I need from you is the dialogue itself—several exchanges, the argument moving through real objections and replies rather than restating positions.

Let me set out what I have in mind for their views so that the scene has bones. Voss's contention is that once you grant that the future might contain infinitely many lives, the arithmetic that makes "more flourishing is better" meaningful simply breaks down: any two futures that are each infinitely good come out equal, or worse, come out however you please depending on how you arrange the terms, so the injunction to maximize over the far future is either empty or arbitrary. Ines should not be a fool about this; let her concede that ordinary summation collapses, and then argue that a more careful comparison survives—for instance, that two infinite futures can be ranked by comparing them across every sufficiently long finite stretch, so that a future which is better in each such stretch and never worse is genuinely better, and that only the shallow disagreements (one ahead early, the other later) deserve to be called ties. Let Voss press her on exactly where this rule bites: what happens when the two futures trade the lead infinitely often, or when the stretches can be ordered in more than one way, or when the difference between the futures is nothing but a finite head start that later washes out. Let Ines press him in turn on whether his skepticism proves too much—whether it would equally dissolve the ordinary comparisons he makes about his own life, and whether a refusal to rank two futures is not itself a kind of ranking, one with consequences. Give one of them a concrete decision to worry at (a bequest, a policy, a choice about where to spend her one life) so the abstractions stay tethered, and end with the argument unresolved but sharper than it began.

Have them reason it out entirely in their own words. I don't want the scene to lean on any particular published result or famous name, since the two of them are inventing the argument in front of each other and I want the reader to feel it being built rather than cited. Keep the register conversational but exact—these are people who think well and care about getting it right—and let each of them land at least one blow the other cannot simply wave away.
