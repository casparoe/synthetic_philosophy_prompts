My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Douglas Hofstadter's account of creativity as variation on a theme, and I'd like an outside verdict rather than another round of the same fight. Here is his summary of it, more or less as he put it: "Hofstadter's central claim is that creativity is just knob-twiddling. Every creative act is a variation on something that already exists — you take a theme, or a style, or an idea, and you turn the knobs that define it, the key or the tempo or the instrumentation or the wording, up or down. Nothing comes from nothing, so the new is always a recombination of the old. And he means this as a computational model, not just a metaphor: a creative mind is a program that perturbs the parameters of an existing concept-space and then filters the output for novelty, which is roughly what he was trying to build in his analogy-making programs. It follows that how creative a variation is comes down to how far it travels from the original, and that radical novelty is an illusion, because the space of possible variations is fixed in advance by the theme you start from."

I think he's got hold of something real but I don't trust the shape he's given it, and I can't articulate why. So: go through the summary and tell me what Hofstadter would cheerfully accept, what he would wince at, and what he would flatly deny — separating a fair simplification from a genuine misreading. Pay particular attention to whether the closing claim, that radical novelty is an illusion because the space of variations is fixed in advance, actually follows from the parts of the summary that are right, or whether it only follows from the parts that are wrong. And then, rather than laying out the interpretive options and leaving the choice to me, commit: tell me which of us is closer to the truth, where you would plant your flag on the substantive question of whether creativity is variation all the way down, and what the strongest consideration is that could move you off it.
