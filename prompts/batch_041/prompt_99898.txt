I run the research side of a small county historical society — two paid staff, a dozen volunteers, a quarterly journal, and exhibit labels for the little museum. Nobody on the team has any training in historiography, we're just people who like local history, and at this point we're deciding what to accept, reject, or hedge by vibes, which means whoever is loudest in the room, or whoever donated the display case, usually wins.

What I want from you is a procedure we can actually run, in an afternoon, on a claim about something that happened here before about 1900. A checklist in order: what to ask first, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, when the procedure just stops with a no, and where it stops being a question about evidence at all and turns into a question about what we want to be true. It has to work on the kinds of sources we actually have, not the kinds in the textbooks: a county history from 1902 written by a man whose own family is in the story, a shoebox of letters with no dates and no envelopes, an obituary that may have been written by the deceased's daughter, a photograph someone insists is from 1873 because that's what's penciled on the back, an oral tradition that's been recited at the fair every year since 1930, and a single old newspaper clipping that is the only thing in existence about a whole event.

Two things I'd like it to be honest about. First, our sources mostly disagree with each other, or there's only one of them — a huge share of what we deal with is a single document. So when is one source enough? When does silence in the record count as evidence that something didn't happen, versus just evidence that nobody bothered to write it down? And does a source being biased — a booster's history, a family's own account, a rival town's newspaper — make it useless, or does it depend on which way the bias pushes, so that a hostile source admitting something is worth more than a friendly one? I don't know how to think about any of that and I suspect there's a way.

Second, several of these claims matter a lot to living people. Whether the town founder did something terrible to a neighboring settlement. Whether a certain house was a stop on the Underground Railroad. Whether a family's land was taken unfairly in the 1840s. Our board cares about feelings and I get it, but I want the procedure to keep three things seperate: what the evidence supports, what we'd like to be true, and what we might decide to say anyway for other reasons. And I want it to tell me which of those calls is mine to make and which ones have to go to the board, because right now everything goes to the board and it's a mess.

Then run the procedure on two of these cases and show me the whole thing working. One: the Underground Railroad house, where all we have is the 1930 fair tradition plus a cellar with a false wall. Two: the founder, where the only source is the 1902 county history, written by a descendant, who mentions the event in passing while defending the family. I'd like at least one of those to come out in a way I won't like, so I can see the procedure isn't just going to ratify whatever we were already going to say. And be straight with me about what the procedure can't do — I don't want a fake algorithm that pretends everything is decidable, but I also don't want to be told it's a judgment call at every step, because then it's not a procedure.
