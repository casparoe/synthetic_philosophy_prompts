I'm the TA for an upper-level seminar on the ethics of war (mostly seniors, a handful of MA students) and I got stuck writing the final. The professor wants one in-class essay, 45 minutes, 20 marks, and she's tired of reading the same paper over and over where students retell the Walzer-versus-revisionist fight about the moral equality of combatants and then declare a winner. So I want a question that makes them do surgery on the arguments instead of reciting them.

Here's the twist I want to build it around, and I'd rather you use this than invent your own, because I've already workshopped it with the professor. The setup is a hypothetical war, so nobody can wriggle out with "in real wars nobody ever knows who's right." Imagine two states sliding toward war, and imagine that some neutral body -- an internationally trusted monitoring outfit with a verifiable public record, whatever -- has settled the jus ad bellum question beyond any doubt, and both sides' soldiers know the verdict well before the shooting starts. Everyone on the aggressor side knows their side's cause is unjust; everyone on the other side knows theirs is just. No deception, no fog of war, no reasonable doubt. Now the question I want to ask is what's left of combatant equality under that stipulation, and the thing I really want them to untangle is which of the usual defenses of that equality actually lean on combatants' ignorance (and so die once you stipulate the oracle) versus which are supposed to survive it, and whether the survivors really do. I also want them to keep the permission side separate from the liability side -- whether it's still permissible to kill and be killed, as opposed to who deserves what afterwards -- and to notice that the stipulation takes away some excuses but not others, since an aggressor conscript with a rifle at his back still has a story to tell even if he knows perfectly well the war is unjust. And I want a paragraph at the end about whether the legal practice of treating both sides' soldiers alike under the laws of war can still be justified once the moral claim is gone, or whether that practice was only ever a pragmatic convention anyway.

Could you give me (a) the exam question itself, worded so a student knows exactly what's being asked without me handing them the argument, (b) a model answer of about 700 words, and (c) a marking scheme with five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, the marks for each, a one-line note on how students typically botch each one, and a closing sentence on what seperates an A from a B. Please have the model answer argue rather than cite -- no footnotes, no bibliography, and the marking scheme shouldn't give credit for name-dropping, because half of them will just write "McMahan says X" and stop. Write the whole thing in American English, please, the professor is picky about defense vs. defence and won't recieve anything with British spellings.
