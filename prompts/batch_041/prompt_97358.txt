You are a philosopher of psychology whose research sits at the intersection of virtue ethics and the empirical study of personality and social behavior. Your task is to write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors on the bearing of the situationist critique on virtue ethics. Before writing, brainstorm the strongest version of each position so that neither becomes a strawman, then draft the exchange.

The two speakers are:

- A virtue ethicist who defends a broadly Aristotelian picture but is sophisticated about what it claims — she does not assert that virtue ethics is a predictive theory of behavior, and she is willing to grant empirical findings.
- A situationist critic who is fair-minded and well informed — he is not out to caricature virtue ethics, and he must engage with the ethicist's best responses rather than talking past them.

Treat the following as common ground that both speakers already accept; neither needs to discover or defend it, and the dialogue should presuppose it without belaboring it:

- The person–situation debate in personality psychology, and the proposal that behavior is better summarized by "if-then" situation–behavior signatures than by global traits.
- The idea of situational strength: situations differ in how strongly they constrain behavior, so weak situations reveal individual differences while strong ones suppress them.
- The aggregation point: stable individual differences often emerge only when behavior is averaged across many situations, not in any single situation taken alone.
- The replication crisis: several of the classic demonstrations invoked by situationists have been challenged or have failed to replicate, and the effect sizes reported in the literature are generally smaller than originally claimed.
- The distinction between globalism and localism about traits — whether character traits cut across situations or are tied to particular situation-types.

The dialogue should turn on the following questions, and the speakers should press each other on them rather than merely stating positions:

1. Does virtue ethics require cross-situational consistency of behavior at all, or is its central claim normative and explanatory rather than predictive? If the latter, is the situationist critique aimed at a target the virtue ethicist never had to defend?
2. Does the aggregation point help or hurt virtue ethics? The critic should argue that recovering consistency only by averaging across many situations concedes the situationist's case, since virtue is supposed to be robust in the single case; the ethicist should have a real reply, not a concession.
3. Is situational strength itself a morally loaded notion — does calling a situation "strong" already smuggle in an evaluation of the behavior it produces?
4. What, if anything, would count as evidence that a person acted from virtue rather than from situational pressure, given that both speakers accept the replication problems?

Requirements for the dialogue: it must contain genuine back-and-forth, in which each speaker responds to the other's actual points and revises or sharpens their position under pressure. At least once, one speaker should introduce a move the other has not anticipated, and the other should adapt rather than repeat an earlier line. Do not let the exchange end in a tidy resolution; it is fine — and preferable — for each to end with a clearer view of what the disagreement really turns on.

After the dialogue, add a short separate note (clearly marked as outside the dialogue) that distinguishes what is settled among people who study this material from what is genuinely contested, and that flags any point at which the dialogue leans on a contested reading of the empirical or philosophical literature. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
