Bit of an odd ask, but here goes. I'm a retired chemist — 35 years in the lab, mostly reaction kinetics — and since I packed it in I've been mainlining philosophy books, including a friend's copy of Wittgenstein's On Certainty, which I honestly can't decide is profound or just a clever way of having it both ways. The trouble is I've ended up with five views that each look fine on their own and I can't tell whether they hang togehter as a package.

(1) I'm a Bayesian about belief: believing something is just having a credence in it, and I never give anything a 0 or a 1, because those are absorbing states — once you're there no evidence can ever move you, and that strikes me as a bad place for a scientist to be. (2) At the same time there are things I'm certain of in a way that isn't a high credence, it's a different category altogether: that there's a world outside my head, that my memory isn't quietly making things up, that the bench in front of me won't turn into a swan when I look away, that tomorrow will keep resembling today. Doubting those isn't a live option for me — I wouldn't know how to start, and if I did I don't think anything could count as evidence either way. (3) My methodological creed, drilled in over a whole career: a claim you can't test doesn't get to be a claim, and untestable 'certainties' are exactly the dogmas science was invented to clear out. (I'm told the uniformity-of-nature bit in (2) can't be tested without going in a circle; I've never known whehter to worry about that.) (4) On disagreement: when someone who knows as much as I do about a thing disagrees with me, I should take it seriously and move my credence toward theirs — that's just what the arithmetic says. (5) And history has taught me to be suspicious of certainties: the fixed stars, the unchanging species, the ether — every era's obvious background assumptions became the next era's punchline, so I try to hold even my firmest views loosely. I gather the picture from the Wittgenstein is that some things are the riverbed and others the water, and the riverbed does eventually shift, so I feel like (5) and (2) ought to be reconcilable, though I can't see exactly how.

What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and for the real clashes: how bad is it, and what's the cheapest thing to give up and what does giving it up cost me? Please rank the clashes by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad — and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
