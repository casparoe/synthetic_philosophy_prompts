I have been trying to sort out an argument about de re modality — about what particular individuals could or could not have been — and I would like a careful diagnosis of it. Let me set out the background first, so that nothing turns on terms of art.

The argument's target is counterpart theory, the view developed by David Lewis, on which no individual exists in more than one possible world. On this view, when we say of a particular individual that she could have been thus-and-so, we are not describing her as being thus-and-so in some other world. Rather, we are saying that she has a counterpart — a distinct individual, in another world, related to her by a relation of similarity — who is thus-and-so there. The rival view, transworld identity, holds instead that the very same individual exists in more than one world, so that her modal properties are properties she literally has in those worlds.

A colleague offered the following argument and treats it as decisive. I have reconstructed it as faithfully as I can.

(1) When I assert that I could have been a musician, my assertion is about me: it takes me as its subject and predicates something of me.
(2) Counterpart theory entails that no individual exists in more than one world, and therefore that I exist in no world other than this one.
(3) So the truth of (1) cannot consist in my being a musician in some other world, since I am in no other world.
(4) On counterpart theory, what makes (1) true is instead that a distinct individual — my counterpart — is a musician in another world.
(5) So the fact that makes (1) true is a fact about my counterpart, and not a fact about me.
(6) If a claim predicates something of a particular individual, then whatever makes it true is a fact about that individual.
(7) By (5) and (6), (1) is not made true by any fact about me.
(8) So (1) is not true; all that is true is a corresponding claim about my counterpart.
(9) So when I say that I could have been a musician, I am not in fact saying anything about myself.
(10) The same reasoning applies to any individual and to any claim about what that individual could have been or could have done.
(11) So no de re modal claim is about the individual it appears to name; de re modality is an illusion.

I cannot accept (11), and I doubt (9) — surely when I say I could have been a musician, I am talking about myself. Notice that the conclusion is far stronger than a refutation of counterpart theory; it denies de re modality altogether, which is part of why I distrust it. Yet steps (1) through (8) each strike me as either obviously true or a plain consequence of the step immediately before. I would like to keep (1), and I am not eager to abandon counterpart theory, but I am not eager to retreat to transworld identity either, since that view has its own difficulties about how one identifies an individual across worlds.

Where does the argument go wrong? If some step is invalid, identify it and explain why it looked valid. If instead the argument is valid and one of the claims in (1)–(6) has to be given up, say which is least costly to abandon, what abandoning it commits me to, and what the defender of transworld identity would have to supply in its place. Please write in American English.
