I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or decision theory, and I have been stuck on the following puzzle. I will state it from scratch, so that the statement itself and not any version of it I may have encountered is what we are arguing about.

A driver is on a road with two exits. Nothing she can observe distinguishes the first exit from the second, and she cannot remember whether she has already passed an exit. At each exit she must choose between exiting and continuing. If she exits at the first exit she collects $0. If she continues past the first exit and exits at the second she collects $100. If she continues past both exits she collects $0. She can always tell when she is at an exit, she wants to maximize the expected amount she collects, and she has no other source of information about where she is.

A colleague offers the following argument that no behavior available to her is worth more than $0.

(1) At the moment of choice her information is the same at the two exits: she is at an exit, and she does not know which.
(2) A decision can depend only on the information available when it is made. So whatever she decides at one exit, she decides at the other.
(3) At an exit there are only two options, exiting and continuing.
(4) If she exits, she leaves at the first exit she reaches and collects $0.
(5) If she continues, she continues at both exits and collects $0.
(6) Her choice is therefore either the one in (4) or the one in (5), and both pay $0.
(7) So no behavior available to her pays more than $0, and the puzzle has no content beyond a trick about forgetting.

I tried the obvious escape, that she may randomize, and it does not obviously help. If she flips a fair coin before setting out and follows the rule exit if and only if the coin comes up heads, then with probability one half she leaves at the first exit for $0 and with probability one half she passes both exits for $0, for an expected $0, exactly as the argument says. But if she is modeled as a controller that flips a fresh fair coin each time it arrives at an exit and exits on heads, then she collects $100 exactly when the first coin comes up tails and the second comes up heads, an event of probability one quarter, for an expected $25. These two calculations cannot both be right, and I cannot see which of them misuses the idea of a choice, nor how a randomized choice should be treated in a case where the agent cannot tell two of its own decision points apart.

What I want from you. Identify the step of the argument that fails, if there is one, and explain why that step reads like a restatement of the setup rather than a substantive claim. Then tell me what rejecting it costs. Specifically: is the value of the puzzle $25, $0, or something that depends on a detail the puzzle leaves unstated, such as what randomizing equipment the driver carries; what must a decision rule for her look like if $25 is the answer; and what general principle about choice under uncertainty do I have to give up in order to reject the step you name. If instead the argument is sound, tell me so, tell me which step to surrender, and tell me what I lose by surrendering it. I would prefer to keep steps (1) and (2), which seem to me to do nothing more than describe her situation, and I would prefer not to give up the calculation that yields $25, but I do not see how to have both. No citations are needed; I care only about the reasoning.
