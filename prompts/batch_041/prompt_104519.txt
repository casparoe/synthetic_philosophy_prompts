I'm in a philosophy of religion seminar and next week it's my turn to present on David Lewis's paper about penal substitution — the one where he imagines a guy who's been justly sentenced and his devoted innocent friend volunteers to recieve the punishment instead, and asks whether we'd think justice had been done. Lewis says our legal system would never allow this, and that this shows we don't actually believe guilt can be shifted from one person to another, so we shouldn't believe it in theology either. I'm going to defend a version of Lewis's argument but with a twist: I think the legal-practice argument only rules out a certain retributive picture, and the real target is the Trinity move — where the substitute is supposedly the same person as the judge, and through the incarnation also the offender — not penal substitution as such. I also want to use a distinction I came up with: between guilt as a status you have (you did it, you're liable) and guilt as a burden you bear (the suffering, the alienation, the shame). I think Lewis only shows we don't believe the status is transferable; the burden might be. And I want to test that against the debt analogy — we let someone else pay your debt, so why not your punishment? My professor is a retributivist about punishment and will definately push on whether our refusal to allow volunteer substitutes is really about the metaphysics of guilt or just about procedural safeguards and abuse risk. There are also two Reformed theology students in the class who will defend penal substitution, one of them knows the contemporary literature on it, and a couple of secular people who think the whole doctrine is obviously barbaric and won't see why we're taking it seriously.

Give me the Q&A prep sheet. For each, a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Also flag any objection where I should just concede rather than fight, and tell me which of my own moves is the weakest and most likely to get picked apart in the room.
