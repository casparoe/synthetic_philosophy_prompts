I am a secondary-school student writing an essay on privacy, and rather than begin from a theory I have been working through cases and writing down my honest reactions, in the hope that someone can tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. My reactions are as follows.

My mother reads the messages on my phone while I am out; I had not locked it. I think this is a violation, but a mild one: I would be annoyed rather than wronged. A man on the train reads the same messages over my shoulder. This is plainly a violation, and a worse one than my mother's, even though he learns no more than she does. My closest friend has known my password for years and has never looked; I do not think that is a violation at all, though I would feel differently about the friendship if I learnt that they had looked repeatedly.

A friend tells a secret of mine to one other person, someone who knows us both. I think that is a serious violation. The same friend posts it anonymously on a forum where nobody knows me. Fewer people who matter now know it, and yet I think that is much less bad. A music service works out from my listening that I am probably pregnant before my family knows, and no human ever sees the conclusion. This troubles me more than the man on the train, although nobody knows anything and nothing follows from it.

Now two cases that I cannot separate in the way I would like. Suppose there were a machine that could read my mind and understand it, but could not record anything, tell anyone, or act on what it found, and I would never know it had happened. I think that is still a violation, and I cannot say what has been harmed. Suppose instead a company records everything I do online and keeps it for ever, but is genuinely incapable of using any of it against me — no leaks, no decisions about me, no human ever looking. That bothers me less than the machine, though it gathers vastly more.

Two more. My school puts the full marks list on the noticeboard; I never chose who would see my marks, and I do not think that is a violation. And my cousin's DNA test reveals something about my own health risks, which she then shares with the family; I feel wronged, even though she gave the sample and I gave nothing.

The principle I would propose is this: a privacy violation occurs when information about me reaches someone I did not choose to give it to. I can already see that it fails on the noticeboard, where I chose nothing, and on the mind-reading machine, where nothing reaches anyone. I have also begun to suspect that the real distinction is between being known and being made usable, since the company makes me slightly usable and the machine only knows me, yet I feel worse about the machine — which suggests the distinction has my verdicts the wrong way round.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of them, that captures the pattern, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in which case. I would like to know what it predicts for at least two further cases of your own choosing, ideally ones that pull in opposite directions, and which of my reactions it forces me to give up. If the only principle that saves every verdict is so qualified as to say nothing, I would rather be told that plainly than have a clause invented to rescue my intuition. Someone will no doubt tell me that this is all a matter of having nothing to hide; I would like to know what that slogan would have to mean in order to be true of these cases, and where it fails.

Please write the answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — just a letter to me would be ideal.
