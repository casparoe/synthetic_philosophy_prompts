Two people I work with, Dev and Nadia, got into an argument over an interpretability experiment and they've asked me to referee. I can't, so I'm handing it to you. Setup first, in plain terms, because the details matter.

They took a language model and a few thousand factual statements, half true and half false, where they knew the ground truth themselves. They trained a simple linear probe on the model's internal activations to predict, from the activations alone, whether the statement is true or false. It does well on statements held out from the same pool. Then the part they actually care about: on questions where the model gives the wrong answer, the probe still points at the right one. And if you just tell the model it got that one wrong and ask again, it often switches to the answer the probe was pointing at. Dev says this shows the model knows the truth and is failing to report what it knows. Nadia says it shows nothing of the sort. Here's how it went.

Dev: the probe generalizes outside the pool it was trained on, even to a different subject area, and the answer is sitting right there in the activations. And the re-prompting result settles it, because the model produces the right answer without being given any new information. If it can produce it, it had it. Refusing to call that knowledge is just refusing to use the word.

Nadia: producing it after you tell it it's wrong isn't the same as having believed it all along. A model that's simply bad at the task will also flip toward the more statistically expected answer once you nudge it. And the probe isn't neutral -- you trained it on the true/false labels, so it found a direction that separates your labels, and that direction can be a proxy, like how typical or expected the statement is, rather than truth. You built the answer key into the search.

Dev: by that standard no probe could ever show anything, since every probe is trained on some labels. The question isn't whether labels were used, it's whether the signal predicts things it wasn't trained on. It does -- it beats the model's own output on questions the probe never saw, in a domain it never saw. And the causal test is what really kills you: push on the probe's direction and the answer changes. That's not a correlation, that's the model using that representation to produce its answer.

Nadia: the causal test shows the direction is load-bearing for the output. It doesn't show the direction carries the truth. Push on a direction for "sounds confident" and you'd change answers too. And the cross-domain result cuts the other way -- a domain-general direction is more likely to be something like plausibility or familiarity, and those come apart from truth exactly on the hard cases, which are the only cases you're looking at. You selected the sample where the model's output and the truth diverge, so of course some other signal lines up with truth there.

Dev: then what would convince you? Every test I can think of is either a correlation or an intervention, and you've found a way to redescribe both. If no possible experiment counts, then "the model knows but won't say" isn't a claim about the model at all.

Nadia: what would convince me is a case where the signal can't be read off the input -- where truth and plausibility point in opposite directions, the probe still gets it right, and the model still can't say it until you intervene on the probe itself. If the result survives that, fine, I'll believe you. If it doesn't, you've got a plausibility detector with a nice name.

I want to know which parts of this are actually settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested -- definately don't hand me a contested position as if it were the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question back at me.
