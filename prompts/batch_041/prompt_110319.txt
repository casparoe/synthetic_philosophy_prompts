I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to twenty years, and I have finally talked my way into running a session at a weekend workshop: about twenty-four adults, mixed backgrounds, no philosophy training assumed, and several of them the sort of people who read the same things I do and will be watching closely to see whether the thing actually works. I have ninety minutes, one room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, and index cards. I want an activity that makes the group feel the force of a distinction I think is badly under-appreciated, and I would much rather they discover it than be told it.

The distinction: an epistemic bubble is a group that is simply missing the relevant evidence — the other side is absent from the room. An echo chamber is a group that has the evidence in front of it and has, in advance, been given reason to discount it. From the outside the two are indistinguishable, since both hold the same false belief, but the remedies diverge sharply. Supplying the missing evidence dissolves a bubble; applied to an echo chamber it does nothing, or makes things worse. What I want them to feel is something more specific than that. In the second case the outside testimony is not weighed and found wanting; it is routed around. The members do not reject the argument so much as never process it, because their trust has already been relocated to a source that has issued a verdict on the argument's kind.

So design me a runnable game. I care about the following constraints more than about the mechanics.

The discrediting has to be endogenous. Do not hand down a rule announcing that one team is untrustworthy; arrange incentives or a procedure — a public credibility ledger, a coherence bonus, a citation tax, whatever you judge to work — so that the participants themselves generate the discounting, and can be shown afterwards that they did. It should also be true, and visible in the debrief, that a reasonable person in their position might have done the same thing.

Nobody should be able to leave with the comfortable diagnosis that they were merely stubborn or merely gullible. The outside source should be genuinely flawed in some minor respect, so that the correct response is a modest update; the interesting quantity is the gap between the update owed and the update performed.

The other side should be physically present, ideally as real participants rather than a voice on a card, so that the failure is visibly not one of exposure: they hear the argument in full and do not move. At some point before any reveal, have everyone write down what would change their minds and what they currently think of the other side's competence, so that the debrief can use their own handwriting against them. No real money, no advance preparation, nothing requiring a second facilitator. The cleanest structure I can imagine is two rounds with identical evidence geometry, one without the trust machinery and one with it, but use that only if it is actually the best option.

Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, whatever roles you need, and the debrief, including which moment in the game maps to which part of the distinction. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to salvage the session when it does. And be honest about what this cannot reproduce — I assume it cannot give them the years of slow accretion, or the fact that real members of an echo chamber are not sitting in a game but living inside the thing — and tell me plainly what I have to supply myself.
