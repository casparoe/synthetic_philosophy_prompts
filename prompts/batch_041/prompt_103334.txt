Last night a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about a puzzle I had always assumed to be settled, and I came away convinced that the way it is usually put conceals the one thing that makes it interesting. Here is the case we fought over. You are somewhere in a territory that a road divides into a Red half and a Blue half, and that a river divides into an A half and a not-A half, so that there are four quadrants. You begin with your credences spread evenly across the four. A spotter on a ridge, whom you trust to say only true things, radios you a single sentence: "If you are in Red, you are in A." My friend said the update is obvious — strike out the Red-and-not-A quadrant and rescale, leaving a third of your credence on each of the three remaining quadrants. I said the sentence on its own fixes nothing, because what it tells you depends entirely on how the spotter decided which conditional to utter: a spotter who says something about Red only when you are in fact in Red, and a spotter who says something about Red only when Red is false and the conditional is therefore vacuous, can send you those identical words and leave you with quite different credences. We never settled it.

I run a graduate seminar in formal epistemology — fourteen students, a single ninety-minute session — and I want an in-class activity that lets them feel the force of both positions in their own hands rather than hearing either from me. The raw material I want it built around is exactly the case above: the four quadrants, the uniform prior, and the one radioed sentence, together with the fact that a spotter's selection rule — which true conditional she chooses to send — can be varied while the sentence stays fixed. I would like the activity to make the resulting posteriors visibly diverge. Four rules are worth staging. One spotter prefers conditionals whose antecedent is true, so that hearing the sentence means you are in Red-and-A. One prefers conditionals whose antecedent is false, so that hearing it means you are in Blue. One chooses at random among whichever conditionals happen to be true, which, because of vacuity, does not yield my friend's flat thirds but rather puts half your credence on Blue-and-not-A. And one utters the sentence with a fixed probability wherever it is true, which is the only one of the four that reproduces the flat thirds. I am confident about these posteriors but would like them checked and made explicit in the write-up.

Please design the activity: the roles or rules, the materials, the payoff scheme (no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the sequence by which the problem surfaces before anyone names it. The participants should place bets, or otherwise commit, while they know only the sentence; the spotter's protocol should be revealed only afterwards, and the same sentence should recur under different protocols so that the swing in their own commitments does the teaching. The debrief should map specific moments of the activity onto the underlying point — that conditionalization is not defined until you have said what the message is evidence *for*, i.e. until you have fixed the likelihood, and that a conditional sentence is asymmetric and underdetermines the update. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself.

Constraints: no lecture up front, and the students should not hear the phrase "updating on a conditional" or any of the standard labels until the debrief; the activity should require no reading, and the write-up should cite nothing; the payoff scheme should be a proper scoring rule or otherwise incentive-compatible, so that betting one's honest credences is optimal; and the numbers should be worked out rather than gestured at.
