I have no academic training in philosophy or in computer science, but I have spent several years reading and thinking on my own about the question of whether the brain is a computer, and I would like your help in working out what Gottfried Leibniz would have said about it. He died in 1716, long before the question could be posed in anything like its modern form, so this is necessarily a reconstruction rather than a report, and I am not asking you to survey what later commentators have claimed on his behalf; I want your own best-supported prediction.

To keep the question from sliding around, let me separate three claims that people tend to run together. The first is modest: that mental activity can be usefully described and predicted as a computation, so that a computational vocabulary earns its keep as a level of explanation even if nothing in the head resembles a machine. The second is strong: that the brain literally is a computer, meaning a physical device with something like a stored program and a general-purpose, reprogrammable architecture of the sort engineers build. The third is a worry that seems to cut against both: that almost any physical system can be described as implementing some computation or other, so that saying the brain computes may be true but empty unless we can also say what does not compute.

Here is the background about Leibniz that I take to be relevant. He built a calculating machine, and he dreamed of a universal symbolic language in which disputants could settle their disagreements by calculation rather than by argument. Yet he also insisted that no arrangement of mechanical parts, however intricate, could account for the fact that something is perceived or felt; he asked us to imagine a machine enlarged until we could walk inside it and inspect its gears, and claimed that we would find only parts pushing on parts, never anything that explains a perception. And on his metaphysics, the ultimate constituents of reality are not machines at all, but simple, indivisible things whose inner life is not composed of parts.

Please predict, as specifically as you can, what Leibniz's positions would have been on each of the three claims. Answer in three parts, one for each claim, followed by a short closing section.

Where would he draw the line between what a machine can do and what it cannot, and would that line fall exactly where he himself placed it, or would the modern notion of computation have pushed him to move it? What would he have made of the triviality worry: would he have accepted that any system can be described as computing something, and would that have led him to reject the strong claim outright, or instead to insist that computation be understood in a restrictive way? Would he have regarded his dream of settling disputes by calculation as continuous with the claim that the brain is a computer, or as a different project that the modern question wrongly conflates? And in your closing section, state what you take to be the strongest reason to doubt your own prediction, some commitment of Leibniz's that cuts against the reading you have given, and say how you would resolve the tension.
