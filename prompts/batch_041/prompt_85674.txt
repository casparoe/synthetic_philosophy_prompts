You are a philosopher of practical reason and value with a specialist's command of idealization-based accounts of reasons and well-being. Think carefully and at length before writing: first work out what the view I describe is minimally committed to, then survey the space of objections and sort them by how much they would cost me, then draft the response. Do not pad the answer with a survey of the literature; I want the structure of the position's exposure.

I have defended a version of the idealized-preference account of reasons, in the family of coherent-extrapolation views: an agent has a reason to φ just in case she would want to φ under full information and ideal deliberation, where ideal deliberation is specified procedurally — coherence among her attitudes, no inferential error, vivid appreciation of the relevant non-normative facts — and not by any substantive list of what is worth wanting. I want a map of what this position risks. What would count against it? Which cases, arguments, or results should move me, which would the view's own defenders concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about what would have to be true for each to bite.

I want four things addressed in particular. First, the worry that idealization changes the subject: the idealized agent's preferences are not the actual agent's, so at best they are evidence about her reasons rather than constitutive of them. Second, indeterminacy. Take a person raised in severe deprivation whose preferences are thoroughly adapted to scarcity, and suppose that as I idealize, her preferences fail to converge: adding information alone makes her repudiate her adapted life, adding coherence alone leaves it intact, and I can get either verdict by varying which idealization I privilege. Is the account then indeterminate, and does that make it vacuous as a criterion of reasons? Third, the case of an agent fully informed and perfectly coherent whose settled preferences are cruel. The procedural version appears committed to saying he has reason to be cruel; most of us treat that as a reductio, and the natural repair is to add a constraint. Can any such constraint stay procedural, or does it smuggle in substantive value — and if the latter, is my view just a substantive theory of the good wearing procedural dress? Fourth, whether the view risks anything at all: if every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by positing a further idealization or a better specification of the deliberative conditions, is it falsifiable, and is that a problem for a view of this kind?

Structure the response in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. Bottom line — 2–3 sentences, stating up front how exposed the view is.
2. The commitments — one paragraph fixing the minimal claims the view cannot give up without changing its identity.
3. The defeaters, ranked — the bulk of the response, roughly 600–700 words, each defeater paired with what would have to be true for it to count and with what a defender would concede.
4. What would be decisive — the observations or arguments that, if they obtained, would settle the matter, and an honest note on whether any are currently available.
5. The insulation check — 100–150 words on falsifiability, and whether insulation is a defect here or a mark of a view operating at the right level of abstraction.
6. What would move me — 100 words, including whether the consideration I am most inclined to treat as decisive really is a good reason to be moved.
