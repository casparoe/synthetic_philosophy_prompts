My friends and I run a small housing co-op, and we have a constitution we can amend by vote. Last month we did something that's been bugging me ever since. We used the old amendment procedure -- the one that needed a two-thirds vote of everyone present -- to replace the amendment procedure itself. The new procedure requires unanimity of all members, present or not, before any further change. The vote to adopt it got about 70 percent, so it passed fine under the old rules. But now a couple of people are saying the change was never valid: if the new rule is really in force, then the act that brought it into force didn't meet its own standard (we didn't have unanimous consent), and if the old rule is still in force, then... we changed it. So we're stuck with either an illegitimate rule governing everything, or a legitimate rule we somehow left behind. It's not just "who decides the deciders." I feel like there's a specific snag here about an amendment being judged by the very rule it creates, and the two verdicts can't both stand. Someone even suggested we add a clause saying the new rule can never be changed again, which I'm pretty sure makes it worse, but I can't say exactly why.

The weird thing is I've seen the same shape twice since. An online community I'm in rewrote its moderation rules using the old moderation rules, and now people argue about whether bans issued under the new regime are retroactively legitimate. And a friend who follows crypto stuff described basically the same fight in a DAO where the token-voting rules were themselves changed by token vote. So it seems like a general thing that happens whenever the rule about changing rules is itself up for change. Is there a name for this exact problem? I keep almost finding it -- there's something in legal theory about the ultimate rule that validates all the others, and something about self-reference paradoxes, but those feel like they're circling it rather than naming it. What I really want is: what is the thing, precisely, and what is it not? Does the problem only show up when the new rule is stricter than the old one (so the transition would fail its own test), or is it lurking in every case? And in practice people seem to just agree that the old rule governs the act of transition and the new rule governs everything after -- is that a recognized move, and does it actually dissolve the problem or just paper over it? Also, please be upfront about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who study it -- I don't want a contested view dressed up as the consensus.
