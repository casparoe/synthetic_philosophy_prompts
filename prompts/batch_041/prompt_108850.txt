I retired from a career in biochemistry a few years back and have been reading economics and philosophy of science ever since, and there's a knot in it I can't untangle. On one picture, market prices are supposed to be this remarkable information-processing machine -- the price of tin somehow encoding everything scattered across thousands of traders who never talk to each other. But the moment you take that seriously, odd things follow. If prices already reflect everyone's information, nobody has any reason to go out and gather costly information in the first place. And if everyone is rational and knows everyone else is rational, there seems to be no reason to trade at all -- yet trading floors are loud, and people do pay to learn things. I keep meeting these as isolated little paradoxes and I want the spec instead.

So before I read any more of this literature: tell me what any adequate theory of prices-as-information has to deliver. The cases it has to get right (trade actually happening, prices moving on no real news, the trader who pays to learn something, bubbles and crashes), the distinctions it can't blur (a price that reveals information vs. one that's correct, information aggregated vs. information produced, private vs. common knowledge), and the constraints it can't violate. Give the case behind each requirement, say which are non-negotiable and which are just taste, and -- the part I most want -- flag any pair that looks jointly unsatisfiable, because I suspect that's where the real content is hiding. Skip the scorecard of particular theories, I'll do that myself.
