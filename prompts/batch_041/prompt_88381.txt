I am presenting a paper at a departmental work-in-progress seminar next month and need a question-period preparation sheet.

The thesis I defend is a version of risk-weighted expected utility. Outcomes are ranked on a single cardinal value scale; the cumulative probability of receiving at least each successive outcome is passed through a nondecreasing risk function that fixes 0 at 0 and 1 at 1; and a prospect's value is the sum of the successive outcome increments weighted by those transformed cumulative probabilities. The upshot is that an agent may prefer a sure thing to a gamble of higher expected value even when her utility for the outcomes is linear, because on this view a risk attitude attaches to the shape of the whole distribution rather than to the curvature of utility at each outcome. Risk aversion, on this account, is global rather than local: it is not a matter of diminishing marginal utility, and it is present even when the stakes are small. The view therefore denies that every admissible risk attitude reduces to diminishing marginal utility, and it permits departures from the sure-thing principle. Because the risk function operates on increments of value, the framework presupposes a common scale on which all the outcomes of a prospect can be placed. My contribution is that the risk function is not a single property of the agent. I argue that it is indexed to a domain of value — money, health, personal attachment, the interests of strangers — so that one and the same agent may be risk-averse in one domain and risk-seeking in another, and I propose a bridging rule for gambles that trade a gain in one domain against a risk in another.

The talk is in a philosophy department, but the room is mixed and nobody there works on this exact question. Two are Savage-style decision theorists who construct representation theorems and treat subjective probability and utility as uniquely recoverable from preference; they will suspect my risk function of being a state-dependent utility in disguise. One is a formal epistemologist concerned with the interpretation of credence and with accuracy-style justifications of norms, who will want to know what licenses the risk function rather than merely representing it. One is a behavioral scientist who studies risk perception; she knows the psychometric literature on domain-specific risk attitudes and will say that my contribution formalizes what her field settled long ago. One works on dynamic choice and commitment and will run the sequential version of the objection — the agent who pays to have uncertainty resolved in one order rather than another, or who is pumped by her own later preferences. And one is the department's standing skeptic about risk aversion, who holds that repeated gambles wash out and that the only defensible ground for refusing a fair bet is risk of ruin or diminishing marginal utility.

Tell me which two objections to concede ground on before the question period begins, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive, and which objection I am hoping nobody asks. Assume I cannot look anything up in the room, so keep the replies self-contained: I want arguments, not references, and no reading suggestions.
