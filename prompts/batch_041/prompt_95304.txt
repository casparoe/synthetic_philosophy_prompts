I'm TA-ing the discussion section of a rational choice course and I've got a 90-minute slot to fill, so I'm screening 12 Angry Men and running the discussion around it. I want the reading to be about information cascades and rational herding specifically, not the usual "peer pressure is bad, one brave man stood alone against the mob" thing that every undergrad writes.

Here's the model I want you to keep in view (just the mechanism, no need to name papers or dates): a line of decision-makers each act in turn, each one has some private signal about the truth, and each sees what the people before them chose but not why they chose it. If the first few choices go the same way, their revealed choices can outweigh any single later person's private information, so a rational Bayesian stops using his own signal and just follows. And once that happens the public record stops getting better, because nobody's private information is entering it anymore. Cascades start on very little, they're fragile (a small new public signal can break one), and the people who start them don't internalize the value of their own information to everybody downstream, which is why there's usually too little early experimentation.

Now read the film through that lens. Things I want you to hit:

- The first vote and the secret ballot. As I remember it, Juror 8 is alone on 11-1 guilty, and the thing that actually breaks the logjam is a secret ballot, which surfaces a second not-guilty vote that nobody could have predicted. That looks like exactly the mechanism the model cares about: a simultaneous secret ballot lets private signals come out without a cascade forming, whereas a public sequential vote can't. Say whether that's right, and how much of the first act is really this.
- The order in which people flip. Who's updating on evidence, who's updating on the tally, and who's just reacting to having been talked to. Is the film's sequence even a cascade in the technical sense, given that in the model nobody ever gives reasons and in the film reasons are the entire content of the deliberation? If what actually changes minds is new public arguement about the knife, the train noise, the old man's walk to the door, the woman's glasses, then is this a cascade story at all, or a different one about deliberation aggregating information in a way the sequential-choice model literally can't represent?
- Juror 8. The film wants him to be the hero. In the model, somebody who defies a cascade on thin private information is just making an error, and here a costly one (a death sentence). Does the film earn his dissent, or does it cheat by making his hunches turn out right? And notice he doesn't have a rival theory of the case for a long stretch -- he wants to talk, he's holding out on the standard of proof rather than on evidence. Is that a rational strategy, or a kind of reason the model has no room for?
- Where the film resists the reading. This jury is definately not a set of interchangeable Bayesian agents: one wants to get to a baseball game, one is a bigot, one has a son he can't talk to, one is a facts-and-order stockbroker, one is an immigrant watchmaker taking the civic duty very seriously. Some flips are information, some are fatigue, some are shame, some are one man's private life leaking into a murder case. Cascade theory assumes the motives are all informational. What does the film show about a herd with mixed motives, and does that matter for the practical question of how you break one?
- The institution. Unanimity, a secret ballot available on request, a hot room you can't leave. The film's implicit claim seems to be that the procedure is what stands between a cascade and a wrongful execution. Is that defensible given the model, or is unanimity also just a way of handing one stubborn man a veto?

End with what the film adds that the model doesn't have. I don't want a summary of the theory with scenes pasted onto it -- I want both the film and the theory taken seriously, and if the film's picture of how a herd breaks is actually different from the theory's, say so plainly. My students have had one week of Bayes and nothing else, so don't lean on any formal literature, but don't dumb the arguement down either.
