I'm a journalist — I write long features about science and ideas plus a newsletter, and I've been circling a piece for a few months on where large language models sit on Dennett's little ladder of creatures. You know the one: Darwinian (born hard-wired, no learning), Skinnerian (learns by reinforcement), Popperian (has an inner model it can run offline, so the bad guess dies inside it instead of killing it), Gregorian (picks up mind-tools — words, numbers, procedures — from other creatures, which is where the human weirdness comes from). I'm fairly sure it's named after a psychologist and not a pope, though I keep meaning to check.

The problem is the piece keeps getting away from me because every time I try to say something confident about where the models sit, I trip over my own commitments about the ladder. So before I file anything I want a consistency check on me, not on them.

You're a philosopher of mind who knows this material cold — Dennett, but also the selectionist and representationalist literature around him — and I want you to be a hard audience. Please don't reassure me, and please don't tell me that every pair of things I believe is in some vague "tension." I'm paying you to discriminate.

Here's where I've landed, as plainly as I can put it:

1. The four categories are cumulative. Each one adds a trick to the one below it, so a Popperian creature is also a Skinnerian creature and a Darwinian one.

2. What makes a creature Popperian, in the strict sense I actually care about, is the offline inner model: a representation of the world that's decoupled from whatever is hitting its senses right now. That's the thing Skinnerian creatures don't have.

3. Skinnerian creatures are pure reinforcement learners. No inner model, no representations in any interesting sense — just associations between stimuli, responses, and rewards, tuned by what happens next.

4. Natural selection is itself a Popperian process, just run at the level of the lineage: the variants are the hypotheses and the wrong ones get eliminated by the organism dying. So Darwinian creatures are already Popperian, at the level of the lineage rather than the individual.

5. I'm a representationalist. Any system that genuinely learns from experience is in the business of representing — you can't adjust your behaviour to how things are without something inside you standing in for how things are.

6. There's no sharp line anywhere in this, it's a continuum, but the four labels still mark real joints and not arbitrary cutoffs I've imposed for convenience.

(I also have a hunch I can't shake that current LLMs are Gregorian without being Popperian, which is either the most interesting thing about them or a sign that I've got the ladder wrong.)

What I want: start by brainstorming. For each of the six, spell out the two or three different things it could be claiming — I suspect I'm sliding between readings without noticing. Then audit the set. Which commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only look like they're in tension (I'd bet there are some of those, and I want to know which), and which single commitment is doing the most damage. Order the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad — I want to know which one I should lose sleep over and which one is a two-sentence footnote. For each, give me the cheapest repair and tell me what it costs: if the fix requires me to redefine one of the four categories until it stops doing any work, say that out loud. And if the LLM hunch is the thing that breaks the set, tell me that instead of letting me keep it.
