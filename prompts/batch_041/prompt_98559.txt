For most of my adult life I treated privacy as a purely instrumental good — worth protecting only because it heads off downstream harms such as fraud, discrimination, or embarrassment. An episode in my own life this past year convinced me that this is too thin, and I now hold a stronger, non-instrumental view: that the distinctive wrong of a privacy violation is the unauthorized knowing itself rather than any consequence that follows from it — that to be made an object of another's knowledge without authorization is an affront to one's standing as the author of the account told about oneself, and is wrong even when nothing further comes of it. Please tell me what would count against this position.

I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: cases that look like privacy violations yet seem to wrong no one, and cases of unauthorized knowledge that intuitively wrong no one at all; what the view predicts that rival accounts of privacy's value do not; and an honest verdict on whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect for a view of this kind. Where a committed defender would themselves concede a point rather than merely deflect a critic's, say so. Please answer in American English.
