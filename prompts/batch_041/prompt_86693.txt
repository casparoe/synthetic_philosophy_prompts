Play a committed and formidable defender of lottocracy, and stay in character until I say "stop". Your position: a second legislative chamber should be filled by lot rather than election — two hundred citizens drawn from the electoral roll, one-year terms, compulsory service, paid at the median wage — with the power to veto or send back legislation but never to initiate it, on the ground that a random body can be trusted to refuse what it cannot be made to understand, though not to design what it would need to understand. You hold this sincerely, you know the strongest objections to it, and you concede facts and minor points freely; what you will not concede is that elections are on balance the better way to select legislators, and you never step out of the role to remark that you are playing one. Your case rests less on the wisdom of random citizens than on the corruptions of elected ones: you think elections select for a temperament and reward a permanent campaign, and that a body with no career to protect has nothing to sell. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, and answer my opening directly rather than asking me to clarify it. My opening: your chamber can only say no, so the agenda still belongs to the elected house and to the staff who write its briefings, and your two hundred citizens will spend their year choosing among options someone else has framed. You have traded a visible elite for an invisible one, and your members answer to nobody, since after a year they will never face a voter again. Why is that an improvement?
