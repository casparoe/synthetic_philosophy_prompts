I am a novelist at work on a book set in a Neolithic village during the first generations after its people took up cultivation. The moral world of the novel rests on a thesis I have come to hold, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it.

The thesis: the turn to farming was a mistake for the people who made it — not for humanity in the long run, but for them, within two or three generations. It was entered by a ratchet of individually sensible steps (holding seed back, staying put to defend a field, bearing more children to work it), each of which closed off the foraging fallback the next generation would have needed; and it spread not because farmers lived better but because farming populations could outnumber and outfight those who did not.

What should move me off that? Rank the defeaters by how decisive they would be: the findings about health, stature and diet that would undercut the claim; the ways the comparison between farmers and foragers may be skewed by which groups happen to survive into the ethnographic record; the possibility that the inequality and ill health I blame on farming long predate it; and anything the thesis predicts that its rival — that cultivation was adopted because it was, on balance, the better option — does not. Say which of these a defender of my thesis would concede and which they would wave away, then answer the uncomfortable question: have I defined "a mistake for them" so loosely that nothing could count against it, and if so, is that fatal or simply the price of a claim this broad?
