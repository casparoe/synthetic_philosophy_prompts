My friend reads a lot of philosophy and has lately been giving me verdicts on whether people should change their minds, and I want to predict her call on a new case. I have no philosophy training myself, so I cannot tell whether she is applying one rule or several.

Her verdicts so far: (1) A mathematician with a complete, checked proof receives an email from a stranger claiming to have found a counterexample; my friend says the mathematician need not lower her confidence at all. (2) A man who believes his wife is faithful mainly because she does not seem like the type finds a flirty message from an unknown number; my friend says he should substantially reduce his confidence. (3) A physicist who has measured a quantity many times herself reads a single underpowered preprint reporting a very different value; my friend says she should keep her estimate and treat the preprint as probably wrong. (4) Someone who distinctly remembers turning the lock is asked offhandedly by their partner whether they locked up; my friend says the question is not evidence against the memory, though checking again for peace of mind is fine.

The new case: a scientist who, from years of her own experiments and a well-supported theory, is certain that a compound does not inhibit a particular enzyme, then runs a rigorous, preregistered experiment that strongly suggests it does inhibit it, having personally examined the raw data.

Please give me your best guess at my friend's verdict, the rule or rules that fit all her past verdicts, what each predicts about the new case, and which further cases would tell those rules apart. I care less about which rule is correct than about which one she is actually running.
