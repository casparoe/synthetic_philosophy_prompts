I teach a one-hour session in a philosophy-of-psychiatry elective for psychiatry residents and clinical psychology trainees. Most have had a single undergraduate ethics course; all of them reach for diagnostic categories when a case gets strange. Next week's topic is a view I will call tranquilism, and I want a plan for teaching it, not an exposition of it.

As I define it for this session, tranquilism is the claim that the only things that make a life go well or badly are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. Three stipulations keep it from collapsing into something the residents already know. First, craving and suffering are independent axes: craving is the wanting-tension that pulls toward or pushes away from something, suffering is the aversive character of what is undergone, and either can occur without the other. Second, pleasure is not outweighed by these two but ranked below them, so it never breaks a tie. Third, craving is reflexive, in that the wish not to crave is itself a craving, and the view owes some account of what follows from that.

Plan the hour. I need the order in which the components must be introduced and the dependency that forces that order: what has to be in place before the two-axis stipulation can even be stated without confusion, and where the reflexivity point has to land so that the residents hear it as a problem the view can address rather than as a refutation. I need the single case to build the whole hour on, and an account of what that case must have and must lack for the two axes to come apart in both directions within it, so that the residents can see a satisfied craving that still costs and a lapsed craving that was never satisfied. I need the misconceptions to expect and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. My guesses: they will hear absence of craving as apathy or anhedonia; they will file the view as hedonism with the pleasure subtracted; they will treat it as a therapeutic recommendation rather than a claim about welfare; they will assume any deliberate effort to reduce craving is self-defeating because wanting to stop wanting is wanting; and they will take the view to imply that the blunted contentment of a patient whose capacities have been reduced is an improvement. I also need one closing question that would show whether they can apply the two axes independently rather than collapsing them into a single scale of feeling better, and that a resident who has merely memorized the three stipulations would answer wrongly. Finally, tell me what to refuse to cover in an hour and to say out loud that I am refusing it.

Open with one sentence stating the spine of the sequence, then give the plan. No more than 800 words.
