You are an expert in the philosophy of biology and in the pedagogy of evolutionary theory—at home in the technical literature on adaptation, developmental and architectural constraint, byproduct, and exaptation, and equally skilled at getting a room of working empiricists to think differently about a familiar idea. I am asking you to design a teaching plan, not to explain the material to me. Here is the situation. I am a postdoc in a computational biology lab, and in three weeks I have a ninety-minute slot at our lab meeting—roughly a dozen graduate students and two PIs, all of whom build population-genetic and phylogenetic models and almost none of whom has read any philosophy of biology. They use "adaptive" and "selected for" more or less interchangeably, and whenever the spandrels critique of the adaptationist program comes up they wave it off with some version of: everyone already knows that not every trait is an adaptation, so this is just a philosopher complaining about storytelling. My goal is not to convince them that adaptation is rare. It is to get them to see the critique as a claim about inference—about what has to be true for the move from "this trait is adaptive" to "this trait is an adaptation" to be licensed—and to leave able to use the spandrel/exaptation contrast without collapsing it into "neutral," "functionless," or "drift." Please plan the session. Start by thinking through the candidate orderings of the ideas before you commit to one, and be explicit about the dependency structure: which idea has to be in place before which, why the order cannot be shuffled without the payoff going missing, and where the audience's existing vocabulary will actively work against you.

In particular, I want the following. First, the sequence, with the dependencies spelled out and a sentence of justification for each placement. Second, the single carrying example around which the whole session should be built. I have been considering the San Marco spaces themselves, the human chin, and the human male nipple, and I am not sure any of them isolates the right thing; tell me what the example must have and must lack, judge my candidates against that, and replace them if you would. Third, the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will actually surface, with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I predict at least these: that Gould and Lewontin are denying that adaptation is common; that a spandrel is simply a neutral trait; that "spandrel" and "exaptation" name the same thing; that the critique amounts to a Popperian complaint about falsifiability; and that calling something a byproduct settles that it can never acquire a function. If there are others—including the objection that the San Marco features are really pendentives that were deliberately chosen, so the architectural analogy is a poor one—tell me whether to raise them in the room or save them, and why. Fourth, a check for understanding I can run at the end that would reveal whether they have grasped the inference rather than memorized the terminology. Fifth, what I should openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes, and how to say so without seeming to dodge.

Write in American English, keep every recommendation concrete and specific to this audience, and avoid generic pedagogy.
