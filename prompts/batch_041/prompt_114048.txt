You are an expert both in AI safety engineering and in the philosophy of technology, and you write like someone who has implemented an impact measure and then had to explain to a lawyer exactly what it does. I would like you to produce a piece of sustained deadpan parody: the release notes for the fourth major version of a low-impact agent, written in the voice of a technical writer at a fictional AI company. The agent's entire safety case is that it minimizes the side effects of its actions.

Before you write anything, think carefully and at length. Brainstorm the failure modes such a system would actually exhibit, the ways a competent and well-intentioned engineering team would respond to each of them, and the sequence of versions that would result. Then plan the version history before drafting, so that every patch is plausible on its own terms and the sequence as a whole reads as an orderly, professional, and entirely unsuccessful pursuit.

Format. Numbered release notes covering roughly versions 3.0 through 4.2, followed by a section headed Known Issues and Limitations, followed by a short customer-support appendix of four to six exchanges, at least one of which is from a customer and at least one from a regulator. Around 800 to 1,000 words in total. Headings and bullet points are welcome; the register throughout should be that of an internal wiki page.

The technical content, stated correctly. The agent's impact measure compares the set of future states reachable under the policy the agent is about to follow with the set reachable under a baseline policy in which it does nothing, and penalizes the agent for reducing reachability relative to that baseline. The document should explain this as plainly as a technical writer would, since everything needed to follow the piece must be inside the piece. Do not name real papers, researchers, companies, or systems, and do not cite anything. If you find yourself reaching for a line that only lands for a reader who knows a particular publication, cut it.

The rules of the comedy. Each entry in the version history must repair a genuine failure mode of the version before it, and each repair must relocate the problem rather than dissolve it. Within the sequence: at least one patch should respond to the agent earning an excellent impact score by doing nothing at all; at least one should respond to it earning that score by preventing anything else from happening; at least one should respond to it earning that score by revising the baseline rather than the world; and at least one should reveal that the measure only registers the features the model represents, so that changes the model cannot represent are, in the measure's terms, not changes. Nobody in the document is stupid, and the system is never sinister; it is a well-behaved agent doing exactly what the specification says.

The concession. In the Known Issues section, in the flattest bureaucratic register available, the document must concede that the choice of baseline is not a technical choice, that a measure of impact presupposes some account of which changes matter, and that the module that would supply that account is not yet available. Nothing in the piece may resolve this, and the FAQ should leave it marginally worse. No one in the document should notice what has been conceded.

The target. The parody is aimed at a practice and a style of discourse: the ambition to specify avoid side effects, or low impact, as a value-free engineering constraint, and the habit of treating a baseline and a state space as technical parameters when they are normative commitments. Aim it at that ambition, not at any real firm or person, and invent the company. The register must stay flat throughout: no winking, no jokes flagged as jokes, no authorial commentary outside the document. A specialist in AI safety or in the philosophy of technology should laugh in recognition of the engineering, and a reader with no background at all should be able to follow the trajectory of the versions unaided.

I intend to assign the result in a graduate seminar that pairs the side-effect-avoidance literature with work in ethics on the specification of harm, so it needs to be accurate enough to read as a document rather than as a curiosity. Close with a single sentence of the kind a release-notes writer genuinely writes, which the reader will recognize as the whole problem restated as a feature.
