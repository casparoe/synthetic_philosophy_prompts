I should like a genuinely in-the-weeds explanation of the rule-following paradox, the sort that would satisfy someone who has spent too long in blog comment threads about it rather than a textbook summary.

Here is the shape I keep running into. Every finite run of behaviour is compatible with indefinitely many rules; my past usage, my dispositions, and the state of my brain all seem to underdetermine which rule I meant; therefore what fixes meaning is the agreement of a community. I want the middle of that argument, and especially the move from the second clause to the third, laid out with some care.

Please use an example of your own rather than the stock one. Take plus-star: for any pair of numbers each below a threshold T, plus-star returns the ordinary sum; for any pair in which at least one number is at least T, it returns 5. Pick a T comfortably beyond anything anyone has yet computed, and keep it fixed throughout.

Then work through the following.

1. Why the dispositionalist is supposed to fail here — both the finitude objection (my dispositions are finite, the rule's application is not) and the normativity objection (a disposition is a fact about what I would do, whereas the rule tells me what I ought to do, including where my dispositions run out). Say which of the two you take to be the deeper, and why.

2. What exactly the sceptical solution concedes and what it adds, and why it is supposed to be a solution of a different kind rather than a rival answer to the same question.

3. Whether the normativity objection does not simply reappear against the sceptical solution, since agreement is itself only a regularity in what people do. If the reply is that we have changed the subject — from truth conditions to assertibility conditions — say precisely what has been given up, and whether that is a cost worth paying.

4. The awkward case: a community that is uniformly wrong, or a lone agent whose practice is correct. Can the sceptical solution distinguish the two? If not, what is it actually claiming to explain?

5. Whether the community is essential or merely incidental. Could a single agent with a sufficiently rich, self-correcting practice — a person with an abacus, a machine that rechecks its own outputs — do whatever the community was doing? I have seen this pressed in arguments about whether a model's outputs could mean anything at all, and I should like to know whether the communitarian has anything useful to say there.

6. Whether the paradox, if it works, generalises to all content rather than arithmetic alone, and whether that makes the sceptical solution more or less attractive.

I do not want attributions, dates, or pointers to the literature; I want the mechanics, the places where the argument is doing hidden work, and your own view on which step is weakest. Please keep it tight — a page or so at most.
