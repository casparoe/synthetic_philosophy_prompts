For years I have noticed that I can be certain a sentence is singing and, a revision later, certain it is dead, without ever being able to say when it died — and I have begun to suspect that this has the same shape as the anti-luminosity argument (roughly, that no ordinary condition is one you can always know you are in) and the failure of the KK principle (that knowing entails knowing that one knows). In three weeks I have to teach both in a seventy-five-minute session for MFA students who read widely but have no formal philosophy, and I want my craft case to carry the whole thing. Do not explain the argument to me; give me the plan of explanation. Set out the order in which the pieces must arrive for the conclusion to feel forced rather than clever — my guess is that the margin-for-error idea has to be in place before either the KK principle or luminosity can be put under pressure, but say so if the dependency runs the other way, and tell me what else must be established first. Then judge my case: tell me what it must have, and must not have, if it is to generate the margin-for-error structure rather than merely illustrate the sorites paradox, or hand me a better one. Map the misconceptions in the order they will surface — I expect "this is just the sorites", "this proves we cannot know our own minds", and a conflation of luminosity with infallibility — say where in the sequence each should be headed off, and name the ones I have not anticipated. Close with a single question I can put to the room that would separate students who have grasped the structure from those who have merely acquired the vocabulary, tell me what I should openly refuse to teach at this level, and say where this plan is most likely to fail: which ordering I will regret, and what would change your mind about it. Seven hundred words at most.
