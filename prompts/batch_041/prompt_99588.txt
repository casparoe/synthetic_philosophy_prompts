The question that was set:

Fitting-attitude accounts hold that for something to be valuable is for it to be fitting — that is, appropriate or merited — to value it, so that facts about value are not themselves reasons for us but instead point to the reasons we have. First, explain the wrong kind of reasons problem for accounts of this sort. Then assess whether the account can be rescued by counting only the reasons that bear on the object of the attitude rather than on the attitude itself, using the Mischief Case as your central example.

The Mischief Case: A demon tells you that unless you resent Sam, an acquaintance who has only ever treated you with kindness, it will torture an innocent person. You can, with some effort, work yourself into a genuine resentment of Sam, and you know that you can.

The answer I have to mark:

Fitting-attitude accounts explain what it is for something to be valuable by appeal to the fittingness of a valuing attitude: something is valuable just in case it merits being valued. The point of the account is to avoid treating goodness as a further property alongside the natural features that give us reasons.

The wrong kind of reasons problem is that not every consideration that gives us a reason to have an attitude bears on whether the attitude fits its object. A demon's threat gives me a reason to admire a mediocre painting, but nothing about the painting changes, so the threat is not the sort of consideration that could make the painting valuable. If the account counts reasons of every kind, it overgenerates: anything we could be rewarded for valuing would come out valuable, and that cannot be right.

The obvious repair is to count only object-directed reasons, the ones that bear on the object of the attitude rather than on the attitude itself. This repair is either circular or empty. If object-directed reasons are simply those that bear on the value of the object, the account tells us nothing, since it was supposed to tell us what value is. If we try to identify them independently, we need a criterion for which reasons bear on the object, and any criterion will either leave out reasons that plainly count or let the wrong ones back in.

The Mischief Case shows how the repair is meant to work. The threat is certainly a reason to bring about my resentment of Sam, and the attitude I have a reason to bring about is just the attitude of resentment, so the threat is a reason for resentment. It is a prudential reason rather than a reason of the right kind, and that is what makes it the wrong kind. What the threat changes is not Sam's merits but the costs and benefits of my having the attitude, so resenting Sam is fitting in the circumstances even though Sam has done nothing to deserve it. The account can therefore keep the demon's threat out of the reasons that make Sam valuable while still allowing that I have a reason for the attitude, which is exactly what the restriction was supposed to achieve.

The restriction remains circular, however, and the circularity cannot be patched with further clauses, since any clause that identifies the right kind of reasons will have to use the very notion of value that the account is supposed to explain. The fitting-attitude account therefore cannot be the correct account of value.

Our rubric gives roughly equal weight to three things: getting the account and the problem right, the quality of the reasoning, and the use made of the test case.
