I am editing a collection of new essays on free will and moral responsibility, and two of my contributors have been arguing past each other for years. The first holds that Frankfurt-style cases do what they were designed to do: they show that an agent can be morally responsible for what she has done even though she could not have done otherwise, so that the principle of alternative possibilities, at least in its unqualified form, is false. The second holds that no such case has succeeded, and that each one stipulates away precisely what is in dispute. Neither has ever written down what the other would accept. I want the document they should have produced instead.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps: a text that both could sign, in which each side can read its own position stated as it would state it, and neither is required to concede anything it does not in fact concede. It should not be a survey of the literature, a verdict on who is right, or a dramatised exchange, and it should carry its own weight without appeals to what has been published. Include the following.

An agreed-claims section, numbered, with everything both sides can assert without hedging: what a Frankfurt-style case stipulates and what it leaves open; what work the counterfactual intervener does; which questions about the case are conceptual and which are empirical; what role determinism does and does not play; what each side is and is not committed to by accepting the verdict on the original case; and what both would count as a case that fails to establish its conclusion.

A disagreements table, one row per sub-question in dispute, with a column for the Frankfurt-defender's position and a column for the PAP-defender's, each phrased in wording that side's own defenders would accept as theirs; a column recording what would move each side; and a column marking the dispute as genuine or verbal. Where the argument turns on how the ability to have done otherwise is read, say which reading is doing the work and which side is entitled to which.

A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, with the argument or case both sides agree bears on each, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Among these, please address whether the case, if successful, tells against any requirement to the effect that responsibility needs an alternative possibility, or only against one particular specification of that requirement; and whether any part of the dispute is really about the grounds of responsibility rather than about the principle itself.

Then work through the four stipulated variants below. Neither side has seen them, and I want them treated as new material rather than as positions already in the literature.

(a) Two interveners. An obstruction intervener would have prevented the agent from acting, or from deciding, had she been about to do otherwise. A substitution intervener would instead have caused her to decide otherwise by working on her deliberative machinery — inducing a desire, or withdrawing a consideration from view — so that whatever she did would still have issued from something recognisably like her own practical reasoning. Do the two sides agree that the original case and the substitution case stand or fall together? Record each side's prediction for the substitution case, and say what the difference between the two interveners reveals about what the case is testing.

(b) A variant in which the agent knows the device is attached, but the device is inert because she decides as the intervener wanted. Is this the same case or a different one, and if different, what does the difference show?

(c) A variant in which the agent's actual deliberation is itself indeterministic, so that at the moment of decision there is a genuine chance of either outcome and the device stands ready. Does the case still do its work for the Frankfurt-defender? Does the PAP-defender concede that no alternative was available? Be explicit about whether the two sides are by this point answering the same question.

(d) A variant in which the only alternative open to the agent is a mere flicker: a possibility of forming a different intention that would at once have been overridden, or that is not connected to her practical reasoning in the way her actual decision is. Does the PAP-defender need the alternative to be robust, in the sense of being one she could have realised through her own practical reasoning, and does the Frankfurt-defender deny only that robustness is required?

Finish with a short note on which disagreements are verbal and why. Format the document as the agreed-claims section, then the table, then the cruxes, then the variants, then the note; keep it under about 1,500 words. End with a single final line of the form \answer{...}, enclosing a two-part label separated by a vertical bar: first, substantive or verbal, for the residual core disagreement; second, the name of the one crux both sides agree would be decisive if settled.
