Alright, I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me, but I've been stuck on this for weeks while writing a book about a family that tears itself apart over old grudges, and I need someone to tell me if there's an actual rule underneath my instincts or if I'm just making stuff up.

Here's the deal. I keep having these strong reactions about who gets to blame who, and they don't line up the way I'd expect. For example:

My brother, who's been cheating on his wife for years and everyone knows it, sits me down and tells me I'm garbage for sleeping with someone behind my partner's back. He's not wrong about the content. But the whole time he's talking I feel nothing. His blame just doesn't land on me. I don't accept it, I don't even feel accused.

My old AA sponsor, a guy who drank himself onto the street and then got sober twenty years ago, says almost the exact same words to me about my drinking. That one lands hard. Lands harder than when my teetotal sister says it, which is strange because she's the one with clean hands.

Now my friend Marco, who drinks as much as I do and is in no position to talk, says the same thing to me. And that one lands too. Maybe even more than the sponsor's. Which bugs me, because by my logic about my brother, Marco should be in the same boat.

My mother, who gave up basically her whole life for us kids, tells me I'm selfish for not coming home more. It lands, and it stings, even though she was a mess when I was young and left us alone alot. Somehow that doesn't cancel it.

Some stranger on the internet, a guy whose entire timeline is him being a hypocrite about everything, calls me a hypocrite. Nothing. Zero. I feel nothing.

A politician who's been taking money from exactly the industry he's now denouncing gives a speech about corruption. I might agree with every word, but the blame feels dead to me. Like he's using it to cover himself.

Then the one that really bothers me: last month I chewed out a colleague for a screwup that I myself made the month before, the same screwup, and the whole time I felt like I had no right to be saying it. He deserved the criticism, I was factually right, and I wasn't doing it to save my own skin. I still felt like I'd forfeited something.

One more, and this is the one I can't sort: a friend of mine who relapsed last year, she's drinking again right now, tonight, as we speak, tells me I'm throwing my life away. Same fault, currently, no excuses, no apology. Does she get to say it? Half of me says obviously not, and half of me says she's the only one in the room who actually knows what she's talking about.

The half-baked rule I've been carrying around is: you lose the right to blame someone for a fault you've got yourself, unless you've beaten it. But that can't be right, because Marco hasn't beaten anything and his blame lands, and my mother has plenty of her own failings and hers lands too. So maybe its not about the fault at all, but about whether the blamer is using the blame to make themselves look better or to dodge their own thing. That's what feels different about my brother and the politician, they're blaming me to get themselves off the hook. But then the colleague case wrecks that, because I wasn't dodging anything and I still felt disqualified.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If I have to give up one of my reactions, which one, and why that one and not another? And what does your principle say about my relapsed friend, and about a case I haven't hit yet, someone blaming me for a fault they've never had and don't understand at all, a person who's never been near the thing telling me how I ought to feel about it. Does the outsider with clean hands have more standing or less? Please don't just tell me all blame is equally valid, I'll throw the book at the wall. Pick a side, spell out the rule, and defend it.
