I'm putting together the final for my graduate metaphysics seminar and I want one long essay on grounding and fundamentality that a student can't just regurgitate the usual litany at me — you know, irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, hyperintensional, grounding is not causation. Everyone can recite that list in their sleep. What I actually want to test is whether they can think clearly about well-foundedness, which is where the action is.

So here's the setup I want to build the question around. A student came to office hours and drew me this little arguement on the back of an envelope, and I've typed it up more or less as she had it:

(1) If A is grounded in B, then A holds in virtue of B.
(2) Nothing can hold in virtue of something that itself holds in virtue of something that... with no end to it — an explanation that never bottoms out isn't an explanation at all.
(3) So there must be something fundamental: something ungrounded in which everything else ultimately rests.

She called (3) the "Anchoring Principle" and seemed pretty pleased with herself. I want the exam question to make them figure out what's actually going on with this. Not "is it valid" in the plug-and-chug sense — I want them to see that there are at least two completely different things people mean when they talk about a grounding regress, that the argument quietly slides between them, and to say what would have to be true of grounding for that slide to be harmless rather than fatal. And I want them to say what hangs on it: do any of the formal features of grounding actually deliver well-foundedness, or is well-foundedness a seperate, substantial commitment that you could reject while keeping every formal feature intact? If a student can't pull apart "grounding is a strict partial order" from "grounding is well-founded," they haven't understood the topic, and I want the question to punish exactly that.

Also make them confront the boring-but-deep point that "fundamental" is a term of art. Is it just defined as "ungrounded," in which case the Anchoring Principle is true by fiat and tells us nothing about the world, or is it supposed to be a substantive thesis about what sits at the base of things? A good answer should hold both readings apart and say which one the argument actually needs.

Please write me: the essay question itself (something doable in about 90 minutes, so it can have a couple of parts), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, out of 25, plus a one-liner per point on how candidates typically fumble it. Don't have the model answer name-drop the literature — that's not what I'm testing.
