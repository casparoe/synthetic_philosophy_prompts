I'm a graduate student in philosophy at work on a seminar paper about advance directives, and I've reached the stage where I need someone to argue with me rather than reassure me. The paper is meant to intervene in a familiar dispute: on one side, the view that the earlier competent self's autonomy reaches forward and binds the person she later becomes — the critical-versus-experiential interests distinction, and what is usually called precedent autonomy; on the other, the objection that this subordinates a living and currently content patient to someone who no longer exists, and that patients with dementia often retain enough of a capacity to value that their present attitudes ought to carry weight. I think both sides share a model that I want to attack, and I'd like your comments on whether the attack lands, where it is unfair to the views it targets, and what I have missed.

What follows is my draft, lightly edited for you. An advance directive is asked to do two quite different things, and I think the literature's disagreements are largely the result of letting the two slide into one another. The first job is sovereign: the directive is the earlier self's legislation for a subject who cannot legislate for herself. On this reading its authority comes from the fact that it was autonomously chosen, and it is content-neutral — a directive refusing all treatment and a directive demanding everything are equally authoritative, because what confers authority is the act of choosing, not the wisdom of the choice. The critical/experiential distinction is what makes this palatable: the earlier self protects the interests that give her life a shape against the merely experiential interests of a self that can no longer have the former. The second job is evidentiary: the directive is our best proxy for what the later, incompetent self's interests actually are. On this reading its authority is defeasible in principle, because it is a prediction, and predictions can be wrong; we follow it because the earlier self is usually better placed to know what the later self will want than a court or a physician is.

These two jobs come apart in exactly the case everyone discusses. A woman at fifty writes a directive refusing treatment in the event of dementia; at seventy she is cheerful, affectionate, socially engaged, and asks for the antibiotic. Take the sovereign reading seriously and her present request is the voice of someone who is not, in the relevant sense, the author of her own life, and we overrule it. Take the evidentiary reading and her present request is data — very good data, since it is the only first-person report available — and the directive loses. The literature mostly assumes the sovereign reading and then argues about how much of the earlier self's authority survives. I want to argue that the sovereign reading has no application here at all, and that its appearance of application comes from a substantive perfectionism that liberal defenders of advance directives cannot endorse. Sovereignty over a self presupposes that the self over which it is exercised is the same practical agent, or that the discontinuity is of a kind that justifies treating one as the other's representative. But the whole reason directives matter is that dementia breaks exactly that continuity — that is why we need a directive instead of just asking. What actually does the work in the standard view is a prediction plus a risk-aversion: we override the contented patient because we think her contentment is not to be trusted, that she has adapted to a diminished life or cannot grasp what she has lost, so that her endorsement is not an endorsement by a valuer. That is a claim about what makes a life go well, and it is doing all the work; strip it out and the sovereign reading collapses into the evidentiary one.

So the authority of a directive should be indexed to the epistemic warrant of its author and to the independence of the later self's present attitudes, not to the bare fact that the author chose. And the doctrinal home for this is not the law of wills, where the analogy usually goes, but the law of trusts: a settlor's terms bind the trustee only insofar as they serve the beneficiary, and the law refuses dead-hand control that outlives its rationale — the rule against perpetuities, the hostility to restraints on alienation, the refusal to enforce conditions that strip a beneficiary of basic rights. A directive is a trust instrument with the peculiarity that settlor and beneficiary are the same person at different times, and the law's long suspicion of settlors who try to govern beneficiaries they will never meet strikes me as exactly the right instinct.
