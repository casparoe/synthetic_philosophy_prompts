Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over whether the brain is a computer.

The two parties should not be identified by their textbook names. Treat them as defined by the following commitments, which I have written to be as neutral as I could manage and which you may sharpen if you can do so without tilting either way.

Party A, the implementation camp, holds that "the brain computes" is a claim about the causal-mechanical structure of neural processes, true or false independently of our descriptive habits; that at least some neural processes are vehicles for the manipulation of representations; and that a computational description of those processes carries explanatory weight, telling us why the system does what it does rather than merely how we might have modelled it.

Party B, the deflationary camp, holds that "the brain computes" is at best a stance we adopt for predictive convenience; that any neural process admitting a computational description also admits a non-computational description at least as explanatorily adequate; and that the computational vocabulary therefore does no causal-explanatory work, whatever its engineering value.

There is a wrinkle I want the document to handle head-on. I take it both sides can accept a ladder of increasingly strong claims, and I suspect their disagreement is not one disagreement but a disagreement about which rung is occupied. The rungs, as I would put them: (1) some neural systems can be described as computing; (2) the computation a system performs explains its behaviour; (3) the system's causal powers are conferred by its computational properties, so that a description omitting them would be causally incomplete; (4) those causal powers are indifferent to the material substrate implementing the computation. The document should locate each party on each rung, and should say explicitly whether any rung is one where the parties actually agree and have merely been talking past each other.

Then produce four sections.

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered, listing what both parties can sign: about what computation is, or how far they can agree on a working definition before they part; about what would count as evidence either way; about which of their questions are empirical and which are verbal; and about what neither side is committed to (for instance, whether either view requires the brain to be digital, or serial, or to run programs in the ordinary sense of that word).

2. A disagreements table, one row per disagreement, with columns for: the question at issue; Party A's formulation, in wording A would accept; Party B's formulation, in wording B would accept; whether the row marks a genuine crux or a verbal dispute, and if verbal, what the two are really doing when they disagree; the case or argument both parties agree bears on the question; and each side's advance prediction about how that case comes out.

3. A short section on the cruxes: for each, what both parties agree would settle it, and what each side would have to give up in order to be wrong.

4. A closing note in your own voice: which argument or consideration would lead you to revise this document, in particular what would convince you that you had drawn the line between substantive and verbal dispute in the wrong place, and where your drafting is most likely to have failed — either by smuggling in a formulation only one side would accept, or by writing an agreed-claim so vague that it costs nothing.

For the cases in section 2, use at least these three, and add others only if they earn their place: bacterial chemotaxis, in which a non-neural system tracks a chemical gradient by something that looks like a comparator plus a reset; the cerebellar circuitry that expands a small input space into a vastly larger one before a linear readout; and a lookup-table device that reproduces a given circuit's input-output behaviour exactly, entry by entry, without reproducing any of its internal structure. The third matters most to me. I want each side's prediction about whether the lookup-table device computes, and whether that prediction changes if the table is replaced by a compact algorithm that generates the same entries.

No citations, and no appeals to named authorities or to what the literature is supposed to say; the argument should stand on the definitions and cases in this prompt, since I will read the result without checking any references. If you attach standard labels to the parties, keep them accurate and do not let the labels do the work. Aim for a document an outsider could read and use to work out which side they are on.
