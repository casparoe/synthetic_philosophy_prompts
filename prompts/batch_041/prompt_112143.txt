Two colleagues in my department have been circling each other for years. One is an expressivist about moral judgment: to call an action wrong, on her view, is not to describe it but to express a con-attitude toward it, and moral sentences are not in the business of stating facts. The other is a cognitivist of a deflationary temper: moral sentences are truth-apt in just the way any declarative sentence is, so nothing here stands in need of a special explanation. They have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they could both sign.

Structure it as follows.

1. Agreed claims, numbered. These should cover the linguistic data any adequate account must accommodate; what a successful theory of moral language owes us; and any specific constructions on which the two already agree about what is going on. They must not be so weak that a party could sign them while quietly abandoning its own position.

2. The residual disagreements, in a table with one row per disagreement, giving each side's own formulation of its view — not a phrasing that begs the question against it. Where a disagreement turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which one it is and why.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. Treat these as candidates to be assessed, not a list to be endorsed: (i) whether an expressivist semantics can validate the same inferences a truth-conditional semantics validates; (ii) whether the expressivist is forced to treat the moral predicate as ambiguous between its asserted and unasserted occurrences, and if so whether that is a genuine cost; (iii) whether "truth-apt" marks a substantive commitment or is mere bookkeeping. Decide which of these are genuine cruxes, which are verbal, and defend the verdict.

4. For each crux, the case or argument both parties would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance; and, where possible, what each would count as evidence against her own view.

The following constructions must be addressed somewhere in the document, because they are where the two positions most visibly pull apart: a moral sentence under negation as asserted by someone with no relevant attitude ("Lying is not wrong," said by a person who is simply indifferent); a conditional whose antecedent and consequent are both moral, asserted by someone who rejects the antecedent ("If lying is wrong, then telling white lies is wrong"); a disjunction with one moral and one descriptive disjunct ("Either lying is wrong or the museum opens at nine"); a moral sentence embedded under an attitude verb, both factive and non-factive ("He regrets that lying is wrong"; "She hopes that lying is wrong"); a comparative ("Lying is worse than cheating"); and a moral sentence under a truth predicate ("It is true that lying is wrong").

Keep the document free of appeals to authority: no citations, no reading lists, no "as X has shown." Every claim must stand or fall on the argument given for it.
