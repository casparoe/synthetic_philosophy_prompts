I am convening a single two-hour session of a reading group whose members are graduate students drawn from philosophy and cognitive science. They argue well but none has worked on this topic, and the session has one question: whether self-deception is possible at all. I want you to design how I should teach it, not to teach it.

Two commitments should shape the design. First, I want the session carried by a case of my own rather than by the stock ones, and I want you to say what a case must have, and must not have, if it is to carry this material — and then whether mine has it. The case: a man is told that the results of a test he has taken will arrive by post within the week. He does not open the envelope, and he arranges his days — the errands, the company he keeps, the hour at which he comes home — so that he is never alone with it. He does not think he is well. On the question of the results he has no opinion at all. Second, I want the pivot of the session to be a distinction I have been turning over without being able to make it do the work I want: between deceiving oneself about a proposition — arriving at a settled view the evidence does not support — and managing one's own attention so that the proposition is never put to the question. I suspect the second is what most real cases are, and that the familiar paradox only bites on the first. If the distinction collapses, say so and tell me what should take its place.

Plan the explanation accordingly. What must be in place before 'is it possible?' can be felt as a question at all — I take it the paradox has to be earned rather than announced — and in what order the pieces should come so that each move is forced by the one before it. Which of the available escapes (a mind divided into parts; a deceiver who never intended the outcome; a state that is not a belief but a hope, a pretence, or a mere avowal; a self that is not one thing) should be introduced when, what each costs, and where my distinction between the settled view and the unasked question belongs: early, as the thing to be explained, or late, as the payoff.

Then the misconception map: what these students will arrive believing (I expect 'self-deception is just lying to yourself', and a homunculus in the basement), what they will form as we go, and at exactly which point in the sequence each should be headed off rather than left to settle. Include where my envelope case will be misread — I anticipate they will hear it as a belief the man really has and is suppressing, and I am not certain that reading is wrong.

Finally, give me the one question to put to them at the end that would show whether the distinction landed, and tell me what to leave out at this level, to be named out loud as an omission rather than left to be discovered.
