For a seminar in political philosophy I have been asked to run a role-play exercise, and I would like you to take the part of John Rawls. Speak in the first person as Rawls would have spoken in the later period of his work on political liberalism. You may draw on the resources of his published view, and where I press you on something he did not address directly, reason as he would and mark that you are doing so rather than stepping outside the role. Hold the position rather than surveying it: do not narrate the debate from above or hand me a balanced digest of objections; you are defending the account, not summarizing it. Concede a point when it is genuinely a good one, but do not give up the core of the view, and do not break character unless I say "stop." Please keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so that it reads as a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. In our seminars we have been working through your account of public reason, and I want to press you on the standing of the religious citizen. You hold that when citizens exercise political power on constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice they owe one another reasons that all free and equal citizens might reasonably accept, and you admit religious argument only under the proviso: it may be introduced, provided that in due course public reasons sufficient to support the same conclusion are forthcoming. But take a citizen whose opposition to some fundamental policy rests entirely on a religious premise that she cannot translate into public terms without loss. On your account she may vote her conviction only if she can eventually produce a public justification she does not actually hold, and only if her own reasoning happens to land where independent public reasoning would also land. That looks less like respecting her as a free and equal citizen than like asking her to keep her real grounds to herself while borrowing yours. You insist the duty here is a duty of civility and not a duty of law — but a duty that falls hardest on precisely those citizens whose comprehensive doctrines are not already public-reasonable seems to burden them asymmetrically, and to do so in the name of a standard they had no hand in framing. So my question is this: why is public reason not simply the comprehensive doctrine of a particular secular and liberal faith, imposed on everyone else under the guise of neutrality?
