So I've been going down a rabbit hole on the Kelly criterion and I've lost track of which of the arguments for it are actually the same argument.

The version I keep running into is: bet the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes the expected log of your wealth, because that maximizes your long-run growth rate. Fine. But then I heard someone on a podcast, an econ guy, can't remember who, say the real reason has nothing to do with log at all — it's that when your wealth multiplies over time, the average over time and the average over a bunch of parallel copies of you come apart, and only the time average is what actually happens to you. That's supposed to be the reason maximizing expected money is wrong, but I don't see how it gets you to log specifically, or how it lines up with maximize the expected log of your wealth, which just looks like a utility function to me. And if log really is my utility function then Kelly is just ordinary expected utility maximization and there's nothing geometric or special about it — but then the whole never bet more than Kelly, at any odds, ever thing is a way stronger claim than any utility choice could justify, since somebody with different preferences could rationally bet more.

The other thing that bugs me is that everything seems to live in the limit of many bets, and I only get to make most of my decisions once. Also I've seen it claimed that betting twice the Kelly fraction gives exactly zero growth over time, which sounds like a great fun fact but I can't tell if it's a deep result or an artifact of how the model is set up.

Can you sort this out for me? What's a genuine theorem here, what's an argument, what's just a heuristic, and which of these supposedly different justifications are secretly the same one wearing different hats. And where would someone who isn't just being a coward actually push back on Kelly?
