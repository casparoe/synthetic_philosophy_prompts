I am in a political philosophy reading group, and we keep circling a structure that I cannot find a name for. It comes in two flavors. In the first, someone says a star athlete or a company founder deserves an enormous reward; someone else objects that the talent or the temperament that produced the success was never chosen; the defender grants this but answers that the person still chose to train, or still chose to take the risk; the objector replies that the capacity to train, or the ability to absorb a risk, was itself unearned; the defender then finds an even thinner slice of agency further down — the moment of grit, the decision to get up early — and the exchange simply repeats. In the second flavor, a student aces a selective exam and is called meritorious; the objection is that the exam rewards being raised among books; the reply is that she did the reading; the counter-reply is that she did not choose to be the sort of child who does the reading; the reply is that she still chose to sit down and do it. In both cases every candidate basis of desert — the trait or act in virtue of which the reward is said to be deserved — turns out to rest on something the person did not choose; the defender rescues the claim by naming a narrower basis; and that narrower basis has the same problem, so the argument never reaches ground that is not itself unearned. What strikes me is not that any single step is implausible — each one is quite plausible on its own — but that the chain appears to have no natural place to stop, while a desert claim seems to require one.

Is there an established name for this structure, for the unterminated regress of the desert base, or for the fact that any control-based desert claim can always be pushed back one more step? I have met several nearby ideas and none of them quite fits. Moral luck and constitutive luck strike me as being about an inconsistency in our assessments rather than about a regress that has to terminate; the literature on tracing, as I understand it, asks whether responsibility for a downstream outcome can be traced back to an upstream free act, which presupposes that there is an upstream act to find rather than denying that the chain ever bottoms out; luck egalitarianism's brute-versus-option distinction draws a line between unchosen and chosen fortune instead of showing that the line cannot be drawn; and the basic-structure objection concerns whether principles of justice govern institutions or individual choices, not the depth of a choice chain. So I suspect the thing I am circling is either one of these in disguise or something that has no name yet. If it has a name, tell me which and how it differs from the others; if it does not, coin one and define it. But please do not stop at the label. I want the carving: exactly where the regress bites, and whether it is a genuine infinite regress or rather a no-privileged-stopping-point problem; whether it tells against desert as such or only against a conception of desert that demands control; whether a defender could stop the chain legitimately by appealing to a convention, a threshold, or the point at which further tracing stops making a difference to what we owe one another; and what the boundary cases are — say, a reward for a genuinely spontaneous act versus one for a lifelong trait — that would mark the edges of the phenomenon.
