I am assembling a decision-tree handout for a graduate seminar on self-knowledge, for a session on the reliability of introspection — specifically, on how much evidential weight a researcher should give to a subject's introspective report. I need the instrument to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they already favor.

Root of the tree. The reader accepts only this: sincere, confident, and mistaken self-reports occur — for instance, a subject who fluently explains why she chose one option when the choice was in fact produced by a manipulation she never noticed. Nothing further is required of the reader at the root.

Build from the logical space, not from a list of names. Derive the branch nodes first, from the questions on which the plausible positions genuinely divide, and only then locate the positions as leaves. Do not name philosophers or works, and do not cite studies: assume the reader has read nothing, and treat the cases below as stipulated hypotheticals rather than real experiments, so that no leaf depends on recalling a particular result.

Targets. Distinguish at the first branch among three targets of introspection, since most disputes about reliability are really disputes about which of the three is at issue: (a) what one is currently experiencing, (b) what standing attitude one holds, and (c) why one holds it or acted as one did — the etiological report.

Two distinctions that matter to me more than the usual ones, each of which must appear as its own node. First, accuracy versus tracking: a faculty can deliver mostly true reports while the truth of a report does not depend on the state reported, for instance because the criteria for applying the self-descriptive term shift with the report itself. Make "is it accurate?" and "does it track?" separate branch points, and let at least one leaf be the view that self-reports are accurate but not tracking. Second, constitution: whether the state reported is determinate independently of the report, or whether the report, or the practice of reporting, partly makes it so.

Scope. Branch also on whether failure in the etiological target generalizes to the experiential target or is confined to it, and on whether a failure of tracking licenses any conclusion about the metaphysics of the states themselves, rather than only about the evidential weight of reports about them.

Cases the tree must route through. The annotated subject: her concurrent reports of what she is experiencing agree with every independent check we have, while her explanations of her own choices go reliably wrong whenever we manipulate the choice without her knowledge. The trained reporter: at first she applies a self-descriptive term at chance, and after feedback she applies it accurately; the question is whether a latent detector has been calibrated or a criterion has been learned. The divergent twins: two people with identical self-reports and identical behavior but different underlying states; is the report evidence about the state, or only about the reporting? The stable denier: someone who sincerely denies having the state that her behavior and physiology best support, and whose denial is itself stable and predicts her later behavior.

Leaves. At least these seven must appear, named descriptively rather than after their defenders, and you may rename them: a fallibilist dedicated-detector view; an inferentialist view on which self-reports issue from the same interpretive machinery we apply to others; a constitutive or expressivist view; a domain-split hybrid on which experiential reports are detector-based and tracking while etiological reports are interpretive and only accidentally right; the accurate-but-not-tracking view; a deflationary view that dissolves the question rather than answering it; and a humility-only view on which the question is answered by the purpose the report is put to. If you judge that a leaf needs a different shape, say so and justify it.

Deliverables. The tree, with eight to ten internal nodes, each phrased as a question a reader can answer without knowing where it leads; no node may offer position names as its options, and no node may simply ask whether introspection is reliable. Each leaf carries its path through the tree as a conjunction of answers, one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, and one sentence stating what it implies for a researcher deciding how much weight to give a subject's report. Then, separately: flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate and say what further question would be needed to distinguish them; and list the combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, diagnosing each in a clause as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
