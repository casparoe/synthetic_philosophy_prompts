A colleague of mine in neuroethics has, over several years, issued the following verdicts on which entities have moral standing—that is, which can be wronged, not merely which we ought to treat kindly. She holds that a typical adult human has full moral standing. She holds that a chimpanzee has substantial standing; a dog, a crow, and an octopus have standing as well, though she ranks the dog somewhat below the chimpanzee and treats the crow and octopus as roughly comparable to the dog. She holds that a mouse has no moral standing, and that a honeybee has none. She also holds that a human who has undergone a hemispherectomy and now functions normally retains full standing. She has not had occasion to rule on any case in which an entity's neuron count is high but its functional organization is absent.

Next month our committee will review a proposal involving a novel cortical organoid derived from human stem cells. The preparation contains more neurons than a dog brain, but the tissue is a disorganized sheet: it has no sensory input, no motor output, no behavior, and no evidence of the recurrent thalamocortical loops that are usually taken to support integrated experience. The researchers want to know whether it could be a subject of harm. Predict my colleague's verdict. Then reconstruct the rule she is implicitly running. I am less interested in the verdict than in the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well—at least one that treats absolute neuron number or brain size as intrinsically relevant, and at least one that treats it as a mere proxy for functional integration or species-typical capacity—what each predicts about the organoid, which rule you think she is actually using and why, and which further case would best distinguish them.
