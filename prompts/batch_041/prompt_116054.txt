A friend of mine got married in June and I can't stop thinking about the vows. Two people stand up in front of everyone they love and promise, basically, to stay. It's the most serious thing we let one person say to another, and they mean it, and everyone cries. But if she walks out next year, nothing happens. No penalty, nobody sues, and the law wont even listen — you can't sue your husband for breaking his promise to love you. You can sue a plumber who doesn't show up.

And it gets wierder the more I poke at it. You used to be able to sue someone for calling off an engagement — breach of promise, they called it, and the damages got nicknamed heart balm, which tells you alot. Then over the 20th century we decided that was sleazy and got rid of it almost everywhere; apparently it was mostly women suing men, on the theory that a broken engagement wrecked her prospects. So we used to enforce the promise to *get* married but never the promise to *stay* married.

Meanwhile contracts class says a bare promise is worth nothing — you need something given in return — unless the other person actually relied on it and got hurt, in which case suddenly it counts.

So what do we actually believe about why promises bind? Work out what theory of promissory obligation this whole mess presupposes. Is it the harm of relying on someone? The transfer of a right to decide something? The value of the relationship itself? Or just social convention — the words only do anything because we all agree they do — in which case why does the ceremony, the witnesses, the license, the specific building matter so much, when the same words said in a car wouldn't do anything at all? And what about promising to love somebody, which isn't the kind of thing you can decide to do?

Tell me which parts the practice actually forces and which parts it merely happens to fit with, where it's silent enough that several theories would each license it, and whether it's even consistent with itself — solemn forever-vows on one side, free exit on the other.
