I sit on the research advisory panel of a mid-sized foundation that pays for STEM outreach aimed at teenage girls — women engineers visiting schools, after-school coding clubs, that sort of thing. We meet next week to decide whether to renew the whole package for another four years or tear it up and rebuild it, and I have to circulate a short written opinion beforehand.

Here's the wrinkle. Our program officer pulled together the international data (we fund work in about a dozen countries, so some of it is our own numbers), and it's the same pattern people keep passing around: the countries that score highest on the usual gender-equality measures — women in parliament, women in full-time work, women in the professions — tend to show the *biggest* gaps between men and women on things like whether they'd rather work with people or with machines, and often the most lopsided occupational segregation in tech. More equality, more boys picking the robots and girls picking the patients. Two of my trustees have already seized on this. One says it proves the gap is biological and we're burning money trying to change it; the other says it proves the opposite — that once you strip out the barriers, whatever differences are left are "real" and we should stop treating them as a problem to fix.

I think both of them are wrong, but I need to hand the panel something it can actually vote on. Please draft the opinion I'll circulate. I want a concrete recommendation — renew as-is, restructure, or kill, with a rough budget shape and a timeline — plus the reasoning, and I want you to take the strongest version of the case against my recommendation seriously instead of flattening it into a strawman. Be careful about the leap from a cross-country correlation to any claim about what's innate, and about the leap from any claim about what's innate to a claim about what's just or what we should fund; say explicitly which parts of the decision are empirical and which are value judgments. Also tell me what we'd need to learn to change our minds, and keep the whole thing short enough that people actually read it before the meeting.
