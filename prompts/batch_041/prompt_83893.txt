This spring I am running a session for my high school's philosophy club on how people come to hold badly formed beliefs about politics and science, and rather than lecture my classmates I would like to hand them a diagnostic quiz that tells them which position on the problem they actually hold, so I am asking you to write that quiz for me. It should run to about nine questions, each of them a short concrete case or claim rather than a request for a label — a student who has never once encountered the strongest argument on the other side, a family that treats every critical news story as further proof of a cover-up, a teenager who trusts one online commentator over every teacher and journalist she has ever met, a tightly closed religious community that turns out to be right about something its neighbors are wrong about, a man who spends hours a day on a platform that seems designed to keep him angry — with two or three answer options apiece, and a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions. Every question should be answerable by someone who has never heard any of the labels, and none of them should simply ask whether the reader thinks echo chambers are a problem.

Here is the angle I care about. I want the questions to come apart on where the trouble is located: in the individual's own habits of belief, in the shape of the community around him (who hears whom, and who is trusted), or in the information environment (who decides what reaches him in the first place). I also want them to separate the diagnosis that someone has simply never heard the other side, which is the kind of thing exposure can fix, from the diagnosis that the group has a standing policy of treating every outsider as untrustworthy, which is the kind of thing more exposure can make worse; I have seen the first called an epistemic bubble and the second a genuine echo chamber, and I would like both of those results to be reachable in the quiz. Beyond that, I want the quiz to sort readers by what they take the cure to be (more exposure, training in how to think, a change in the incentives of the platforms, or the slow rebuilding of trust and relationships), by whether they think what has gone wrong is a matter of beliefs at all or of loyalty and identity, by whether they think an outsider has any right to intervene or whether being corrected by one is itself part of the injury, by whether they take the phenomenon to be symmetric, with all of us in some version of it, or asymmetric, with some communities far worse off than others, and by whether they think a closed community can be intellectually healthy, so that insulation is not automatically a defect.

Please make sure the results include at least these positions: the exposure view, the view that exposure backfires and the remedy is something closer to inoculation or critical thinking instruction, the structural view that the real levers belong to the platforms, the view that the failure is one of trust and relationship rather than information, the view that intervening in other people's beliefs is paternalistic and that the cure can only be self-administered, the cynical view that nobody is better off than anybody else, and the view that a closed community is not by itself defective. If some of these cannot be separated by the questions you can write, say so plainly rather than pretending they come apart, and if some combination of answers corresponds to a position that nobody actually holds, tell me which combination it is and whether you think it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because no one has bothered to defend it.

I would like the whole thing written as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic type, no tables or columns, and no list set off from the text, so the questions should be numbered as they come in the sentences and the scoring key spelled out in ordinary sentences. I do not need citations, names of papers, or suggestions for further reading, because I will not be able to look anything up while I work on this; where a position has a standard name, use it, and where it does not, describe it in a few words.
