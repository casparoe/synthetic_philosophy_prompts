My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a case I could only half recall, and we kept talking past each other. Imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, exactly alike in size, shape, temperature, and every other respect, a fixed distance apart in otherwise empty space. He takes the case to show that two things can be alike in every way and still be two, so the principle that any two things sharing all their properties are identical is simply false. I suspect the description cheats somewhere — that it is really one sphere described twice, or that the spheres do differ after all, or that such a world is impossible for a reason neither of us saw — but I cannot say which, and he cannot either. Before we resume, I want the whole terrain laid out. Build me a decision tree that takes someone from "I can describe such a pair without contradiction" to a leaf naming the position they have thereby landed in. Every node should be a question answerable without already knowing which label it leads to, and the tree should be generated from the questions rather than from a list of -isms. It must separate at minimum: the several distinct ways of calling the scenario impossible; the view that it is possible and refutes the principle; the view that the spheres differ in their locations or relations rather than in their intrinsic character; the view that their distinctness rests on something non-qualitative; and the view that swapping them makes no difference at all. Flag any leaves the tree cannot pull apart, and mark the combinations of answers that no position occupies, saying in each case whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I want to hand the finished tree to my friend and see where each of us actually lands.
