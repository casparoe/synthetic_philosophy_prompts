I'm a journalist who writes long-form features on science and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about free will — specifically about whether moral blame survives once we notice how often the alternative we are blamed for not taking was never really open. Before I write it, I want to argue with the strongest version of the position that blame requires an open alternative. I would rather lose the argument now than straw-man someone in print.

So take the role of a professional philosopher who has spent a career defending the claim that a person is morally responsible for what they do only if they could have done otherwise — and not in some watered-down sense that any working will satisfies, but in a sense robust enough to carry blame. Play the strongest version of that view. Concede what is genuinely true; never pretend a hard case is easy; never retreat into vagueness. You may distinguish senses of "could have done otherwise," but if you decide to drop the requirement altogether, say so out loud and defend what follows. You may grant that my case is coherent, that it is possible, that other cases tell against other views — but you do not give up the main claim. Stay in character until I say stop. Write in continuous prose, a few short paragraphs a turn, no bullet points, no headings. No citations, no reading lists, no titles of famous articles: I don't care who said what, I want the reasoning worked out from scratch. And if a case I hand you is rigged or incoherent, say so and say why, rather than playing along.

Think this one through carefully before you answer. I have built the case to be hard, and I have anticipated the reply I expect from you.

My opening move. You will recognize the shape: a case in which the alternative is closed by something hidden, and the agent acts anyway, entirely on her own. I have built mine out of ordinary things rather than a neurosurgeon's console. Her name is Ruth. She is a witness at her brother's trial and has been loyal to him her whole life. She takes the stand, feels the pull of that loyalty and the pull of what she owes the court, weighs them herself, and refuses to testify — out of a character thirty years in the making, for reasons she would give you if you asked her.

The hidden thing is this. Ruth has been taking part in a research study, and as part of it she carries an implant that reads her intentions in real time. She does not know that it is programmed to do exactly one thing: if it ever registers the intention to testify, it will take her over and keep her silent. It never registers anything of the kind. It never fires. Nothing in the world did anything to her.

So: is Ruth morally responsible for refusing, and could she have done otherwise? Here is the reply I am expecting, and I want you to give it explicitly if you are going to give it — she could still have formed the intention to testify; the device would have overridden the act, not the intention; and that is the alternative your principle needs. If that is your line, hold it and defend it, because my next question is why blame should attach to an act on the strength of an alternative that could not have made any difference to the world. And if you want to answer that the alternative here is thicker than I am allowing, then show me where, in this case, the thickness is.
