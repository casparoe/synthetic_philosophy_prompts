The question I set was:

"Mill is remembered as the great champion of individual liberty, and yet his later thinking about socialism is famously sympathetic. Using the Chapters on Socialism (the manuscript he was still working on when he died, later printed in the Fortnightly Review) along with the treatment of property and distribution in the Principles of Political Economy, set out (a) which criticisms of the existing competitive economic order Mill accepted, (b) why he nevertheless held back from endorsing socialism, and (c) what his claim that the distribution of wealth is a matter of human arrangement rather than something fixed by nature, unlike the laws of production, has to do with his stance on socialism."

And here's Dana's answer:

"Mill's whole attitude to socialism hangs on one distinction. The laws of production, he thinks, are like physical truths — we can't just decide to change them — but the distribution of wealth is a matter of human institution, something society can remake as it chooses. This distinction, which he works out in the socialist writings, is the wedge that lets him take the socialists seriously without throwing economics overboard, because it means the present inequality of wealth is not a fact of nature; it's just how our current arrangements happen to work.

On the criticisms, the Chapters on Socialism — which he published in 1869, and which really are the turning point in his views — show him giving away a lot of ground. He concedes that the position of the labouring classes under competition is bad, that they're exposed to market swings through no fault of their own, and that the existing distribution hands the owners of capital a power over workers that nothing but custom and law justifies. He's plainly drawn to workers' co-operatives and other experiments in association, which he sees as a way of getting the benefits of combination while keeping some independence.

Where he pulls back is over the individual. He isn't chiefly troubled by whether socialism could be made productive — he'll grant that a well-run socialist economy might produce plenty. What troubles him is that under common ownership the individual becomes dependent on society for everything, and that this kind of dependence stunts the growth of character and the freedom to think and live differently from your neighbours. He also keeps insisting that the case for socialism simply hasn't been proved yet, and that we need experiments before anybody commits.

So the distribution point cuts both ways. Because distribution isn't fixed, socialism is a live option; but because he prizes individual independence so highly, he won't come out and say it's the answer."
