I'm a science journalist who has spent the better part of a year reporting a long feature on the state of evolutionary psychology — not another "is it science?" piece, but an attempt to sort out which parts of the field's core program have actually earned their confidence and which parts are coasting on a good story. As part of that reporting, I sat in on a small workshop on evolutionary approaches to human behavior last spring, and on the second night I ended up at a dinner table between two researchers who went at each other for two hours. I took notes, and I'd like you to adjudicate. The first, whom I'll call Dana, is an anthropologist who runs field studies of foraging and life-history decisions in small-scale societies. The second, Ravi, is a cognitive scientist who runs reaction-time and card-selection experiments on how people reason about social exchange. Neither is a crank, neither was drunk, and I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it. Here is the exchange, compressed but faithful. (1) Dana: "The problem isn't that the hypotheses are evolutionary. It's that they're built so that nothing can count against them. Men weighting youth and fertility cues, women weighting resource-holding — if you find the pattern it's an adaptation, and if you don't find it in some society it's because the environment is novel or the mechanism got overridden. That's a story told after the fact." (2) Ravi: "That's a caricature, and the selection task is the counterexample. We made a prediction before running the studies: if social exchange is handled by a specialized system, facilitation should appear for rules about costs and benefits between people and not for arbitrary content. That prediction could have failed, and it has been replicated many times." (3) Dana: "It's been replicated, but it doesn't single out your module. At least three non-modular accounts predict the same pattern: people reason with learned schemas of permission and obligation rather than with a cheater detector; the effect is really about how readily the potential counterexample comes to mind given the framing; or, more aggressively, the task is a rational probabilistic inference and there's no facilitation to explain in the first place. A prediction that four theories all make doesn't discriminate among them. Risky on paper, uninformative in practice." (4) Ravi: "Then say my hypotheses are contested, not unfalsifiable. That's what a live research program looks like. And the demand has to cut both ways. Your optimality models treat the organism as though it were designed to maximize fitness while you stay ignorant of the genes and the development underneath — what your side of the field calls the phenotypic gambit. When a foraging model fails you add a constraint; when it succeeds you've shown adaptation. And you don't know the ancestral selection regime any better than I do." (5) Dana: "That's exactly where the asymmetry is. When I test an optimality model I can measure the thing that's supposed to be doing the selecting — calories in, calories out, who starves, who leaves surviving offspring. The ancestral environment isn't measurable, and that isn't a flaw in my study design; it's gone. So when you say a trait was shaped by selection during the Pleistocene, the premise that carries the whole argument is one you cannot independently check. You're left with the trait and a plausible story about why it would have paid." (6) Ravi: "You can check it indirectly — comparative work, the archaeological and fossil record, developmental evidence. If a mechanism is a species-typical adaptation it should be present in every population and visible early in development, and it shouldn't be rebuilt by local ecology. And mismatch hypotheses make real predictions: remove the modern novelties and the behavior should shift back toward the ancestral pattern." (7) Dana: "That's the move I trust least. Between them, 'adaptation' and 'mismatch' cover every possible result. If the mechanism shows up everywhere, it's a species-typical adaptation; if it tracks local ecology, the mechanism is intact but the inputs are calibrating it; if it's absent, the environment is novel. Unless you specify in advance which conditions count as ancestral and which count as novelties, the disjunction is unfalsifiable even though each arm of it is testable on its own." (8) Ravi: "Then make me specify in advance. We can preregister the predictions, define what counts as a novel environment, run the comparison across subsistence ecologies. But notice what's just happened: you've stopped saying my hypotheses can't fail and started saying I need to be clearer about when they would fail. Those are different charges, and only the second is a research agenda." (9) Dana: "I'll grant the label is blunt, and I'll grant that some work in the field makes real predictions that have survived — the disgust and pathogen-avoidance work, the kinship-terminology work, the predictions about incest avoidance. My complaint was never that no evolutionary hypothesis about the mind can be tested. It's that the famous claims get treated as settled when the evidence is thinner than the fame suggests, and that the incentives reward a good story over a discriminating test. And here's one thing you haven't addressed: when a specific module prediction comes back negative, the response is often to redraw the module's boundaries — the cheater detector quietly becomes a broader system for hazard and precaution, and suddenly the anomaly is evidence for the theory."

Please go through this move by move. Rate each of the nine moves on a scale running from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Tell me which criticisms land and which overreach, where the two of them are simply talking past each other (I have a suspicion that testability and evidential discrimination are being run together, but I want you to tell me whether that's right), and what the crux is — the one thing that, if it went one way rather than the other, would settle most of the disagreement. I'd also like to know what both of them are missing, and, if you're willing to commit, who is more right overall. One thing I don't want is a verdict on whether evolutionary psychology is a science in the abstract; if the argument really does collapse into a demarcation dispute in disguise, say so plainly and tell me what the more useful question would be instead. There's no need for citations or a reading list — I'm after the argumentative structure, and I'll chase the sources down myself. Please write in American English, and gloss any technical term as you go, since this has to survive editing for a general-readership magazine.
