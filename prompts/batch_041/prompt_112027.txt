I am a retired population biologist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy of science, and I want to try the kind of two-way case analysis that field does so well.

The case is the reception of Arthur Jensen's 1969 Harvard Educational Review article, "How Much Can We Boost IQ and Scholastic Achievement?", in which he argued that the Black–White gap in measured IQ was substantially heritable and that the failure of compensatory education to close it was therefore unsurprising. I do not want a moral verdict on Jensen, nor a survey of the ensuing controversy; I want the conceptual anatomy.

Take the concept of heritability as the lens, and be exact about what the coefficient is — a ratio of variance components in a specified population, at a specified time, across the range of environments that population actually occupies — and what it is not: a property of a trait, a decomposition of any individual's phenotype, or a forecast of what an intervention would do to a population mean. Bring in the reaction-norm picture, on which a single genotype yields different phenotypes across environments (the familiar thought experiment of two genotypes raised along an environmental gradient makes the point cleanly), and use it to sort Jensen's inferential steps into three piles: those the concept licenses, those it merely fails to forbid, and those it contradicts. I am especially interested in the step from a within-population heritability estimate to a between-population explanation, and in what happens to the estimate as environmental variance is compressed.

Then reverse the lens. Does the episode reveal a limit in the concept itself? Is heritability merely silent where Jensen needed it to speak, or does its silence there tell us something about how such coefficients ought to be reported and consumed? Where would a better-specified question have made the dispute tractable, and where does the disagreement stop being about the coefficient at all?

Answer in no more than 650 words.
