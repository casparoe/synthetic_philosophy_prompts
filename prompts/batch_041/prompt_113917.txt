I'm writeing a novel and there's a dinner-party scene where an 18th-century historian — basically Gibbon, I'm not even pretending otherwise — gets cornered by people who've somehow read two hundred years of scholarship he never lived to see. I need his actual reactions, in character, not a summary of the debate. He can't just shrug; I need to know what he'd concede and where he'd dig his heels in.

Four things they'd put to him:

- The lead thing. Roman pipes, Roman wine, the senatorial class slowly pickling itself. Does he take that seriously, or is it exactly the kind of materialist explanation he'd treat as beneath the dignity of history? And here's the part I like: it's a rival to his own favourite cause, so does he welcome it or quietly resent it?

- Climate and plague — the sun going dim for a year or two in the sixth century, the plague that came after. Same question. Does his scheme have room for a cause that has nothing to do with anybody's character or choices, or does that break his whole way of writing history?

- The people who say nothing fell at all, it just changed: new kings, new elites, but the same farmers, same tax collectors, same life in most places. Is that a correction or an attack on the premise of his book? Which of his own claims could he give up without the whole thing collapsing, and which one would he defend to the death?

- The rude one. Is "decline" even a description? If somebody told him that calling it a decline just smuggles in his taste for the Antonines and good Latin prose, what's his reply?

Then what I actually need for the scene: in his own voice, does he say Rome fell? And what does he do with the east — the awkward fact that the empire he's burying kept going for another thousand years?

Last one, and it's the one I care about most. A character in the book argues that the fall is basically survivorship bias — a farmer in Gaul in 500 would have said nothing had ended, and we only see a fall because we've got a name and a date to hang it on. Would Gibbon find that clever, or a trick? And I've invented a piece of evidence to test him with: a run of tax receipts from a small Gallic town showing the same clerks, the same cycle, the same handwriting across 470 to 490. If that's the record, does his story survive it, or does he just say receipts are the wrong kind of evidence?

Keep him in his own voice — the irony, the footnote sneer, the polite contempt. Dont give me a survey of modern historians and no reading list, I've got enough books I havent read.
