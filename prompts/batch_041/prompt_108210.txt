I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I share a flat with a former quantitative trader; a recurring argument between us keeps ending the same way, with him saying that a rational agent simply maximizes expected value, as though that settled the matter. The latest occasion was a game he proposed: a fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads, and the payout doubles with each tails — one unit if heads on the first toss, two if the first is tails and the second is heads, four, eight, and so on — so that the expected payout is infinite. He maintains that because the expected value is infinite I should be willing to pay any finite sum to play, and he treats my refusal as a demonstration that I am being irrational rather than as a difficulty for his principle. I cannot tell whether he is making a deep point, a trivial one, or quietly changing the subject, and I would like that untangled before our next round.

What I want from you is a menu of the distinct precise claims that 'a rational agent maximizes expected value' could be making, laid out as separate readings rather than as a single explanation, with a verdict on each: which readings are true but empty (a stipulation about what the words will mean, say, or a tautology about preference satisfaction), which are substantive and defensible (a claim about long-run frequencies when the same gamble is repeated, for instance, or about the coherence of a set of preferences), and which are simply false. For each reading, please say what kind of 'expected value' it requires — money, utility, something else — and whether it treats the expectation as a guide to a single unrepeatable decision or only to sequences of decisions. I would also like you to tell me which readings would actually get him from 'the expectation is infinite' to 'you are irrational for refusing,' and what would have to be added for that step to work: is the move from money to utility a repair of the principle or a change of subject, does the argument need the game to be repeatable or the decision to be one among many, and does anything survive if the expectation is not even well defined?

It would help if you tested the readings against two contrasting cases as well: the one-off gamble in which he would stake a serious fraction of his savings on a favourable but volatile coin flip, where I think he is wrong, and the repeated small-stakes wager that he is plainly right about, since whatever reading makes him right in the second case is presumably the one he wants in the first. The final answer should read as a list I can bring to the kitchen table, and there is no need for references — I am interested in the arguments, not in who has made them.
