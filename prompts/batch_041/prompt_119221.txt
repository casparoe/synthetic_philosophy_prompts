The exam question was:

"Epiphenomenalism holds that conscious experiences (qualia) are caused by physical processes in the brain but do not themselves cause anything. Many philosophers think this can't be the whole story, because on this view our beliefs and reports about our own experiences would be untrustworthy. (a) Reconstruct the objection as carefully as you can. (b) Say precisely what the objection is supposed to show. (c) Give the epiphenomenalist's best reply and say how much it saves. About 400 words."

The student wrote:

"Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences, qualia, are caused by physical processes in the brain but have no causal effects of their own. When I wince at a pain, the real cause is the neural event, not the pain; the pain is a by-product, like the steam whistle on a locomotive.

The problem is that my beliefs and reports about my experiences are themselves physical events. If qualia are causally inert, then my belief that I'm seeing red can't have been caused by the redness of the experience. And if the judgment isn't caused by the experience, the experience can't be what makes the judgment true — the judgment would come out the same whether or not there was anything it was like to see red. So our judgments about consciousness would be systematically unreliable, which is bad news, since those judgments are the only evidence any of us has that qualia exist in the first place. So the objection shows epiphenomenalism is self-contradictory: you can only state the view by trusting the very judgments it says can't be trusted. And since it undercuts the evidence for its own central posit, it collapses into eliminativism — if the judgments are unreliable then we've got no reason to believe in qualia at all, so the epiphenomenalist ends up handing the eliminativist the conclusion for free.

The best reply is the correlation move. Even if the quale doesn't cause the judgment, the neural state that causes the judgment is the very state that causes the quale, so the mechanism producing my judgments tracks the presence of qualia via the physical side. Reliability doesn't require being caused by the thing you're judging about; it only requires that the judging mechanism be counterfactually sensitive to it. So the epiphenomenalist can keep insisting that qualia do no causal work while still holding that our beliefs about them are reliable. I think this basically works, though it does feel like a bullet being bitten."
