A twin study reports a heritability (h²) of about 0.65 for a measure of numerical reasoning. The sample was recruited from a single well-resourced metropolitan school district, where almost all the kids had access to similar schooling, nutrition, and after-school enrichment. The same study also reports that kids whose parents hold a bachelor's degree score, on average, about 0.4 SD higher on the measure than kids whose parents don't.

(a) What does the 0.65 figure actually quantify, and what does it not tell us about any one child?
(b) Can we conclude from the study that part of the average difference between the two parental-education groups is genetic? Explain.
(c) Suppose the district introduces a well-funded tutoring program. Based on the heritability estimate, what, if anything, can we predict about how much the program could raise scores? Explain your reasoning.

And here's what the student turned in:

---
(a) Heritability is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population that's attributable to genetic variance. So h² = 0.65 means that in this sample about 65% of the variance in numerical reasoning is associated with genetic differences, and the rest with environmental and other non-genetic sources. It's a population-level statistic, so it says nothing about any individual kid — you can't look at one child and say 65% of their score is genetic. That's the classic error and I'm not making it.

Because the estimate is specific to the population and the range of environments that were sampled, we shouldn't just generalize it to other populations or treat it as a fixed property of the trait. Heritability is not the same as genetic determination or immutability: a trait can be highly heritable and still shift a lot if you change the environment. So 0.65 doesn't mean numerical reasoning is set in stone.

(b) The study does support a genetic contribution to the between-group difference, at least in part. Numerical reasoning is substantially heritable within this population, and parental education is itself partly heritable (it correlates with genetic variants that affect cognition), so it's reasonable to infer that some of the 0.4 SD gap reflects genetic differences between the two groups rather than purely environmental advantage.

(c) The tutoring program could still raise scores, because heritability doesn't mean the trait can't change. But here's the subtlety: because the sample came from an unusually uniform, high-resource environment, environmental variance was restricted, which means the 0.65 probably understates how much genes matter. In a more diverse environment, where kids face very different conditions, genes would likely account for an even larger share. So I'd predict the tutoring program has a modest ceiling — it can help, but it probably won't close gaps that are substantially genetic.
