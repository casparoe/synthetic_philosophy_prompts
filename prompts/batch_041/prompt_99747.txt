At a departmental colloquium, two colleagues got into an extended dispute about Benjamin Franklin's project of moral perfection, and since I was sitting between them they asked me to referee. I have tried to reproduce their exchange accurately. The moves were as follows.

(1) A said that Franklin's project is a category mistake. Franklin treats morality as a checklist of thirteen habits to be ticked off, when moral goodness is a matter of practical wisdom and responsiveness to reasons. The giveaway is his own admission that he could never master order; if the method fails at the most mundane virtue, it is not a method for moral perfection but for a certain kind of tidiness.

(2) B replied that this misreads the project. Franklin is not offering a theory of moral goodness; he is offering a technology of self-improvement in the tradition of spiritual exercises. His candour about order is not a refutation but a demonstration of the method's diagnostic honesty. And his virtues are not arbitrary: they are organised around usefulness to self and others, which is a recognisable consequentialist criterion, not a mere checklist.

(3) A came back that this makes the project something other than moral perfection. If the criterion is usefulness, Franklin is a utilitarian who happens to use the language of virtue. Worse, his humility was added only after a friend told him he appeared proud; so the virtue is a response to social feedback about his reputation, not an inner transformation. That makes the whole project about managing appearances.

(4) B answered that this is a genetic fallacy. The origin of a practice does not settle its value. Franklin's method is social and feedback-driven because character is social; the fact that he added humility when a friend pointed out his pride is evidence that the method works, not that it is hollow. And his humility is not merely reputational: he cannot boast of the reality, but he credits the appearance with making him a better conversationalist.

(5) A pressed that this concedes the point. If the best that can be said is that Franklin acquired the appearance of humility, then the project is about appearance all the way down. And if order, the virtue he ranked so highly, was the one he could never master, then the method cannot even handle the most basic self-management. He should have called it the art of improvement, not moral perfection.

(6) B replied that the failure of one virtue does not invalidate the method; it shows that the method is diagnostic, not miraculous. And perfection in this context need not mean sinlessness; it can mean a regulative ideal. Franklin's project is a serious attempt at self-cultivation, and the fact that he never fully succeeded is part of its honesty.

They both looked at me. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, plausible, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. In particular, address whether Franklin's project is best understood as virtue ethics, consequentialism, or something else; whether the order failure undermines the method; whether the humility addition is a problem; and whether moral perfection is a coherent goal. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
