I run an evening continuing-education course in a science-and-society programme, and next week's ninety-minute session is on whether DNA really is a code, or carries information in any sense worth the name. My students are mostly software engineers and product managers with no biology since school. They are quick, they argue, and they arrive already convinced that DNA is code and genes are programs. I do not want you to explain the topic to me. I want a teaching plan.

Set out the order in which the components of the idea must be introduced, and say why each depends on the one before it. Identify the single example that should carry the whole session, and spell out what that example must have and what it must lack if it is to do its work. I have a hunch that it should be a case in which one and the same stretch of DNA yields different products in different cellular contexts, but tell me whether that is right; and if a different example would carry the session better, say so and say why. The codon table and the central-dogma arrow diagram are not to do the carrying — diagnose for me why they would fail. List the misconceptions the students will bring or will form, and give the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Tell me the one question to ask at the end that would show the idea has landed rather than merely been recognised. And tell me what to leave out at this level, saying openly that I am leaving it out.

The plan must get across the following, and must make the dependencies among them explicit: the difference between information in a plain difference-making sense and information in a content-bearing sense that presupposes a sender, a receiver and a code; the arbitrariness of the genetic code and its dependence on the translation machinery, so that the mapping is not a property of the DNA itself; the resulting thought that sequence-specific causation is real while content-bearing information is either absent from the DNA or conferred on it from outside; and the parity point that DNA is one causal factor among many in development, so that any privilege it enjoys needs an argument. There is also a moment, I suspect, when the students will decide the whole question is merely verbal; work out where that moment falls and how to convert it into the real question.

Do not give me readings, references or citations; there is no internet in the room and I am not asking for a bibliography. Write in British English. Finish by saying how confident you are in the plan and naming the single claim in it about which you are least confident.
