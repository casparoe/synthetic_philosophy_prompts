I'm on the grant committee of a small foundation that funds longtermist / far-future stuff (yes, one of those), and I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. I'm a longtime LessWrong/ACX lurker, so I know just enough jargon to get myself into trouble, but I want the memo to actually be right rather than just sound clever.

The situation: we have $10M and it all goes to one grant. Two finalists, and the disagreement between them turned out to be wierdly deep.

Proposal A is the boring one -- fund a program we have decent evidence actually works, helping maybe 100k people over the next few years. It'll definately do what it says. But the person pitching it makes a bigger argument too: they say we should assume humanity is probably near the end of its run, because if you're a random human drawn from all the humans who will ever live, you'd expect to land somewhere in the middle rather than near the start, so the number of people still to come is probably small -- meaning a longtermist dollar buys less than a dollar spent on people who are already here. (I think this is the doomsday argument or a cousin of it.)

Proposal B is the longtermist one: basically a bet that civilization survives long enough to get enormous. The pitch isn't just "if the future is big, this is worth a lot." It's that we should be near-certain the future IS big, because of the self-indication thing: there are two possibilities, a small future (a few billion more people total) and a huge one (10^30-ish); since you're an observer, and the huge world contains vastly more observers, the huge world is vastly more likely to be the one containing you. So B's payoff is near-certain and it crushes A.

So the two proposals are running opposite anthropic assumptions, and I can't tell whether that makes the whole comparison a wash or whether one of them is clearly the right way to think. My gut says the second guy is pulling something, but I know the first guy's assumption is the one that hands you the doomsday result, so I don't want to just wave it away. There's also a bonus argument from B that I can't tell is profound or confused: they say that since (under their assumption) you're more likely to exist in a world where B is funded and works, funding B makes you more likely to exist, so we should fund it partly for our own sake. That smells wrong but I can't articulate why. And one more wrinkle: our foundation's whole stated mission is longtermist, so voting for A feels like we'd be betraying the point of the place.

Write the opinion I'll circulate. Recommend a concrete course of action -- A, B, or something in between, and if it's in between, spell out exactly what the conditions are -- with the reasoning. Take both anthropic assumptions seriously instead of just asserting that the weird one is weird; say which assumption each proposal is actually running, which one you think we should adopt (actually pick one, don't just tell me it's contested), and how much work the reference-class choice is doing here.

And tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if it went the other way, would flip your recommendation, and where is your reasoning most likely to be wrong?
