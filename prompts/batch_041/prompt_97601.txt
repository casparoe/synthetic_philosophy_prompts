ok so I need help with something fairly niche and I'm a bit out of my depth. I just got appointed to the science advisory board of a mid-sized foundation that hands out basic-research grants (I'm a cell biologist, thats how I got the seat), and my first real task is to write and circulate the boards written opinion before our vote next Thursday. I'd rather not wing it.

The decision: we have one big grant cycle this year, two finalists in fundamental physics, and money for exactly one of them.

Proposal A comes from a group that says the only way to make progress on the deepest questions is to commit hard to a specific theoretical framework, one that posits an entire new family of unobservable entities, and let that framework dictate which experiment to build. They're up front that if the framework is wrong the whole program is a very expensive dead end.

Proposal B is from a group that says we should do a model-independent measurement — designed so that whatever the truth turns out to be, we learn something that will still matter in twenty years. They argue that the history of their field is basically a graveyard of gorgeous theories that made sharp novel predictions and then got thrown out entirely, so betting on any one framework is a sucker's game.

I need the memo to actually pick one. Not 'both have merit, it depends' — a real recommendation, the reasoning laid out, and a sentence on what we'd be giving up by not funding the other one. If the honest answer is 'fund B now and tell A to come back next cycle with a leaner ask', thats fine too, just say it and say why.

Three things I want you to deal with head-on, because the board keeps going in circles on them:

1. One member keeps repeating that the only thing that should make us believe a theory's unobservable entities exist is a track record of novel predictions, and since so many past theories predicted brilliantly and turned out to be describing stuff that doesn't exist, we should never fund work that commits to unobservables at all. Another member thinks thats backwards — refuse to commit and you never make progress. I want the strongest honest version of the first view in the memo (I suspect its stronger than the second member wants to admit), then where it goes wrong, or what it proves too much.

2. Somebody floated a proposed standing rule for us: only fund a project if it would still have been worth doing even if the theory behind it turned out to be false. Is that a genuinely good decision rule, or does it quietly smuggle in an answer to whether theories are describing reality or just organizing data? I honestly cant tell, and I don't want to sign onto something that decides a philosophical question by accident.

3. I keep worrying the two proposals aren't even answering the same question — one is about what to believe about the world, the other about what to do next in the lab — and that we're being forced into a false choice. If thats right, say so, and tell me how to structure the recommendation around it instead.

And I'll be honest about why I care: my own subfield went through exactly this. The central entity in the model everyone used for fifteen years got quietly dropped, and the people who built careers on it are fine, they just moved to the next thing, and nobody ever asks whether they'd been right. So I'm primed to think the pessimist has a point. Please don't just flatter that instinct.

One more thing — please don't give me a reading list or cite papers, I won't be able to check any of it before Thursday and it'll just make me look like I'm bluffing. Just the memo, short enough that I could read it out loud in about ten minutes.
