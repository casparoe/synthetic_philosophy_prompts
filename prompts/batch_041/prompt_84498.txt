A friend in my decision-theory reading group has an argument that I suspect proves too much, but I cannot find the loose joint.

(1) If a counterparty can relieve you of money by a finite series of trades, each of which you would accept at the moment it is offered, then your preferences are irrational.
(2) If your preferences run in a cycle — you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — then such a counterparty exists. Give you C, then offer to swap C for B for a small fee; you accept, since you prefer B to C. Offer to swap B for A for a small fee; you accept. Offer to swap A for C for a small fee; you accept. You are back where you started, minus three fees, and you said yes every time.
(3) So cyclic preferences are irrational.
(4) Nothing in (1) or (2) uses the fact that the cycle is simultaneous. All it uses is that each successive trade is attractive at the moment it is offered. The same counterparty can work on someone whose preferences are perfectly coherent at every moment but shift over time. Suppose she wants a thing now and will be indifferent to it later: the counterparty sells it to her now at a price she is glad to pay, then buys it back later at a lower price she is equally glad to take. She ends with exactly what she started with, minus the difference, and she accepted both trades. Repeat at leisure.
(5) So someone whose preferences change over time is irrational in just the way the cyclic agent is.
(6) That is absurd. Revising what you prefer in the light of evidence, or discounting the future, or simply growing tired, is not a failure of rationality; if it were, rationality would demand that a person's preferences be frozen forever.
(7) So either (1) is false, or the move from (1) and (2) to (5) is illegitimate.

I would give up (5) before (1) or (3), because I still want the pump to count against cyclic preferences, but I cannot see what licenses the split: as far as I can tell, (4) is just (2) with the word simultaneous crossed out. Which step should go, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, is there a principled difference between being caught in a synchronic cycle and being caught across time, or does saving (3) from (5) require an extra premise that was quietly doing the work all along?
