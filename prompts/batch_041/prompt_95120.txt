A chapter in an introductory collection on self-knowledge that I have been reading opens with Moore's paradox, and before I take anything from it I would like an audit of its exposition. The chapter's account runs roughly as follows.

It introduces the paradox with the sentence "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining," which it presents as Moore's own example. That sentence, it says, is self-contradictory and therefore cannot be true: in asserting that it is raining I have already committed myself to believing that it is, so the second half of the sentence takes back exactly what the first half asserts. What the example is said to reveal is that we have infallible knowledge of our own beliefs — because I cannot be mistaken about what I believe, a sentence reporting my belief is one I could never be in a position to get wrong, and Moore's sentence is the one thing I could never sincerely utter. The chapter then says that the same structure appears in "It is raining, but I do not know that it is raining," and concludes from this that the real subject of the paradox is the word "know" rather than belief. Its closing move is a dissolution: since asserting that it is raining implies that I believe that it is raining, the second conjunct merely cancels what the first one commits me to, and once this is noticed there is no paradox left to explain. Two details are offered in support: that the past-tense and second-person counterparts behave differently — "It was raining, but I did not believe that it was raining" and "It is raining, but you do not believe that it is raining" are, it says, entirely unobjectionable — and that the difficulty therefore lies in the meaning of the first-person pronoun.

I am not a philosopher and cannot see where the exposition goes wrong, though my impression is that philosophers treat the paradox as a live and unresolved problem rather than a confusion to be cleared up. What I want is an audit I can act on. Take the chapter's claims one at a time and tell me, for each, whether a philosopher working on this paradox would accept it as stated, accept it only with a qualification, or reject it — and where a claim should be rejected, what the correct statement is and why the difference matters. I am particularly interested in whether the closing dissolution follows from the parts of the exposition that survive your audit, or whether it depends on the parts that do not. I am not asking for a reading list or a survey of who has said what; I want to know what I can carry away. If you can, finish by naming the single claim in the chapter most likely to mislead a reader who took it away.

Two further questions, if the audit can accommodate them. First, does the absurdity belong to the sentence or to the act of asserting it? In a conditional such as "If it is raining and I do not believe that it is, I shall be caught without an umbrella," the same words seem to pass without absurdity and nothing is asserted; if that is right, the chapter's appeal to the meaning of the first-person pronoun looks like the wrong kind of explanation. But I would like to know whether the absurdity really does disappear whenever the sentence is not asserted, or whether it survives in some embedded positions, and what either answer shows. Second, I have been experimenting with a language model that will produce "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining" on request, and will just as readily produce "It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining." The system neither asserts nor believes anything in the chapter's sense, and nothing about either output strikes me as absurd. Does the paradox get any grip on a case like that, and if it does not, does that tell for the chapter's account or against it?
