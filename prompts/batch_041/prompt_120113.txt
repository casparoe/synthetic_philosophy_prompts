Exam question:

In debates over whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler, some historians emphasize Hitler's contingent role, while others emphasize structural conditions. Consider the following claim:

"Even if Hitler had been killed in 1930, something very much like Nazi Germany would still have arisen, because the structural conditions—economic crisis, weak Weimar institutions, right-wing nationalism, and the Nazi Party apparatus—made it inevitable."

Assess this claim. In your answer:

(a) Distinguish a Great Man explanation from a structural explanation.
(b) Explain what it means to say that structural conditions "made an outcome inevitable," and distinguish inevitability from high probability.
(c) Using the notion of a robust process—a process that would produce roughly the same outcome across a wide range of small perturbations in initial conditions—evaluate whether the cited structural conditions establish robustness.
(d) State what kind of counterfactual evidence would be needed to support the claim, and whether such evidence is available in principle.

Student's answer:

"The Great Man theory, associated with Carlyle, holds that history is driven by exceptional individuals; structural explanation holds that outcomes are produced by impersonal social, economic, and institutional forces. The claim that Nazi Germany would have arisen without Hitler is structural. It does not deny that Hitler mattered; it says his role was replaceable. The structural conditions listed—economic crisis, weak Weimar institutions, nationalism, and the Nazi Party—are necessary conditions. Without them, Hitler could not have come to power. But the claim goes further: they were sufficient. If they were sufficient, then Hitler was not a cause at all, only a symptom. To say the conditions 'made it inevitable' is to say that, given those conditions, the outcome had probability 1. That is stronger than saying it was likely. The notion of a robust process helps here. A robust process yields the same outcome under many perturbations. If the Nazi seizure of power was robust, then removing Hitler would be a small perturbation, and some other leader would have taken his place. The Party apparatus and the nationalist movement would have produced a functionally equivalent regime. So the structural claim is plausible. A critic might say that Hitler's specific ideology and tactical skill were not replaceable, but that is a Great Man argument, which assumes individuals are irreplaceable. Since the structural conditions were sufficient, the Great Man view is epiphenomenal. To support the counterfactual, we would need a possible-worlds comparison, but historical counterfactuals are difficult to test directly. So the claim is partly metaphysical rather than purely empirical. In conclusion, the structural explanation is stronger because it explains the broad pattern, while the Great Man explanation only describes the surface."
