You are an epistemologist specializing in the rationality of belief under higher-order evidence, and you are unusually good at the particular task below: converting a slippery philosophical question into a small set of sub-questions, each with a number attached, and then being candid about which of those numbers is doing the work. Think carefully and at length before committing to anything, and begin with a brief brainstorm of the positions and cases that divide the field. Do not cite papers, authors, or reading lists, and do not appeal to what is standard or received; everything must rest on your own reasoning about the cases. Do not hedge your way out of the question: if you think the question is partly verbal, or that it splits into several readings, say so and then quantify over the readings rather than declining to give a number.

Background. A belief is epistemically akratic when the agent holds it while believing that holding it is epistemically irrational, that is, unjustified by her evidence. Consider two claims:

W: Epistemic akrasia is sometimes rational. There are cases in which a fully rational agent may believe p while believing that believing p is irrational.

S: There are cases in which rationality requires the akratic state. An agent's total evidence demands that she believe p while believing that believing p is irrational.

Fix ideas with a case of the following shape.

The track-record case. Mira reasons carefully from first-order evidence E and arrives at credence 0.85 in p. Stipulate that 0.85 is the apt credence on E, and that her reasoning from E to p, considered on its own, is impeccable. She then learns a track-record fact: over a large, well-documented sample of problems of just this form, her first-order reasoning has been correct only about 40 percent of the time. Crucially, the statistic was compiled by Mira's own reasoning, using the very faculty whose track record is now in question; she has no independent source for it, though she also has no reason to think the computation was botched. Variant (a): the same track-record fact arrives from an independent, credible source. Variant (b): the track record concerns the evidence rather than her competence; she learns that E-type evidence has been misleading in the sample far more often than she had assumed.

Your task:

1. Brainstorm briefly: what positions are available, and which features of the case do the real work?

2. State your credence in W and your credence in S.

3. Decompose S into the sub-questions it depends on. Give each a rough probability and one line of justification. Address at least: whether a fact about the reliability of a process is evidence about p at all, or only about the standing of her belief; whether a self-generated track record can defeat the very reasoning that generated it, or whether the case collapses under its own weight; whether an agent can rationally occupy both levels at once, or whether level-splitting is incoherent; whether there is a unique rational response to the total evidence or instead a permissible range, and whether permissivism would make akrasia trivially available; whether higher-order evidence should be subtracted from first-order confidence or should displace the first-order evidence with evidence about the agent; and how much variants (a) and (b) move the verdict.

4. Show the combination explicitly, saying whether you multiply, average, or weight the sub-questions, and why.

5. Identify the sub-question you are least confident in, and the argument or evidence that would move your final number most.

6. I put my credence in S at 0.35; a colleague puts hers at 0.85. Tell me where our disagreement most likely lives: in the case, in the coherence of the akratic state, or in the notion of rationality in play.

7. Finally, say what would count as resolving the question, and whether you think it is resolvable at all.
