My seminar on animal minds ended this term on insects and I want to close with a poem, so I'm outsourcing it. The knot we couldn't cut: there are something on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any moment, a billion billion give or take a few orders of magnitude. Nobody in the room thinks it's likely that any particular ant or fruit fly has an inner life. But unlikely isn't impossible, and if you put even a one-in-a-thousand chance on a single insect having a life that can go better or worse, and multiply that by a number that size, the expected insect suffering and insect flourishing on Earth starts to dwarf the whole rest of the animal kingdom. Half the room says fine, that's just what the arithmetic says, swallow it. The other half says the arithmetic is the mistake: sentience probably isn't a switch you assign a probability to and then multiply, it's more like a dimmer, and if what's there is a faint flicker rather than nothing, there's no reason the flicker has to count as zero until it crosses some threshold.

Write me a poem, no fixed form, that stages that standoff without settling it. The constraint I actually care about: let the form do the multiplying. I was thinking stanzas that double in length as the poem goes, while the individual lines get shorter and thinner, so the poem swells and dims at the same time — which is basically the whole fight. You'll land around 15 lines if you start from one, but use your judgement. Someone who sat in the seminar should be able to tell which move is the multiplier's and which is the dimmer's, and the poem should leave the two next to each other at the end rather than picking a winner. Don't name-drop any philosophers, just write the thing.
