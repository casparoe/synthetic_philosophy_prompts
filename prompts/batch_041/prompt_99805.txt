A close friend recently told me she would rather not look at a set of results that might tell her something important about her future, and I told her this was straightforwardly irrational. The argument I gave her — and have been giving freely since, in that dispute and two others — is that for anyone who maximizes expected utility and updates by conditionalization, the expected value of free information can never be negative, since she can always ignore what she learns and choose exactly what she would have chosen anyway. I would now like an honest audit of the general claim, not of the narrow theorem I keep paraphrasing.

The claim I actually hold is this: there is no rational case for preferring ignorance; acquiring a piece of information and then acting cannot leave a rational agent worse off in expectation than acting without it; so anyone who says she would rather not know is either confused or protecting something other than her own welfare. Tell me what would count against it, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, and locate the defeaters in the assumptions that are doing the work rather than in the theorem itself. For each of the following, describe the agent or the world in which the information is genuinely harmful, and say whether a defender of my claim should concede it as a real limitation or dismiss it as changing the subject: an agent who does not maximize expected utility, for instance one who is averse to ambiguity, prone to regret, or weights probabilities nonlinearly; an agent whose preferences, or whose available actions, change once she learns the fact; information about the agent's own future choices or her own dispositions; a setting in which others observe what she learns or how she acts and respond to it; information that is not free but merely unpriced, or whose processing consumes the very resources it was meant to conserve; and an agent who is not a good Bayesian and will over- or under-react to what she learns. Keep two distinctions clean throughout — between the ex ante expected value of information and how things turn out ex post, and between the value of information to the person who receives it and its value to whoever decides whether it is produced or disclosed.

I would be most moved by a case in which the information is genuinely available, genuinely free, and still leaves the agent worse off by her own standard of welfare. And I want a verdict on whether the claim survives in any form worth stating, or whether the words free and you can always ignore it are carrying all the weight — in which case I would like to be told plainly that the result was always about an idealized agent and that my appeal to it in my friend's case was doing no real work. If a defensible weaker claim lies nearby, say what it is and what it costs me to hold it.
