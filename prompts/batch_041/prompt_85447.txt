I teach a seminar on statues, street names, and the ethics of commemoration, and I want to close the term with Kazuo Ishiguro's The Buried Giant — not as a reward after the hard theory, but as a candidate contribution to it. I have read it three times and I still cannot say what position it takes, and I have begun to suspect that my indecision is itself a philosophical mistake about what a novel of this kind is doing. I would like you to take it seriously as an argument and tell me where it lands. Work as a philosopher of collective memory who is also a careful critic: someone who can hold a conceptual distinction and a scene in view at once without letting either swallow the other.

Here is the framing from the seminar, so you know what I am asking the book to speak to. Our debate has three moving parts. First, what commemoration is. On one view it is memory-work aimed at the past, an acknowledgement owed to the dead. On another it is addressed forward, to a public that does not yet exist, and functions as a standing commitment that an event will not be forgotten — in which case a monument is less a record than a promise, and a promise can be broken by those who inherit it. Second, what erasure is. We have looked at damnatio memoriae, the official practice of striking a name from inscriptions, which shows that erasure is not the absence of memory-politics but its most emphatic form: you can only erase what someone took the trouble to inscribe, and the erasure itself has to be decreed. Third, what a memorial is for. We have discussed monuments built to disappear on purpose, sinking into the ground as visitors mark them, so that the structure is gone and the remembering is left with whoever happened to be standing there. I raise these three because Ishiguro's mist seems to me a fourth thing that none of them covers, and I want you to say what it is.

The novel hands you a thought experiment no real society can run: one physical cause of forgetting, deliberately installed by a king after a massacre, falling on victors and vanquished alike, and then deliberately removed. Read it against the seminar's question — to whom is commemoration owed, and what exactly is wrong with forgetting? — and give me a verdict. Before you commit, spend a few lines laying out at least three candidate readings and say why you are rejecting the ones you reject; then choose, and defend the choice.

I want a definite position, not a survey. If you conclude that the novel refuses to adjudicate, that is itself a claim and you must argue for it as one, saying what the refusal accomplishes and what it costs. Several things must be faced rather than smoothed over. Take Wistan's position seriously as the strongest anti-commemoration argument in the book: he wants the mist lifted not to honour the dead but to restore a live grievance, and he is candid that what follows is war. Say whether the novel lets him win that argument or quietly stacks the deck against him. Address the fantasy device head-on: does a single, literal, symmetric cause of forgetting make the thought experiment cleaner, or does it dodge the real difficulty, which is that actual forgetting is partial, interested, and asymmetric — some people's monuments stand and others' do not? Consider whether the mist is better understood as amnesty than as erasure, and what it means that this amnesty requires no testimony and no acknowledgement from anyone. And consider the title's image against the seminar's: a giant that is buried but not marked, not destroyed, not decreed out of memory — still there, stirring. Ask whether that is a fourth model of commemoration or a picture of what commemoration is trying to prevent.

Point me to the three or four scenes the seminar should spend its time on, and say what each one does for your reading. The final crossing, where the couple's continued union depends on a ferryman's assessment of their love and their son turns out to have died of plague years before, looks central to me, but tell me if I am wrong about that. End by naming the place where the novel resists your reading, and say what that resistance shows — then say what the book adds to the debate that the arguments alone cannot supply.

Write roughly 1,500 words. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; argue from the novel and from the concepts. Do not quote the text — I have it in front of me, and the seminar is about the reading, not the wording. Where you are unsure of a plot detail, flag it rather than reconstructing it confidently.
