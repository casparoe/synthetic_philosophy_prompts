I have been trying to understand Good's theorem on the value of information, and I constructed the following argument in a seminar. I now suspect it proves too much, but I cannot locate the flaw.

(1) Good's theorem states that the expected value of information is non-negative: for any decision problem, any possible piece of information, and any prior, the expected utility of the strategy "receive the information, then decide" is at least as great as the expected utility of "decide without the information," provided the information is free.

(2) Therefore, a rational agent should always prefer to receive information, if it is free.

(3) If a rational agent should always prefer to receive information, then she should never avoid information.

(4) But there are cases in which it seems rational to avoid information: a patient may refuse a genetic test that would reveal a fatal, untreatable disease, because knowing would blight the years she has left; a person may decline to learn the ending of a novel; a student may avoid checking her exam score until after the weekend.

(5) Therefore, either Good's theorem is false, or its implication (2) is false.

Where does this argument go wrong? If I have to give up one of the steps, I would rather keep (1) and (4) and give up (2), but I want to know what that costs and whether there is a better diagnosis. Please identify the step you reject, explain why it looked plausible, and set out what rejecting it commits me to.
