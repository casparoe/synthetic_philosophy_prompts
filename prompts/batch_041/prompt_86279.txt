I've been going down a philosophy-of-death rabbit hole ever since a friend in my rat-adjacent book club gave me his steelman of Epicurus. His version, roughly: Epicurus proved that death can't be bad for you, because badness needs a subject who experiences it, and when you're dead there's no you left to do the experiencing — so the whole death-is-nothing-to-us line isn't a comforting slogan, it's basically a proof. The only live question, he says, is whether the moment of dying itself involves suffering, and most contemporary philosophers agree Epicurus was essentially right and the rest is just details. He also tacked on that fearing death is exactly as irrational as mourning the time before you were born, so the fear is just a bias, full stop.

I can't tell if this is airtight or if he's smuggled half the conclusion in. I'm enough of a rationalist that death seems obviously bad to me, but I know that could be my own bias talking. So: go through his summary claim by claim and tell me what someone working on this would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what's flatly wrong. Specifically, does the "badness requires an experiencer" premise really rule out death being bad for you, or does the deprivation way of thinking about it slip past that? And is there some puzzle about when the harm of death even occurs — I half-remember something about it not being before you die, not while you're dead, and not after, which sounds like it should be impossible but people apparently take it seriously. Also, does the past/future symmetry he brings up actually cut the way he says, or is the real story the asymmetry — that we feel something about future nonexistence but nothing about the past? No citations or reading lists needed, I just want to know which parts of his summary survive and which parts are him doing the work for Epicurus.
