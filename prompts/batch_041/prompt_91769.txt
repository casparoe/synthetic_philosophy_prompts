I run the platform team at a mid-sized SaaS company and I chair our blameless incident reviews, and the recurring problem isn't finding causes, it's settling arguments about them. After an outage we'll have three or four rival stories up on the whiteboard and the meeting ends up ratifying whichever one the most senior or most confident person happens to be attached to. Last month we burned two hours on a payments outage arguing whether it was a race condition in our code, a bad config push, or the cloud provider throttling us, and we went with the race condition because it was the most interesting, then shipped a fix that did nothing. I was moaning about this to a friend who did a philosophy degree and she said what I was after was inference to the best explanation, which I've read about but have never managed to make concrete. So: please turn it into a decision procedure I can actually run in a 45-minute review with six engineers, no statistician in the room. I want to know what to write down for each candidate explanation, what order to compare them in, what counts as a decisive test we can run before the meeting ends, and — the bit I care about most — how to tell an explanation that's genuinely better supported from one that's merely prettier or more satisfying to tell.

Then run the procedure on two or three hypothetical incidents so I can see it work: (a) an outage where an elegant single-cause story and a boring two-cause story both fit every scrap of evidence we have; (b) one where an exciting explanation — someone was attacking us, or a subtle data race — fits one striking piece of evidence but predicts nothing else, while a dull explanation predicts a pile of small things we did actually observe; and (c) a case where the procedure should refuse to name any cause at all and output 'unknown, here's what we'll watch next time' instead, even though the room is desperate for closure. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as a checklist, and say plainly what the procedure can't do — I'd much rather it told me when it's useless than gave me false confidence. Two more constraints: it has to survive a room where people will try to game it by dressing up a guess as an 'explanation', and it can't assume we have clean logs or reproducible failures, because half our incidents happen once and we never see them again. Write the whole thing in British English.
