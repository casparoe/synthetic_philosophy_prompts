A friend in my semantics class has an argument that the lying/misleading distinction is empty and I honestly cant tell whether it's a trick or just right. (1) To lie is to assert something you believe false. (2) What you assert isn't fixed by the literal words alone; its fixed by what a reasonable, competent hearer would take you to be putting on the table as true. That's just what asserting is -- a public act aimed at an audience. (3) In all the parade cases -- the reference letter that says only that the candidate is always punctual, the answer "some of the students passed" when you know they all did, "I'd rather not say where I was last night" -- a reasonable hearer takes the speaker to have put the false thing on the table. That isn't a side effect, it's the whole point of the manoeuvre. (4) So in each of those cases the speaker asserted something false, and believed it false. (5) So they lied. (6) So there's no moral difference between lying and merely misleading; the distinction is an artifact of a literalist theory of assertion. I want to keep (1) and I basically believe (2), but the conclusion eats everything: on this reasoning sarcasm, hyperbole, and answering "fine" when you're not are lies too, and the word stops doing any work. So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost -- if I drop (2), what fixes what I said? If I keep (2) but block the move to (4), how can misleading be wrong at all? And please keep straight which parts of this are actually settled and which are live disputes among people who work on assertion and implicature -- I don't want a confident answer that dresses up a contested position as the consensus.
