I'm putting together the final for an upper-level decision theory seminar and I need one essay question plus a model answer and a marking scheme. Topic is the coherence argument for expected-utility maximization — the money-pump thing for preferences and the Dutch-book thing for credences. Please don't just ask them to state the argument; every year half the class writes down the intransitivity pump and stops.

Here's the crux I actually want tested. The argument seems to show that if your preferences cycle (or your credences violate the probability axioms) you'll lose out — someone can take money off you for sure. But "you'll lose out" isn't obviously the same claim as "your preferences are irrational," and the bridge between them is doing a suspicious amount of work. I want the question to make students walk across that bridge and say whether it holds.

Two things I want them to have to reckon with specifically:

(a) The forewarned agent. Suppose the person with the cycle A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A has read all the same papers the students have, and announces up front that they see the pump coming and simply won't play — they'll refuse to trade at every step. Does the argument still land on them? Or does it only ever bite someone naive enough not to notice the sequence? The students should have to say what the money-pump argument would have to establish about the preference state itself (rather than about the agent's trading behaviour or strategy) in order for the forewarned agent to be irrational anyway, and then say whether it establishes that.

(b) The comparison with credences. It's tempting to say the Dutch-book argument has an easier time, because the bad bet loses in every possible world, so it looks like a defect in the credences as a representation of the world rather than merely in the agent's betting behaviour. I want them to pressure-test that: is the "sure loss" in the credence case really a defect in the state, or does it lean on the same assumption that the agent accepts every bet offered? And does that make the two arguments more parallel than they first look, or less?

Frame the question around a concrete agent with the cycle who's fully forewarned. It should be answerable in 90 minutes. I want a model answer around 650 words, and a marking scheme built on 4 or 5 must-hit moves, 20 marks total, with a one-line note for each on the specific fudge a B answer makes — the move that looks like it engages the objection but actually dodges it. If there's a move an A answer has to make that students reliably skip, flag that in the scheme too.
