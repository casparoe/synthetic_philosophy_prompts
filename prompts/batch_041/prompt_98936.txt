I keep encountering the phrase "no taxation without representation," and I have now watched it pressed into service in three different arguments. In a debate over whether non-citizen residents should be able to vote, an interlocutor used it to claim that anyone who pays sales, property, or payroll taxes thereby earns a vote. In a discussion of lowering the voting age, someone used it to argue that sixteen-year-olds who hold jobs are taxed and must therefore be enfranchised. And in a conversation about compulsory voting, it was invoked to say that voting is a duty owed in return for representation rather than a right one may simply decline. I suspect these are not one claim but several, and that at most one of them is what the slogan originally asserted.

Please lay out, as a menu of distinct precise theses rather than a single interpretation, what that sentence could actually be claiming. I would like the readings organized along the axes on which they differ: what "taxation" ranges over (income only, or all taxes including sales and property; paid by the person or merely incident on them); what "representation" means (an actual seat at the ballot, having one's interests advocated, being governed by officials one can remove, or the "virtual representation" of being counted in someone else's constituency); whether the slogan asserts a necessary condition, a sufficient condition, or a biconditional; whether the bearer of the claim is an individual or a collectivity such as a colony or class; and whether the "no" expresses a moral verdict about legitimacy or a prudential prediction about what the taxed will do.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and then say which readings actually support each of the three uses I described. I am especially interested in the direction of the conditional: I take the historical slogan to run "no representation, therefore no legitimate taxation," whereas the franchise-expanding arguments need "taxation, therefore entitlement to vote." Explain exactly what turns on reversing that arrow, and whether the reversed reading can be defended at all.

Finally, press the reading that the arguments seem to need. If "taxation" is taken in the broad sense that captures non-citizens and working teenagers, does the slogan not also enfranchise children buying sweets, tourists, and foreign shareholders, and if so, does that prove too much for the uses to which it was put? And if "taxation" is narrowed to keep the conclusion manageable, does anything of the slogan survive? Set this out so that I can take the menu to a debate and say, for each reading, what it commits its user to.
