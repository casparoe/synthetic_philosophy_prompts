Write a sestina that enacts Lakatos's contrast between a progressive and a degenerating research programme, spoken in the voice of a scientist late in a career who is still defending the programme they spent their life building. The six end-words are to be the programme's hard core: not the substantive commitments of any particular science, but six words chosen so that they can stand for central commitments that are held immune to refutation by methodological fiat. The single formal rule that carries the whole thing is that those six words must never vary from stanza to stanza -- the same six, in the sestina's fixed permutation, every time. That is the negative heuristic: the core is protected by decision, not by evidence. Everything that changes is the protective belt, which is to say the lines around the recurring words; the interest of the poem should come from watching the same six words accrue sense and then lose it as their contexts shift.

Build the poem in two halves. In the first three stanzas the belt-work should be progressive: each stanza should reach outward, generating a prediction that outruns the facts it was assembled to save, so that a reader can feel new content being added and can imagine each new claim going wrong. This is the positive heuristic at work -- a plan that keeps pushing the programme into new territory. In the last three stanzas the same machinery should be degenerating: the belt stops predicting and starts re-describing, each stanza absorbing something already known or some stubborn anomaly by relabelling it, so that the reader feels the difference between a programme that is growing and one that is merely covering itself. The turn from the third stanza to the fourth is the pivot, and it should be legible as a turn.

End with the envoi as the moment of supersession rather than contradiction. Lakatos's point is that a programme is not refuted by a recalcitrant fact but displaced by a rival that does better, so let the envoi keep the six words but admit one word from outside the set -- the anomaly that will not be absorbed, or the core of the rival programme -- and let the closing leave the speaker not defeated in argument but overtaken. The technical vocabulary need not appear in the poem; the form should be doing the arguing.

Someone who knows the methodology should be able to say, stanza by stanza, which half is which and why, and should recognize the envoi as supersession rather than refutation. Someone who has never heard of a research programme should still find a good poem about a person who has spent a life defending something that may no longer be worth defending.
