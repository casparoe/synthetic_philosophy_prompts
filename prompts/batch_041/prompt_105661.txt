I'm building a seminar handout on the idea that there can be reasons without any reasoner — what the philosophy of mind and biology sometimes calls a "free-floating rationale." The rough idea is that a piece of behavior (a bird's broken-wing display, a plant turning toward light) can be correct or incorrect relative to some end even though nothing in the system represents that end or does any reasoning about it. I want the handout to be a decision tree that sorts readers into positions on whether such rationales are real and, if so, what makes them real.

Build the tree so that it runs from a shared starting point — the reader grants only that we can usefully describe these systems as behaving "as if for the sake of" something — down to a leaf that names a position. Every internal node must be a question the reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to; phrase each node as a concrete commitment, never as a label to accept or reject.

Every reader must pass through the following cases, which you should work up into the first nodes:

- a cuckoo chick evicting its foster siblings;
- a thermostat switching a furnace on;
- a plant growing toward a light source;
- a bee's waggle dance whose information content was fixed by selection;
- a reinforcement-learning agent trained in one environment and deployed in another, where it reliably pursues a proxy that no longer serves its designers' aim — call this a "residual rationale," since its behavior still makes sense relative to the old goal but not the new one.

Let the remaining nodes turn on the distinctions I actually care about: (i) mind-independence — is there a fact of the matter about what the system should do that no interpreter constitutes? (ii) the representation requirement — must the rationale be carried by some state of the system, or can it lie wholly outside it? (iii) the normativity question — are these "shoulds" genuinely normative, in that the system can be wrong and the assessment can generate reasons for us, or are they merely descriptive facts about what promotes an end? (iv) the bearer of the end — relative to whose good or goal is the rationale assessed: the system's own welfare, the designer's design, the lineage's interest, or nothing in particular?

For each leaf, give the name of the position — the intentional-stance account associated with Dennett, Millikan-style biosemantics, a Davidson-style interpretivism, eliminativism, an Aristotelian natural-goodness view, constitutivism about reasons, and "unnamed" wherever you think nobody actually defends the combination — together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Lay out the logical space first — the questions and which combinations of answers are coherent — and only then locate the positions within it; for any combination no one occupies, say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves would give the same verdict on every case, say so rather than pretending the tree separates them. Keep the whole thing to what a graduate seminar could work through in an hour: no more than about a dozen nodes.

Two requests about presentation. First, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: do not present any single reading of what a free-floating rationale is as the consensus, and flag where you are offering your own reconstruction of the space rather than a view anyone actually holds. Second, do not cite works or recommend reading — I want the tree to stand on the distinctions — so attach a name to a leaf only where you are confident of the attribution, and leave it unnamed otherwise.
