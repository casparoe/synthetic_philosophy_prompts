I'm not an academic, just someone who reads alot of popular history and science and keeps getting into arguments with friends about whether germs, climate, and volcanoes can be historical actors. Like, people say a volcano caused a cold summer and then crop failures and then revolts, or that germs killed more people than armies did, or that a drought toppled a civilization. But I can never tell what they're actually claiming. Is the volcano a cause? A nudge? A background condition? An actor with agency? A metaphor? I want a decision tree or taxonomy that sorts the possible positions on this. Not a list of famous theorists — I don't know their names and I can't look them up — I want the tree built from the questions that actually split the positions, so I can figure out where I land.

Please start from the broad claim that a non-human event made a difference to a human outcome, and build a tree where every branch node is a question someone could answer without already knowing the name of the position it leads to. Then put the positions at the leaves. Descriptive names are fine; if you're not sure a name is standard, don't pretend it is. I'd rather have an unnamed position than a fake citation.

Use concrete cases like these to make the questions answerable:
- A huge volcano cools the climate, crops fail, people starve, and a government falls, but nobody at the time connects the volcano to the harvest.
- A drought makes a peasant revolt more likely, but the revolt only happens after a rumor spreads that the king poisoned the wells.
- A new germ kills most of a population, and the survivors' language and gods change. Nobody intended any of it.
- A volcano destroys a city; later poets say the gods were angry, and their poem helps inspire a war.
- A climate shift changes trade routes, but only because merchants were already looking for new ones.
- A microbe causes a disease that weakens an army, and the army loses a battle it would have won.

I want the branch questions to cover at least these distinctions, and any others you think are needed:
- Is a non-human thing a cause only if it's a difference-maker, or can it be a cause as part of a larger conjunction?
- Does it have to be sufficient on its own to count as a historical cause, or only necessary-in-context?
- Does it have to be known or knowable by humans at the time to count?
- Does it have to operate through human intentions and meanings, or can it bypass them?
- Can agency exist without intention? If yes, what marks it: doing work, resisting, having effects, being enrolled in a system, something else?
- Does calling a non-human thing an actor add explanatory power, or only moral or rhetorical charge?
- Does non-human causation threaten human freedom or political responsibility, or does it just redistribute causal weight?
- Is the right unit of analysis the event, the thing, the system, or the assemblage?
- Does scale matter — is a germ an actor for one death, a pandemic, or neither?
- Can a non-human thing be responsible, or only humans can, and if only humans can, why does the language of actors keep coming back?

Aim for maybe 10 to 12 leaves. For each leaf, give the name of the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing someone who holds it has to swallow. If two leaves can't really be seperated by your tree, flag them as not separable rather than pretending they're distinct. If some combination of answers is empty, say so and say whether you think it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Don't give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations. I can't check them and I don't want guessed-at titles. If you need to refer to an idea, describe it in plain words.

At the end, tell me what would change your mind about the tree: which argument or case would make you redraw it, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
