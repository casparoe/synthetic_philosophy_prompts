I'm a high school student trying to work out what I actually believe about the Church-Turing thesis, and I would like a probability estimate from you with the reasoning laid out, not a survey of who has said what.

The reason I want it decomposed is that the thesis seems to me to join two different kinds of thing: a formal notion, Turing computability, and an informal one, the idea of a procedure that is effective or mechanical in the ordinary sense. If the informal side does not pick out a determinate class of procedures, then it is not clear that the thesis has a truth value at all, and I would rather see that possibility priced in than assumed away. So please give me separate credences for three readings, and then a single overall number for whichever reading you think is most defensible, saying which one that is.

The first reading is conceptual: that effective procedure has a determinate extension and the thesis is true by analysis of that notion, so that a counterexample is close to unintelligible. The second is human: that anything an idealized human mathematician could compute, given unlimited time, patience, and paper, is Turing-computable. The third is physical: that anything any finite physical device could compute is Turing-computable.

For each reading I would like the sub-questions it depends on, a rough probability for each, and how you combine them into the number. For instance: whether the agreement of the standard formalisms with one another, such as Turing machines, lambda calculus, and recursive functions, is evidence for the thesis or only evidence that the formal notion is robust; whether a physical counterexample would touch the conceptual reading at all; whether the thesis is better understood as a single proposition or as a family of claims that take their content from a background physical theory; and whether the thesis, because it abstracts away from time and resources, is in danger of being nearly vacuous.

One scenario I would like you to price in, since it is the sort of thing that would move me most: suppose a device reliably answers, in bounded time, questions about whether specified Turing machines halt, and does so by some physical process rather than by simulating the machine, as if it read the answer off a measurement. Treat its physical possibility as something to be estimated rather than assumed; I want your probability that some such device could exist in principle, and what its existence would do to each of the three numbers above.

Then tell me which sub-question the overall figure is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move it furthest. Please write the whole answer as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables, the way you would explain it in a long letter.
