I would like you to work out what principle, or small set of rival principles, unifies my intuitions about when several things compose a further thing. My verdicts, as plainly as I can give them, are these.

Two bricks set side by side, touching along a face, are two objects and not one; the same bricks mortared into a course are a wall, and the wall is one object. Loose sand in a heap is not one object — I would resist saying how many objects the heap contains — but sandstone is one object. A car assembled from its parts is one object. A Lego model is one object, though its bricks are only clipped together and I can pull them apart in a moment; a bucket of loose Lego bricks is not one object. A cloud is not one object, however steadily it drifts. A swarm of bees is not one object, though it moves, forages, and defends itself as a unit. A dog is one object and its tail is part of it; once severed, the tail is still a piece of matter but no longer a dog, and the dog has lost a part. A Portuguese man o' war is one object, though it is a colony of specialized zooids, none of which survives on its own. A lichen is one object, though it is a fungus and an alga living as a single thallus. A biofilm — the sheet of bacteria and their secreted matrix on a wet surface — is not one object. Two human embryos that fuse into a chimera yield one organism. A forest, or a coral reef taken as a whole, is not one object. The bronze lump and the statue cast from it are one object described twice, not two objects occupying one place. The Eiffel Tower together with my left shoe is not one object, though there is a scattered collection of matter there.

Two of these sit badly together. The man o' war is one object and the bee swarm is not, yet each is a collection of organisms whose collective behavior is the whole point. The cellular slime mold Dictyostelium discoideum — single amoebae living independently, then starving and aggregating into a crawling slug that later raises a stalk and spores — seems to fall between them, and I cannot say which side it is on. The Lego model and the heap of sand are awkward in the same way: both are assemblies held together by nothing but clips or friction, and I call one an object and the other not.

The principle I would offer, tentatively, is that some things compose a further thing when they are either functionally integrated — organized so as to work together toward a single end — or bound into a continuous mass. But functional integration would make the swarm one object, and continuous mass would make the Lego model many, so I expect the principle to need repair or replacement.

What I want from you: the principle, or the two or three candidates that divide the cases, each stated precisely enough that I can apply it; the verdict or verdicts that motivate each clause; what the principle says about cases I have not listed, in particular a severed hand surgically reattached, a brain-dead body on a ventilator, a virus, a prion, a city, a corporation, and the heap to which I add one grain at a time, where I can find no point at which an object begins; and an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which I should therefore give up. If the only principle that saves every verdict is an unprincipled disjunction — organisms and artifacts, and nothing else — say so, and say whether that is a defect or a discovery. I would rather be told that one of my verdicts is wrong than be handed a clause invented to save it.
