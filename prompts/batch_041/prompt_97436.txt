I run the control room at a regional electric utility, and since a near-miss during last summer's heat dome I've been sitting on the ad hoc ethics panel our operations vice-president set up. The case we keep circling is this: when demand outruns supply, we can either shed load deliberately — rolling blackouts that we schedule in advance, which means we knowingly cut power to particular neighborhoods, some of which include customers on home dialysis or oxygen concentrators — or we can let the system ride and risk an uncontrolled, cascading failure that blacks out a far larger area for far longer with no warning to anyone. The engineers treat it as an arithmetic problem, but to me the real question is the difference between a harm I bring about on purpose and a harm I merely let happen. Half the panel says we own whatever we deliberately switch off; the other half says we own the whole collapse if we sit on our hands. I've been using a rule of thumb of my own — roughly, that I may act if the people I hurt would have been hurt anyway, no sooner and no worse, had I done nothing — and I suspect it's too crude, because in the blackout case the customers I cut off might well have kept their power had the grid held.

What I want from you is a decision procedure the panel and the shift supervisors could actually run, under real time pressure and without a philosophy degree: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure should simply return a no, and the point at which it has to stop and hand the call to a person because the question is about values rather than facts. These situations come up two or three times a summer, and the shift supervisor has to decide alone in the moment, with the panel only reviewing the call afterward. Please run the procedure on three cases — the rolling blackout; a preemptive shutoff of power to a wildfire-prone area to keep our own lines from starting a fire; and a third that I would expect it to resolve against my instincts, since I would rather know where it bites — and then tell me honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where a reasonable operator following it could still go wrong. I'm not looking for reading recommendations; I want something a tired supervisor at three in the morning can actually use.
