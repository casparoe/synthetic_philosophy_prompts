I'm in a third-year bioethics seminar that's currently on the "what is death?" unit, and I've somehow ended up leading next week's discussion, so I'm definately overthinking this. What I want to hand out isn't a reading list — it's a decision tree people can actually work through to find out which view they hold about when someone is dead, rather than just grabbing whichever label they've heard before. Root it at the question I keep getting stuck on: whether there's one correct definition of death that has to apply to every purpose, or whether "dead" is more like a legal status we assign for particular purposes (organ procurement, withdrawing ventilation, inheritance, criminal liability, that kind of thing). From there, build the branches out of the real fault lines, not out of names: the standard three-level distinction between a definition of death, a criterion that operationalises it, and the bedside tests; whether the criterion is whole-brain, brainstem-only, higher-brain (cortex, or the capacity for consciousness), or circulatory-respiratory; whether death has to be a single moment or can be a process smeared out over time; and whether the dead donor rule has to hold come what may or whether it's just a convention we could give up — my seminar keeps going in circles on the donation-after-circulatory-death cases where circulation gets restarted to the organs afterwards.

Each branch node needs to be a question someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so concrete cases rather than "so, are you a whole-brain theorist?". Please make sure these cases show up somewhere in the tree: the patient in a persistent vegetative state with a working brainstem; the locked-in patient who's conscious but can barely move anything; the brain-dead patient on a ventilator whose heart is still beating; someone on ECMO; the anencephalic newborn; and the circulatory-death donor whose heart has stopped but could in principle be restarted. Leaves should be named positions, with "unnamed" wherever nobody actually defends the combination, and one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment. Two more things I'd find genuinely useful: flag any pairs of leaves the tree can't really separate at the bedside even though they come apart in principle, and tell me which combinations come out empty and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Roughly 7-9 branch nodes and about 8 leaves, so it fits on two sides of A4.
