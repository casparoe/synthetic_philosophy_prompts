Hey — I need help writing a toast and I keep botching it in my head, so I'm hoping you can do better. You're a philosopher of concepts who moonlights as a type designer (or a type designer who's read too much Hofstadter and too much Wittgenstein and can't stop noticing both) — so you can talk shop with the designers in the room and still keep everyone else with us. Think it through carefully before you write: start by brainstorming a few different angles on how to make this land, pick the best one, and then just give me the final toast — I don't need to see the brainstorm.

The setting: my old type-design teacher, call her Ruth, is retiring after nearly forty years of teaching lettering and type design, and I'm giving the toast at her dinner. About sixty people. Most are type designers and letterers and ex-students, a few are current students, and roughly a third are her family — husband, two sisters, grandkids — who have never set a letter in their lives and mostly want to hear something warm about a person they love.

Here's my problem, and it's the whole reason I'm asking. Ruth has spent her entire career on one question and she is completely, cheerfully dogmatic about her answer: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Her line is that there is no essence — no single feature, no shape, no skeleton, nothing every a has that every non-a lacks. She'd wave at a blackletter a, a Futura a, the a in an old uncial manuscript, a six-year-old's handwriting, the fat a in a supermarket logo, and say: find me the thing they all share that the 'o' or the '2' or the 'e' doesn't also have. There isn't one. What there is, she says, is a big space of letterforms where 'a' names a whole neighborhood rather than a point — a region of forms that a community of readers has agreed to treat as a's, and what holds the neighborhood together isn't a shared feature but a shared tolerance: you can push an a a long way, but the second it starts reading as an o or a d you've left the territory. She calls it 'a-ness as a neighborhood, not a point' — half a joke, fully a thesis. She'd also insist that 'a' and 'g' are the two letters that come in two completely separate canonical forms (single-story and double-story) and that nobody can say which one is the 'real' one, which she takes as evidence for her side.

So the obvious toast — 'Ruth taught us the essence of the letter a' — is the one thing I can't say. She'd consider it flatly false and half the room would wince. But I also don't want a seminar in a dinner jacket that loses her grandkids by the second paragraph.

What I'd like the toast to do: land as affection, and let her own idea actually do the work rather than just be quoted. The move I'd love it to make, if you can pull it off, is that her 'no essence, just a neighborhood' view is also a description of how she taught — she never handed anyone a template of the correct a, she handed them a territory to move around in and trusted they'd stay inside it. If you can turn 'a-ness as a neighborhood' into something that lands for the family too — she gave people room, she let them wander and still counted them as one of hers — that's the toast I want. Include one good joke that both halves of the room can laugh at, the designers and the grandkids both, and keep it to about five minutes spoken.

One more thing: after the toast, give me a short list of any lines in it that Ruth might stand up and flatly contradict — anything that smuggles an essence back in, or calls the essence a shape, or otherwise says something she'd call false. I want to know where teh landmines are before I'm standing there with a glass in front of her family.
