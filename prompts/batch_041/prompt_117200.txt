I am a software engineer by trade — I write scheduling and records software for a hospital network — and I sit on my state's medical board as the public, non-physician member, which is why the drafting has fallen to me. At our next meeting the board votes on an appeal from an applicant I will call Dr. R. She completed medical school and a full residency abroad, has practiced for eight years in a hospital there, and passed our clinical skills assessment without difficulty, but she has now failed the written licensing examination four times, each time by a handful of points, with the misses concentrated in the fact-recall sections rather than the case-based ones. A rural hospital that has been recruiting her says it will lose its only internist if we do not license her; a competing hospital group has written in to oppose, arguing that a physician who cannot state the reasoning behind a treatment choice on demand is exactly the risk the examination exists to catch. Our statute permits us to license an applicant who demonstrates the competence to practice safely and leaves it to us to define how competence is demonstrated, and it also permits a restricted license on conditions we set — in this case she has offered two years under a named supervisor with quarterly chart audits.

Two of my colleagues have turned the case into an argument I cannot referee on my own. One of them, a retired professor, says the real question is the difference between knowing-how and knowing-that: the examination measures whether she can state facts, while the job requires her to do things, and the two can come apart, so failing the first is weak evidence about the second. The other calls that sophistry — medicine is mostly reasoning about unfamiliar cases, and a doctor who can act but cannot explain is a technician, not a physician. I have also been told, in the hallway, that the distinction is itself contested, and that some philosophers hold that knowing-how is just a species of knowing-that, which would cut against her appeal rather than for it. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete disposition with a time frame, spell out the conditions if you favor a restricted license, say what the license actually certifies, and take a position on whether the knowing-how/knowing-that distinction should change the outcome — including which reading of that distinction the board should adopt and why. Treat the opposing view at its strongest rather than waving it away, and keep it under a page and a half, since our chair will not read more. Three similar appeals are queued for the spring, so I need criteria that generalize beyond Dr. R. I do not need citations; nobody on the board would read them, and I would rather have the argument than a survey.
