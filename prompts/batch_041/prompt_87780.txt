I spent most of last night refereeing an argument I never managed to settle, and I would like an outside verdict, since both parties walked away convinced that the other had quietly conceded something. The occasion was a seminar we are all sitting in on, about explanation in history; the dispute was over what it means to say that a historical outcome was contingent — whether a kingdom really can be lost because a horseshoe nail was lost, and what follows if it can. Neither of them is a crank, and the exchange got technical enough in places that I lost track of where the actual disagreement was rather than the apparent one, so please take the report as literal and read it closely.

Anton opened with this: the more closely you look at any large outcome, the more it dissolves into a chain of small accidents — a storm, a mistranslation, a messenger who took the wrong road — so the air of inevitability in the standard accounts is retrofitting. Had things gone otherwise, he said, the resulting world would be just as coherent as ours; the alternatives are therefore all equally legitimate, and there is no principled way to answer counterfactual questions, because anything can follow from anything. Counterfactual history, on his view, is fiction with footnotes. Mira granted the sensitivity and denied the inference. She said he was running determinism together with predictability — a system can be perfectly deterministic and still unforecastable, if its initial conditions cannot be specified finely enough — and that "the nail mattered" is not a claim about chance at all but an ordinary causal claim, of the same logical shape as "the short circuit caused the fire." If counterfactual dependence is how we cash out causation generally, she said, then refusing counterfactuals in history means giving up causal explanation in history, which is absurd.

Anton's reply was that history affords us exactly one run and no control, whereas physics can rerun the experiment, so historical counterfactuals cannot be tested and the genre is unfalsifiable: a nail story can be constructed for any outcome you please, and no evidence can embarrass it. He added that we only ever tell nail stories about the outcomes that happened — nobody writes papers about the nail that did not matter — so the whole thing is hindsight bias wearing a method's clothing. Mira answered that a counterfactual has to be rewritten as little as possible while staying consistent with what we know about how things work, and that whether the nail mattered is an empirical question about how fragile the system was: if the supply of nails was one bad harvest away from collapse, the nail is a genuine hinge; if there were ten other roads to the same message, it is not. She called this robustness, and said one tests it by asking how many nearby ways the world could have gone still terminate at the same place. Anton's last move was that robustness is itself a metaphor; that if small causes carry this much weight, the structural explanations our seminar has been trading in are mostly noise; and — roughly his words — that he was not denying the outcome was caused, only denying that we can know which small thing did the causing.

Please adjudicate turn by turn. For each move, say whether it is right, wrong, or right for the wrong reason, naming the precise error where there is one and the precise insight where there is one; where a move admits more than one reading, distinguish the readings and rate each. Do not average the two positions or conclude that both have a point, since I can do that myself, and do not let Anton's final concession pass as a mere clarification if it is not one. Tell me which distinctions the two of them keep collapsing into each other, which of them is closer to right overall and on what grounds, and what they are both missing — I have a suspicion that there is a question underneath their argument that neither of them has asked, and I would rather hear your candidate for it than another round of the same argument.
