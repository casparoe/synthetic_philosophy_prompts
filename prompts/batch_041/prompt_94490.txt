"Good's theorem concerns an agent who must choose among a finite set of acts and who, before choosing, may perform a costless experiment whose result she will learn. Let the possible results be e₁, …, eₘ, with prior probabilities P(eᵢ), let U(a) be the prior expected utility of act a, and let U(a | eᵢ) be its expected utility conditional on result eᵢ. The theorem says that the expected utility of the policy 'observe, then choose the act with the highest conditional expected utility' is at least as great as the expected utility of choosing immediately: Σᵢ P(eᵢ) maxₐ U(a | eᵢ) ≥ maxₐ U(a).

Proof. For any fixed act a, U(a) = Σᵢ P(eᵢ) U(a | eᵢ), by the law of total probability applied to the agent's expected utilities. Since maxₐ U(a | eᵢ) ≥ U(a | eᵢ) for each result eᵢ, we have Σᵢ P(eᵢ) maxₐ U(a | eᵢ) ≥ Σᵢ P(eᵢ) U(a | eᵢ) = U(a). As this holds for every act a, it holds for the best act, which is the claim. The inequality is just the fact that the maximum of a set of numbers is at least as great as their weighted average.

Assumptions. The one real assumption is that the experiment is free. Everything else follows from the agent's having a preference ordering over the acts; in particular the proof never relies on any assumption about how she revises her probabilities on learning a result, since the equality U(a) = Σᵢ P(eᵢ) U(a | eᵢ) is a consequence of the probability calculus alone.

Costs and the claim that information can never hurt. If the experiment costs c, subtract c from the value of the informed policy, so the experiment is worth performing whenever its gain exceeds c. Since the gain is at least zero, free information is always worth taking and paid information is worth taking whenever it is cheap enough. A classmate who says that gathering information can never leave you worse off is therefore right: the informed policy's expected utility is at least that of the uninformed one, so an agent who runs the experiment is guaranteed to do at least as well as one who does not."
