Two economists in my department spent an afternoon arguing over the socialist calculation debate, and I was drafted as referee. Their dispute was anchored to a scheme one of them had sketched on a whiteboard: a central board posts parametric prices for every produced good, nudges each price up or down in proportion to observed excess demand, and instructs every enterprise manager to minimize average cost and to expand output until marginal cost equals the posted price, while workers pick jobs at posted wages and consumers spend their income as they please. The board's only job is to keep adjusting prices until nothing is in shortage or surplus.

Lena said this settles the theoretical question. Once the board iterates its way to market-clearing prices, she argued, the same marginal conditions hold as in a competitive equilibrium, so rational allocation is possible without private ownership of capital; the Austrian claim of impossibility is refuted, and all that remains is an engineering question of how fast the board can iterate and how good its demand data are.

Omar said the iteration presupposes exactly what is in dispute. To read a shortage as a signal to raise the price, rather than as a sign that a manager is hiding capacity or that the input will be obsolete within the year, the board must already know the production functions — and those are not data sitting around waiting to be collected. They are generated by entrepreneurs guessing and being corrected. A rule telling managers to minimize average cost is also empty unless their effort and local knowledge can be monitored, which in most real production they cannot. So the scheme either has to centralize knowledge it claims to draw on, or it fails on incentives.

Lena replied that markets do not converge either — bubbles, monopolies, sticky prices — so if failure to converge refutes the board, it refutes the market too; and the tacit-knowledge point proves too much, since a manager inside a firm faces the same problem of transmitting local knowledge upward, yet firms demonstrably produce. Omar answered that firms face competition and can go bankrupt, which prices their errors, whereas a board obliged to keep every enterprise afloat has no comparable discipline; and that a market need not converge at all, it need only keep reallocating toward whoever is currently best informed, with the losses landing on the misinformed.

Lena then offered a patch: let the board run a prediction market on each input, so scarcity prices are elicited from traders betting on shortages rather than guessed by the board. Omar said that just smuggles private speculation and profit back in, so the result is either not socialism or is the market with extra steps.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each claim either of them makes, say who is right and briefly why, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing. In your answer, give your overall verdict in the first sentence and then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Also tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and which argument or piece of evidence would make you reverse your verdict.
