I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been asked to run the next session of the reading group my team keeps. The topic I have been handed is the disagreement in the philosophy of biology between etiological (selected-effects) accounts of function and causal-role (systemic) accounts. Please do not explain the disagreement to me; I have read enough to follow it, and what I need is a plan for teaching it rather than a summary of it.

The audience is a dozen engineers, no biology past secondary school, no philosophy at all, and no preparation. Fifty minutes, a whiteboard, nothing handed out in advance. Set out the order in which the ideas have to be introduced, and why that order rather than another: what has to be in place before the question — what makes some effect of a trait one of its functions rather than a by-product — can even be heard as a question; where the two accounts should enter relative to each other; and the point at which the audience is entitled to ask why these are rivals rather than answers to two different questions. My own guess is that the sequence has to earn the rivalry before it announces it, but tell me if that is the wrong shape.

Then the case that carries the whole session. I do not want the heart-that-pumps-blood-and-also-makes-noise example and I do not want the appendix; both are the sort of thing people have half-heard and will feel they already know. Give me one case to build the fifty minutes on, and say what it must have and what it must lack. I expect the divergence cases to come in four shapes — something that once did a job and no longer does it, something that does a job now that nothing selected it for, something that would do the job but has no history at all because it arose by accident, and something that is meant to do the job but currently cannot — and I want to know which of these should carry the session and which should be held back until the moment the two accounts actually come apart, and why.

I am expecting two misconceptions and want the rest from you: that function means purpose and purpose requires a designer, and that one of the two accounts is simply correct and the other refuted. Give me the full map in the order the misconceptions will surface, and for each one the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off, not the general remark that I should address it. I am particularly worried about the moment when the case of a trait that no longer does anything useful leads half the room to conclude that the historical account is dead while the other half concludes that the role account is, and about the broken instance, which I expect to be read as proof that function-talk is really about current contribution.

One thing matters to me more than the rest, and I would like it handled at the right point in the sequence rather than tacked on at the end: whether the historical account can accommodate artifacts at all, code included. If the answer is that a designer's intentions count as a selection process, I want to see what that does to the analogy, because most of the code I work with survives for reasons nobody intended, and I cannot tell whether that makes something a function of the code or merely an effect of it. Say also where a code analogy will help this audience and where it will quietly mislead them.

Finish with the single question I should ask at the end that would show me whether the audience has understood the distinction rather than picked a side, and with what I should openly decline to cover at this level. I would rather cut the propensity or dispositional account of function, and anything about functions and mental content, than let the session sprawl, but tell me if cutting those leaves the audience with a false picture. No citations or reading list, please; I have no way to check them and no time to use them.
