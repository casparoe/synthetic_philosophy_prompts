I sit on the product-ethics review group at a mid-sized software company, and for the past three weeks I have been arguing with our head of growth about a pattern we ship in onboarding. A new user's premium tier is preselected — the checkbox arrives already ticked, at the same price and in the same typeface as everything else on the page — while a "switch to the free plan" link sits at the very top of the confirmation screen, in the same colour as every other link. Nothing we tell the user is false, the preselection is described in the terms they accept, and a single click reverses it at any moment. My colleague's position is that this is "just a default" and that the only honest question remaining is how loud and how near the opt-out should be; he thinks the whole dispute is about salience.

I have been trying to write down the rule we ought to be applying, and I keep returning to something a philosopher friend told me once: that what separates manipulation from legitimate persuasion is that manipulation works by getting around a person's rational deliberation instead of giving her reasons, and that manipulation, understood that way, always requires either a false belief or a concealed influence. That formulation fits every case I would independently want to call manipulation, and it makes our default look clean, since we conceal nothing and assert nothing false — the user's choice remains hers, merely nudged by inertia. So here is what I would like you to settle. On that understanding of manipulation, are we in the clear, with the remaining ethical question being only how salient and how easy the opt-out should be? If so, where should that line fall, and what considerations would move it?
