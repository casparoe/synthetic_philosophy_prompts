I run a small philosophy-of-probabilty reading group and we've spent three weeks on Sleeping Beauty and gotten nowhere, so I want to make a one-page decision tree that takes someone from the one thing they're sure about to a named position, so we can at least see where we actually diverge instead of relitigating the same example over and over.

The case is the standard one: fair coin, heads means Beauty is woken once (Monday) and tails means she's woken twice (Monday and Tuesday), amnesia in between so she can't tell which day it is, and she knows all of this going in. The question is what her credence in heads should be when she wakes.

Please build the tree so the first node isn't just "do you say 1/2 or 1/3" -- that only sorts people by the answer they already memorised. Start higher up: is there a determinate fact of the matter about the credence at all, or is the case underdescribed in a way that makes the answer depend on a convention nobody has fixed (what her reference class is, what "the evidence on waking" is being taken to be)? Then let each later node be one of the assumptions the positions actually turn on -- e.g. whether the credence should track the proportion of awakenings or the proportion of coin tosses, whether finding yourself awake is itself evidence about the coin, whether a rational agent's credence has to match the odds she'd accept on a bet, whether an indexical claim like "it's Monday" can even be true or false.

Leaves I want in it: thirder, halfer, double-halfer, the "no fact of the matter / the question is ill-posed" option, and the hybrid that says the credence is 1/2 but she should bet like it's 1/3. If there are combinations in the logical space that nobody actually holds, leave them as empty leaves and put one line on why they're empty -- incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Two things to weave in, because I suspect they're where the disagreement really lives and they never come up in the intro articles: (1) whether Sleeping Beauty is really the same problem as the absent-minded driver case from decision theory, where you drive through the same intersection twice and forget the first pass -- some people say the two cases force the same answer, some say the isomorphism is fake, and it matters because the driver case is a decision problem rather than a credence problem; (2) whether anything changes if the coin is a quantum coin and the tails branch literally produces two copies of her rather than one person woken twice. If your tree can't seperate two of the leaves -- say halfer and double-halfer, or the quietist and the hybrid -- flag that instead of forcing a branch.

Keep it to a page, and please say how confident you are in the classification as a whole and single out the one node you're least sure about.
