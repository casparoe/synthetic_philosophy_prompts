I direct a small historical society, and on Saturday I give the opening address at our town's midsummer festival — a few hundred people in a field, most of whom have been coming since childhood. The festival is said to be eight centuries old. My own archival work says otherwise: it was assembled in the mid-1920s by a schoolmistress and the publicity office of the railway company out of three unrelated local customs, and the traditional costume was designed for a pageant in 1934. I will not say any of that from the podium, but I will not say the eight-hundred-year line either, and I have promised myself I won't fudge it.

Write the address. It has to land with the festival committee, who believe the old story and kept the thing going through decades when nobody else came, and with the teenagers and newcomers at the back who suspect the whole business is a fabrication. Keep the vocabulary of the academic literature out of it — invented, constructed, imagined, narrative — and keep out scholarly hedging. Five minutes, spoken aloud, no citations, no further reading. Somewhere in it, in plain speech and without either debunking or nostalgia, answer what makes a tradition real when it isn't old, and why that answer takes nothing away from the people standing in that field. End with something they can do.
