I'm helping organize a graduate seminar on art and politics, and for the opening session I'd rather commission a short imagined dialogue than deliver the usual lecture on whether political commitment ruins art. Set the scene in the cramped back office of a fictional little magazine, The Watchfire, on a rainy evening in 1926, the night before the editor must decide whether to serialize a young novelist's new book. Two characters, equally sharp, neither of them a strawman. Ada Mercer, the editor, speaks for the position associated with W. E. B. Du Bois: that art is never innocent, that under racial subordination an artist's formal choices are themselves political acts, and that a people's art is finally answerable to its struggle for freedom. Theodore Aldridge, the novelist, speaks for aesthetic autonomy: that the work worth keeping is the one that refuses to be anyone's instrument, that difficulty and unresolved ambiguity are forms of honesty, and that "propaganda" is what we call art that has already decided what we are to feel.

Their quarrel is anchored in something concrete. Aldridge's novel is narrated by an unreliable informer who betrays a political movement to protect his sister's child; it is formally difficult, ends without judging him, and the magazine's white patron has asked for a redemptive final chapter and "more sympathetic" protagonists. I want the dialogue to press past slogans into the details: whether "propaganda" is fixed by the artist's intention, by the work's effects, or by its form; whether the demand for legible, uplifting art rests on an empirical bet about political consequences that could simply be wrong; how the double audience — a Black readership and a white one that must be moved or shamed — changes what counts as effective or honest art; and whether beauty is a means to an end or its own justification. Let each character make the strongest case against the other rather than talking past each other: Aldridge should force Ada to concede where her own judgments are aesthetic rather than political, and Ada should force Aldridge to say who is underwriting his freedom from purpose and what he owes readers who will take his ambiguity as permission.

They needn't resolve the dispute, but they should not merely agree to disagree either; end on a specific, live question the reader is left to decide. Please keep any direct quotation out of their mouths — no lines lifted from real essays — and let them argue entirely in their own words.
