I've been trying to get my head around living wills. My aunt signed one maybe fifteen years ago, and now she's got pretty far along dementia — most days she seems honestly content, laughs at the TV, asks for ice cream — and the nursing home is still going by the old document, meaning no antibiotics if she gets pneumonia. It bugs me and I cant quite say why. We don't let people bind their future selves like that anywhere else: you can't sell yourself into slavery, you can back out of a contract, nobody thinks my 22-year-old self got to decide who my 45-year-old self marries. But here the earlier version of her gets to overrule the one who's actually sitting in the chair, and only because that one can't argue back. So what does the practice assume about who a person is over time — that there's one self that persists and the earlier one is the authoritative one? That I'm really a string of selves and we just pick which one counts? Someone also told me there's a difference between the things I care about because they fit the story I tell about my life and the things that just feel good or bad right now, and I wonder if the whole thing hinges on treating the first kind as the real ones. Does the practice actually need any particular answer to the personal identity question, or would a bunch of different views all spit out the same policy? And if we took seriously that the demented her is just as much the real her, what would have to change — could we honor any advance directive at all, or only the ones where she's still sort of in there? I'm not after a reading list, just help seeing whats baked into this.
