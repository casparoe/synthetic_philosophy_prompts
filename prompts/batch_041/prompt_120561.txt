I've ended up on Brownings side of the ordinary men fight and I want it stress-tested. My position: the men who did the shooting at Józefów weren't zealots, they were middle-aged working-class guys from Hamburg, cops and family men, and what actually explains the killing is the situation — a unit that had already spent years on expulsions and ghetto duty so the next step just felt like the next step, an order that came with an explicit offer to step out if you didn't feel up to it, hundreds of men standing around in the woods together, and the fact that almost nobody took the offer. Ideology and character, on this story, do basicly no work, and Goldhagen's nation of eager executioners is reading the verdict back into the testimony.

What I want is the case against that. Not vibes about situationism being unfashionable — the specific findings, comparisons, or arguments that should actually move me, and how far. Things I'm already chewing on: (a) whether "the situation" quietly swallows ideology, since how a guy reads an order is itself partly a matter of what he already believes, in which case my view might be the kind that can't lose; (b) the refusers — a handful stepped out even at Józefów, and if the men who refused differ systematically from the men who didn't, doesn't the variance need a person-level explanation?; (c) whether the psychology the whole picture leans on still holds up, given how hard Milgram's obedience reading and the Stanford prison study have been hit in the last decade or so; (d) whether the same testimonies could carry the opposite conclusion just as easily, which would mean the case is evidentially underdetermined rather than settled.
