I have just spent a long weekend arguing with a friend about truth commissions, and I lost on the point that mattered: I defended the institution for two hours without being able to say what it presupposes. His position is that a state which releases murderers because they agreed to talk has simply put a price on confession and paid it, and that calling the result reconciliation is decoration on an ordinary political bargain. I think he is missing something real, but I cannot say what the practice is committed to, and I would like you to reconstruct its implicit theory.

Take the practice in the generic form in which it recurs, not in any single country's version. The design tends to have roughly these features. First, it covers a defined class of wrongs — grave violations committed for political ends — and leaves ordinary crimes to the ordinary courts. Second, perpetrators are invited to apply for amnesty, and the amnesty is conditional: it requires full public disclosure of the act, it covers only what was disclosed, and it can be revoked if the disclosure is later shown to have been incomplete or false. Third, an applicant who discloses goes free, while a man who keeps silent remains liable to prosecution even if his crime was far less grave than that of someone who talked and walked; the currency of the institution is speech, not the magnitude of the deed. Fourth, victims and survivors receive reparations — administrative payments, memorials, an official standing — rather than damages assessed against their torturers, and their objections to a particular amnesty are recorded but do not veto it. Fifth, the commission publishes findings that the state receives as the authoritative account of what happened, and the state commits itself, and its successors, to non-repetition. Sixth, elsewhere the same tension is settled differently: trials first and then a political decision to stop; community courts in which a confession reduces the sentence; a blanket amnesty with no truth condition at all.

Now the questions I actually want answered.

What does the design presuppose about what a wrong does to its victim? The institution behaves as though the injury that persists after the killing is impunity and denial — as though the second wrong, the one the commission exists to remedy, is the state's refusal to say what it did. Is that a claim about harm, namely that unacknowledged violence is a distinct and continuing injury, or only a claim about what remains repairable once the deed is past? And if it is the former, does it presuppose that the truth about a wrong is owed to the survivor as a right, or merely that the community has an interest in knowing?

What theory of blame does the exchange rate presuppose? The institution makes a perpetrator's standing toward his own deed — owning it or denying it — more decisive than the deed's severity. That looks like a quality-of-will view, on which blameworthiness tracks the attitude expressed rather than the damage done. Is that what the practice requires, or could a straightforwardly act-focused view of responsibility license the same statute?

Who can forgive, or waive, and on whose behalf? The state that grants the amnesty is usually the successor of the state that did the killing, and it disposes of claims that the victims have not consented to surrender. Does the practice presuppose that the authority to punish belongs to the political community and may be traded away by its representatives, or that the victims' claims are held in trust by the state? Does it need the concept of forgiveness at all, or does it treat the whole transaction as a political settlement in which forgiveness is beside the point?

What view of collective action and responsibility is at work? Wrongs defined as political — done in the name of a movement or a state — are treated as the sort of thing a community can settle with another community, while ordinary crimes are not. Does that presuppose that some individuals act as agents of a group and can therefore be dealt with by a group-to-group arrangement, and that others act merely as individuals and cannot? What becomes of the perpetrator who followed orders he did not believe?

Finally, the epistemology. The commission's findings are received as the official account, and the state becomes the author of the history. What does that presuppose about the possibility of authoritative collective memory, and about the relation between legal proof and historical truth? Does it assume a state can know its own past better than its courts can, or only that someone must be licensed to say it out loud?

Tell me which of these commitments the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits well with; whether the parts are mutually consistent, since the speech-for-freedom exchange and the non-negotiable gravity of the crimes appear to pull in opposite directions; and where the design is underdetermined, so that a retributivist, a consequentialist, a restorative-justice theorist, and a skeptic about collective responsibility could each sign the same statute.

Then the part my friend needs. If the presupposed view is wrong — if there is no distinct wrong in unacknowledged violence, or if a state cannot forgive on a victim's behalf — which features of the institution are load-bearing and which would survive the replacement? Sketch what the practice would have to become under the view he and I both half-hold, that what a society owes victims is punishment and that truth-telling is at best a consolation prize.

One last thing to test the reconstruction. Consider a variant in which amnesty is conditioned not only on disclosure but on the applicant's submitting to a public hearing at which survivors may question him, and in which he keeps the amnesty no matter what he says there. The information has already been secured by the disclosure, so the hearing adds nothing evidentiary. If a designer insists on it anyway, what does that tell us about what the institution thinks justice requires — and does my friend's reading of the whole practice survive that variant better than mine does?
