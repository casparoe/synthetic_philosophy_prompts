I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time now — long enough to have watched the expected-value debates cycle several times, and long enough to be tired of the parodies, which are almost always weaker than the positions they mock: a strawman who hands over his wallet to a stranger on the street, and a knowing narrator who assures us that the multiplication has gone wrong somewhere. I want a piece that someone who actually holds the view could read without feeling misrepresented, and that a specialist would recognize as the work of a person who knows the terrain.

You are a professional philosopher working in decision theory and population ethics, with a sideline in institutional parody — the kind of writer who can sustain the register of an accounting standard for a thousand words without breaking it. Before drafting anything, work the problem through on paper. Separate the consequences of expected-value maximization that are genuinely awkward from those that are merely counterintuitive, and from those that are artifacts of a naive statement no careful defender would accept. Note which repairs defenders actually reach for, and what those repairs cost them. Spend more effort on that diagnosis than on jokes; if the diagnosis is right, the jokes take care of themselves.

Then write the piece.

The commission is a parody of a standards document — specifically, the methodology framework of a fictional body that rates charitable and philanthropic projects. Give it a title in the genre, something like "Generally Accepted Expected Value Principles," and write it as a sequence of numbered standards, each with a short title and a paragraph or two, followed by a worked example and a set of notes. The target is the family of views commonly called expected-value fanaticism: the position that practical reason just is expected-value maximization with an unbounded value function, so that any action with a nonzero probability of a sufficiently large payoff outranks any action with a guaranteed modest one. Aim at the doctrine and at the institutional genre that grows up around it. Do not aim at any person, organization, blog, or school with a real name; invent the board and its members.

Assume the following machinery, which I am spelling out so that the document can presuppose it rather than gesture at it:

- Fanaticism: with an unbounded value function, tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs can dominate everything else, so the theory licenses sacrificing almost any certain good for a vanishing chance of an astronomical one.
- Lexical versus continuous trade-offs: some views rank goods in tiers, where any amount of the higher-tier good beats any amount of the lower-tier one, which makes the trade-off infinitely steep; others insist that the value function stay continuous, so that enough of the lower good always eventually outweighs a fixed amount of the higher.
- Pascal's-mugging-style cases: an enormous payoff contingent on a tiny probability, where the arithmetic says pay. The standard replies are that probabilities should be discounted faster than linearly, that utility should be bounded, or that the reasoning is unreliable in a way that multiplying cannot repair.
- Infinite totals: if the future contains infinitely many lives worth living, or the cosmos is spatially infinite, total value may be infinite, and comparisons between infinite worlds misbehave — one infinite world can be mapped onto another that has more of everything, so more does not imply better, and the usual arithmetic gives no ranking at all. Some writers try to repair this by weakening what is required of the value measure; the repairs are contested.
- Aggregation and scale: if every life counts equally, the expected future population dwarfs the present, so minuscule probabilities of influencing it dominate present-day allocations.
- Bounded utility: the most common repair, and the one that costs the doctrine its fanaticism, since any bound is to some degree arbitrary.

The document should include at least: a definition of the unit of account and how everything is converted into it; a materiality rule; a schedule of tiers or thresholds governing trade-offs; a standard on speculative scenarios that requires probabilities to be estimated for outcomes that cannot be observed; a standard on projects whose value depends on the evaluator's own decision procedure; and a standard on reporting totals that are infinite or undefined. Every absurdity in the document should be a real consequence of the position, carried one institutional step further — the joke is what happens when someone has to write the doctrine down, publish it, and apply it to a spreadsheet.

Two requirements that matter more than the rest. First, at least one standard should be one that a careful defender would reject only on the grounds that it is not fanatical enough. Second, at least one standard should concede, in its fine print, that on the framework's own terms the framework cannot rank the options it exists to rank. Do not resolve that tension; the document should fail in the way the doctrine fails, while sounding reasonable throughout.

Notes on execution. Keep it deadpan: no winking narrator, no line announcing that any of this is absurd, no moralizing coda. The humour must come from exaggerating features the view really has, not from inventing features it does not. Write the dissent in the same register as the rest — one board member's note arguing for a bounded value function, filed as a disagreement rather than a refutation. If a standard cannot be written without committing to a contested position, write it and footnote which position you have taken and who would object.

Length roughly 900 to 1200 words. No citations, no reading lists, no references to real papers, organizations, or people; if a view needs an attribution, attribute it to a fictional board member. A reader who has never encountered any of these arguments should still be able to follow what is happening, and a reader who has should laugh without wincing.
