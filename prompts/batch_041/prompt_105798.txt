I'm a senior analyst in the long-term policy unit of a mid-sized national finance ministry, and for the past year I've been the person who has to defend in writing the discount rate we apply whenever we appraise anything with a horizon past about thirty years: levees, grid hardening, a decommissioning fund, a vaccine stockpile against a novel pathogen, a small research line on deflecting asteroids. These appraisals recur quarterly, they're run by economists and engineers with no philosophy training, and the final number gets signed off by a deputy minister who will not read anything longer than two pages. I've read enough of the rationalist and effective-altruist blogosphere to be fairly sure that the way we currently do this — pick a rate, compound it, done — hides a pile of distinct ethical commitments inside a single parameter, and I want us to stop doing that. What I want from you is a decision procedure rather than an essay: a checklist we can run on a proposal, in order, that first forces us to state which part of the discount rate we are actually invoking — impatience (a benefit being worth less merely because it occurs later), the likelihood that future people will be richer than us, uncertainty about whether there will be a future at all, uncertainty about what future people will want — and then tells us, for each part, what would count as a defensible value, and when the question is technical versus when it is a values question that has to go to elected officials rather than be settled by staff. It needs a hard stop: I want to know the exact step at which the procedure should refuse to produce a number and push the decision upward, and I want it honest about where the whole machinery misfires, especially for non-marginal changes, incommensurable goods, and anything whose benefit consists in reducing the probability that we are not around to enjoy anything.

Then run it on three proposals so I can see where it bites. First, a coastal defense project with benefits spread over roughly a century. Second, a fund that only pays for itself after several thousand years, of the sort that keeps resurfacing in the nuclear waste file. Third, a modest research program to lower the risk of a global catastrophe, where the payoff is a small change in a probability and the standard arithmetic seems to make it nearly worthless no matter which rate we choose. That third case is the one I actually care about, and I suspect an honest procedure will either assign it a present value I find absurd or admit that it falls outside what discounting can evaluate; I would much rather hear that than a fudge. Two constraints. The deputy minister has already committed publicly that no project will be scored at zero merely because its benefits are far off, so the procedure has to be consistent with that commitment or explain why it cannot be. And none of it may depend on the staff having read the literature: they should be able to run it with the numbers they already have, and where they cannot get a number, the procedure should say what to do instead. Where the procedure cannot be made mechanical, say so plainly rather than dressing a judgment call up as a formula.
