I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political science, and my training is almost entirely in quantitative causal inference: potential outcomes, identification strategies, DAGs, the fundamental problem of causal inference, the standard warnings about what counts as a well-defined treatment and what SUTVA rules out. I've spent the last two years on a dissertation using synthetic control and difference-in-differences, so I genuinely think in terms of treated units, donor pools, and counterfactual trajectories. This term, mostly as a break from my own work, I've been sitting in on a history seminar on the origins of the Cold War, and I keep having the strange experience of understanding every sentence and being unable to say what any of them would mean in my own vocabulary. The seminar is organized around the familiar three-way quarrel — the orthodox or traditionalist reading, the revisionist reading, and the post-revisionist attempt to distribute responsibility more widely — along with the recurring proposal that the whole question is malformed: that 'who started it' is a question about a narrative or a policy program rather than about a single event, and that the proper object of study is the structure of the postwar system rather than anybody's decision.

I'd like you to do something specific and fairly unforgiving with this. Take the central positions in that debate — roughly, that Moscow is to blame, that Washington is to blame, and that the question itself is the wrong one — and restate each of them as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: unit-level and average treatment effects, counterfactual dependence, interventions and manipulability, confounding and common causes, overdetermination and conjunctural causation, path dependence, and the distinction between a cause and a background condition. Treat this as a genuine translation exercise and not a debunking; I'm not looking for the claim that historians are just doing bad causal inference in prose. Where a historian's claim can be rendered as a claim about what would have happened under an intervention, render it, and tell me what the treatment variable would have to be, whether it is well-defined, and whether the required model is doing the argumentative work rather than reporting it. Where it can't be rendered, say exactly where the translation breaks. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which historiographical claims survive intact, which survive only after being quietly altered (and what got altered), and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I have three suspects in mind — the notion of blame or responsibility as something distinct from causal contribution; the idea that a whole system or structure rather than an agent can be what made an outcome inevitable; and the suggestion that the counterfactual I would need is not merely unknown but indeterminate, since there is no fact of the matter about how a Stalin who had been removed from the scene in 1946 would have behaved. For each item of residue, tell me whether you take it to reveal a real expressive limitation of the causal-inference framework, something it cannot represent without changing the subject, or whether it shows that the original claim was doing normative or rhetorical work it would be better off doing openly. I don't want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations; I want the translation and an honest ledger of what it fails to carry.
