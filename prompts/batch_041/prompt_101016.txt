Me and my flatmate have been going back and forth about the "separateness of persons" thing as an objection to utilitarianism — she's doing a philosophy module and I've just been reading around on my own, so I might have it garbled. The standard version I keep bumping into is that utilitarianism treats society as if it were one person writ large, adding up pleasures and pains across different people the way a single person weighs their own Tuesday against their own Wednesday, and that this fails to take seriously that you and I are different people with different lives. Fine, I get the slogan. But I've been trying to pin down what exactly is doing the work in it, and I hit on something I can't tell is profound or just confused, and I'd like you to sort it out for me.

Here's the thing. I can think of two quite different things you might mean by aggregating across persons. One is what I've started calling ledger-aggregation: you've got a fixed pile of good and bad outcomes that has to be distributed somehow, and the only question is how you trade one person's loss against another's gain, like moving entries around in a single account book. The other is what I'll call replacement-aggregation: you're allowed to make one person worse off in order to bring about a different person's good, or even to bring about a different *number* of people, so the set of people whose lives you're adding up isn't held fixed at all — the total just goes up because there are more positive entries, even if some existing ones get red-inked. Now, the separateness-of-persons complaint seems tailor-made for replacement-aggregation: if people are separate, how can one person's existence or happiness be a reason to write off another's? But ledger-aggregation seems harder to attack this way, because there you're at least respecting that each entry belongs to somebody and you're not conjuring new people to pad the books. And yet people seem to run the separateness objection against plain old sum-ranking utilitarianism, which is mostly about the ledger kind. So what I want is: (a) explain, as carefully as you can and without leaning on "everyone knows Rawls said so," what the separateness-of-persons objection actually is, and what work each of my two labels would and wouldn't do for it; (b) tell me whether the objection, pressed hard, also has to condemn the way a *single* person trades off their own present against their own future — because if my Tuesday-me and my Friday-me are as separate as you and I are, the objection seems to prove too much, and if they're not, I want to know what the principled difference is supposed to be; and (c) give me a verdict on whether my two labels carve anything real or whether I've just reinvented a distinction that collapses the moment you look at it. Take your time and go deep — I'd rather have a careful messy answer that lives in the hard parts than a tidy one that skates over them.
