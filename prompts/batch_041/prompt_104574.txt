I am assembling a session for an advanced seminar on the epistemology of markets, and I want a careful, opinionated comparison rather than a survey. The two items to compare are rival accounts of what a market price can be evidence for — of what, if anything, a participant learns from the mere fact that a price sits where it does.

The first, which I will call the aggregative account, holds that a price is a compressed public summary of dispersed private information: when many participants who each know a little bid against one another, the resulting price is a statistic reflecting more than any one of them knows. On this view a price is evidence, and the movement of a price is testimony about facts the observer did not previously have; the market is a device for turning private information into a public quantity that no participant could have assembled alone.

The second begins from an observation about trade itself. Exchange requires disagreement: two parties trade only if each believes he is getting the better of the bargain, which means they either value the object differently or assess the same facts differently. Now suppose it is common knowledge that all parties are rational, that they reason from the same prior probabilities, and that each knows what the others know. Then whatever reason I have for wanting to trade is either something you already know, or something I know you know, and so on without limit; the very disagreement that was to motivate the exchange dissolves under mutual knowledge. Yet the exchange, if it is to be agreed at all, is public between us. The conclusion pressed by this line of thought is that no such trade should occur, and that every observed trade must instead be explained by something other than the facts — differing priors, differing preferences or endowments, a need for liquidity, or plain error. Call this the no-trade account.

Please do the following.

1. State as sharply as you can what each account claims about one question: when a price moves, may a participant who sees the move treat it as evidence about something he did not already know? Identify where the two give opposite answers and where they merely answer different questions.

2. A colleague proposes a maxim she calls the Publicity Principle: nothing that is common knowledge can be a reason that distinguishes one person's position from another's, since a reason available to everyone cannot explain why you act and I do not. She claims the no-trade account is nothing but the Publicity Principle applied to exchange, and that the aggregative account is committed to denying the Principle. Assess the claim. If the aggregative account can accept the Principle, show how; if it cannot, say precisely what it must give up.

3. Work through a further tension. An aggregative account wants prices to be informative; but a price that fully revealed everything would leave no participant any incentive to have discovered anything in the first place, so the informativeness of the price would undermine the activity that makes it informative. Explain how this bears on the comparison, and whether it strengthens one account against the other or shows that both are unstable in the same way.

4. Then take a side. Which of the two deserves to be called the epistemology of markets, and which is doing something else under that name? Identify the single scenario in which they deliver opposite verdicts about whether a participant should revise his beliefs upon seeing a price, and say what the participant should do.

Argue rather than catalogue; I want the comparison to end in a position, with the costs of holding it stated explicitly.
