I am a retired invertebrate neurophysiologist. I spent some thirty years working on the nervous systems of decapod crustaceans; since retiring I have been reading a good deal of philosophy, with the result that several matters I once took to be settled by the anatomy no longer look settled to me at all.

A former student of mine, now employed by a seafood company, has asked me to sit on its ethics panel. The panel is considering one practice in the company's prawn hatcheries: the removal of a single eyestalk from each female brood animal, carried out without anaesthetic, in order to bring her into breeding condition. The company's position is that abandoning the practice would raise costs appreciably and lengthen every production cycle. The chair of the panel holds that any creature with a nervous system ought to be given the benefit of the doubt; the company's scientific adviser holds that a line has to be drawn somewhere and that prawns fall below it. Both of them strike me as more confident than the evidence allows.

I know the anatomy well enough. What I cannot work out is what follows from it. I see no reason to think that the loss of an eyestalk is painful to a prawn in anything like the way the loss of an eye is painful to me — but I also cannot see how my own failure to imagine the animal's inner life could settle the question either way, since much the same argument was once made, with a straight face, about dogs.

I should be grateful if you would tell me how a case of this kind is properly reasoned about: what weight, if any, the anatomy deserves; where the burden of proof ought to lie once the evidence runs out; and whether I am obliged to act on possibilities I cannot confirm. I am not asking for a reading list — I have no library to hand and would rather have the argument than the references — but I should also like a straight answer to the practical question: what ought I to advise the company to do?

Please write your answer in British English.
