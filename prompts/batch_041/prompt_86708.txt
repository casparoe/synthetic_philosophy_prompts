I am an undergraduate in a moral psychology seminar, and I am writing the final reflection paper for the unit we have just finished on judgments of intentional action. I would rather not simply summarize the readings. I want to take my own verdicts on a set of cases as the data and work out what they commit me to. I am hoping you can help me identify the principle, or the small set of rival principles, that best fits the pattern, tell me what each predicts for cases I have not yet considered, and be honest about which of my verdicts a principled account would force me to abandon.

The examples we were shown in lecture mostly varied whether the side effect was good or bad, so I have tried to vary other features as well, one at a time. I am reasonably confident about all of the following except the last.

1. Nadia manages a small dairy. To cut costs she switches to a cheaper cleaning agent. She has read the safety data sheet and is certain that the rinse water will run into the creek behind the plant and kill the trout there. She has no feelings about the trout one way or the other; the switch is entirely about cost. Did Nadia kill the trout intentionally? Yes.

2. Her cousin Omar runs a dairy two valleys over. He switches to a different agent, and he is certain the runoff will break up an algal bloom and let the trout return. He is indifferent to the fish and is switching only to save money. Did Omar restore the trout intentionally? No.

3. Riverbend is a firm with a well-known environmental ethos: every process is assessed by its effect on the watershed, and profit is treated as a tolerated byproduct; managers who raise profits by degrading the watershed are quietly eased out. A manager there, Sofia, introduces a process that she is certain will substantially raise profits, an effect she does not care about. Did Sofia raise profits intentionally? I find myself saying yes. The whole point of the place is that you are not supposed to do that, and she knew exactly what she was doing.

4. At a haulage firm it is an open secret that the December rush pushes some drivers past the legal limit, that a few are caught, and that they lose their licences; the practice is winked at, and there is even a standard form for recording the losses. A dispatcher, Tomasz, builds the December rota knowing with certainty that two drivers will be caught and lose their licences. He does not care about the drivers either way; he cares about the volume. Did Tomasz cause those two drivers to lose their licences intentionally? I say yes.

5. Suppose instead that Nadia had only been told the agent might be toxic, putting the risk at roughly sixty-forty, and switched anyway, and the trout died. Did she kill the trout intentionally? I say no; without the certainty the word seems too strong.

6. A registrar, Piotr, moves the office to a new filing system, knowing it will change the numbering on the forms. Did he change the form numbers intentionally? This is the one I cannot settle: on Monday I say yes and on Tuesday I say no.

The trouble is that cases 3 and 4 pull in opposite directions. In 3 the side effect is good and yet I call it intentional, which makes it look as though what matters is that it broke the local norm. In 4 the side effect is bad and I call it intentional even though it fits the local norm, which makes it look as though what matters is that it was bad. I cannot get both out of a single simple clause, and I would rather not end up with a principle that has a separate clause for every case. My own first attempt was that an agent brings about a side effect intentionally when she is certain of it and is not trying to avoid it, but that makes case 2 come out intentional, which I do not want, so it plainly needs repair or replacement.

What I would like from you: a principle, or a couple of rival principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a fresh case; the cases above that support each clause; what each principle predicts for a few cases I have not listed, for instance a side effect that is neither good nor bad and violates no norm, or one the agent actively regrets; and, most importantly, which of my six verdicts I should give up, and why that is the one to surrender rather than another.
