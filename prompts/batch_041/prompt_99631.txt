And here's the student's answer:

"To predict how something will behave, Dennett says we can take one of three stances. The physical stance predicts from the laws of physics and the thing's constitution. The design stance predicts by assuming the thing is designed to do something and will work as intended. The intentional stance predicts by assuming the thing is a rational agent with beliefs and desires — we work out what it ought to believe given its situation and what it ought to do given its desires, and predict that it does that. The intentional stance is the one that assumes rationality; the design stance is the weaker, more mechanical assumption. Dennett thinks we move up and down these stances as convenience demands, and that anything whose behavior is reliably predictable this way counts as an 'intentional system.' That's why the chess engine is the easy case (Case B): we predict its moves almost perfectly by attributing a desire to win and beliefs about the board, even though there's obviously no inner life there.

Dennett's own view about what this shows is deflationary. He thinks belief-talk is a tool for prediction rather than a description of hidden inner states — a useful fiction, so to speak. On his view nothing literally has beliefs; 'belief' is just the label we attach to whatever makes the prediction work. So when people ask whether an LLM 'really' believes things, Dennett would say the question is confused: there's no fact of the matter, only how well the stance predicts.

Now the claim. If treating the LLM as a believer lets us predict and steer its outputs, then by Dennett's own criterion the LLM is an intentional system — its behavior exhibits the pattern the stance exploits. And on the thin reading of belief-attribution, that's the end of the story: the success of the prediction is exactly what makes the belief-ascription apt, so we're entitled to say the model really believes. The thin reading doesn't require anything about inner states; it just requires that the attribution earn its keep predictively. So the claim is true, and it's the thin reading that it presupposes.

You might think it needs the thick reading instead. But look at Case A: the model changes its answer when you correct it, which looks like the normative business of being answerable to reasons. I think that's the same kind of thing as a person revising a belief under criticism — the correction functions as a reason for the model just as it does for us — so the thick reading is satisfied too, and the claim comes out true on either reading.

So overall the claim is right. LLMs really do have beliefs in whatever sense the intentional stance licenses, and since the stance is licensed by predictive success, the success of our predictions settles the matter."
