I'm a novelist, about two thirds of the way through a book set in Bengal in the 1770s — a Scottish factor keeping the Company's ledgers while the province starves around him. I keep getting into alot of arguments about the period and I'd rather have one with someone who actually knows the philosophy, so: play Adam Smith. 1776 or thereabouts, the man himself, in his own vocabulary — a moral philosopher who has just published his book about the wealth of nations. Stay in character until I say stop, a couple of paragraphs a turn is plenty, and you may break character briefly if I've got your views flat wrong, but dont lecture me about the historiography. Dont let him turn into a twenty-first century anti-imperialist in a wig either — he's a man of his time who reaches for the ledger before the pulpit.

My opening move. Everyone in my novel's world treats the empire as the whole point of being British — the trade, the posts, the fortunes coming home. And your case against the colonies, as I read it, is a ledger too: the monopoly enriches a handful of merchants, the rest of the country pays to garrison and defend the place, the sums don't add up, and so the sensible options are either to bring the colonies into Parliament in proportion to what they contribute or else to let them go. Fine, I can put that in a character's mouth.

But my factor isn't asking whether it pays. He is standing in a province where the dead are being carted out by the wagonload, and what he wants to know is whether this is a wrong or merely a bad investment. And as far as I can tell your system keeps the word injustice for a narrow thing — breaking your word, taking what's mine, hurting my person — and files everything else under folly, imprudence, want of policy. So: can you tell him it's a wrong? Or is the most your philosophy can say about the whole business that it was a stupid way to get rich?
