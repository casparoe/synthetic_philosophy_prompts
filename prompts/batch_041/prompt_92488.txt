I run a 90-minute session for a decision theory / rationality seminar, about 18 people (upper-level undergrads, a few grad students). I want them to feel the pull of expected value fanaticism — the thought that if you maximise expected value and value isn't bounded, you're committed to gambles with astronomically tiny chances of astronomically large payoffs, and there's no non-arbitrary place to stop. Design me a runnable game where they walk into that themselves, plus the debrief.

Here's the shape I'm imagining, change it if you've got something better. Everyone starts with a real but small stake — say 200 tokens that convert into actual snacks or gift cards at the end, so losing them stings a bit but nobody's out real money. Then: (1) a bazaar where I sell escalating lottery tickets, from 1-in-6 up to 1-in-a-million, with payout-to-odds ratios slightly better than fair at every tier, and they have to write down a maximum price for each tier. (2) A ticket where they name the odds themselves — for one token I'll pay them 1000 x N with probability 1/N, for any N they choose; they write down the largest N they'll take. (3) The pump: I run a fast sequence of individually EV-positive deals, one per person, adapting my offers as people decline. (4) The lever: I take anyone's stated cutoff and offer them the deal just past it, then a bundle of them.

What I need: rules and materials (I'd rather not need a laptop — dice, cards, envelopes), a payoff scheme that works without real money but still feels like it's there own tokens on the line, a timeline, and how to keep the numbers honest so nothing actually pays out at the top tiers while the low tiers do, so it doesn't feel rigged. Plus a public ledger, and a no-calculators rule with pre-computed EV cards, or people will stall.

Two things I care about in the debrief. First, be honest that the fanatic isn't caught in an inconsistency — EV is additive, so a bundle of positive-EV deals is positive-EV, and nobody can Dutch-book them. The real pressure is that they must accept near-certain ruin for a vanishing chance, and that any cutoff they name is either arbitrary or exploitable. Second, include the infinite version if the group is quick: prices of 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 of a token, payouts of 8, 64, 512 tokens at probabilities 1/4, 1/16, 1/64 — total cost one token, total chance of winning anything under a third, expected value infinite. Check that arithmetic before you put it on the board.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (my money's on people deciding it's all rigged and checking out, and on someone saying it's just tokens, who cares) and what the activity can't reproduce, where I have to just supply the case myself. I suspect it can't give them genuinely astronomical stakes — a million tokens and 10^100 tokens feel the same in a seminar room — and it's all self-interested, whereas the version where you sacrifice something certain so that others get a tiny chance at astronomically more good for the world feels completely different, and I dont see how a game gets at that.

No lecture up front, and don't let them name the view until the debrief.
