I work as a software engineer and have no training in philosophy. A friend who teaches clinical ethics recently set out an argument for me as a numbered chain of steps, and I have not been able to decide whether it is sound. Each step looks defensible when I read it on its own, but the end of the chain is a conclusion I cannot accept, and I cannot locate the point at which it goes wrong.

(1) Suppose a couple is going to have a child, and that they could bring about any one of several possible children, differing in traits that affect how well their lives go.

(2) The couple has a reason to bring about the child whose life can be expected to go best.

(3) That reason cannot rest on harm done to the child who is not brought about. A child who is never conceived is not made worse off by the choice, and no one is wronged when the couple takes the better option. The reason is therefore not owed to any person; it is a reason about welfare rather than a claim held by someone.

(4) The reason does not depend on the difference between the children being a disease. It applies equally to traits within the normal range.

(5) The reason does not weaken when the life of the child with the lower expected welfare would still be well worth living.

(6) The couple therefore has a reason to bring about the child with the best expected life, whether the difference at stake is a disease, a disability, or an ordinary trait.

(7) A welfare reason that is owed to no one, and that no one's claim blocks, is still a reason the couple ought to act on, provided nothing of comparable weight stands against it.

(8) Nothing of comparable weight ordinarily does stand against it: a preference for a child who resembles them, or an unwillingness to be troubled, does not outweigh a difference in how well the child's life goes.

(9) A couple who can choose among embryos and who chooses on any other basis therefore acts against a reason they ought to act on.

(10) If selection is available and affordable, conceiving without selection is choosing a lottery over the same possible children when the couple could have chosen the best of them.

(11) So the same verdict extends to unselected conception.

(12) Nothing in the argument is specific to the couple's own child, so the reason it identifies is a reason to bring about the best lives one can, wherever one can.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (2) and (5) than (11) or (12). It seems plainly true that a parent has a reason to want the child who will fare better, and plainly false that the difference between two lives stops mattering the moment both lives are worth living. But I am not prepared to accept (11) or (12). Which step should I reject, why did it look acceptable, and what would I be giving up by rejecting it?
