Ok so I've been going around in circles with a friend about whether the world is getting less violent, and at this point I don't want another round of the argument — I want a diagnosis of why nobody ever wins it.

Here's the shape as I see it. One side says: look at the long run. Homicide rates in medieval Europe, violent death rates in small-scale societies, deaths in war since 1945 — all of it points down, and the last century only looks bloody because there are so many more of us now. The other side says that's a story told by picking the metric: absolute numbers of violent deaths in the last century are the highest ever recorded; whether small-scale societies were more violent depends on archaeological samples that are tiny and probably unrepresentative (bodies with blunt-force trauma tell you about the deaths someone bothered to bury, not the baseline rate); and if you widen violence to include structural stuff — people dying of preventable disease, poverty, state neglect — the trend is alot less obviously down. Then a third person always chimes in that violence is an evaluative concept, so there's no fact of the matter either way.

My own hunch, which I want you to test rather than just agree with: the fight is mostly about the denominator, and the interesting part is that choosing a denominator is a value judgment in disguise. Take a metric I made up — violent deaths as a share of all deaths. On that metric the long-run decline looks enormous, which is nice, except the same metric would report more violence if we cured cancer, which seems absurd. So which metric is the right one, and is there any non-arbitrary way to answer that? Or does the arbitrariness itself show the question is ill-posed? There's a related wrinkle: we can only count violence that happens, so any violence prevented by deterrence is invisible to every metric, which quietly biases the whole thing.

So: is this a verbal dispute about the word violence, a real empirical dispute about the data, a fight about which cases count as data in the first place, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Tell me what each side actually commits to, where the hidden empirical questions are doing the work, and what would count as progress here — a distinction, a better dataset, a formal result, or just admitting the question is a Rorschach test. And be honest with me if you think it can't be settled at all.
