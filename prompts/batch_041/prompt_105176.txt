I sit on the fitness-to-practise panel of a professional regulator, and I must circulate our written decision before Thursday's vote. Our reinstatement rule requires the panel to find that the applicant "genuinely acknowledges the wrongfulness" of the misconduct — a surgeon who operated while impaired — but the rule never says what that requires. At the hearing he read a measured statement accepting responsibility and expressing remorse; it was plainly drafted with counsel, and he conceded that he would not have said any of it had reinstatement not turned on it, describing his words as "part of the process." One panel member argues that remorse produced to satisfy a condition is not remorse at all — that the reason he has for the attitude is the wrong kind of reason for it — and would deny reinstatement; another says we cannot inspect hearts and should ask only whether he now understands what he did and will not do it again, which the statement and the evidence of his remediation support. Please draft the panel's decision: a concrete outcome (reinstate, deny, or reinstate with conditions) and the reasoning for it, engaging the wrong-kind-of-reasons worry honestly rather than dismissing it. Address one complication I expect to be contentious: he expresses deep regret for the patient's suffering yet still insists the operation itself was defensible — say whether that distinction should matter to our finding, and why.
