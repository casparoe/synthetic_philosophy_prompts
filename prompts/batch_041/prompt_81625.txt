After thirty-five years as an experimental behavioral biologist — my own work was on foraging and social learning in birds — I have spent the early years of my retirement trying to get my bearings in the philosophy of religion, and specifically in the project of treating religion as a natural phenomenon rather than as something to be assessed only on its own terms. I have the scientist's habits and the philosopher's ignorance: I know how to operationalize a variable and design a control, but I do not yet know which questions in this literature are load-bearing and which are merely fashionable. What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey.

Please begin by declining to treat "religion" as a single explanandum. My working suspicion is that it is a folk category and a poor target for explanation, and that the real subject matter is a bundle of partly separable traits — belief in unseen agents, ritual, afterlife beliefs, moralizing gods, communal bonding, the felt authority of certain experiences. Organize the open questions the way an ethologist would, using the four questions we are trained to ask about any trait: its mechanism, its development in the individual, its function, and its evolutionary history. Tell me where those four quadrants come apart for this subject matter — whether the answers must be given separately for each trait and each quadrant, which quadrants are systematically neglected, and whether the four-question scheme itself misleads when the trait in question is a cultural representation rather than a bodily organ.

Then tell me which handful of open questions within this space deserve the effort, and defend the ordering. Fix the effort as follows: assume I have roughly one sustained project left in me, five to seven years of part-time work, no laboratory, free access to the empirical literature, and no capacity to collect my own data. Rank the questions by how much other work depends on them, how tractable they are for someone with my background rather than for a trained philosopher or an ethnographer, how neglected they are, and whether a resolution would change anything downstream — and be explicit about where those criteria pull against one another. I would rather see three questions argued for than fifteen listed.

I am especially interested in three candidates I keep circling, and I would like you to say whether they belong near the top or whether I am overweighting them. First, whether a naturalistic account can explain the content of religious representations and not merely their distribution — that is, why these particular beliefs rather than any other beliefs of the same psychological form. Second, whether the by-product and adaptation accounts are genuine rivals at all, or whether they answer different questions in the four-quadrant scheme and have been made to collide by a confusion of levels. Third, whether the debunking arguments in this area need the naturalistic explanation to be true, or only to be better supported than its competitors.

For each question you rank highly, say what evidence or argument over the next few years would move it up or down the list, and name at least one popular question you would deprioritize, with your reasons.
