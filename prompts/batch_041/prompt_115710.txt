I'm a novelist and I'm in the middle of a book that's half about the First World War and half about a grad student in the sixties who gets obsessed with the scrap among the German historians over whether the war was mainly Germany's fault — the fight where saying mostly yes got you accused of calling an entire people murderers. I've got the feel of that argument from reading around, so don't give me a reading list, that's not what I'm after. What I'm after is the philosophy under a machine that's still running today.

Here's what I keep tripping over. The international order has two habits that seem to want opposite things. In one room: since Nuremberg, the line is that the worst things a state can do — start an aggressive war, commit genocide — are done by individual people, and only individual people can be charged with them. No state ever stands in the dock; you indict the man. In the next room: states get held to account in a completely different register. Germany signed a clause in the Versailles treaty accepting responsibility for the war and then spent the better part of a century paying reparations out of the pockets of people born long after the guns stopped. Countries issue official apologies. Sanctions land on a country as such. A nation's schools are expected to carry the memory of what it did.

What I want from you is the implicit theory. What view of responsibility do those two habits presuppose? Does the pair require that a nation can be an agent — that it can intend, decide, and deserve blame — or can everything on the reparations-and-apology side be rebuilt out of duties that fall on individual citizens, with "Germany owes" as shorthand for a bundle of personal obligations? If it can be rebuilt, tell me whats lost, because something feels lost to me: a tax bill isn't an apology, and an apology doesn't come out of a treasury. If it can't, then I want to know which parts of the practice strictly need the collective-agent picture, which parts merely sit comfortably with it, and where the whole thing is underdetermined — where two or three rival theories of responsibility would license exactly what we see, and nothing in the practice can choose between them.

Then the part I actually need for the novel. Does the practice have to pick one, persons or nations, or does it presuppose that both are true at once in different registers — criminal guilt for the man, political and remedial responsibility for the country? If it's the second, is that coherent, or just a fudge that survives because the two registers never meet in the same courtroom? And push it both ways for me. What concretely changes if we drop the collective side and only ever blame individuals — what happens to reparations, to apologies, to the idea that a German born in 1950 inherits anything at all? And what changes if we go the other way and let states be real moral agents who can be guilty and not merely liable? I dont much care which side is right. I want to see what each practice is quietly assuming, and where the assumptions rub against each other.
