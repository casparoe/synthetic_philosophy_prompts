I've been reading rationalist blogs for a decade and I write software for a living, so the vocabulary I actually think in is interfaces and contracts: preconditions and postconditions, invariants, breaking changes, semantic versioning, service-level agreements, and, of course, async promises, which either resolve or reject and are supposed to have a handler on every rejection path. I also have the standard rationalist reflex of treating a promise as a commitment device — something you bind yourself with because you know you would otherwise cave later.

Take the main positions on why promises bind — that the obligation comes from a social practice plus the fairness of doing your part in it; that the wrong lies in inviting someone to rely on you and then failing them; that a promise is at bottom a prediction about your own future conduct; and that it is a way of handing someone else authority over what you do next — and restate each in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, without quietly importing the software side's assumptions (enforcement, opt-in dependencies, deterministic failure modes) and calling the result a translation. Then give me the residue report: what carries over cleanly, what carries over only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. I especially want to know whether the vocabulary can mark the difference between a promise that is broken and a dependency that merely fails; whether my commitment-device gloss is one of the four positions or a fifth that changes the subject; and, if the residue turns out to be large, whether that indicts the software vocabulary or the philosophers' notion of binding.
