I've been reading Hume's Of Suicide — the one that got pulled from his essays and only came out after he was dead — and there's a stretch near the start that I can't get my head round. Here it is:

'In order to govern the material world, the almighty creator has established general and immutable laws, by which all bodies, from the greatest planet to the smallest particle of matter, are maintained in their proper sphere and function. To govern the animal world, he has endowed all living creatures with bodily and mental powers; with senses, passions, appetites, memory, and judgment; by which they are impelled or regulated in that course of life, to which they are destined. These two distinct principles of the material and animal world continually encroach upon each other, and mutually retard or forward each other's operation. The powers of men and of all other animals are restrained and directed by the nature and qualities of the surrounding bodies; and the modifications and actions of these bodies are incessantly altered by the operation of all animals. Man is stopped by rivers in his passage over the surface of the earth; and rivers, when properly directed, lend their force to the motion of machines, which serve to the use of man. But tho' the provinces of the material and animal powers are not kept entirely separate, there result from thence no discord or disorder in the creation: On the contrary, from the mixture, union, and contrast of all the various powers of inanimate bodies and living creatures, arises that surprizing harmony and proportion, which affords the surest argument of supreme wisdom.

The providence of the deity appears not immediately in any operation, but governs every thing by those general and immutable laws, which have been established from the beginning of time. All events, in one sense, may be pronounced the action of the almighty: They all proceed from those powers, with which he has endowed his creatures. A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation.'

What I want is a careful reconstruction of the arguemnt, step by step, and then your honest answers to the things that keep tripping me up:

1. How is this supposed to get him to 'suicide is no breach of our duty to God'? Give me the premises as he'd state them, and mark the step where he's relying on something he hasn't argued for.

2. The rivers and the falling house: what work is the analogy actually doing? Is the thought just that a man using his own powers is on a par with a man redirecting a river, or is there more in it?

3. He says all events may be called God's action 'in one sense'. What's the other sense, and is that hedge load-bearing or is it just him covering himself? Related: doesn't the whole thing only go through if God never acts in a particular event, which is exactly what the people he's arguing against deny? I don't know if there's a name for that move, I just want to know if that's what he's up to.

4. It looks to me like the arguemnt proves too much. If every use of an endowed power under a general law is fine by God, then murdering my neighbour is also fine by God, and Hume presumably doesn't want that. Where in the passage is the distinction supposed to live, and if it isn't there, does the argument only tell against people who think God micro-manages deaths?

5. 'that surprizing harmony and proportion, which affords the surest argument of supreme wisdom' — is that Hume in his own voice, or is he borrowing the design-theorist's language in order to turn it against them? I can't tell whether it's sincere, sarcastic or just a concessive gesture, and I'd rather have your read than a diplomatic one.

I'm not after the literature or a summary of the essay, I've read the thing — I want this passage worked through properly, including where I'm over-reading it.
