I'm in a reading group on minds and machines (mixed crowd — a couple of cog sci people, one historian of biology, me, a philosopher who mostly does ethics), and we keep running aground on the same slogan: competence without comprehension, and all the talk of Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning. I can repeat the lines, but I've realised I can't actually explain what the inversion is, and when I try I just end up restating it louder. So: act as a philosopher of mind who works on this stuff and walk me through it properly. Start by brainstorming the different things the slogan could be doing, then give me the explanation. I want the two inversions laid out seperately — what each one reverses, which premise ends up doing the work that a mind used to do, why each felt like a scandal when it landed — and then the part I actually care about: whether they are one move or two. My suspicion is that Darwin reverses the order of explanation between design and designer (design stops being evidence for a mind and becomes an output of a mindless process), whereas Turing severs a different link, the one between doing the thing correctly and having any grasp of what you're doing: competence in, comprehension out. But I can also see a reading where the Turing inversion is really the other thing, the one about swapping can machines think for an operational test, and I want you to say which reading the slogan needs and whether they come to the same place. If the two inversions aren't the same inversion wearing different hats, tell me where the analogy breaks — Darwin's mindless process is still a mechanism churning out design-like products, but the Turing machine was designed by a mind in the first place, so is anything really being explained, or is the comprehension just being relocated one floor up into the designer, the programmer, or the selective environment?

Then press hard on comprehension itself, because that's where I get lost. If comprehension is just a higher grade of competence — being able to answer questions about what you're doing, notice when you've gone wrong, explain yourself — then the slogan risks being true but boring, and I want you to say so if that's the case. And if it's something else, what is the something else, and how would I ever tell from the outside that it's missing rather than merely not on display? Take a handful of cases and say for each what the inversions do and don't license you to say: a thermostat, a chess engine, a compiler, a slime mould finding its way through a maze, an immune system telling self from non-self, an ant colony, and a language model writing a fluent paragraph about Pascal. For each one I want to know whether the competence is real and the comprehension genuinely absent, or whether the comprehension has simply been pushed onto whoever built or trained the thing, and whether that difference is even detectable in principle.

Two things to avoid: don't hand me a reading list or a who-said-what tour, I can look that up myself and I want the argument, not the lineage; and don't smooth over the joints — if a step in the story is doing more work than it can bear, say so and say which step. Think it through carefully before you start writing, and flag anywhere you're genuinely unsure rather than papering over it.
