I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-odd years working on beetles — mostly the dull end of systematics, describing things and arguing about whether they were new species — and I've spent retirement reading philosophy, mainly because I kept losing arguments at conferences and never knew why. The one that won't leave me alone is the species problem. I don't want an essay about it, I want an instrument.

Here's the ask: build me a decision tree (or a "which position do you actually hold" quiz, whatever's cleaner) that starts from someone who already accepts that life evolves and that populations change, and walks them down to a named position on what a species is. Every branch node has to be a question they can answer without knowing which position it leads to — no node that just asks "are you a realist about species?" Give me the question, in plain terms, and the answer that splits the branch. I'd like eight or nine leaves, and please label each leaf by the claim it makes, not by who held it; I don't know the literature and I'd rather not fake it.

Cases I want the tree to actually use, because these are the ones that broke my intuitions: a ring of populations that interbreed around the ring but not where the ends meet; a parthenogenetic weevil lineage with no sex at all; two cryptic forms that look identical until you sequence them; a narrow hybrid zone where the two sides stay distinct; and a long fossil series that grades smoothly from one form into another with no obvious break. Each of those should be the thing that pushes someone down a branch, not decoration.

Two views I half-understand and want the tree to place, because I suspect they're where the real action is. First: there IS exactly one thing that makes something a species — being a stretch of a lineage between two branching events — and all the usual criteria (interbreeding, diagnosability, ecological distinctness) are just fallible clues to that, none of them necessary. Second: species are real because a bundle of properties reliably hang together for a shared reason (gene flow, say), even though no single property is necessary and different bundles do the job in different groups. If those two land on the same leaf, say so and tell me what separates them; if they don't, tell me which node splits them.

Then the parts I care about most: which combinations of answers nobody occupies, and why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?). Which leaves your tree can't pry apart even though they sound different? And which single node is doing the most work — the one that, flip it, moves the most people.

Keep it to the tree and your notes on it, no reading list, I'm not going to chase down citations. Also theres no rush, I'm retired.
