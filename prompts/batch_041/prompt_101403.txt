I have no background in philosophy — I studied accounting and now run a small business — but for the past year or so I have been reading popular books on the subject and arguing about them with a friend who teaches physics, and one puzzle has lodged itself in my head that I cannot resolve on my own. It is this. Suppose two rival accounts of reality agree on absolutely everything any observer could ever detect — every instrument reading, every measurement, every experiment, every experience — and differ only over some matter that no observer could reach even in principle: whether two things that behave in every respect identically are in fact one thing or two, or whether there exists some entity that by its very nature leaves no trace on anything observable. I have come to hold the view that in such a case there is no further fact of the matter about which account is correct — not that the fact is hidden from us, but that the question of which ontology is right simply has no answer, so that treating one account as more true than the other is a mistake, or at best a matter of convenience. I want to know what would count against this view: which observations, arguments, or formal results should move me off it, and which should leave me where I am. I assume some of these defeaters are decisive and others are merely awkward, and I would like them sorted that way, from fatal to tolerable.

Two things trouble me particularly, and I would like the answer to address them directly. The first is that I keep sliding between saying there is no fact of the matter and saying merely that we could never know the fact of the matter, and I cannot tell whether that slide is a real equivocation that damages the position or whether the two are closer than they sound. The second is that I suspect the view might be defensible only by turning into something much stronger and stranger — that reality itself is somehow relative to what observers can access — and I want to know whether that is the price of the position or whether it can be had more cheaply. Please also tell me what a careful defender of the view would themselves concede counts against it, not only what an opponent would throw at it, and whether the view as I have stated it risks anything at all or has been phrased so cautiously that nothing could ever count against it — and if the latter, whether that is a defect or simply the cost of a view about ontology.
