I am the head TA for a 300-level course called Meaning and Mind, and I am grading the third problem set, which covers the rule-following material (Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein, the skeptical challenge, and the standard responses). I want each script marked against the same rubric and annotated point by point, so that when I return them the students see exactly where the argument holds up and where it does not. Our scale is a letter grade from A to F with plus/minus, and the convention in this course is that an A-range answer is accurate and complete on every part; a B-range answer is solid on the main points but contains a real error or a gap that affects the argument; a C-range answer contains an error that goes to the central claims of the question. Please give the grade first and then the comments, keyed to the parts of the question, and say explicitly which errors are decisive and which are merely worth noting; I do not want a general impression of the answer's quality, I want the specific places where it succeeds and where it goes wrong. Where a part is wrong, tell me what a fully correct answer would have said instead. Please do not soften the comments on my account; I am marking a stack of these and need the same standard applied to all of them.

The question was as follows.

"Kripke's Wittgenstein presents a skeptic who claims that nothing in my past behavior or mental life determines whether, by 'plus', I meant addition rather than quaddition. (a) Reconstruct the skeptical argument in your own words, making clear both what the skeptic claims and what he does not claim. (b) Kripke distinguishes straight solutions from skeptical solutions. State the distinction, and use it to classify each of the following three responses: the dispositionalist response, the community/assertibility response, and the non-reductionist ('primitive grasp') response. (c) Now replace the arithmetical predicate with a perceptual one. Suppose a subject has applied the word 'gred' only to mid-spectrum red patches viewed in daylight, and we ask whether she meant gred (a predicate that agrees with all her past applications and diverges under, say, sodium lighting) or red. Does the skeptical challenge carry over to this case with the same force, with more force, or with less? Defend your answer."

One student's answer read as follows.

"(a) The target of the skeptic is not arithmetic but the fact of meaning. By hypothesis I have never added numbers greater than or equal to 57, so every answer I have ever given is compatible both with my having meant addition and with my having meant quaddition, where quaddition agrees with addition on all the cases I have actually computed and returns 5 otherwise. Because my finite history of use is consistent with indefinitely many such rules, and because any interpretation of my rule can itself be reinterpreted, so that rules for interpreting rules get us nowhere, there is no fact of the matter as to whether I meant plus or quus. The skeptic does not deny that 125 is the sum of 68 and 57, nor that I should go on saying '125' in ordinary life; he denies only that anything constitutes my meaning one thing rather than the other. Kripke's Wittgenstein accepts the argument rather than rebutting it, which is why he offers a skeptical solution rather than a straight one.

(b) A straight solution rebuts the skeptic by producing the fact that fixes meaning; a skeptical solution concedes that there is no such fact and instead explains why we are nevertheless entitled to our ordinary talk of meaning. The dispositionalist gives a straight solution — to mean plus is to be disposed to give the answers the plus function requires — and Kripke's decisive objection is that my dispositions are finite while the function is infinite: I will make mistakes, run out of time, and die before I have answered every sum, so my dispositions cannot pick out the infinite function. The community response is likewise a straight solution: I mean plus because my answers agree with what my community is disposed to accept, and it is the community's verdict, not my own private history, that supplies the missing fact. The non-reductionist position is also a straight solution: it says there is a primitive, sui generis fact of grasping a rule, one that resists analysis into dispositions or anything else, and it is the most honest of the three.

(c) The perceptual case is less threatening. With 'plus' the rule ranges over an infinite domain, so the gap between my finite use and the infinite rule is stark; with 'gred' the range of relevant shades and lighting conditions is finite and in principle surveyable, which is just the sort of gap the skeptic exploits in the arithmetic case but which we can close here. And whereas the arithmetic skeptic is confined to my outward behavior, the perceptual subject has introspective access to the very shade she sees; that felt quality is precisely the kind of inner fact that fixes what she meant, and it is exactly the sort of fact Kripke's arithmetic skeptic cannot appeal to. So the analogy weakens the challenge rather than strengthening it."
