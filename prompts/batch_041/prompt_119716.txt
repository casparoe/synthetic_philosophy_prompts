I am a high school senior and the chair of my school's philosophy club, and for the better part of this year I have been refereeing a running argument between two of our most stubborn members. I have concluded that the argument is not going to be resolved by another meeting of the same kind, and I would like you to draft the document the two of them should have produced instead: a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration.

The two positions are these. The first member is a Burkean. She holds that inherited rules, customs, and institutions carry a presumption in favor of preservation, and she appeals to the principle commonly named after G. K. Chesterton — that one should not take down a fence until one knows why it was put up. Her view is that a reformer's inability to state a fence's purpose is itself a reason to keep the fence, because the purpose may be genuine and yet invisible to someone who has never had to live with the consequences of its absence. The second member is a reformer. She grants that institutions have functions, but she holds that the Chesterton principle, as it is actually deployed in argument, operates as a burden-of-proof rule that always lands on whichever party is presently being harmed, and that our ignorance of what a rule is for is not evidence that the rule is for anything. Her phrase for it is that it is a machine for converting our ignorance into somebody else's obligation.

I want the document to have four parts, and I want it to be a text both of them could actually put their names to.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These should be claims that are neither vacuous nor slanted — things both would defend in front of the club — covering what the fence principle does and does not assert, what follows from the fact that institutions sometimes have functions different from the ones they advertise, and whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal. My own suspicion, which I would like tested rather than assumed, is that a good deal of it is: that when she says there is a reason to preserve the fence she often means only that there is a reason to investigate before removing it, and that the reformer hears the stronger claim.

Second, a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording that the side holding it would accept as fair and that the other side would accept as not being a caricature.

Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one of the two. I believe one is whether ignorance of a function counts as evidence that a function exists; another is whether the relevant question concerns a fence's origin, its present effects, or the effects of removing it; and a third is whether the asymmetry between the two kinds of evidence justifies a general presumption of preservation or only a case-by-case demand for evidence.

Fourth, for each crux, a case, test, or argument that both of them would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — not a hedge, but a specific expectation that could turn out false.

To keep the document from drifting into abstraction, I have asked both of them to work with the following material, and I want you to use it.

- The distinction between a documented fence, whose purpose is on record, and an orphaned fence, whose purpose has been lost, and the question of whether the presumption should differ between the two.
- The handrail that turned out to be a brace: a staircase railing that everyone described as a safety feature, which a reformer removed after installing what she took to be a safer replacement, and whose removal caused structural damage, because the railing had also been holding up a wall. The moral both of them should have to face is that a fence's stated purpose can be a misdescription of its function.
- The empty ritual: a rule whose only remaining function is to mark membership in a group that no longer exists, and whose costs fall on people outside the group.
- The adaptive-testimony problem: people's stated attachment to a longstanding arrangement is partly produced by the arrangement itself, so their testimony is not neutral evidence of its value — together with the conservative's reply that this argument, applied consistently, discredits testimony that the arrangement causes harm just as much.
- The visibility asymmetry: the costs of the status quo are borne now and are plainly visible, while the benefits of a fence are counterfactual and invisible, so neither side's evidence base is neutral. I want the document to say what each of them thinks this asymmetry biases us toward, since they seem to agree that it exists and disagree entirely about its direction.
- The regress point: the instruction to know a purpose before removing a fence is itself an inherited rule, so ask whether the fence principle applies to itself, and what each of them takes the answer to show.
- A procedural proposal I want them to evaluate on its merits: a fence register, under which anyone who removes an inherited rule must record the reason for removal and post a demolition bond, forfeited if a specified harm appears within a stated period. The conservative's position is that this is the Chesterton principle made operational and fair; the reformer's is that it is a tax on the injured, and that the bond will in practice be posted by whoever can afford it rather than by whoever benefits.

Where the two of them agree that a crux cannot be settled by any evidence available to us, I want that said explicitly, along with what they agree it implies for how the disagreement ought to be conducted in the meantime. Please write it in a register suitable for publication in the club's end-of-year journal, and please do not pad the agreed-claims section with statements so thin that no one could deny them.
