I run a 90-minute data-ethics seminar for a mixed group of CS and philosophy grad students, usually 16-20 people. I want an in-class activity where they build a risk-scoring rule by hand and, without being told the punchline, walk straight into the fairness impossibility result — the one where you can't have a score that's calibrated within each group and also has equal false-positive and equal false-negative rates across groups, unless the groups already have the same base rate. I want them to hit the wall themselves, not hear me describe it.

Give it to me in these sections, in this order:

- **Setup and materials** (~120 words): the room layout, the handout I need to print, how groups are formed, what each group physically does.
- **The three acceptance criteria, and the order to reveal them** (~250 words): I'll be playing a regulator and handing the criteria to the groups one at a time. Tell me exactly how each one is phrased to them, in plain language a non-specialist can act on, and what each criterion is really demanding underneath.
- **Minute by minute** (~150 words): a timeline for the 90 minutes, including when I drop each new criterion and when I stop them.
- **What I should expect to see happen** (~150 words): the moves groups will make, and the moment they start to feel the squeeze.
- **The debrief** (~300 words): map what happened in the room onto the actual result — which criterion is doing which moral work, why someone would demand each one, and what you give up when you drop one. Name the culprit (unequal base rates) explicitly, and give me the honest escape hatches: fix the upstream base rate, or accept that you have to pick a criterion and defend it as a value judgment rather than pretend the math decides.
- **What goes wrong the first time I run it** (~150 words): the predictable failure modes and how to head them off.
- **What this activity can't do, that I'll have to supply** (~120 words): be frank about the parts I can't get the game to reproduce and will have to argue for from the front of the room.

A few things to bake in. The handout is a printed table of about 40 made-up past cases, each with a couple of noisy features and an outcome column, split into two groups whose base rates I set deliberately different (say around 30% positive in one, 15% in the other). No computers — they do this with pencil and the table. Each group has to output, for each of the two groups, a threshold on a score they construct, plus a one-paragraph defense.

I want one specific twist in the debrief that I don't want telegraphed earlier: the outcome column is a noisy proxy for the thing we actually care about, and the noise is worse for one group than the other. Near the end the facilitator reveals that the outcome column was generated by a process that catches one group more reliably than the other, and asks what that does to the whole exercise — because a rule can be perfectly calibrated and error-balanced on the proxy and still be unfair on the construct. Don't let this be the headline though; the headline is the three-way tradeoff.

Predictable failure modes I want named: someone will game it by making the score so coarse that the criteria are vacuously satisfied; someone will refuse to engage and just moralize about the tool existing at all; someone will try to fix the base-rate gap by relabeling cases; and I'll probaly run out of time before the debrief because the last criterion always takes longer than I think.

And keep it honest on limits: the activity can't show that the tradeoff is a theorem rather than an artifact of my little table, it can't get at the general continuous-score or many-group versions, and it can't make them feel why anyone would ever ship a fair-but-inaccurate tool over an accurate-but-unfair one — I'll have to make that argument myself.
