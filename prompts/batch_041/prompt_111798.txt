I run a small company that rents and services heavy construction equipment across three states, and for eighteen years I have run it on a single rule: when the work is there and the machines are cheap, buy, hire, and borrow, because the window closes long before you can be certain it was open. It has served me well. I have also watched two competitors who did the opposite — held cash, expanded only when the numbers were undeniable — go under, each of them during a boom they had convinced themselves was a bubble.

Now I cannot tell which way the times are going. Rates have moved, public money is flowing into some kinds of projects and away from others, and the manufacturers want a decision on a fleet order by the end of next month. My partner, who has kept our books for a decade and whose instincts run opposite to mine, says the era that rewarded my method is over and that we have to become flexible: hold cash, lease instead of buy, wait and see, take the work that comes. He may be right, and I still cannot do it. Every time in my life I have tried to be cautious I have lost, and not through bad luck — by the time I was certain, the job had gone to someone who was not. When I ask him what flexibility would consist of in practice, he says that we have to be able to change with the times, which is rather like telling a man to be taller. I am fifty-six years old and I have one temperament. He answers that this is only survivorship, that I was lucky to be standing in a rising market, and that the same stubbornness that built this company will bury it.

So my question is larger than whether to order the machines. How much of what happens to a person in a business like mine is his own doing, and how much is the times he happens to be standing in? If no one can know in advance which way the times will turn, and a man cannot exchange his nature for one better suited to them, what is the actual counsel — for me, this year, with a partner across the table who is effectively telling me to become someone else? Is his advice advice at all, or is it the sort of thing that can only be said once the outcome is known? And if the counsel is that a person should arrange his affairs so that something other than his own nature does the adapting, I want to know what that would concretely be, and whether it preserves the method that built this company or quietly replaces it.
