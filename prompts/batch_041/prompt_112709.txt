For thirty-five years I worked in experimental geochemistry, and since retiring I have been trying to make up for lost time by reading philosophy with a group of similarly retired scientists and engineers. Next month it is my turn to lead, and I have chosen the topic of convention as Lewis analyzes it and the question of how conventions and norms evolve. The group is intelligent and mathematically literate but has almost no philosophy beyond a single undergraduate course; they will be suspicious of anything that looks like wordplay and will want to know what work the distinctions are doing. I do not want you to explain Lewis to me; I want a teaching plan for the session. Begin with the order in which the ideas have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it: the coordination problem, the difference between a regularity and a rule, the role of expectation and conditional preference, the common-knowledge condition, the existence of an alternative regularity that everyone would prefer on condition of others' conformity, and then the evolutionary question of how such a convention can arise, persist, and change without anyone agreeing to it. I especially want the group to see why the alternative-regularity clause is doing real work, and why it is not the same as saying the convention is a matter of indifference. Tell me where in that sequence they are most likely to nod along while actually misunderstanding, and how I should head off each misunderstanding at the moment it is most likely to form.

I have a carrying example I would like to use, drawn from my old lab, and I want you to tell me how to make it do the work rather than merely illustrate. In the shared instrument facility, users reserve the autoclave by clipping a colored clothespin to a pegboard; there is no written rule, no one assigned the clothespin, and a whiteboard or a sign-up sheet would have served just as well, yet the practice is now expected and newcomers are quietly corrected if they ignore it. I want this example to carry the session. What must it have, and what must it conspicuously lack, for it to be a Lewis-style convention rather than an explicit rule, a mere habit, or a moral obligation? When should I introduce a second, contrasting case—perhaps two people passing in a narrow corridor, or the choice of which side of the road to drive on—to make the arbitrariness vivid without letting the familiar traffic example take over? Finally, give me the misconceptions I should expect and the exact point in the sequence where each should be addressed: that a convention is an agreement, that it is a norm with independent normative force, that any regularity is a convention, that common knowledge is just everyone knowing, that an arbitrary convention is therefore arbitrary for an individual to follow, that an inefficient convention will be replaced, and that an evolutionary explanation either vindicates or debunks the convention. End with one question I can put to the group that would show whether they have understood the structure rather than memorized the example, and tell me what to leave out at this level and say explicitly that we are leaving it out.
