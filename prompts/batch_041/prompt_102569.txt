ok so I've been reading rationalist blogs since like 2009 and I've ended up with a pile of views about rationality that I'm now pretty sure don't all fit together, and I'd like a consistency audit before I embarass myself in a reading group. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Credences should be Bayesian, and the correct theory of instrumental rationality is expected utility maximization — you pick the action with the highest expected utility given your beliefs and desires.

(2) Satisficing is genuinely rational, not just a budget hack. An agent can be fully rational stopping at the first option that clears some aspiration level, even when a better option exists that they never bothered to look at. "Good enough" is a real standard, not second-best maximizing.

(3) The right theory of bounded rationality is resource-rational analysis: a bounded agent should optimize subject to its computational and information constraints. Heuristics are at best approximations to that constrained optimum, and the interesting question is always "optimal given what resources?"

(4) Rationality is purely instrumental. There's no rational criticism of your final ends; only the means are up for evaluation.

(5) The heuristics-and-biases stuff is basically right — when people deviate from the Bayesian / expected-utility benchmark, that's an error, and the whole "ecological rationality" save (heuristics are smart adaptations, not biases) is cope.

A friend of mine calls the thing I'm worried about "the redescription treadmill": you can always redescribe a satisficer as a maximizer who happens to have search costs baked into the objective, and if you can always do that, then satisficing either dissolves into maximizing or it's just irrational. I genuinely can't tell whether I'm on the treadmill or not.
