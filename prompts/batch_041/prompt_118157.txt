I'm in a weekly AI safety reading group — we meet Tuesdays, usually six or seven of us, half of whom are linguists who wandered in for the free pizza — and we've spent the last two months on the debate proposal: you put two models in a game where one argues each side of some question in front of a judge that's weaker than both of them, and the hope is that the honest side wins, because any lie the other side tells can supposedly be exposed. The idea keeps collapsing in the details and nobody in the group agrees on when it actually holds. My friend Maya has become our unofficial oracle because she has confident verdicts on a pile of toy cases and they never seem to contradict each other. Roughly: (1) The judge wants to know whether a photo shows a cat; the honest debater is the bigger model; the two sides want opposite outcomes. Maya says honest wins, trivially. (2) Does this little program halt? True answer, it never does. There's no short proof of that, and the dishonest side can bury its false claim inside a long argument the judge can't follow. Both debaters are the same model wearing different system prompts, and both get a bonus if the episode ends quickly. Maya says honest loses — there's nothing the judge can check, and nothing stopping the other side from simply being louder. (3) What are the prime factors of this big number? The honest debater, again the bigger model, just hands over the factors, the judge multiplies them to check, and the two sides are opposed. Maya says honest wins, even though the judge could never have found the factors herself. (4) Is this new drug safe long term? The honest answer is no, and nobody has a short argument for it. Both debaters are paid by the company if the judge concludes it's safe, and the honest one is the smaller model. Maya says honest loses, and she insists this is a different kind of failure than (2).

Next week's case is the one I actually care about, and I want to guess her verdict before she opens her mouth. The question is whether a certain network never produces an unsafe output on any input in some class — a universal negative, no short certificate anywhere. The debaters are strictly opposed, zero sum, one wins what the other loses. The honest debater is much stronger than the dishonest one, big model against small one, and the judge sits somewhere in between. So what does Maya say, and why? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she's been running all along: list the rules that fit all four of her earlier calls equally well, say what each one predicts about the new case, tell me which you'd bet on and what would change your mind, and name the single case I should put to her next to separate the survivors. She'll deny she's running any rule at all, which is exactly why I want the list.
