I am a backend engineer at a company that builds a workflow platform used by universities and hospitals to run institutional processes: appointments, credentialing, certifications, approvals, offers of admission, formal complaints. I have a casual acquaintance with the philosophy of language — I have read Austin and Searle on speech acts and I grasp the broad outlines of the performative/constative contrast and the standard taxonomy of illocutionary acts — but I have no research-level command of the area, no patience for the secondary literature, and, more to the point, I am not going to have any way to look things up while I read your answer. My working vocabulary is the one I actually use at my job. Everything in our platform is a command sent by an authenticated principal to a state machine: validators check preconditions against current state and against the sender's capability tokens; a command either commits atomically or fails with an error code; committing emits an event to an append-only ledger; clients attach idempotency keys so that retries do not double-apply; some commands are unilateral and some require an acknowledgment from a counterparty before they take effect; multi-step processes are coordinated as sagas with compensating transactions; we distinguish provisional from committed state; and notification fan-out is at-most-once. We also maintain a customer-editable rule table whose rows have the form: a document of type F, submitted by a holder of role R, counts as a G. That table is how our customers encode their own institutional rules, and it is the closest thing to normativity anywhere in the system.

What I want from you is a faithful translation of the Austinian and Searlean picture into that vocabulary, followed by an honest residue report. Go concept by concept: the distinction between what an utterance says and what it does; the three-way distinction among the act of saying something, the act performed in saying it, and the act performed by saying it; felicity conditions as against truth conditions; the difference between an infelicity that voids the act and an infelicity that merely shows the speaker to be at fault; the idea that certain acts are constituted by rules rather than caused by them, and that a bit of behavior counts as something else only relative to a system of such rules; the differences in direction of fit among acts; the taxonomy into assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, and declarations; the requirement that the hearer take up what is being done; and the speaker-meaning idea that a speaker intends to produce an effect in an audience by means of the audience's recognition of that intention. For each, give the translation, then classify it as exact, distorted, or impossible, and say precisely what was lost. I am especially interested in three places where I suspect my vocabulary fails. First, can a system that knows only preconditions, committed effects, and emitted events distinguish an act that misfires from an act that is performed but abused? My team's current view is that this is merely a difference in which error code you return; I think that is wrong, but I cannot articulate why. Second, do ack-required commands capture the requirement that the hearer take up the act, or do they conflate uptake with agreement — which we certainly do not conflate in the platform, where acknowledgment of a command and consent to it are separate fields? Third, does the rule-table pattern genuinely give us constitutive rules, or are those rows just declarative configuration that presupposes the very normativity they are supposed to generate? Do not paper over the gaps by importing philosophical assumptions into my vocabulary and calling the result a translation: if a distinction survives only because you have quietly reinterpreted one of my preconditions as a normative entitlement, say so explicitly. I would also like your verdict on the residue itself. Where my vocabulary cannot express something, is that a defect of the vocabulary, or is it evidence that the original distinction was doing less work than its admirers claim?

Format the answer as prose in American English, organized so that I can read it as a checklist: one short section per concept, with the translation and the residue clearly separated, and a closing section of no more than a page that answers the three questions above and states your overall verdict on the fidelity of the translation. No citations, no reading lists, and no dates — I will not be able to consult anything, and I would rather have your reasoning than a bibliography. Where you think I have misstated the philosophers' position, correct me plainly rather than accommodating my error.
