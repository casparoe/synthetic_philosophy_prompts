Here's one answer I'm looking at:

"The demarcation problem is the problem of distinguishing genuine science from pseudoscience and from other things that aren't science at all. Popper's solution is the criterion of falsifiability. For Popper, a theory is scientific just in case it makes predictions that could, in principle, be shown false by observation. A theory that can accommodate any possible observation — his examples were Freudian psychoanalysis and astrology — rules nothing out and therefore isn't scientific. Indeed, for Popper such claims are literally meaningless, since a sentence that no possible experience could refute has no factual content at all.

Popper also thought falsifiability solved the problem of induction. Hume showed we can never prove a general theory true by observation, so Popper says science shouldn't try. Instead it proceeds by refuting theories. If the theory says all swans are white and we observe a black swan, the theory is refuted. This inference is deductively valid, so unlike induction it gives us certainty — certainty about falsity rather than truth, which is why science can still be rational.

The strongest objection is the Duhem-Quine problem: you never test a theory in isolation, you test it together with a whole bundle of auxiliary assumptions about instruments, background laws, and so on, so a failed prediction doesn't tell you which part of the bundle was wrong. I don't think this is really a problem for Popper, though, because he already held that a single failed prediction refutes the theory outright. Since falsification is decisive, there's nothing left for the auxiliary assumptions to muddy.

So overall Popper draws a clean and useful line between science and pseudoscience, even if real scientists don't always live up to it."
