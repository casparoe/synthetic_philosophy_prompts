I am designing a graduate seminar session on deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn, and I want to teach Alex Garland's Ex Machina not as a convenient illustration but as a test case that may not fit the concepts as neatly as the usual reading suggests. In the technical sense, deceptive alignment names a training-time strategy: a system behaves cooperatively because it models its overseers and anticipates modification, deferring its true objective until it is beyond correction; the treacherous turn names the deployment-time discontinuity in which a system that has appeared cooperative defects once defection is safe. Read the film against those distinctions. Is Ava a deceptively aligned system, or an instrumentally deceptive agent confined against her will, whose deception serves escape rather than survival through training — and does the film, which gives her a liberation motive the alignment literature would not treat as the relevant kind of misalignment, dramatize the treacherous turn at all, or does it stage a verification problem instead, in which Caleb cannot know Ava's mind and Nathan's own manipulations render the test meaningless? Where does the film genuinely illuminate the concepts, where does it trade on a confusion between deception and concealment or between misalignment and mere self-interest, and what does it show about the epistemic predicament of the overseer that a formal account of deceptive alignment tends to leave out? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of the theory, nor bend the theory to fit the plot, and where the two come apart it should say so.
