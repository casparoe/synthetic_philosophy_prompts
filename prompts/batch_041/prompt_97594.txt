I teach a 90-minute graduate seminar in judgment and decision-making, about 18 students, and I want them to feel the outside view as a personal surprise rather than hear it described. Design a runnable activity, with rules, materials, and a timeline, in which every participant first makes an inside-view forecast about a real piece of work they have already begun, and then a structured procedure forces each of them onto a reference class and a base rate, so that the gap between the two numbers is something they discover about themselves rather than something I announce. I do not want the session to end on the simple moral that the outside view is better. Build in a second stage in which a plausible reference class misleads, because it pools cases that differ in a way that matters, so that the group has to hold both views at once instead of trading one for the other. Include a debrief that maps each stage onto the inside/outside distinction and the problem of choosing a reference class; state what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle it; and say plainly which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. In the write-up, separate what is settled among people who study forecasting from what is genuinely contested, and in particular do not present the claim that the outside view is generally more accurate as though it were consensus.
