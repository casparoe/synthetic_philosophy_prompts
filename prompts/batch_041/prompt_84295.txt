I teach a 90-minute session on radical interpretation to a seminar of about fourteen third- and fourth-year students in philosophy of language and mind. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel, rather than be told, why an interpreter cannot fix the meanings of a speaker's words without simultaneously fixing the speaker's beliefs, and why the notion of a community whose thought is wholly alien to ours cannot be a coherent target of interpretation. I want them to build a small translation manual for an invented language from a corpus of utterances and observed circumstances, and to run into the circularity for themselves when they try to write down separately what the words mean and what the speakers believe. Include a contrast between interpreting a frozen transcript and interpreting a speaker they can actually question, so that the debrief can bring out that interpretation is an interactive achievement rather than a puzzle solved from a text alone. Give me the materials (the corpus and the invented language), the rules and roles, a timeline, and a scoring rule that rewards manuals which fit the data while making the speakers largely rational and largely right about the observable world, so that the group feels the tension between those two demands. Add a debrief mapping what happened onto the interdependence of belief and meaning, the principle of charity, and the trouble with the idea of a wholly alien conceptual scheme; say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself. Write in British English.
