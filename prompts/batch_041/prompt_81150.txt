I've just retired after 35 years in marine microbiology and I've gotten weirdly into philosophy, so I'm running a one-off 90-minute session for our local philosophy of biology meetup -- about 14 people, mixed backgrounds, no one there has done formal philosophy. I want an activity that makes them feel the chauvinism/liberalism bind about life: define it too tightly and you exclude weird stuff you'd want to count; too loosely and you include things you don't. Set it up as a planetary protection committee deciding whether a sample from a subglacial lake is alive, where the call determines whether the mission gets sterilized, published, or quarantined. Give them a few borderline cases (virus, viroid, prion, plasmid, mitochondrion, mule, worker ant, spore) plus one made-up case I'll hand them on a fact sheet -- an autocatalytic set of peptides in a mineral pore that copies its composition but has no membrane, no genome, and no metabolism outside the pore. The rules and roles should force them to argue about the definition rather than just list criteria, and I definately want them to run into the fact that every definition either excludes something they already count as life or includes something they don't, and that the choice is tied to what they're trying to protect or do. They shouldn't hear the phrases cluster concept or natural kind until the debrief. Give me rules, roles, a timeline, materials I can actually make, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the definitional vs cluster vs natural-kind issue. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how I should handle it, and which part of the problem the game can't reproduce so I know what I have to say myself. Keep it runnable without me lecturing up front.
