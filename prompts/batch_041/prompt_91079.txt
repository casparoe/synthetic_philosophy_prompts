I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the old days (LessWrong, then SSC, then ACX, you know the drill), and I've got a story I keep half-starting and then abandoning, so I'm just going to hand it over to you. The thing I want to poke at is causation by omission — the nagging feeling that "he watched her drown" and "he pushed her under" are doing different amounts of causal work even when the outcome is identical and the counterfactual is identical. But please don't give me the standard trolley or lifeboat fare, I've read all of it and it's definately been done to death. I want something properly in the weeds.

Here's the seed I want you to build around. I've been calling it omission laundering (steal the term, improve it if you've got something better). It's the practice of arranging your affairs so that the harm you want done gets done by other people's failures to act, rather than by anything you positively do — so on the books you're never the cause of anything, you just... didn't. Set the story somewhere that makes this concrete. I'm imagining a near-future coastal city where every adult is enrolled in a rota, four hours a week, as the remote operator for a fleet of autonomous rescue drones. The drones don't launch on their own; a human on shift has to confirm each launch, and the law treats a non-confirmation as manslaughter if the person drowns. It works fine for a while, and then someone notices you can aim the whole apparatus: route a person into the water at a time and place where the on-shift operator is someone you've deliberately arranged to be distracted, or overloaded, or morally compromised, and the death becomes their omission rather than your act. And then it gets worse, because a single rescue can require a chain of confirmations, so the but-for chain fragments and no single operator looks like the cause at all. Give me a protagonist who used to sit the shifts and now works as a router — someone who designs the timing, never touches the water, never touches the button.

What I actually want out of the story is the philosophical payoff, and I'd like you to be deliberate about it: I want to feel the pull of "omissions aren't causes" and then have it break. Make me want to say, at the start, that the launderer is innocent because he only failed to act — and then show me the case where that intuition curdles, where the omission is so deliberately engineered, so specifically fitted to the outcome, that treating it as mere non-action feels like a con. Ideally the story should leave me unable to say cleanly what the difference between causing and allowing really is, without just reasserting it. I don't need a twist ending or a tidy moral; I'd rather it be honest and unresolved, with the ambiguity left sitting there. Maybe 1500 to 2500 words. British English please (I'm in the UK and it bugs me when a story set in a British-ish city has people 'filling out' forms).
