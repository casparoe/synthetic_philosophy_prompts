I have no academic training in philosophy, and what follows is my own attempt at a position rather than a report of anyone else's, so please take it as it stands.

For years I have been bothered by the old puzzle about ravens. If the claim that every raven is black is equivalent to the claim that every non-black thing is not a raven, then a white shoe appears to be a sliver of evidence that every raven is black. I have come to think that the puzzle is not really about how much a white shoe tells us, but about what a piece of evidence is evidence for. My position is this: a hypothesis is not a sentence floating free of a purpose but a tool for settling a particular question, and an observation is evidence for it only if it could change how one ought to act on that question. A black raven can change what I do about ravens; a white shoe cannot change anything I would do about ravens; therefore it is not evidence for the claim, whatever logic says about a statement and its contrapositive being one and the same. I would add that although truth is preserved when you turn a statement around, the subject matter is not, and that evidence attaches to subject matter rather than to truth-value.

I do not want a summary of the puzzle or of the familiar replies to it. I want a map of what counts against my position: which arguments, cases or observations should move me, which are merely awkward, and which would be fatal, ranked by how decisive they are, together with an honest note on which of them I would have to concede on my own terms rather than merely being told by a critic. Three things interest me most. First, whether the position can be put in a way that is not merely a redescription of the problem: if the requirement that evidence be able to change what one does is the whole of it, does the view ever predict anything I could check, or does it simply draw the line wherever I want it drawn? Second, whether it survives cases in which an observation is useless in practice yet seems plainly to bear on the truth of a general claim — a test nobody will ever act on, or a generalisation about something nobody can do anything about. Third, what becomes of my talk of subject matter when the same fact admits two equally natural descriptions, one of which makes the white shoe relevant and the other not: is that a defect in the view, or a fact about evidence that every view must live with?

I should say what would move me most. I would be most persuaded by a case in which an observation plainly irrelevant to ravens ought nevertheless to change what a reasonable person believes about ravens. I would be least moved by the reply that the white shoe does confirm the claim, only by a vanishingly small amount, since that seems to grant my point about what we should do and merely relabel it. Tell me if I am too quick there.
