I cover evolutionary biology for a general-interest science magazine, and I have reached the point where I cannot write another story about the fights over levels of selection without a standard to hold the claims to. Researchers send me papers and press officers send me releases announcing that selection has been shown to act on genes, or on groups, or for the good of the species, and I have no principled way to tell a claim that adds something from a redescription of something we already knew. So before I write, I want the specification. What does any theory of the units and levels of selection have to deliver to be adequate to that job — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the formal results it must live with, the questions it must answer — with the case or consideration behind each requirement?

Take the following as the minimum it has to cover, and add whatever else a working theory cannot duck: a gene that cheats the meiotic lottery and so spreads at the expense of the rest of the genome; an inherited microbe that spreads by turning its host's offspring into daughters, at the host's cost; a sterile worker ant that rears the queen's offspring and has none of its own; an alarm call that raises the caller's chance of being eaten and lowers everyone else's; a trait that is good for the group and bad for the individual, and its mirror image; and the fact that the entities we call individuals are themselves historical products, assembled out of things that were once free-living.

Spell the distinctions out rather than naming them: between the level at which selection is acting and the level at which a trait's benefit accrues; between an entity's being selected and its being the thing whose variation is merely being tracked; between a collective as a bearer of fitness and a collective as a unit that reproduces; between selection and drift or transmission bias. Then the formal constraints. The bookkeeping result usually called the Price equation splits a change in frequency into a within-group and a between-group part, and it is an algebraic identity, true of any partition of any population, so a theory that leans on it has to say what its results rule out and what they merely restate. The same goes for the condition usually called Hamilton's rule, which tells you when a costly behaviour can spread by benefiting relatives: say what it settles about the level at which selection is acting and what it leaves open.

Then the part I most want. Tell me which of these requirements are genuine and which are one camp's preference dressed up as a constraint. Flag any pair or subset that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which one you would surrender. Finish by identifying the single requirement that does the most to exclude the main contending positions.

Take a position throughout. I do not want a menu of options and a shrug; make the calls, give your reasons, and tell me where your position would be most vulnerable if someone pushed on it. No reading list and no citations — what I need is the reasoning.
