My two friends in philosophy club got into a big arguement about whether the Solomonoff prior is malign for consequentalists, and they want me to referee. I'm a junior and I honestly can't. Background, since I had to look it up: the Solomonoff prior assigns prior probabilities to hypotheses by favoring shorter computer programs that output the data you've seen. Friend 1 says that if a consequentialist really adopts that prior, they have to assign some probability to hypotheses where the world contains a simulator who made you and will reward or punish you based on your next choice. Since there are infinitely many such programs, and some are pretty short, the expected utility of any action gets dominated by whichever simulator story has the shortest code. So the universal prior forces you into Pascal's-mugging-style behavior. Friend 2 says that's not the prior's fault. The prior is just a distribution over programs, and simulator hypotheses are a tiny subset; in any real case the posterior is dominated by simple physical hypotheses, so their probability is negligible. Friend 1 says negligible isn't zero, and with infinite stakes any nonzero probability can dominate. Friend 2 says that just shows unbounded expected utility is broken, not that the prior is malign; you shouldn't let a tiny tail dictate your whole policy. Friend 1 says that's ad hoc: if you're a consequentialist, you can't just ignore tiny probabilities of huge payoffs. Friend 2 says the Solomonoff prior isn't even computable, so you can't actually run this calculation, and the whole setup is a theoretical idealization rather than a real decision procedure. Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point, say who's right about what, and what both are missing? Also say how confident you are in each verdict and which point you're least sure of.
