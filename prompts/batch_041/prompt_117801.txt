I write fiction, and for the past two years I have been working on a book about money — not markets exactly, but the way money behaves inside a life the way weather behaves inside a landscape. As part of the research I have been sitting in on public conversations about universal basic income, and I have become preoccupied with a figure of speech that recurs in them. A campaigner I admire, who has worked on basic income for more than a decade, opens almost every appearance with it: the economy, she says, is a game of Monopoly. Its rules are not natural laws; they were written by someone, and they can be rewritten by someone; a basic income is one such rewriting, a house rule, no more metaphysically loaded than deciding that passing Go pays two hundred. What she wants from the comparison is a deflationary conclusion — that a basic income is an arbitrary amendment to a made-up rulebook rather than an intervention in something organic, and that objections to it which appeal to the natural order of the market are therefore empty. I find the comparison seductive and I do not trust it.

Two things trouble me, and I would like your help deciding whether they matter.

The first is that Monopoly is not a neutral toy that happens to resemble an economy. It descends from The Landlord's Game, which Lizzie Magie created in 1903 and patented in 1904 as a teaching device for Henry George's argument about land monopoly. Magie wrote two sets of rules for it: an anti-monopolist set in which all the players are rewarded when wealth is created, and a monopolist set in which the object is to build monopolies and ruin the others — and it is the second, the polemical one, that reached us. The model my campaigner holds up as the plain picture of how markets work was itself manufactured as an argument about how markets fail, and its didactic intent has been sanded off in the hundred years since.

The second is that the game contains features I suspect do a great deal of hidden work: a Bank that is not a player, cannot lose, and which the printed rules permit simply to issue more money when it runs short; a board that is finite, fixed and fully known in advance; players who begin with equal stakes and equal information; dice that are fair; and a terminal condition — the game ends when one player holds everything, and the rules make that outcome the win. (There is a small irony here I keep turning over: the rule most people remember from childhood, the jackpot for landing on Free Parking, appears in no printed rulebook at all. The house rule has colonized the memory of the game. If the lived economy is the house-ruled version, I am no longer sure which document counts as the rulebook.)

What I want is an audit. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table will do: what plays the role of the players, the dice, the board, the properties, the rents, the Bank, the Chance cards, the rulebook, the endgame. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and sort the breaks into those that matter for the conclusion she draws and those that are merely decorative. Tell me which features of Monopoly the analogy quietly imports into the economy, and whether any of them are carrying her argument without her noticing. Say plainly whether the deflationary conclusion survives contact with the disanalogies — in particular, whether a Bank whose solvency is stipulated rather than earned can stand as a model of a monetary authority at all. And tell me whether the history I have just recited cuts for her or against her: a game designed to show that a particular set of rules reliably produces a single winner is not obviously the neutral picture she takes it for. Finally, if the comparison will not bear the weight, propose a better one — something that preserves what is true in her intuition, namely that the distributive rules of an economy are made rather than found.
