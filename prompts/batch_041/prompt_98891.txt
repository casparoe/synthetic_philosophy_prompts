I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the eulogy at the memorial for my oldest friend, a cognitive scientist who spent his whole career on the timing of conscious experience. He died suddenly a few weeks ago. The room is going to be a mess of different people: his sister and her family, who are devout and keep saying they take comfort in the idea that he had a peaceful moment at the end where he knew he was loved; his lab, who are mostly hard-nosed experimentalists and will wince at anything that sounds like a soul leaving the body; and a bunch of us old friends who just want to hear something true and not too clever. I want to give a five-minute eulogy that doesn't lie to either half. Here's my problem. He used to talk about the color phi phenomenon -- two dots flashed in different places, one color then another, and people see a single dot moving and changing color halfway, even though the second color can't have been on the screen yet. He said it showed that the brain edits experience, and he'd go on about the difference between what he called the Orwellian version (you have the experience, then memory rewrites it) and the Stalinesque version (the rewrite happens before the experience is even finished, so there was never a first draft). He thought the two might be indistinguishable from the inside, and that this messed up any clean story about what someone experienced at a particular moment. He used to say the brain is a novelist who can't stand a loose end, and that consciousness is the last draft he lets you read. I don't know if that's technically right, but it's the most honest thing I know about how to talk about his last minutes.

I want to use that in the eulogy -- not as a lecture, but because it's the only way I can say something comforting without claiming he had a final experience in real time that we can't actually know he had. I need the speech to explain just enough of the color phi thing and the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction for a room that doesn't know it, to land for the scientists as something they recognize as his idea rather than a Hallmark card, and to give his sister something she can hold onto without asking her to believe a timeline that probably isn't true. Can you write it? I'd rather it be warm and a little funny in places, since he was funny, and it should end with something people can actually carry out of the room. Also, don't make it sound like I'm delivering a paper. I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'll be reading it out loud, so keep the sentences sayable. And honestly I'm not sure I even understand the distinction right, so if I've got it backwards, just fix it in the speech without making a big deal about it.
