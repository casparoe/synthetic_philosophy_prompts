Two colleagues in my department fell into an argument about a variant of Parfit's hitchhiker and neither would concede, so I would like an adjudication of the exchange rather than a verdict on who "won."

The setup: you are stranded in the desert and will die unless you reach town. A driver offers you a lift on one condition — that you pay him a large sum on arrival. You know that once you are safely in town you will never see him again, will suffer nothing for stiffing him, and will feel no desire to pay. The twist is that the driver is not naive: he will take you only if he becomes convinced, before you set off, that you really will pay.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

A: The only rational thing to do at the moment of payment is to keep the money; an earlier promise is not a present reason when no one can hold you to it. What is rational is to make your promise credible while you are still in the desert — a signed note, a hostage, whatever — precisely because you know your later self will defect. The disposition to pay is just a commitment device, valuable instrumentally, for producing a credible signal.

B: That gets the case backwards. The point of the example is that you can rationally will, in the desert, that you will pay once you arrive — and that this willing is not merely a means of deceiving the driver. It is rational to be the kind of person whose word binds them, and the value of that is not exhausted by its signalling function. That your later self would feel no self-interested pull to pay shows only that your later self would be unreasonable, not that paying would be irrational.

A: "Willing that you will pay" is doing no work. You cannot intend to do something you know with certainty you will have no reason to do — at best you can wish it. And if a past act of will could by itself supply a present reason, then every past decision would be self-justifying, including the ones you now recognize as blunders; that proves far too much.

B: There is a difference between a commitment made with full information about your future self and a blunder you now see as one; only the latter is a candidate for revision. And if you deny that a past self's commitment can bind a later self at all, you lose more than the hitchhiker case — you lose the whole structure by which plans and promises give us reasons across time.

A: Then draw that line without circularity. You are helping yourself to the conclusion that the desert promise is the binding kind.

Please adjudicate point by point: for each of the main claims, say whether it is right, wrong, or right only with qualification, and identify what each party is missing. Address in particular whether the rationality of the plan formed in the desert and the rationality of the act performed in town can come apart, and whether "willing" is a genuine act or a disguised prediction. Say who is more nearly right overall.
