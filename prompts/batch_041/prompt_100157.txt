I am in the second year of a master's program in practical philosophy, and I have run into a problem of precisification that I would like help with. My partner's family is German, and over the winter holidays her father, a retired civil servant who spent his whole career inside a rigid administrative hierarchy, defended a decision he made decades ago by saying flatly: Befehl ist Befehl. I offered the American objection — that a person answers to his conscience before he answers to an office — and we circled each other for an hour without landing a blow. I came away with the uncomfortable suspicion that I had not disagreed with a position at all, but with a sentence, and that the sentence was doing four or five quite different jobs at once, some of them defensible and some of them indefensible. Since then I have noticed the same formula in English, an order is an order, coming from people with no German connection whatever: a nurse invoking a consultant's instruction, a junior analyst invoking a compliance policy, a friend invoking a court order. Each time it is produced as though it settles the matter, and each time I cannot say exactly what has been claimed. I would like to know what I am actually up against, partly because I am trying to work out what I myself believe about when a person must refuse.

Please act as a philosopher of action and a comparative moral psychologist with a strong background in the philosophy of authority. Before you answer, spend a few lines brainstorming the various things such a formula might be asserting, then lay out the results as a menu of distinct readings, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical, rather than choosing one and expounding it. For each reading I want the precise claim stated in its own terms, a verdict (analytic or trivial; substantive and defensible; substantive but false; or true but vacuous), the conditions under which it would hold, and an explicit note on whether that reading would actually license the conclusion my father-in-law drew — that he was right, or at least blameless, in doing what he was told. Among the readings I would like you to consider, at minimum: that a valid order obligates irrespective of its content; that an order functions not by outweighing the reasons against compliance but by excluding them from deliberation altogether; that the orderer is simply better placed epistemically than the ordered; that obedience is owed to the office rather than to the person; that order, coordination, and predictability are goods in their own right, so that the value of the rule is not exhausted by the value of what it produces; that responsibility for an act migrates upward with the authority to command, so that the subordinate's hands are clean; that refusing to set one's private judgment above the institution is itself a moral accomplishment rather than a moral failure; and that the alternative to the slogan — everyone judging for himself — is a state of affairs no one could want. Say where these readings conflict, which of them collapse into one another, and which is the one people most often mean without knowing it.

Then, briefly, do the same for the counter-slogan, that one answers to conscience rather than to office: does it have readings that are equally self-serving, and does it fare any better? You may assume the setting of mid-century administrative obedience as well as the ordinary contemporary cases I listed, and you need not supply citations or a reading list — I want the analysis, not a bibliography. Close by telling me which side, if either, has the better of it, and what a genuinely precise version of each slogan would look like if it were rewritten to say only what it can honestly support.
