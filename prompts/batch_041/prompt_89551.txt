Something about privacy law has been bugging me and I can't tell if its a contradiction or just me. Here's the pattern. Write a plan in a notebook and leave it in a drawer -- the cops need a warrant. Type the same plan into an email to myself and let it sit on a server, and the argument is that I handed it to a third party, so the company holds it now and the government can just ask them for it. Bank records, call logs, same move. But then the system seems to say the opposite elsewhere: what I tell my doctor or my lawyer is protected harder than almost anything else on earth, and the same courts that run the third-party argument have said the contents of my phone are private even though the carrier is right there the whole time. In Europe the sharing-equals-forfeiting move doesn't seem to do the same work at all.

So: what does that bundle presuppose about what privacy *is*? Not which cases came out right -- I want the implicit theory. Is privacy a kind of property in information, so it can be handed over like a title deed? Is it a right against being *seen*, so that once the seeing has happened there's nothing left to protect? Is it really about relationships, which would explain the doctor and the lawyer without any property talk at all? Is it about controlling *use* rather than knowledge, in which case the sharing argument looks like a category mistake? And what does the practice have to believe about the difference between a message's content and its metadata -- that meaning lives in the content and the envelope is just logistics, or something about how trivia adds up into a portrait of a life?

Then do the two things I always want from this kind of question. First, seperate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely happens to fit: could a control view, or a contextual view (right information, right flow, wrong context), license the same results, or does the doctrine actually commit to secrecy? Where is it genuinely underdetermined, such that several theories would sign off on it? Second, the flip side: if you replaced the presupposed view with one of the rivals -- privacy as a matter of who can *use* what, or privacy as something a group has rather than an individual -- which of the moves above become indefensible, and which survive untouched?

And one wrinkle I can't fit anywhere. A court file sitting in a courthouse basement is technically public; put the same file in a searchable database and people act like something has been violated. Same with my neighbor's doorbell camera filming the sidewalk, which is public, versus him standing at the window writing down when I come and go, which we'd call stalking. Does the practice presuppose that privacy attaches to places and to effort rather than to information? Because if so, that seems to sit badly with everything in the first paragraph, and I'd like to know which one it actually believes.
