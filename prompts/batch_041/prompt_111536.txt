I am an undergraduate midway through a course on the ethics and economics of markets, and we have just spent a week on Aquinas's treatment of buying and selling (Summa Theologiae II-II, q.77, a.1) as background to the modern debate about price controls. I can follow the broad shape of the argument, but two stretches of the text leave me stuck, and I would like help working out how they fit together with the rest. The first comes from the body of the article, where he turns to cases in which one party is in pressing need:

"Secondly we may speak of buying and selling, considered as accidentally tending to the advantage of one party, and to the disadvantage of the other: for instance, when a man has great need of a certain thing, while an other man will suffer if he be without it. On such a case the just price will depend not only on the thing sold, but on the loss which the sale brings on the seller. And thus it will be lawful to sell a thing for more than it is worth in itself, though the price paid be not more than it is worth to the owner. Yet if the one man derive a great advantage by becoming possessed of the other man's property, and the seller be not at a loss through being without that thing, the latter ought not to raise the price, because the advantage accruing to the buyer, is not due to the seller, but to a circumstance affecting the buyer. Now no man should sell what is not his, though he may charge for the loss he suffers."

The second is a remark in the reply to the first objection:

"I add this condition, because the just price of things is not fixed with mathematical precision, but depends on a kind of estimate, so that a slight addition or subtraction would not seem to destroy the equality of justice."

My difficulty is that the first half of the article appears to fix the just price by the worth of the thing -- any excess breaks what he calls "the equality of justice" -- whereas the passage above seems to let the price move with the seller's loss, and the second passage seems to rest the standard on a social estimate rather than on anything determinate. My seminar leader reads the need passage as a concession to circumstance, my study partner reads the estimate remark as making the just price conventional, and I cannot tell whether these two readings genuinely conflict or whether I am simply misreading what "worth" is doing in the argument. What I ultimately want to know is whether the article commits Aquinas to a standard determinate enough for a legislator to enforce -- say, by capping rents or setting a wage floor -- or whether it addresses only the individual conscience.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, and please write in British English.
