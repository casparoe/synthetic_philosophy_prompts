I chair the interpretation committee at a regional museum, and on Thursday we have to sign off on the caption for a newly re-installed burial assemblage. The curator wants the caption to identify the grave goods as evidence that the community believed in an afterlife; a colleague objects that the same objects are equally consistent with a burial that merely displayed the deceased's rank, so the caption asserts more than the objects can show. Our standing policy requires captions to state only what the evidence supports, but it never says how to decide what the evidence supports. One member has proposed adopting a rule she calls minimal cognitive attribution: a caption may ascribe a belief to past people only if every materially equivalent explanation that omits the belief is worse. Please draft the short memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend specific caption language, say whether to adopt the minimal-attribution rule as standing policy or what should replace it, and give the curator's position its strongest hearing rather than treating it as a failure of nerve.
