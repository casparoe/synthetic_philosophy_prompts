I sit on the sustainability committee at a mid-sized university, and we've been rolling out a travel and catering policy that's starting to feel philosophically weird to me. The travel bit says staff should take the train when they can, and when they do fly they have to log the trip in a portal; the university then buys offsets for the logged emissions. The catering bit makes the meat option opt-in rather than the default at departmental lunches, and there's a little dashboard that shows each department's estimated footprint. The offsets are cheap and the dashboard uses national averages, which makes the causal story even murkier. We've had the same fight about a get-out-the-vote drive, where someone said one vote is even more negligible. A colleague on the committee keeps saying the whole thing is moral theater: one flight, one lunch, one vote, one person's choices are causally negligible against a global total, so treating them as morally weighty is just a way of making people feel responsible for a structural problem. I can see the point, but the policy doesn't feel pointless to me, and I want to understand what its actually committed to.

So: what philosophical commitments does a practice like this presuppose about individual responsibility for collective harms? Does it have to assume that individual acts have real causal efficacy, or can it get by with something like complicity, expressive value, virtue, or a duty to do one's part in a collective project? Some people say the right model is a threshold: once enough people do the thing, the harm tips, so each act is a tiny chance of being the pivotal one. Others say it's about complicity in a harmful structure. I want to know which of these the policy is actually presupposing, and which rival views it quietly rules out -- for example, does the offset purchase presuppose a consequentialist ledger while the mandatory logging presupposes a deontological rule and the meat default presupposes a virtue-ethical or habit-based picture, and if so is that a problem or just a division of labor? And where is the practice underdetermined: what would a straightforwardly consequentialist, or a straightforwardly Kantian, or a responsibility-skeptical theory say about it, and what would have to change in the policy if we replaced the implicit view with one of those? I'd also like to know whether the practice needs to treat the individual act as the right unit of moral assessment at all, or whether it could be redescribed as a way of building and signalling a collective commitment, in which case the inefficacy objection might be aimed at the wrong target.
