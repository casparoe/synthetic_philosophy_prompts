I've been re-reading Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — the book where he takes Clément Marot's short Renaissance French poem (the one he calls 'Ma mignonne', addressed to a sick young woman) and translates it dozens of times, each version obeying a diffrent set of formal rules. It's left me stuck on a question I'd rather you settle than survey. My own half-formed distinction: contraints in a poem come in two kinds. Load-bearing ones — an acrostic that spells a name, a line count that is itself the subject of the poem — have to survive translation, because strip them out and you've got a different poem. Ornamental ones — a rhyme scheme, a metre, the local habit of the language — are negotiable, and you may trade them away when the sense demands it. I suspect that distinction is too clean, but I can't tell whether it's actually false or just needs patching, so write me an essay that decides: either repair it and defend the repaired version, or junk it and put something better in its place.

Whatever you land on, test it against the case I can't get past. A source poem has a rhyme the poet plainly had to strain to reach — you can hear the wrench — and the speaker's groping for a word that won't come is what the poem is about. A translation that finds a smooth rhyme has destroyed the poem; a translation that forces its own awkward rhyme has maybe only relocated the awkwardness to a place the original never hurt. Is that preserving form, or is it a different formal event that merely resembles it? Reach a verdict, and don't let it be "it depends on the poem" — if it depends, say on what, and give me the rule. 800 to 1000 words, argued on the merits; no citations or reading list needed.
