And here's the student's answer:

Strawson's basic move is to say that moral responsibility is not a metaphysical fact about whether our wills are free; it's a feature of the interpersonal practices in which we demand things of each other and react with resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, and so on. When we take the participant attitude, we treat someone as a member of the moral community, someone who can be addressed and who can answer for what they do. When we take the objective attitude, we treat them as an object to be managed, predicted, or treated -- as we might treat a child, a patient, or someone who is not fully a member of the community. Determinism is irrelevant because even if our actions are caused, we cannot help taking the participant attitude in ordinary life; it's built into the human form of life. Strawson also distinguishes excuses from exemptions. An excuse applies when someone is still a participant but the particular action is not attributable to them in the normal way -- for example, they didn't know what they were doing, or they were pushed. An exemption applies when someone is not a participant at all, and so not a candidate for the reactive attitudes -- for example, a very young child or someone with a severe mental illness. In the tumour case, the tumour caused the man's violence, so it looks like an excuse: he is still a person, but the act is not really his. If the tumour is severe enough, though, it becomes an exemption, because he is no longer capable of participating in the moral community. In that case we should take the objective attitude and stop blaming him. Strawson's optimistic voice says we can continue to hold people responsible even if determinism is true, and I think he is right to say that the pessimistic worry is just a confusion. The only thing that matters is whether someone can participate in the practice; causation by a tumour is not enough to exempt, but it is enough to excuse.
