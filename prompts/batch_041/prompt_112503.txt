Im putting together the final for my upper-level metaphysics seminar (seniors plus a few MA students, 75 minutes, closed book) and I need one essay question on origin essentialism — Kripke's line that you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg. The problem is I've graded this before and half the class just recites teh intuition pump back at me plus a paragraph on rigid designation. I want something that forces them to actually choose a place to draw the line and defend it.

So here's what the question should be built around. Two cases, one of which I made up:

(a) The twins. A zygote splits and you get two people. The sperm and egg are shared, so the gamete pair can't be what individuates them — the splitting has to be doing that work. But the splitting is also part of how each twin came to be, so by the same reasoning that makes the sperm and egg essential, it looks like each twin essentially results from a twinning: neither could have been an only child. That strikes me as nuts, and I want the students to tell me which premise has to go, and why the fix isn't just arbitrary line-drawing.

(b) A case where the same genome turns up from a different gamete pair — let them fill in the biology themselves (a clone, an IVF mix-up, an egg with its nucleus swapped out, whatever they can defend). They should have to say whether the doctrine is really a thesis about particular cells, about genomes, or about the whole causal history, and what each reading costs. If the answer is "the cells, contents irrelevant," I want them to see that they've made identity turn on a fact with no intrinsic footprint; if the answer is "the genome," I want them to see they've stopped talking about gametes at all.

Make the question unanswerable by recitation. Model answer around 700 words that actually picks a side and defends the line-drawing rather than surveying. Marking scheme: five must-hit points worth 20 marks total, one line each on how students typically blow that point, plus a sentence at the end on what separates an A from a B.

Two last things. Tell me where the model answer is weakest — which step you'd most readily give up, and what argument would actually make you give it up. And end the whole thing with a final line that is just \answer{...} containing a one-sentence statement of the crux the question is testing, so I can paste it into my spreadsheet.
