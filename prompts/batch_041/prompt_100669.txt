So I've been lurking on LessWrong and the old OB archives for like a decade now, and there's this one that keeps coming up that I've never fully gotten past: Good's theorem, i.e. the result that info can't hurt you, that free information is always welcome, a Bayesian should never pay to avoid a signal, etc. The proof is clean enough (max of an average is ≤ the average of the maxes, you can always just ignore the extra data), and everyone cites it like it settles the matter.

But then in real life basically everyone avoids information constantly and it doesn't obviously seem irrational. My roommate refuses to look at his brokerage account when the market tanks. My sister won't get a screening test she's eligible for. I skip plot summaries of movies I'm about to watch. And whenever I bring this up the standard move is "oh thats just because the disutility of knowing is itself part of the payoff," which honestly feels like a dodge rather than an answer.

So my actual question: given that Good's theorem proves information is never harmful, is all this avoidance just akrasia / a failure of instrumental rationality that I should be trying to train out of myself? Or is there some assumption in the theorem I'm not seeing that makes these cases actually not counterexamples?
