I'm a science-and-ideas journalist — long features mostly, the occasional column — and I've spent the last few weeks reading my way into futarchy for a piece, which I'm now half regretting, because the more I read the less sure I am what the interesting version of the idea even is. As I understand it (I think Robin Hanson coined the term, though I keep seeing people claim adjacent versions), the pitch runs roughly: we vote about values, we bet about beliefs. The public picks a single measurable national metric — median income, life expectancy, some composite index — and then nobody votes on policy at all. Instead we run conditional prediction markets on the question "if we adopt policy X, where will the metric be in five years," the policy with the best market forecast gets implemented, and if the number doesn't come in the way the market said, it gets rolled back. The appeal is obvious enough. What I can't get a grip on is what happens to the idea when you put it in front of somebody whose case for markets was epistemolgical rather than ideological.

So: Hayek. I've read enough of him to be dangerous, and I want to know what he'd actually have made of this, because my editor keeps pushing me toward the lazy version — Hayek loved markets, futarchy is markets, therefore — and that feels wrong to me in a way I can't yet articulate. The bits I keep chewing on: his point that prices carry dispersed knowlege that nobody holds individually is about coordinating what people are already doing, not about producing forecasts of what would happen if we did something we aren't currently doing — so does betting on a conditional have the same knowledge-generating power as having real skin in the game of producing and consuming? Is a conditional decision market a discovery procedure in his sense, or is it exactly the kind of designed, single-metric, means-ends apparatus he spent his life warning against? And the values half bothers me too: if the electorate has to fix one metric in advance, hasn't it smuggled in a social welfare function that he'd have said doesn't exist — and if it hasn't, what does the vote on values actually do that a market couldn't? There's also the practical stuff I'd want him to weigh in on, like whether a thin market that pays out on a metric the bettors can themselves influence is still telling you anything.

Give me his likely take in whatever structure actually fits: what he'd endorse and on what grounds, what he'd call a category error and on what grounds, and where his own arguments genuinely pull in two directions — I'd rather have two readings plus the thing that would settle it than one confident verdict. Flag clearly what's reconstruction from arguments he actually made versus you extrapolating, and don't sand off the places where he'd have been ambivalent or just wrong. And if the honest answer is that this is a parlour game — that the conditional market is a new enough object that his framework can't get a grip on it, or that I'm quietly conflating two things he kept apart — say that straight out instead of humouring me. I can take a "your premise is confused, here's why" much better than a tidy five-bullet answer I can't use in print.
