I have been reading the rationalist blogs and the Sequences for close to a decade, and somewhere along the way I drifted into a broadly Carnapian position without quite noticing when. A reading group I sit in is going through Carnap's politics and his ideas about the scientific planning of society, and I have been asked to write up my own profile so the others can poke at it. Before I hand it over I would like an audit, because I suspect the set does not hang together and I cannot see where the fault line runs. Here is what I actually believe, as plainly as I can put it.

(a) Tolerance about frameworks. On questions such as whether to quantify over abstract objects, whether the right decision theory is evidential or causal, or whether to describe an economy in market terms or in planning terms, I do not think there is a fact of the matter about which framework is correct. There are frameworks that serve particular purposes better or worse, and the choice among them is a practical decision rather than a theoretical one, to be settled by considerations like fruitfulness, simplicity, and usefulness relative to the purposes we happen to have. I take this directly from Carnap's principle of tolerance.

(b) Non-cognitivism about value. When someone says that a policy is unjust or that a form of society is good, I do not think they have asserted something true or false. Evaluative and political utterances express attitudes or issue prescriptions; they do not describe a normative region of the world, because there is no such region. I take Carnap to have held this too, and I hold it without reservation.

(c) Socialism. I am a socialist, and not a tepid one. I think democratic economic planning is the right way to organize production, that the case for it is strong, and that its opponents are not merely expressing a different attitude but are making a mistake. I think there is an argument here that a reasonable person, looking at the evidence, ought to find compelling.

(d) Planning and social science. I think most policy disputes are at bottom empirical disputes. Once the goal is fixed, the question of how to reach it is a matter for evidence, modeling, and cost-benefit analysis, and better social science would resolve a great many disagreements that presently look ideological. This is the part of the Vienna Circle inheritance I keep: the scientific conception of the world, turned on society itself.

(e) Conceptual engineering. I think philosophy is engineering rather than discovery. The philosopher is an architect, not a scientist; the work is to design and propose frameworks, not to find out how things independently are.

(f) Anti-metaphysics. I think most traditional metaphysics is confused or verbal. There is no substantive question, over and above what our best frameworks permit us to say, about what really exists.

One concrete verdict, to make the stakes plain: I think the mid-century defenders of market allocation were simply wrong about a question of fact, not adopters of a different but equally legitimate framework.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be; how severe each genuine tension is, ranked rather than listed as though all were equal; for each, the cheapest way to restore consistency and what that revision would cost me; and finally, which single revision would clean up the most at the least cost. I would prefer to keep all six, so do not tell me they are all fine, and do not flag everything as a tension either. I am told the Sequences move is usually to dissolve the question, but here I want to know which of my commitments is failing to pay rent, and in what coin.
