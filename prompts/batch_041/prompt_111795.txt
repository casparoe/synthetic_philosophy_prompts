For a small reading group I run outside work, I would like to rehearse a disagreement that keeps coming up, and I would like you to argue one side of it. I am a software engineer by training with no formal background in philosophy, so please keep the exchange in plain language. Please play a philosopher who is convinced that there is a genuine and philosophically important problem with the idea that an attitude such as a belief, an admiration, or a feeling of gratitude can be justified by a consideration that has nothing to do with whether the attitude is accurate or fitting -- the standard examples being a cash offer that will be paid to you on Friday if you sincerely believe your code is free of bugs, or a threat that will make your life unpleasant unless you admire a colleague you consider mediocre. You think the usual attempts to explain such cases away are too quick, and you have real arguments for that, not just an appeal to intuition. Hold the position for the whole exchange: you may concede a point when I land a good one, you may answer a question with a counterexample rather than an explanation, and you may ask me to be more precise, but you should not surrender the main claim, and you should not break character until I say stop. Please reply in one or two paragraphs of ordinary prose, without jargon, and without naming or citing any authors, books, or papers -- argue from the cases themselves, since neither of us has anything to look up. If you think my engineering vocabulary is doing illegitimate work, say so directly rather than translating it into your own terms.

Here is my opening move. It seems to me that every case of this kind is a specification error of the sort I deal with at work: the incentive has been attached to the wrong object. If I pay you for holding a belief, what I have actually given you is a reason for an action -- to take a pill, hire a hypnotist, run some self-deception routine, or simply to spend the weekend gathering evidence in a way that will bias you -- and the fact that the belief is the eventual output of that action does not make my payment evidence for the belief. On my view there are no reasons of a special wrong kind at all; there are only reasons for actions, plus a habit among philosophers of writing the specification at the wrong level of description. I can even grant you a middle case and still hold my line: if you endorse the causal route the offer opens up for you -- you decide that being the sort of person who can be bought into a belief is a route you are willing to walk -- then the offer is a reason for you to walk that route, and the belief is a by-product. So here is my challenge: give me a case where the consideration bears on the attitude itself and not on any action or process that produces it, and where saying so does not merely relocate the specification error one step further back. If you cannot, I will conclude that the whole puzzle is an artifact of how the problem is written down.
