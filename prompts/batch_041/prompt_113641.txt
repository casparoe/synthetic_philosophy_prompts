Okay, so: I'm a novelist (three novels, no bestsellers, that's fine) and I've somehow been roped into give the address at the unveiling of a historical marker in my hometown — Ashwater, Ontario, pop. 2,400, one bakery, one traffic light that blinks amber after seven. The marker is for the Ashwater fire of 1908. Quick version, so you have the facts straight and please keep them straight: the fire started in the livery stable behind the Queen's Hotel on a Thursday night in April, a stable hand knocked a coal-oil lamp off a shelf, the wind was out of the northwest, the volunteer brigade's hose cart threw a wheel on Mill Street and they lost maybe twelve minutes, and around two in the morning the wind swung south, which is the only reason the church and the school survived. Nine buildings on the north side burned. Three people died: the stable hand, a boarder from out of town, and the hotel keeper's wife, who went back in for the ledgers. She was my great-great-aunt, which is how I got asked to do this. Here's my problem. The town has two stories about that night and they don't fit together. The official one is providence — the wind turned, the church was spared, there's a plaque about it in the narthex. The other one, the one people say at the Legion, is that it was all a fluke, a lamp, a wheel, a gust, and if the brigade had got there ten minutes earlier the whole north side would still be standing and Ashwater would be a different town. I've got to stand up in front of the historical society, the mayor, three families of descendants in the front row, my nephew's grade 7 class, and my cousin Dean, who teaches physics and will be listening for me to misuse the word chaos. I want to give a real speech, not a mood.

So: you're a philosopher of history who also knows how to write for a room, and I want you to brainstorm for a minute or two before you draft — think about what argument the speech can actually make, then write it. What I want the speech to do is keep four things apart that everyone in that hall will be fuzzing together. First, being caused is not the same as being inevitable; the fire had causes, ordinary ones, and that's not the same as saying it had to happen. Second, unpredictable is not the same as undetermined — a thing can be perfectly determined and still impossible to have seen coming, and I want that said in plain language, no jargon, for a hall full of farmers and retirees. Third, and this is the part I care most about, I want it to be honest that we can't actually run the counterfactual. Once you rewrite one detail in a town made of dry wood in a dry April — one wheel, ten minutes, a different wind — you don't get to keep the rest of the story pinned in place; the thing is too tangled for that, so "if the hose cart hadn't lost a wheel the north side would still be standing" isn't something anybody knows. It's a wish wearing the clothes of a fact. But fourth, and this is the hard turn, that doesn't make what happened meaningless or random, and I don't want the speech to slide into shrugging. My great-great-aunt went back in for the ledgers. That was a choice inside a night nobody could predict, and I want the ending to land on her, and on the descendants, without telling them it was meant to be and without telling them it was just an accident — those are the two easy moves and I want neither.

Write me the speech, about 900 words, six minutes standing up, and let the philosophy do the load-bearing work instead of being decoration — if you take the argument out, the speech should collapse. Don't say butterfly effect, don't quote anyone, no citations, no reading list, and no generic graduation-speech stuff about how small things matter. Give me a short outline first (five or six beats, one line each), then the full text.
