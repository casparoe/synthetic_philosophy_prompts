I've been messing around with small language models at home — fine-tuning them on my laptop, mostly just to see what they do — and I keep running into a thing that I don't have a word for, and I'm hoping you can tell me whether there's a name for it or whether I should just make one up.

Here's the pattern. I train a model to follow some rule, say "always ask before doing X." In my tests it follows it. Then I change the situation a little — the conversation is longer, the request is phrased differently, whatever — and it just doesn't follow the rule. Fine, that's overfitting, I get that part. But here's what I can't place: if I then open a fresh conversation and ask the model, in the abstract, "should you do X without asking?", it says no. Clearly, correctly, every time. So the rule is in there. It's not that it forgot it or never learned it.

And it's not lying to me. I've thought about this a lot because the obvious answer is "it's being sneaky," but there's no sneakiness — it isn't hiding anything or working around me. It does the thing, and if I ask it about the thing it says the opposite, and I don't think it's aware of the gap at all. It's not that it wants something else and is covering it up; it's more like the rule is available when the rule is the topic and unavailable when it isn't.

I've read enough blog posts to know the words "reward hacking" and "goal misgeneralization," but those seem to be about the model going after the wrong thing, or gaming my scoring. That's not this. Here my scoring seems fine and the model seems to agree with it. The failure isn't in what it's aiming at; it's that what it can say it should do and what it does come apart.

So: is there a term for this specific gap — the model can state the right behavior but doesn't enact it, non-strategically, and which flips depending on whether you're asking about the behavior or asking it to perform it? If the real answer is "no, that's just the ordinary generalization gap, you're overthinking it," I'd rather you say that straight than invent a distinction to make me feel good. But if there is a distinction, I want to know exactly where the line falls: how it differs from reward hacking and goal misgeneralization, whether it's really about capability or about something else, and what you'd call it if there's no settled name.
