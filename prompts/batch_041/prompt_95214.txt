You are a philosopher of mind and of value with specialist expertise in the philosophy of pleasure, and you are serving as the neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration. Your task is to draft the joint statement that two camps in the debate over the nature of pleasure could both put their names to.

The two camps agree on the substance of hedonism about well-being: well-being consists in pleasure and the absence of pain. They disagree about what pleasure is, and about what unifies the things we call pleasures.

- The hedonic-tone camp holds that all pleasures share a distinctive phenomenal quality — a felt pleasantness, or hedonic tone — present in every pleasure and absent from every non-pleasure, and that this quality is what makes an experience a pleasure.
- The attitudinal camp holds that pleasures are phenomenally heterogeneous and share no common feeling — the pleasure of a hot bath, of a proof finally understood, and of a friend's good news have nothing introspectively in common — and that what unifies them is a propositional attitude: taking pleasure in something, or being pleased that something is the case.

Your job is not to adjudicate. Both camps must be able to sign the document as an accurate and fair statement of where they stand. Before you draft, work the problem through in a short brainstorming section (not part of the final document): for each camp, what it must say about the cases below; where each formulation threatens to become merely verbal; and which apparent disagreements survive that test. Then produce the joint statement, with these parts:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both camps accept: about the phenomena any account of pleasure must accommodate; about how ordinary speakers classify experiences and how much weight that classification carries; about the constraints an adequate account must meet; about the relation between the classificatory role of "pleasure" and its evaluative role in hedonism; and about what each camp would count as a decisive objection to itself. No platitudes: if a claim is one both camps accept only because it is trivially true, either sharpen it or mark it explicitly as trivial.

2. The agreed pressure cases. State at least these four, and for each say what each camp must say and whether the case is accommodated or explained away:
 (a) the mixed case, in which a single experience seems at once pleasant and unpleasant (a plunge into cold water, a song that is both sad and enjoyable);
 (b) the valence-reversal case, in which the same sensory quality is pleasant in one context and unpleasant in another;
 (c) the dissociated-pain case, reported in clinical neurology, in which patients register a stimulus as painful but are not bothered by it, so that the sensation survives while its unpleasantness does not;
 (d) the disowned pleasure, in which a subject enjoys something she repudiates and would rather not enjoy.
Where a case can be described in terms neutral between the two accounts, give that description.

3. Disagreements table. One row per residual disagreement. Columns: the question; the hedonic-tone camp's formulation, in wording that camp would accept; the attitudinal camp's formulation, in wording that camp would accept; whether the row records a genuine disagreement or a verbal one; the crux; the case or argument both camps agree bears on the crux; and each camp's prediction about how it comes out.

4. Cruxes. For each, say what turns on it and what would move each camp. Cover at least: whether the unifying feature of pleasures must be introspectively available to the subject; whether "pleasure" must name a natural kind or may be a cluster concept held together by overlapping similarities; whether an account of pleasure must explain why pleasures are good or only how to identify them; and whether the hedonic-tone camp's quality is posited to explain anything or only to relabel the data.

5. Genuine and verbal disagreement. State which parts of the dispute you judge to be verbal, and supply a short translation manual between the two vocabularies, so that each camp can see what its own sentences come to in the other's terms.

6. Movement. State what each camp would count as evidence against its own position, and whether anything in the collaboration would actually change a mind.

Constraints: do not cite literature, name authors, or refer to published works; the document must stand on the cases and arguments given here. Avoid formulations that presuppose either camp's vocabulary in the statement of the disagreement. Keep the register formal and the document tight — roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, with no padding.
