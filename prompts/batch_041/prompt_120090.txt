I need help drafting a decision memo and I'm crunched for time, so sorry in advance for the length.

I work in the general counsel's office at our state Environmental Facility Siting Board. The Board votes on a permit at its hearing on the 14th, and the chair wants a memo in everyone's hands before then.

The project: a chemical manufacturer wants to build a plant on a stretch of river bottom. It'd run for about 40 years, then get capped and abandoned. Our consultants' best guess — and honestly everyone's — is that somewhere in the 150-to-250-year window the cap fails and a contaminant leeches into the shallow aquifer under three counties. Not instantly lethal, but bad: shortened lives, kidney damage, some cancers. And permanent on any human timescale.

Here's the strange part. That plant would be the economic anchor of those three counties for the next fifty years. It'd pull in tens of thousands of workers, and it'd reshape who moves there, who stays, who meets who and ends up having kids with who. So the people who'd be around in 200 years drinking that water — almost none of them would exist at all if we turn the plant down. They're not people who'd otherwise have been healthy and would get sick; they're people whose parents would never have met. Deny the permit and those particular people never get born.

Now the statute. It says the Board may deny a permit if it finds the project "will cause substantial harm to the health of persons residing in the state." The chair read that out loud at our last meeting and said: fine, so if the plant doesn't make anybody worse off, there's no harm to any person and the statute just doesn't reach this. Approve. The vice chair said that reading can't be right, because it'd mean we could never deny a permit on long-horizon health grounds no matter how bad the contamination was, and the legislature obviously didn't mean to hand us a get-out-of-jail-free card for anything that takes two centuries to bite. They've both dug in.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. What I need: (1) a concrete recommendation — approve, deny, or approve with conditions, with a time frame for any conditions — (2) the reasoning that gets us there, in a form the chair can actually engage with, so please take his reading seriously and don't just gesture at "future generations" or "the precautionary principle," and (3) a short section on the statutory language itself, since we'll see another one of these applications next year and I'd like the Board to be able to tell the legislature something useful.

One more thing to fold in: not everyone down there is a newcomer. There's a set of old families who've been in those counties for generations and who'll still have descendants there in 200 years no matter what we decide. For those descendants the comparison is apples to apples — they'd exist either way, and without the plant they'd be healthy. So I don't think the chair's argument covers the whole picture, but I want you to say exactly how far it does cover it.

Keep it under 1,200 words, write it like a real staff memo — headed, addressed to the Board — and don't hedge.
