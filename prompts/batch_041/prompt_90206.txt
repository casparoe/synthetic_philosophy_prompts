I'm a fourth-year PhD student in statistics, and my dissertation is on the evaluation of probabilistic forecasts, so nearly everything I think with comes out of that literature: proper scoring rules like the Brier and logarithmic scores, the reliability-resolution-uncertainty decomposition of a score, skill scores computed against a reference forecast, and the general habit of treating a model as good to the extent that it would have scored well on data it hasn't seen. I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar this term, and I keep encountering the claim that Popper's central thesis about science is that inquiry aims at verisimilitude, or truthlikeness, rather than at probability, and that these two desiderata famously pull apart, since adding content to a theory lowers its probability while (allegedly) raising its truthlikeness. I have no philosophical training beyond that seminar. I'd like you to restate that position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work with. Take a theory to be a forecasting device, take the aim of inquiry to be minimizing expected score against however the world actually turns out, and see how much of Popper's picture survives: that a good theory is bold, that it must forbid a great deal in order to say a great deal, that being false does not by itself make one theory worse than another, and that scientific progress consists in getting closer to the truth rather than in accumulating probable claims. Tell me where "closer to the truth" lands in my vocabulary: is it expected score, is it the resolution term, or is it something the machinery simply cannot express?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. A scoring rule, as I understand it, is defined relative to a fixed outcome space and a fixed collection of events, so I can compare forecasts only when they are forecasts about the same things; and skill scores are relative to a benchmark, so the nearest thing I have to "closer to the truth" is "better than the reference forecast," which sounds comparative where the philosophers sound absolute. I'm also suspicious that my field's working maxim — that we want forecasts as sharp as they can be while remaining calibrated — is either the same idea as Popper's in different dress or a subtly different one, and I can't tell which. So: which parts of the verisimilitude story translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? In particular, what becomes of the comparison of two theories that are both false and that carve up the world along different lines, and of the suggestion that truthlikeness increases with content? Finally, I'd like your view on the residue itself: where my framework cannot follow the philosophers, is that a defect of the framework — something a better theory of forecast evaluation would supply — or a sign that the original notion of truthlikeness was less determinate than it sounded? My officemate thinks it is the latter and that the whole debate is scoring rules with extra metaphysics; I want to know what I would be conceding if I agreed with him.
