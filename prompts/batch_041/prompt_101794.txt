I edit the in-house magazine at a mid-sized management consultancy, and for our year-end issue I want to commission a piece of sustained deadpan satire on the way my own industry has swallowed the vocabulary of satisficing and bounded rationality. I have been reading Herbert Simon — the original work on aspiration levels, and his insistence that an agent who stops searching at a "good enough" threshold is not merely a lazy optimizer — and the contrast with what the term now means in a consulting deck is almost too neat: "satisficing" has come to mean "we couldn't be bothered to look further." I would like you to write the piece as a parody of an internal corporate decision policy: a numbered set of clauses, the sort of document a firm issues to govern how its analysts are supposed to reach conclusions, written in the flat, faintly self-congratulatory register that such documents always have. The conceit is that a company has genuinely attempted to implement satisficing as its house doctrine, and the comedy should arise because the doctrine, taken seriously, keeps producing problems the document itself cannot acknowledge. So the jokes have to come out of the real content, such that a reader who knows the literature laughs and a reader who does not still finds it funny: I want the policy to blunder into the fact that aspiration levels are endogenous, ratcheting upward after success and sagging after failure, so that a firm which "stops when satisfied" can drift from slovenly to impossible and back again without ever changing its rule; into the fact that a stopping rule is itself a choice, so the policy must either regress into a rule for choosing rules or quietly smuggle back in the very optimization it has just renounced; and into the descriptive/normative equivocation, that bounded rationality began life as a claim about how agents actually behave rather than a standard anyone should be proud to meet.

Please keep it to roughly 700 words, formatted as numbered clauses with a short preamble and a signature from an invented officer (a Chief Decision Officer, say — pick a name and a firm for me). The register must stay deadpan throughout: the document should never wink at the reader or admit that anything is absurd, and the absurdity should be visible only to someone who is paying attention to what the clauses commit the firm to. The trap I want to avoid is the generic "corporate language is empty" joke, which has been done to death; the target is narrower — this particular appropriation of this particular idea — and the satire should be fair, in the sense that a specialist in bounded rationality should recognize that whoever wrote it understands aspiration-level dynamics and the difference between a heuristic that works in an environment and a heuristic that merely feels thrifty. No citations or reading lists, please; I only need the document itself.
