I've been reading about John Stuart Mill and I keep hitting a wall. I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, so maybe I'm just pattern-matching wrong, but his colonial stuff looks like two different programs running in the same process and I can't tell which one actually makes the decisions.

Here's his track record as best I understand it. When the East India Company took over Oudh in the 1850s, Mill — who worked for the Company basically his whole adult life, writing the dispatches out to India — wrote a memo defending the takeover. His line was that the Nawab's government was so bad the people were better off under Company rule, treaty or no treaty. But the annexations done on the technicality that a ruler had died without a natural son, with the adopted heir set aside, he thought were theft dressed up as law: nobody had misgoverned anything, the Company just wanted the revenue. Then with Governor Eyre in Jamaica, Mill was one of the main people behind the committee trying to get him prosecuted for murder — the people killed were British subjects, and no amount of saying the colony was in revolt makes it lawful to hang and flog people with no process at all. Mill also fought the Contagious Diseases Acts, the ones that let police grab women near army towns and force them into exams and lock hospitals. Note the aim there was actually decent, fewer diseases in the army, but the whole burden landed on women and none of it on the men. And in general he thought British rule in India was a good thing for the time being, a trust, with Indians eventually fit to govern themselves. He was also, I'm fairly sure, disgusted by the reprisals after 1857, the mass hangings and the rest.

So which rule is he running? Two fit everything above, as far as I can see. Rule one, call it improvement: what decides is whether the ordinary people end up better off — better governed, safer, richer, more able to run their own show later. Consent and legal forms are secondary. Rule two, call it forfeiture: people's claims count the same as anyone's, and you can only override them if they broke the deal themselves — misrule, cruelty, breach of treaty — or if the burden is shared lawfully. Riding over people who did nothing wrong is wrong even if the average goes up.

Now the case I actually want a call on. The India Office is weighing a big irrigation canal in a famine district. It would protect a few million people from famine and make the province alot richer. To build it, the government takes the farmland of a handful of villages. The farmers are tenants with no paper title, so under the proposed rules they get next to nothing, and the local commissioner says they'll be ruined — and they've done nothing wrong, no rebellion, no breach of anything, they're just in the way. Does Mill back the canal?

Rule one says yes. Rule two says no, and says it's the lapse annexations again, just with dirt instead of a throne. Whats your best guess at what he'd actually say, which earlier case is the closest analog, and what further case would best seperate the two rules? If there's a third rule that fits his verdicts even better than either of mine, tell me what it is. And if the real answer is that he's running improvement as the goal with rights as a side-constraint, say so and tell me where the constraint actually bites. Don't worry about citing which book any of this is in, I don't care, I just want the function.
