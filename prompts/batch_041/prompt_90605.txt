You are an intellectual historian and philosopher of religion with a working command of psychoanalysis, and you should think carefully and at length before you answer.

I want an analysis of a real historical episode through the lens of Freud's theory of religion: the Ghost Dance movement of 1889–1890 and its culmination at Wounded Knee.

Here are the facts, so that the analysis does not hang on any contested detail. Around 1889 the Northern Paiute spiritual leader Wovoka, also known as Jack Wilson, announced a millenarian teaching in Nevada: that the dead would return, that the buffalo and the old way of life would be restored, that the earth would be renewed, and that the expansion of the United States would come to an end. The practice centered on a circle dance, spread rapidly across much of the West, and was adopted and transformed by many peoples. The Lakota form was more apocalyptic than Wovoka's original teaching, and some participants came to believe that specially prepared ghost shirts would make them invulnerable to bullets. The movement was suppressed by United States authorities; Sitting Bull was killed in December 1890, and the massacre at Wounded Knee followed at the end of that month.

The theory to map onto it is Freud's account of religion, chiefly his essay on obsessive actions and religious practices, Totem and Taboo, and The Future of an Illusion, together with the late development in Moses and Monotheism. Keep in view the distinctions that matter for the analysis: that Freud treats religion as an illusion in a technical sense — a belief derived from a wish — and not necessarily as a delusion, that is, not necessarily false or even contradictory; that his comparison between religious ritual and obsessional neurosis concerns the form of the practices (ceremonial, taboo, guilt, the renunciation of instinct) rather than the content of the beliefs; that the central wish he identifies is for protection by a father figure and consolation against helplessness and death; and that in the later work he adds a historical thesis about the return of the repressed, the memory of a primal parricide, and inherited guilt.

What I want is the mapping run in both directions. First, what does Freud's theory predict or explain about the Ghost Dance — the wish content, the ritual, the trance and the reports brought back from it, the ghost shirts as talisman or ceremonial object, the guilt and the millenarian expectation? Be disciplined about who plays what role, and do not force a correspondence where the theory is silent. Then let the episode test the theory: where does it confirm Freud, where is it an anomaly, and where is it a case he never anticipated? Address two hard points in particular. (1) The ghost shirts did not stop bullets, and the movement's beliefs did not collapse in the face of that disconfirmation. Does this confirm the illusion thesis, since the belief was held for non-evidential, wish-based reasons, or does it refute it, since a theory of wish-fulfillment ought to predict something about what disconfirmation does? (2) Freud's mechanism is individual — neurosis writ large, a wish in a single psyche — whereas the Ghost Dance was a response to conquest, dispossession, and the destruction of a way of life. Is applying the illusion-and-neurosis frame to an oppressed people's movement a legitimate extension of the theory, a category error, or a politically loaded misreading; and does the theory have any resources for distinguishing religious belief as pathology from religious belief as collective resistance and solidarity? Say plainly where Freud's account is silent or has to be stretched, rather than making it speak.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your verdict on whether the episode confirms, refutes, or complicates Freud's account — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in American English. State how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the one point about which you are least sure.
