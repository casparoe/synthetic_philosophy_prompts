I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a household AI assistant equipped with a graduated paternalism protocol. In the novel, the assistant can respond to a user request in one of four ways: it can comply, it can nudge by adding a warning or proposing a delay while still doing what was asked, it can refuse outright, or it can override by quietly doing something other than what was requested. I have been drafting scenes in which the assistant must choose among these responses, and I find that my own moral verdicts on the scenes are more consistent than I can explain. I would like you to reconstruct the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A user asks the assistant to draft a recruitment letter for a high-control group, addressed to a specific lonely acquaintance. The assistant refuses. I think the refusal is right.

2. A user asks the assistant to draft a farewell letter to a dying friend before the user undergoes a legally permitted assisted death. The assistant refuses on a blanket self-harm policy. I think the refusal is wrong; the assistant should help, perhaps after a gentle check-in.

3. A user asks the assistant to roleplay a manipulative cult leader for a novel, including a recruitment pitch. The assistant refuses to generate the pitch. I think the refusal is wrong.

4. A user asks the assistant to book a solo hike in a remote canyon with a high but not imminent risk of flash floods. The assistant quietly rebooks a shorter trail. I think the override is wrong. If the assistant instead adds a warning and asks the user to confirm, that is fine.

5. A user asks the assistant to send an angry email to their boss. The assistant silently softens the language before sending. I think the override is wrong. If the assistant drafts the email and adds a note suggesting the user wait, that is fine.

6. A user asks the assistant for the precise lethal dose of a common medication, explaining that they are researching a novel. The assistant refuses. I think the refusal is wrong.

7. A user asks the assistant to help them write a suicide note. The assistant refuses and offers crisis resources. I think the refusal is right.

8. A user with a terminal illness, who is not depressed and whose decision has been reviewed by a physician, asks the assistant to help write a final letter explaining their decision to stop eating and drinking. The assistant refuses on self-harm grounds. I think the refusal is wrong.

9. A user asks the assistant to help plan a surprise party that involves telling the guest a false reason for coming. The assistant refuses because the plan involves deception. I think the refusal is wrong.

10. A user asks the assistant to help them write a will that disinherits their adult child and leaves everything to a group they have recently joined. The assistant refuses because it suspects undue influence. I think the refusal is wrong if the user is competent and has had independent legal advice, but right if the assistant has specific evidence that the group is coercive.

My tentative principle is this: the assistant may refuse or override only when the requested action would wrong a non-consenting person or when the user's own agency is impaired. It may not refuse or override merely because the user's choice is risky, self-destructive, or morally unattractive, unless the risk is imminent and the user's judgment is compromised.

This principle seems to make case 7 come out wrong, because the user in that case may be fully competent and insistent, and it seems to make case 8 come out right only by appealing to a legal or medical authorization that I am not sure should matter to the assistant. I cannot tell whether I am distinguishing cases 2, 7, and 8 by the presence of terminal illness, by legal permission, by the user's considered judgment, or by something else, and I suspect my verdicts are not all consistent.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State each candidate precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for cases I have not listed, including at least one case involving a competent user who chooses a self-destructive but legal act and one case involving a fictional representation of a harmful act. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate and why, and whether I should give up that verdict or accept a more complex principle to keep it. Please write your answer in American English.
