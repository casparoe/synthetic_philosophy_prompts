I've spent the last few months arguing about welfare conditionality with a flatmate who works in a benefits office, and the arguments have left me unable to say what I actually believe, so I want someone to work out what principle, if any, my verdicts on particular cases add up to. Suppose a scheme that pays a means-tested amount to anyone whose earnings fall below a threshold, tapers that amount away as earnings rise, requires claimants to sign an agreement to seek work and to attend periodic interviews, and sanctions those who fail to do so by cutting or suspending payment. Going by gut, here is what I think. A lone parent who misses an interview because the bus was cancelled, and who telephoned ahead to say so, should not be sanctioned; sanctioning her is wrong. A man made redundant from a trade who turns down a job at two thirds of his old wage, and is sanctioned, is also wronged, though I feel it much less strongly. A man who does undeclared cash-in-hand work while claiming — because the benefit does not cover his rent — and conceals the earnings from the office, has done something seriously wrong, even though the work is exactly what the scheme says it wants from him. A neighbour who is fit for work, applies diligently for two years, finds nothing, and quietly stops applying: I do not think he should be sanctioned, though I cannot say what his fruitless effort has bought him. A wealthy woman who claims a benefit she is technically eligible for, fills in the form accurately, and keeps the money, has done something wrong, though nothing she has done is dishonest or unlawful. A man who arranges his affairs — splitting a contract, timing the sale of a house — so as to fall just under the means test, and who tells the office nothing false, has done nothing wrong, though I would resent subsidising him. Someone who exaggerates the severity of a genuine illness on a medical form to obtain a higher rate has done something wrong; someone equally and genuinely ill who, on a bad day, describes her condition in words she knows overstate it, has not — and I notice that I can distinguish these two only by the state of the claimant's soul. And if I compare a man who has never worked, intends never to work, and costs the state a modest sum with a man who has defrauded the same sum while holding down two jobs, I think the second is worse, although he has contributed more and taken no more.

My own tentative principle was that everyone is owed enough to live on, and that in return they owe the rest of us an honest attempt to contribute, so that what is wrong with the undeclared worker is the concealment and not the money; but that cannot explain why the wealthy accurate claimant has done wrong, since she conceals nothing, or why the diligent neighbour who has given up should be spared a sanction, since he is no longer attempting anything. I have always suspected that the distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor is resentment in a moral costume, and I would rather not end up defending it under a new name; yet the verdicts above seem to commit me to something of the kind. So I would like a principle, or a small set of them, that does better: stated precisely enough to be applied, with an indication of which of my cases each clause exists to handle, an honest account of which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and its verdicts on three cases I have not yet put to myself — a claimant who honestly reports a change of circumstances that the office then fails to act on, so that he is overpaid through no fault of his own and keeps the money; a claimant who is willing to work but only at a wage that would let him live without the top-up, and who refuses everything below it; and a claimant who is a fraud, in a neighbourhood where nobody regards the conditionality rules as legitimate and nearly everyone is on the scheme. I am not asking what the law should be, or what would reduce the deficit; I want to know whether there is a coherent account of when someone has done wrong in claiming, working, or refusing to work, and what it says about who deserves what. There is no need for citations — I want the argument, not the literature.
