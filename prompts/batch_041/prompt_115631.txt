I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I have reached the stage of a piece where what I need audited is my own understanding rather than my prose. The subject is Simpson's paradox — the family of cases in which a comparison that holds inside every subgroup points the other way once the subgroups are pooled. The case below is invented and its arithmetic is exact, so every claim you make can be checked against it.

A hospital network ran two care-management programs side by side over the same period. Program X took 200 patients whose illness was mild at intake, of whom 190 avoided readmission within thirty days, and 800 patients whose illness was severe at intake, of whom 400 avoided readmission. Program Y took 800 mild patients, of whom 720 avoided readmission, and 200 severe patients, of whom 90 avoided readmission. Severity was recorded at intake, before any program was assigned; assignment was left to the treating clinician's discretion; both programs were available at every site in the network; no patient was refused, and every patient the network saw during that period appears in these counts.

Pooled, Program Y kept 81 percent of its patients out of hospital, against Program X's 59 percent. Inside each severity group the ranking flips: among the mildly ill, X succeeded with 95 percent against Y's 90 percent; among the severely ill, 50 percent against 45 percent. I have eight accounts of what is going on. Some are explanations statisticians actually give; I wrote others myself, as traps, so treat none of them as respectable by default.

(a) Confounding by indication: clinicians steered the sicker patients toward one program and the milder ones toward the other, so the within-severity contrasts, and not the pooled ones, are what reflect how the two programs perform.

(b) The pooled numbers are the relevant ones: a patient arriving at the network does not know her severity in advance, so Y's 81 percent, not the stratum-by-stratum figures, is her chance of avoiding readmission.

(c) Nothing needs explaining: the two claims are about different quantities, so the impression of a contradiction is a category mistake rather than a puzzle awaiting a solution.

(d) The reversal is an artifact of the effect measure rather than of the case mix: some summaries, the odds ratio foremost among them, are not collapsible, so a pooled contrast can disagree with the within-stratum contrasts even where nothing is confounded.

(e) Severity is a post-treatment variable, so stratifying on it opens a causal path that ought to stay closed, and it is the within-severity numbers that are distorted.

(f) This is an ecological fallacy: the analysis has quietly shifted its unit from the patient to the program.

(g) The pattern is an artifact of selection: the counts describe patients who reached the network's hospitals rather than a defined population, so the reversal may be a Berkson-style product of who ends up in the data.

(h) The crux is weighting, not arithmetic: both comparisons are true, and what should be reported is the within-severity contrast standardized to a stated patient mix, since the pooled figures chiefly reflect how the network happened to distribute its patients between the two programs.

For each account, give a rating from 0 to 10 for how well it accounts for the pattern in these particular numbers — 0 for an account that is false or inapplicable to this case, 10 for one that accounts for it completely — then order all eight from most to least useful to me, and append one or two sentences on what each gets right and, where it fails, precisely where. I would sooner see a confident mislabel named as such than a hedge. Close with the one sentence I should put in the piece, the one sentence I should not, and the single further number I should ask the network for that would most change your ratings.
