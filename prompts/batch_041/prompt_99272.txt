A friend of mine who reads a great deal of online writing about the long-term future has been trying to persuade me to give away most of my savings, and his entire argument rests on an analogy he repeats whenever I push back. Protecting humanity's long-term future, he says, is like buying a lottery ticket. The jackpot is enormous — something on the order of a vast number of future lives lived well, or the whole of civilization's unrealized potential — and the odds that any one donation of mine actually averts a catastrophe are minuscule, perhaps one in a million or far less. But because the jackpot is so immense, the expected value of the ticket is astronomical, and a rational person ought to be willing to pay almost any finite price to hold it. He adds that this case is even better than an ordinary lottery, because the tickets are not mutually exclusive: I can buy many of them, each tied to a different risk, and each carrying its own small, independent chance at the same enormous prize.

I have no training in philosophy or economics — I have simply been turning this over on my own — and something about it feels wrong even though I cannot put my finger on what. So I would like you to audit the analogy rather than the conclusion it is meant to support. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly: in the lottery picture, what plays the role of the ticket, the price, the odds, the jackpot, the other players, and the house that runs the draw, and what each of these corresponds to in the actual situation of trying to safeguard the future. Then tell me where the comparison breaks down, and — this is the part I care most about — which of those breaks genuinely matter for the use my friend is making of it, as opposed to being incidental.

I am suspicious of several things in particular and would like your judgment on each. A real lottery has odds one can estimate and a jackpot that is fixed and bounded, whereas here both the odds and the size of the prize seem to be not merely unknown but arguably unbounded. In a real lottery the prize is money I could actually spend, whereas here the prize is a future that exists only if I win, so there is nothing to collect when the ticket loses. The person buying the ticket is not the person who would receive the prize. And ordinary lotteries are the standard example of a bad bet precisely because the price exceeds the expected return — so it is strange to invoke them to show that almost any price is worth paying. I would also like to know what the analogy quietly imports that may not be true of the target: for instance, whether casting the situation as a lottery smuggles in the thought that the outcome is a matter of luck rather than of skill or coordination, whether it assumes the many small chances are independent in the way that separate draws would be, and whether it treats the prize as a single jackpot when in fact the future is better pictured as a whole range of possible outcomes of very different values, more like a lottery with a continuum of prizes than one with a single top prize.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion he wants — that I should be willing to pay almost any finite price for even an astronomically small chance at the prize — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the analogy all along. And if the lottery is the wrong picture for this, say what a better one would be.
