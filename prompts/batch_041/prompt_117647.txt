Question 3. A subject who has never seen a coloured lamp looks at a white wall lit by a strong red light and, seeing nothing amiss, sees the wall as red. Her experience is subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception of a red wall. Call the assumption that any two experiences a subject cannot tell apart from the inside are experiences of the same fundamental kind the Uniformity Assumption. Set out the strongest argument you can, from this case together with the cases of illusion and hallucination, for the Uniformity Assumption; explain how the disjunctivist rejects that argument; and say whether the disjunctivist's reply leaves her able to explain why the perceiver is in a better epistemic position than the subject undergoing the illusion.

Student's answer:

The tinted-wall case is meant to show that the two experiences cannot be told apart from the inside: the subject has no reason to doubt that she is seeing a red wall, and nothing in her experience marks it out as illusory. Since her experience is exactly like the veridical perception of a red wall, and since in the veridical case the experience is of something red, the illusory experience must also be of something red; the only difference is that in the illusory case the wall is not in fact red. Generalizing, what we are immediately aware of in any perceptual experience is a private appearance, and the physical object, if there is one, is only inferred from it. That is the argument from illusion, and its conclusion is that we never perceive physical objects at all. The same point is made by hallucination, where the subject seems to see something that is not there at all.

The disjunctivist denies the Uniformity Assumption. On her view the veridical perception is a case of being directly aware of the wall and its redness, while the illusion is a different kind of state altogether, even though the subject cannot tell the two apart. She can allow that the two experiences have the same phenomenal character — the difference lies in what brings them about, the wall's colour in one case and the lamp in the other — and she rejects the premise that the illusory experience must have an object of the same sort as the veridical one, rather than the conclusion drawn from it.

On the epistemic question, the disjunctivist says that in the good case the subject's experience guarantees the truth of her belief, whereas the subject of the illusion has no such guarantee. Since introspective similarity does not settle the kind of experience one is having, the perceiver's advantage is real even though it is invisible from the inside.
