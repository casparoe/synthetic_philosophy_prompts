I spent forty years as a mineralogist, much of it on specimen provenance and meteorite classification, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy, mostly on authenticity and value. My professional instincts and my reading do not agree, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my own verdicts on a set of cases add up to. In my old trade the governing distinction was between what a thing is and what it witnesses, and I have never been able to say in a principled way when the second matters.

The meteorite first, because it seems easy. A stone from space and a terrestrial stone of the same composition, same mass, same appearance: the meteorite is worth vastly more by weight, and I think that premium is entirely rational, not a market fetish. Now the diamond. A stone grown in a laboratory that passes every test a gemmologist can apply, and a natural stone that by any measure is less perfect: the natural stone sells for several times the price, and here my instinct is that the buyers are fools, that the two stones are the same stone and the premium is sentiment. The two cases look alike to me and my verdicts on them do not, and I would like to know which verdict to give up.

Then my own material. A mineral specimen from a commonplace locality, with a forged label claiming a famous one, indistinguishable to the eye from a genuine specimen from that locality: worthless to me as a scientific object, even if the forgery is never detected, because the label is the whole point; the specimen is evidence of a place and a process, not a pretty object. But a specimen whose label was lost and whose locality was later recovered by trace-element analysis gains value, and rightly, though nothing about the specimen changed except what we know about it. A plaster cast of a type specimen is no use to me where the type specimen is wanted, and I would not keep one in a research collection, yet I have no objection to a cast in a teaching collection, and none at all to a mounted skeleton that is half resin, provided the label says so. A skull that is sixty per cent sculpted plaster and is catalogued as a skull is not merely less useful than a whole one; it is a fraud, even if the sculptor guessed correctly.

The art cases I offer with less confidence, having no stake in them. A painting reattributed from a master to a studio assistant, the canvas untouched: it seems to me a lesser painting afterwards, and I believe that is now a fact about the painting and not only about its price. A forger's copy of an etching, indistinguishable from the plate's own impressions under any examination: not the same artwork, though I cannot say what a viewer is missing. A cathedral rebuilt after a fire to the original drawings with stone from the original quarry: not the same building, but not a replica either, and I would not know which word to write in a catalogue.

I have a tentative principle and I suspect it is too crude. A thing is valuable partly as an object and partly as a record of the process that made it; what is wrong with a forgery is not that it is a worse object but that it is a false record, so that where we are using the object as evidence a false record is fatal, and where we are not, the fuss is sentiment. But by that principle the meteorite premium is sentiment too, which I will not accept, and it does not explain why the reattributed painting is a lesser painting rather than merely a lesser investment.

Give me a principle, or a small set of them, stated precisely enough to be tested, with the case that forces each clause. Tell me what they deliver on cases I have not put to you: a Stradivarius against a modern violin that no one can tell from it in a blind test; a laboratory-grown ivory substitute marketed as the ethical choice; a restored painting that is four-fifths repaint; a digital file bit-identical to the original scan of a photograph. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts cannot be kept and which I should abandon.

One case I keep going back and forth on, and it is why I am writing. A colleague of mine fabricated a set of measurements that later turned out to be correct, every number of them confirmed by independent work. I cannot decide whether that paper is a forgery, a lucky guess, or both, and I suspect my answer depends on which of the verdicts above I am willing to drop.

Please answer in continuous prose, as you would in a letter to a colleague. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no reading list; I have enough names I cannot check and would rather have your reasoning.
