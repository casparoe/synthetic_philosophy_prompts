International humanitarian law grants every combatant the same privilege to kill enemy combatants and the same entitlement to prisoner-of-war treatment, whatever the justice of the cause they serve, and it does not treat participation in an aggressive war as an individual crime; the crime of aggression has been confined to leaders, while members of non-state armed groups are regularly prosecuted for the mere act of taking part in hostilities. What theory of moral and legal responsibility does this pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose that ordinary soldiers are not culpable for the cause they serve -- because they cannot reasonably be expected to judge the justice of the war, or because their obedience is the state's responsibility rather than their own -- or does it presuppose something thinner, that immunity is a convention tracking state authorization rather than moral desert? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits, and say how the same body of law can be consistent in immunizing soldiers for the war they fight while convicting them for the manner in which they fight it. Then say what would have to change in the practice if one held that liability tracks the justice of the cause. Finally, tell me which consideration would most likely lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most exposed to error.
