The student wrote: "The puzzle, as Davidson sets it up, is that akrasia looks both impossible and obviously actual. If an agent genuinely judges, all things considered, that A is better than B, and nothing prevents her from doing A, how can she freely and intentionally do B? Davidson's solution is to deny that the akratic action is really intentional — the desire for B causes the behavior without rationalizing it, so the action is closer to a bodily movement than to an action proper. This is why akrasia is such a strain on theories that tie intentional action to the agent's best judgment. Hyperbolic discounting then gives an empirically grounded version of the same phenomenon. Under hyperbolic rather than exponential discounting, value falls steeply over short delays and only shallowly over long ones, so a smaller sooner reward can beat a larger later one, and then lose to it once both options are pushed back by the same interval. The effect was first demonstrated in animal choice experiments and later replicated with humans, and the upshot is dynamic inconsistency: the agent's ranking at one time differs from her ranking at a later time, so what looks like weakness of will is really a preference that changes with the clock. Once you see it this way the philosophical puzzle largely dissolves, because the agent never really held the all-things-considered judgment that the later option was better — she merely had a preference that happened to favor it at a distance. This also explains commitment devices. Since naive discounters are the ones who anticipate their own future reversal, they are the ones who demand commitment devices like automatic savings or Christmas clubs, whereas sophisticated discounters, who don't foresee the reversal, see no need for them. So the economics doesn't so much solve the philosopher's problem as replace it with a better-described one."
