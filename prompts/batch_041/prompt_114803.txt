I run a small reading group — people who did philosophy as undergrads and then wandered off into law, history, one retired engineer — and next month it's my turn to do the absent-minded driver problem. I've got 75 minutes, a whiteboard, and no math beyond what I can sketch. What I want from you isn't the explanation, it's the plan for giving it: what has to land first, what carries the whole thing, where they'll go wrong, and how I'll know if it worked.

One thing to get out of the way: the stock version is everywhere. Two identical junctions, turn off at the first and you get 0, at the second you get 4, blow past both and you get 1, you can't tell them apart. Half the group will have skimmed a blog post about it. So I want to build the session on a version I've fiddled with, and I want that to be the carrying example. Here it is: a driver heading to dinner. Two junctions, visually identical. Off at the first, you reach a so-so diner — worth 0. Off at the second, you reach the place you actually wanted — worth 4. Overshoot both and you're home eating leftovers — worth 1. You cannot tell the junctions apart when you arrive at one. The tweak is that the driver commits to a whole policy before leaving the driveway — a rule that says what to do at a junction — and cannot re-decide mid-route; the policy has to be fixed in advance and then just executed. So the thing the group has to chew on is what it means to pick the best policy here, given that the policy has to be fixed before you know which junction you're standing at, and given that once you're at a junction the two junctions look exactly alike.

Don't just solve it at me. Tell me the order the pieces have to go in so the puzzle bites instead of just seeming like a trick. My instinct is that they need the act-versus-policy distinction before anything else, then the idea that a policy gets evaluated before the trip starts, then the fact that the driver's two decision points sit in one information set — but I don't know if that's the right order or if I'm smuggling something in. The one thing I'm sure of is that if I lead with the numbers they'll just do arithmetic and miss the point entirely.

Then the misconceptions. I'm expecting at least these and I want to know which are real and where in the sequence each one should get headed off: (a) that it's a puzzle about memory or absent-mindedness as a psychological condition, so the fix is "just pay attention"; (b) that expected-utility reasoning obviously has one right answer and the puzzle is just a trick with no lesson; (c) that the puzzle shows decision theory is broken and we should throw it out; (d) that the "right" move is to commit to turning off, so the whole thing is trivial; (e) that it's really just Newcomb's problem or the sleeping beauty thing in a car. And I want to know what I should refuse to get into at this level — I have a feeling the sophisticated-versus-resolute business is where the real action is, but I don't know if that's a fifteen-minute detour or the whole point, and I'd rather cut it cleanly than butcher it.

Last thing: give me one question to ask at the end that would actually show whether they've understood why the puzzle is a puzzle, rather than just being able to recite the setup. Something that separates the person who got it from the person who can only repeat the numbers back.
