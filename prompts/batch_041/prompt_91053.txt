I'm a data analyst who has drifted into doing the statistics for a small clinical-research group, and I keep losing the same argument with my colleagues: an association looks one way in the pooled data and the opposite way once the data are split into subgroups, and someone has to decide which number goes into the abstract. Over a few years I've noticed that my gut verdicts about these are fairly stable even though I've never been able to state the rule behind them. I'd like you to try to recover the rule. Here are the cases, each with the verdict I actually have.

(1) A graduate-admissions comparison. One program is selective and receives far more women than men; another is lenient and receives far more men than women. Pooled, women are admitted at a much lower rate than men; within each program, women are admitted at a slightly higher rate than men. My verdict: report the within-program numbers; the pooled gap is an artifact of who applies where. (2) A treatment comparison. The treated group survives less often than the control group overall, but the treated group contains far more severely ill patients, and within both the mild and the severe stratum the treated group survives more often. My verdict: stratify by severity and report the treatment as beneficial. (3) A job-training evaluation. Participants earn more than non-participants two years later, but among those who were employed at the two-year mark, participants earn slightly less than non-participants. My verdict: trust the pooled figure; splitting on employment at the two-year mark destroys the very thing the program is supposed to do. (4) A drug-and-hospitalization study. Pooled, the drug lowers mortality, but among patients who were hospitalized during the study, those on the drug died more often. My verdict: trust the pooled figure again; the drug's whole point is to keep people out of the hospital, so conditioning on hospitalization manufactures a comparison that no one could be randomized into. (5) A cancer study. Pooled, a treatment looks mildly harmful, but within each tumor-grade stratum it looks beneficial. The grade was assigned by a pathologist after the treatment had already begun, but it reflects a fixed biological feature of the tumor that the treatment cannot plausibly change. My verdict: stratify by grade and report the treatment as beneficial — the late recording date doesn't bother me, because the grade isn't something the treatment did. (6) A cholesterol study. Pooled, the drug looks roughly neutral, but within strata defined by the patient's on-treatment LDL level — measured after the drug has had time to act, and lowered by it — the drug looks strongly beneficial. My verdict here is stratify, but I'll admit this is the one I'd abandon most easily; it feels like the mirror image of case 3, and I can't say what distinguishes them except that I like this result.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (5) against (3) and (4): all three split on something recorded after the treatment began, yet I stratify in one and pool in the other two, and any rule phrased in terms of recording dates will get at least one of them wrong. The best I've managed on my own is: stratify when the third variable is fixed before the treatment is assigned, and pool otherwise. That handles (1) through (4) and it obviously mispredicts (5), and it would also tell me to pool in (6), which I'm not sure I'm willing to do. So: is there a single principle — a rule about when the subgroup comparison is the right one and when the pooled comparison is — that yields all six verdicts, or all of them but one? Please state it as precisely as you can, say what it does with the (3)/(4) versus (5) pair, tell me which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate if it cannot accommodate all of them, and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a study where the reversal appears only after splitting on a variable that is a common effect of both the treatment and the outcome, such as whether a patient was later referred to a specialist.
