I am a fourth-year PhD student in normative economics, and this year I serve as the graduate fellow on my university's Joint Budget Priorities Committee. The committee must, twice a year, recommend how to divide a fixed incremental pool between two constituencies—the library and the teaching labs—and its recommendations are advisory to the provost, who has final sign-off. Each constituency submits an ideal proposal that exceeds the pool, and our current practice is simply to split the difference, which pleases no one and has no principled basis. I want to replace that practice with a procedure derived from the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, but usable by committee members who have no game theory. Please write that procedure: the questions to ask in order, the data each question requires, how to define the disagreement point and the two ideal points, how to normalize the two sides' claims so the comparison is not merely rhetorical, how to locate the KS point on the feasible frontier, and what to do when the frontier is unknown, non-convex, or when there are more than two constituencies. Include the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to the provost because the question is one of values rather than calculation, and note where a committee member's judgment cannot be eliminated. I also need to be able to explain why this is not just Nash bargaining with extra steps, and why the procedure may recommend outcomes that ignore need or historical injustice.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a routine split of a modest pool between the library and the teaching labs; (2) a case where one side's ideal point is much more expensive to satisfy than the other's, so the KS point awards the larger relative gain to the side with the larger aspiration; and (3) a case where the procedure recommends an outcome the committee will find politically unacceptable—for instance, giving less to a historically underfunded unit than a difference-splitting compromise would. For each case, state what the procedure yields and why. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: which features of the KS solution—scale invariance, individual monotonicity, the ideal point as benchmark—survive the translation and which are lost, and where the procedure may misfire because cardinal utility comparisons across constituencies are being manufactured rather than measured. The committee has one hour per meeting and no economist; assume the provost will not accept a recommendation that cannot be explained in two pages.
