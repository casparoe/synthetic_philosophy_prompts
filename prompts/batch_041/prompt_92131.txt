I cover politics and ideas, and there's a mismatch in how we run elections that I cant stop noticing. Take the US or the UK: you win a seat by getting more votes than anyone else in one patch of land, and then the moment you're in, you're treated as the representative of everybody in that patch — the people who voted against you still turn up at the office with their problems and you're expected to take them. Meanwhile US courts insist these patches be drawn with roughly equal populations, so the thing being counted and equalized is individual people, not towns or neighbourhoods or trades. And the person holding the seat votes with a national party most of the time anyway. Countries that use party lists do it the other way round: you vote for a party, seats get handed out in proportion, and often there's no particular person you can corner about your benefits claim.

So what does our setup actually presuppose — about what a representative is, what a vote is for, and who or what is being represented? I want the implicit theory. Does it really require that individuals are the units of representation, or only that they're the units of counting? Does the constituency-casework norm presuppose something stronger, like a trustee view of the office, or is it perfectly compatible with a delegate view? Which rival accounts does the whole thing quietly rule out — that the thing represented is a community, a party programme, or an interest rather than a person? And where is the practice just underdetermined, so that two or three different theories would license the exact same institutions?

Then tell me what would actually have to change — in the law, in the casework norm, in how members see their own job — if you swapped in a PR list system, or took seriously the idea that what's being represented is a group rather than a pile of individuals. Keep it concrete, I want to see where the seams are.
