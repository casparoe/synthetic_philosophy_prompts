I've somehow ended up giving the opening speech at the replica of our cave next month and I'm dreading it. Background: I sat on the committee that recommended closing the real cave to visitors back in the nineties, on conservation grounds, and it gutted the town for about a decade. Now there's a full replica built into the hillside twenty minutes away — same panels, same pigments, same techniques, the painters spent six years learning the originals stroke by stroke, and a couple of the panels in there are actually easier to read than the real ones, because the real ones are under calcite now and you can't get within three metres of them anyway.

The audience is the problem. Mostly townspeople and the guides, who need visitors to walk in and feel they've been inside the cave; then a handful of conservators; the regional culture minister; and my old colleague, who published a piece arguing that a copy is a lie with better lighting. One of the councillors wants a small plaque at the entrance — down low, small print, nothing that spoils the mood — saying these are exact copies, plus a big sign with just the cave's name on it. His line is that thats honest, since it's true and nobody reads plaques. I'm not sure it isn't.

I don't want to do the aura speech, everyone in that room has heard it. What I want to get right is what a visitor actually gets in there and what they don't, without humiliating the town and without patronising the guides, who know exactly what they're selling. The distinction I keep coming back to is between a look and a history: the replica is a very good record of a look and a worthless record of a history — every mark in there is the trace of a hand that was here in 2015, not of a hand that was here before there was anything you'd call a town, and no amount of skill closes that gap. If that's right it should shape the whole speech, including whether the plaque is a lie. And there's one nagging thought I can't resolve: in two hundred years people will want to conserve the replica as an artefact in its own right, and I can't tell whether that shows the distinction is real but temporary, or that it was never doing any work to begin with. If you can settle that, use it.

Write the speech, six or seven minutes. Take an actual position on the plaque — yes or no, not a both-sides thing — and finish by telling me, in full, what the entrance sign should say if it were up to me.
