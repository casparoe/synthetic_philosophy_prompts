ok, long-time lurker on the rationalist-adjacent forums here, and I want to settle something that keeps recuring there. The recurring fight: is the left/right distinction actually real — does it pick out a joint in nature, or is it a bin we stuff unrelated people into — and seperately, what actually holds each side together? It's been rehashed for years and nobody budges. The realists point at how attitudes cluster, how the axis predicts votes and personality, how you can place a stranger decently well from a single opinion. The sceptics point at how the contents of the sides keep swapping, how left and right mean near-opposite things in different countries and decades, and how what's supposedly an ideological bundle is really a coalition held together by tribal signalling and a shared enemy. My hunch is that this isn't one disagreement but at least two, with a verbal one layered on top: one camp is asking whether a certain correlation structure exists, the other is asking whether a single label is the right tool for it, and neither notices they're answering different questions. Is that right? Or is there a hidden empirical question doing the heavy lifting (is there actually one axis, or several?), or is it bedrock — two camps with incompatible intuitions about what makes a social category real? Somebody once suggested treating left/right like a homeostatic property cluster — a bundle of traits that hang together because of shared underlying causal mechanisms rather than any common essence, the way species are often described — and I thought that was the sharpest move in the whole thread, but it fizzled out.

So: diagnose it, using what each side actually says rather than a literature review, and tell me honestly what would count as progress — a distinction, a dataset, or just admitting the question is under-specified. And please don't palm me off with it's-just-a-social-construction, that's the non-answer I keep getting. British English please, and go easy on the jargon where you can.
