Suppose there is an agent — call her the Cumulative Fallibilist — who holds three things at once. She holds that a proposition can be rationally accepted without being certain, provided only that it is probable enough on her evidence. She holds that she accepts several hundred propositions of just this kind, each of which she takes to have a probability of roughly 0.99 on her evidence and which she takes to be independent of one another. And she holds that acceptance is the attitude that licenses her to use a proposition as a premise, to assert it, and to act on it.

Two difficulties are waiting for her. In the lottery case she accepts, of each of a thousand tickets, that it will not win, since each of those propositions is probable to 0.999; yet she also accepts that one ticket will win. In the preface case she accepts each of her several hundred claims, and reasonably accepts that at least one of them is false. In each case a single principle — that acceptance is closed under conjunction, so that whatever one accepts one may accept jointly — turns a set of individually blameless acceptances into an accepted contradiction, or at any rate into an accepted falsehood.

Write an essay on which of these acceptances must be abandoned, and reach a verdict. Weigh the strongest arguments on each side rather than surveying positions, and do not lean on names or sources: I am interested in the arguments, not in who made them. You should choose among at least the following three broad options, though you may modify or combine them. First, acceptance is not closed under conjunction, and consistency is no requirement on the set of propositions an agent accepts, so both paradoxes are merely apparent. Second, rational acceptance demands more than high probability — for instance, that the proposition not be hostage to a lottery-like partition of possibilities, or that one's attitude toward it be stable under the supposition that one's evidence is incomplete — so the agent described above is simply irrational to accept as she does. Third, the two paradoxes have different sources and call for different treatments, so no single rule of acceptance will do.

Whatever you defend must satisfy a constraint I shall call the self-application constraint. The rule of acceptance you recommend must be one that the Cumulative Fallibilist could rationally adopt by her own lights, while continuing to accept that she is fallible and that at least one of the things she now accepts is false; and it must be a rule she can state without exempting herself from it. An account of acceptance that works only for agents who are certain of it, or that can be stated only by someone standing outside the very situation it is meant to govern, fails this constraint. Consider carefully whether the self-application constraint leaves the second option standing, and whether the first option can be held without quietly reintroducing consistency at the level of what one is willing to assert.

Please write your essay in British English, using British spellings and idiom throughout. Do not close on a non-committal note: state which option you take to be correct, and justify it.
