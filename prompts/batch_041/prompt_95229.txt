I have been working through the epistemic regress problem on my own, without any academic training in the subject, and I recently set a written question on it for a friend who is studying the same material. I promised to mark his answer, but I would rather not do so on my own judgement alone, so I am asking you to mark it and to explain the mark.

The question I set was this: "Explain what the epistemic regress problem is, and set out how foundationalism, coherentism and infinitism each answer it. For each view, say what it claims about the reasons an agent must actually have — must the chain of reasons be one the agent could produce on demand, or one the agent has in some sense already traversed? Conclude by saying which of the three you find strongest, and why."

His answer runs as follows. Justification is normally passed along: a belief is justified because it rests on further beliefs, which rest on further beliefs in turn. If we insist that every justified belief must be justified by another justified belief, then nothing is ever justified, because the chain either runs on for ever, or turns back on itself, or stops at something that is not justified in the same way. These three possibilities are the three theories. Foundationalism takes the stopping point: some beliefs are basic, justified without being justified by anything else, for instance beliefs about one's own present experience. Coherentism takes the circle: a belief is justified by belonging to a system of beliefs that support one another, so justification is not linear and there is no first belief to look for. Infinitism takes the endless series: every belief is justified by a further belief, and nothing is basic.

The standard objection to foundationalism is that it only works if the basic beliefs are certain and indubitable. A foundation that might be mistaken cannot carry the weight put on it, and if it can be doubted then the regress simply starts again one level down, so foundationalism either fails or collapses into scepticism.

Coherentism avoids this, but the price is that the system only has to hang together internally. A coherent set of beliefs about a world that does not exist would count as justified, which is the isolation objection. Coherence here just means that the beliefs do not contradict one another, so a set of beliefs that never clash but have nothing to do with one another would also qualify.

Infinitism looks impossible, because no finite mind can hold an infinite number of beliefs. Its defenders reply that the reasons need only be available rather than actually traversed: whenever a further reason is asked for, one can be given. But availability is a matter of what the agent already believes, so reasons cannot be available to an agent who does not already hold them, and no agent holds infinitely many. The infinitist reply therefore does not succeed.

Even so, infinitism seems to me the strongest of the three. Foundationalism needs indubitable foundations, which are not to be had; coherentism lets any consistent fiction count as knowledge. Infinitism at least keeps the demand for reasons without weakening it, and the complaint that we cannot hold infinitely many beliefs is a complaint about our psychology rather than about what justification requires.

That is the whole of his answer. Finally, say what an excellent answer to this question would have done differently. Please write in British English.
