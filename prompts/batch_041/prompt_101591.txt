I'm putting together materials for a graduate workshop on decision theory, and I've been asked to referee a long-running argument between two colleagues: one is a committed defender of risk-weighted expected utility — the view that a rational agent may transform the probabilities of outcomes through a monotone "risk function" before aggregating them with utility, so that the good–bad ranking of outcomes, and not only their probabilities and values, enters into choice — and the other is a committed defender of classical, Savage-style expected utility maximization, for whom the sure-thing principle and the associated separability of the representation are genuine requirements of practical rationality, not optional conveniences. They have been circling each other for months: one hears "you are simply building irrationality into an axiom," the other hears "you are insisting on a principle that the Allais and Ellsberg patterns already discredited." I would like the document they should have written together instead — an adversarial-collaboration statement that both of them could put their names to.

What I want in it: first, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, covering what each formalism actually asserts, what the representation theorems do and do not deliver, which preference patterns are common ground (that people exhibit Allais-type and Ellsberg-type preferences, for instance), and what the dispute is not about — I suspect, for one, that both can agree it is not a fight over whether agents can be *represented*, since representability is cheap, but over which representations earn the label "rational." Second, an exact statement of each remaining disagreement, each phrased the way its own side would want it put: the status of the sure-thing/separability axiom; whether a non-linear weighting of probability can be distinguished, in principle and in practice, from a re-description of the utility function — that is, whether the risk function has content that is separately identifiable; whether the agent's attitude is toward risk as such or toward the *resolution* of uncertainty; and whether anything normative follows from the money-pump and Dutch-book constructions that the expected-utility side leans on. Third, a disagreements table whose rows are the cruxes: for each, a neutral statement of the sub-question, the argument or case both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please include at least one crux built around the "small world versus grand world" problem — the fact that real agents never face a decision over a genuinely complete partition of states — which I think cuts differently for the two views and which neither side has fully faced. To keep the cruxes concrete rather than verbal, invent one or two small decision problems (a one-shot gamble with a modest number of outcomes and an unambiguous good–bad ranking will do) and have the two sides state in advance what each predicts about the same pair of choices, including where their predictions coincide and where they part.

Finally, because I will be circulating this to students who would otherwise read it as a verdict: mark clearly what is settled among people who actually work on this and what is genuinely contested, and do not present either camp's position as the consensus. If some part of the apparent dispute turns out to be verbal — a disagreement about how to describe a shared set of preferences rather than about what to choose — say which part and why.
