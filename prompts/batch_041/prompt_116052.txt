I teach a graduate course in game theory and social choice, and in two weeks I have to run a seventy-five-minute session on the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution. My students have all seen the Nash bargaining solution and the usual equilibrium concepts, but none of them has worked with cooperative bargaining theory or with axiomatic characterizations of a solution. About half are philosophers who are happy to argue from first principles and uncomfortable with geometry; the other half are economists who will grab the formula and stop listening to the motivation. I want a plan for teaching the idea, not an explanation of it.

Tell me the order in which the components have to arrive and why each must be in place before the next can be, since I suspect the whole thing collapses if the ideal point is introduced before the students have a firm grip on what the disagreement point does. I want the session carried by one worked case, and I would rather you construct it than borrow a textbook one: two parties dividing a surplus and the capacity to enjoy it, where one party's fallback is markedly better than the other's, so the disagreement point sits well off centre, and where the Pareto frontier has a pronounced kink rather than running smooth. Say what that case must have for the ideal point and the ray to be something the students see rather than something I assert, and what it must not have, so that they are not distracted by features of the example that do no work.

Then the misconceptions. I expect they will treat the ideal point as a feasible outcome being offered to them, that they will hear the monotonicity axiom as a restatement of Pareto optimality, that they will assume the solution equalizes the parties' utilities rather than something else, and that they will take independence of irrelevant alternatives to be a platitude any decent solution must satisfy. Tell me which of those they actually arrive with, which they form during the session, which of my four guesses is wrong, and at exactly what point in the sequence each has to be headed off.

I also want the moment where the two solutions visibly come apart to be unavoidable, because the session fails if they leave thinking Kalai–Smorodinsky is Nash with a different formula, and I want one question to put to them at the end that would show whether they can say what work the monotonicity axiom is doing. Tell me what to leave out at this level and to say out loud that I am leaving it out, including whether to say anything at all about extending the solution past two players, where I am not confident the ray construction survives and do not want that papered over.

Write the plan in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would set it out in a long email to a colleague. Do not give me readings or citations.
