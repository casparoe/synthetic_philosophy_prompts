You are a decision theorist with a specialist's command of rational choice over time — intransitive and cyclical preference, the money-pump argument, and the competing accounts of how an agent should choose when her choices are made in sequence rather than all at once. You have been seconded to the conduct-review unit of a financial regulator. Your job here is not to write theory; it is to hand a working procedure to reviewers who are smart but not specialists.

Context for the procedure. The unit reviews "journeys": products that walk a user through a series of small decisions — a swap between two funds, an upgrade, a rollover, a change of contribution rate — where each step is presented on its own, and the user is free at each step to accept, decline, or reverse. The reviewers must decide, for each proposed journey, whether it exposes users to being drained by a sequence of individually acceptable choices, and what, if anything, to require (a cooling-off period, a global rather than stepwise presentation, a one-time irreversible commitment option, a disclosure, or nothing).

Before you write, brainstorm on paper: which distinctions in the theory actually change the verdict on a real journey, and which are refinements that a reviewer can safely drop. Then write the procedure itself. It should be a checklist a reviewer can run in fifteen minutes, ordered as a sequence of questions, with the following explicit elements:

- Each question, and what counts as a sufficient answer.
- The points at which the procedure stops with a "no, this is fine" or "no, this must be refused."
- The points at which the procedure hands the decision to a person, with the reason stated — because the question is about the user's values, or about contested empirical facts, rather than about rationality.
- The steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure; say so plainly.

The procedure must keep, in usable form, the distinctions that matter: that exploitability is a property of a choice pattern plus an environment, not of a single choice; that a counterparty needs certain powers (to reverse the steps, to set the terms, to know the pattern) before a pump is even possible; that cyclical preference, changing taste, and changing information can produce the same sequence of choices for very different reasons; and that the three familiar strategies for choosing across time — re-deciding at every step, anticipating your own future choices and pre-empting them, and committing once to a plan and holding to it — give different verdicts about the same journey. Where the theory is genuinely contested, give the reviewer both readings rather than silently choosing one.

Then apply the procedure to three cases, in enough detail to show it working:

1. A savings app that offers, at monthly intervals, a swap into whichever of two funds has performed better that month, with no fee, full reversal allowed, and the user able to decline each time.
2. A subscription flow that escalates: a free trial, then an upgrade offered only at the moment of cancellation, then a further tier offered only at the moment of the second cancellation, each individually cheap and reversible, each presented in isolation.
3. A case where your procedure returns an answer the requesting business will not like — for instance, a journey that is popular, profitable, and undisputed by users, which the procedure nevertheless flags. Do not soften the verdict to be agreeable; show the reviewer how to defend it.

At least one of the three must be a case where the procedure misfires or returns an indeterminate result, and you must say why.

Close with a short section headed Limits: what this procedure cannot decide, the kinds of user for whom it will give the wrong answer (for instance, users whose preferences are stable but non-comparable, or whose choices track real changes in circumstance rather than a defect in rationality), and the assumptions the whole exercise rests on that a reviewer has no way to check. Do not pad; if a step is unnecessary, cut it.

Write the final procedure in plain prose with clear headings and numbered steps, in a register that a non-specialist reviewer can follow on first reading, and avoid any appeal to named authorities, works, or citations — the reviewers cannot consult them and you should not need them. Aim for roughly eight hundred to a thousand words.
