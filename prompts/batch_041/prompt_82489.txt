Okay, longtime lurker here (I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Sequences were still being posted) and I want to actually stress-test a view instead of just nodding along with it.

Here's the position. Moral trade is real, it's legitimate, and its massively underused. Rough sketch in case it isn't obvious: you and I have different moral views, and each of us cares a lot about something the other is basically indifferent to. I think factory farming is the great moral atrocity of our time and I could care less about, say, drug legalization; you're the mirror image. So I vote, donate, and argue the way you want on drug policy, and you do the same for me on animals. Neither of us is lying about our values -- we're just trading. Comparative advantage, but for moral behavior. And since moral disagreement is about as widespread as anything in human life, the gains from trade should be enormous. I'm not saying we have to like each other's values, just that a world where people do this beats a world where they don't, by almost everyone's lights.

So: what would count against this? I want a real map of its exposure, not a list of "some people find it icky." Which arguments, cases, formal results, or empirical facts should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? What would defenders of moral trade themselves have to concede counts against them? And be honest with me about whether the view has been quietly qualified into something that risks nothing -- once you carve out coercion, third-party harms, and insincere signaling, is there anything left that could go wrong? If not, is that a problem, or is that just what a defensible view looks like?

Things I'd especially like you to hit: (a) cases where the trade is voluntary, mutually beneficial, and nobody else is harmed, but it still seems obviously wrong -- vote-swapping schemes in tight elections, paying someone to change their vote, that kind of thing -- and whether I can distinguish those from the good trades without just gerrymandering; (b) the vise where either values are comparable (so everything has a price and my view collapses into "money buys politics") or they aren't (so "trade" is a category error and there was never a view here); (c) whether the whole thing is self-undermining, i.e. moral behavior might be valuable partly because it's sincere and expressive, and trading might erode exactly that; (d) how much of the payoff is empirical -- if it turns out almost nobody can actually find a trade partner, is my claim just weakened or actually falsified? The thing that would move me most personally is a case where I'm forced to either bless a trade that's plainly corrupt or admit my principle doesn't do the work I said it does.

American English please. And go ahead and tell me if you think the position is well insulated -- I'd rather hear that than get a polite list of caveats.
