I'm a novelist, about two thirds of the way through a book set in a small English parish, and I need one of those found-document chapters — a page or two of the parish magazine's advice column, the bit where the vicar answers readers' letters. The running joke of the novel is that he spent a sabbatical reading Wittgenstein on religious belief and came back unable to answer anybody straight, but I want it to be more than a running joke, so here's the brief.

Write me about 700 words of that column: four or five short letters from parishioners, and his reply to each. Deadpan throughout, the way real parish magazines are deadpan — no winking, no nudges to the reader, nobody inside the column thinks anything funny is going on. It should still be funny to someone who knows those remarks on religious belief, and readable, maybe even a bit moving, to someone who's never heard of him.

The humour has to come from the philosophy being right, not from the vicar being a fool. Every reply should be one a careful, sympathetic reader of that material could actually give — belief as a way of living rather than a hypothesis, the picture that shapes a life rather than a theory that predicts, the thought that asking for evidence is already to have stepped outside the practice. He isn't stupid, he's consistent, and that's the problem.

The letters should include: a man who wants to know, plainly, whether the resurrection happened; a woman whose son has stopped believing and who wants to know what she should say to him; someone asking whether you have to believe the creed to be married in the church; two parishioners who are plainly contradicting each other about a matter of fact and want him to adjudicate; and, last, a letter from the bishop asking him to put in writing what the parish actually teaches.

Don't let him off the hook on that last one. The bishop's question has to be one he can't answer without turning his own position into exactly the kind of claim he's spent the column dissolving, and by the end the reader should be honestly unsure whether he's wise or just evasive. Also — no quotes from Wittgenstein, no page refs, no titles of books; I'd rather have his voice than his citations. It's fine if he gets caught out once or twice, or loses his temper a little. My editor keeps telling me the arguement has to be seperate from the comedy, and she's right, so keep them seperate.
