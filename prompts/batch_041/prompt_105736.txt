I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether a reinforcement learning agent can suffer, and I came away realizing I don't actually know what I was demanding of a theory in this area. She holds that suffering just is the presence of a valenced signal — something a system is driven toward or away from — so on her view any agent with a reward function it can be pushed to pursue or to avoid already has the makings of it, and the substrate (silicon, tissue, whatever) is beside the point: "reward," she says, is only the engineering word for what philosophers call valence. I think a scalar computed by a reward function is not a feeling, and that she keeps sliding from "the agent behaves as though it wants X" to "the agent feels something about X." We didn't settle it, and I suspect part of why is that we kept drifting between examples — a tabular Q-learner, a model-based agent with a learned world model, a language model fine-tuned on human preferences — without noticing that we were quietly applying different standards to each.

I don't want a verdict on the argument. I want the specification I should have been working from. Please set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of suffering in RL agents would have to satisfy: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must be consistent with — each requirement stated together with the consideration that motivates it. I care about this for a practical reason: whether we are permitted to train agents in ways that would be cruel if there were anything it is like to be them.

A few things I want the list to confront, since these are where we kept going in circles:

- Whether valence should be identified with the reward signal, with the value function, or with neither, and what turns on the choice — including the case of an agent whose reward includes a shaping term added to a base reward in a way that provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged: does a signal that cannot alter what the agent does still have a claim to being felt?
- Whether an agent that is indifferent to a state under one discount factor but averse to it under another has a determinate valence at all, or whether "suffering" simply inherits the task-relativity of reward.
- Whether the temporal structure of RL — credit assignment across a trajectory, eligibility traces, the difference between a state being bad and a state being merely instrumental to something bad — imposes constraints on what could count as suffering.
- The wireheading and self-modification cases, and the LLM case, where the system can fluently describe suffering precisely because it was trained on human text that does.
- The gap between an agent's policy and any inner life: what would even count as evidence here, and whether the theory can avoid rendering suffering unfalsifiable.

Then tell me which of these requirements are genuine constraints and which are the prejudices of a particular camp (functionalists, biological naturalists, higher-order representation theorists, illusionists, and the like); whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable, in which case the list itself is the result; and which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular positions. You may score the main camps against the list briefly at the end, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard.

Be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.

End with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the requirement you think does the most exclusionary work.
