So I'm in this philosophy of mind reading group and next month it's my turn to lead, and I've decided to stake out a claim I actually believe rather than just summarize the reading: I think the strong reading of the Aunt Hillary bit from Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach is basically right — the dialogue where the ant colony turns out to have a mind and the real point is about levels and emergence, neurons being a colony of cells and so on. Not "it's a cute metaphor," but: a mature ant colony is a genuine cognitive system in its own right, a thing with its own mental states, and "mind" there isn't a figure of speech. My argument runs roughly: (i) the colony has a persistent internal state — the distribution of ants, the pheromone fields, the physical layout of the nest — that screens off the individual ants for the purposes of predicting the colony's behavior; (ii) it has a dedicated sensorimotor interface, the scouts and foragers, that can be repurposed for whatever the colony currently needs; and (iii) it does division of cognitive labor, recruiting and releasing whole subsystems (a big foraging push, then switching over to nest-site selection, that quorum-sensing thing where the colony commits to a new home once enough scouts have signed on). I've been calling (i) component-transparency — the idea being that if you can predict what the system will do from its own state without knowing which ant is filling which role, you're already treating the colony, not the ants, as the unit of cognition. So the thesis is: component-transparency plus a repurposable interface plus recruitment adds up to a real mind, and the colony has all three.

The audience is what worries me, because it's a weird mix and I can already feel the shape of what's coming. There are two functionalists in the group, both happy with multiple realizability in the abstract but squirmy the moment the realizer isn't an organism with a nervous system — they'll go straight for the combination problem and for whether the colony is a system or just an aggregate, and they'll want to know what it would even mean for the colony to misrepresent something. Then there's a myrmecologist who actually works on ants and thinks colony cognition is lazy talk papering over stigmergy and simple individual rules; she'll want to know what the colony represents, and seperate she'll ask whether the colony has interests or a self at all. There's also a Searle-ish person who thinks intentionality is individual by definition and that so-called we-intentions require subjects who can represent a we — he'll frame everything as "what makes it a subject rather than a mechanism?" And there's a first-year who always asks the slippery-slope question, so I'll get "then does the stock market have a mind? does your gut microbiome? where does it stop?"

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Order the objections by how likely they are to come up times how much damage they'd do, phrase each one the way that particular person would actually phrase it (the functionalists and the Searle guy sound nothing alike), give me a ~two-minute reply for each, and mark where the reply is soft — the spot the follow-up will land. Tell me which objections I should just concede up front rather than fight, which I can bat away in a sentence, and which ones have real force against the thesis as I've stated it; and if any of them show that my three conditions are doing less work than I think, say so. Then the one I'm dreading and don't want to admit I'm dreading: I have a feeling somebody's going to ask why, if my three conditions are the bar, the colony passing them isn't just an artifact of my having picked conditions the colony happens to pass — i.e., that component-transparency is me rigging the criterion to get the answer I wanted. Tell me how bad that one is and whether I can survive it.
