I'm closing a long feature on Émilie du Châtelet — the mathematician and physicist who translated Newton and who, in a private Discourse on Happiness written in the 1740s and published only after her death, worked out a theory of her own — and I want to end the piece with a poem rather than a summary of her. What holds me is the tension in her account: happiness is something we are the masters of, it requires passions rather than their extirpation, and among its conditions she puts a willingness to be susceptible to illusion — she counsels keeping our illusions carefully, since they furnish us agreeable feelings and let us put a little art into our lives, and she is perfectly aware of what they are. Write a villanelle in her voice that lets the form do that work: two refrains that keep returning whether or not they are true, one carrying the pleasure of the illusion and one carrying the knowledge that it is one, so that by the closing quatrain a reader feels why the knowing does not dissolve the needing. It should be recognizable to someone who has read her — the practical conditions she lists, the passions kept in reserve for the years when they will be needed, the three ruling passions she confessed to, play, love, and study — and it should land as a poem for someone who hasn't. No versified summary: the refrains should be doing the thinking.
