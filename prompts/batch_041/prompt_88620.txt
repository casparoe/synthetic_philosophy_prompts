I am taking a course on ancient political thought, and a classmate recently summarized Aristotle's views on money and exchange in a way that has stayed with me. His account ran roughly as follows: Aristotle regards money as a purely conventional token with no natural value, invented only to make exchange easier; he distinguishes household management from acquisition in order to condemn the latter, since exchange itself is artificial and morally suspect; he singles out retail trade as inherently unjust because it profits from the needs of others; his objection to usury is that it makes money breed, which is unnatural, and so he rejects interest on any loan; and his underlying account of exchange is a labor theory of value, according to which goods exchange in proportion to the labor embodied in them, with money serving merely as a veil over the real, labor-based economy. The conclusion he drew was that Aristotle offers a moralistic rejection of markets and finance rather than a serious analysis of them.

I suspect this is not wholly wrong, but I do not know where the distortions begin. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what Aristotle would deny or leave open. I am particularly interested in whether the labor-theory claim captures his account of commensurability and reciprocity or imports a modern category that distorts the texts, and whether his critique of usury really commits him to rejecting interest on every loan. I do not need a bibliography or reading list; I want the conceptual audit itself, with enough textual orientation to show why each verdict follows.
