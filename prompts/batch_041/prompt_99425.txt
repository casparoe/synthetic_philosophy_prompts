Question (as distributed): In no more than 450 words: (a) state what a heritability coefficient estimates and explain why it is a property of a population in an environment rather than of an individual or of a trait as such; (b) distinguish narrow-sense from broad-sense heritability and say which is relevant to predicting response to selection; (c) a familiar pattern is that the heritability of cognitive ability comes out high in some samples and near zero in others — explain what this pattern does and does not tell us about the roles of genes and environments; (d) say whether a high heritability estimate bears on the likely success of an environmental intervention.

Student response (reproduced in full): Heritability is a statistic that partitions the phenotypic variance of a trait in a given population into a portion attributable to genetic differences among individuals and a portion attributable to environmental differences. Conventionally written h-squared, it is the ratio of genetic variance to total phenotypic variance, h-squared equals V-sub-G over V-sub-P. Because both numerator and denominator are variances, the coefficient describes the population, not any single person: it is a category mistake to say that seventy percent of a particular child's height is genetic. Two populations can differ in heritability for the same trait simply because they differ in the range of environmental variation they contain, which is why heritability estimates are always indexed to a population and a set of environments and can change if either changes. The numerator can be construed in two ways. Broad-sense heritability includes all genetic variance — additive, dominance, and epistatic. Narrow-sense heritability includes only the additive component, the part that predicts how offspring resemble parents. Since response to selection depends on the additive component, it is narrow-sense heritability that matters for predicting evolutionary change, while broad-sense heritability is the relevant quantity when we ask how much of the variance is genetic in any sense at all. The classic IQ pattern illustrates that heritability is not a fixed property of a trait. In samples with a restricted environmental range — say, children raised in highly advantaged homes — environmental variance is small, so the genetic share of the remaining variance looks large; in samples with a wide range of deprivation, environmental variance is large and heritability falls, sometimes to near zero. This shows that genes matter more when environments are uniform and less when they vary, which is just to say that the same genotype can be expressed differently across environments. It does not show that the trait is more malleable in one group than another. Finally, high heritability does not imply that an environmental intervention will fail: height is highly heritable, yet a change in nutrition shifts the whole distribution. What heritability tells us is how much of the current variance is associated with genetic differences, not what would happen if we intervened on the environment. In the same vein, the difficulty of finding specific DNA variants that account for a high heritability estimate — the so-called missing heritability — is evidence that the estimate itself is inflated.
