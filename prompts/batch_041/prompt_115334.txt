Write a poem in three movements and a coda, all on one concrete object of your own invention: a municipal swimming pool in a small American town that was drained and filled with gravel two summers ago and is now about to be paved over for a parking lot, in a town where the pool, until the late 1960s, admitted only white swimmers. Each movement should be spoken in the voice of one of the three ways Nietzsche says a community can put its past to use in the second of the Untimely Meditations: the mode that holds up models of past greatness to drive present action, the mode that reveres and preserves what has been handed down, and the mode that sits in judgment on the past in order to be free of it.

Some constraints. Never use the words monumental, antiquarian, critical, history, or nostalgia; let each movement be identifiable from what it does rather than from what it names. Give each movement its own verse form, and let the formal difference carry the philosophical weight — one might build, repeat, and escalate; one might inventory its holdings without ever varying its cadence; one might break its own pattern in the final line. One single line must recur in all three movements, word for word, and must mean something different each time it appears. Each movement should also register the excess Nietzsche warns the mode slides into once it stops serving the needs of the living: the model that flattens the difference between a past greatness and its clumsy present imitation; the reverence that mummifies and resents whatever is new; the judgment that finds it is condemning its own ancestry, and therefore itself.

The coda should be four to six lines and should turn on the fact that the criterion — whether the town's use of its past serves life — is not something the past itself can answer. Leave the speaker at the edge of the parking lot with no verdict.

Roughly forty to fifty lines altogether, with a title, in American English. A reader who knows the essay should be able to identify each movement and find the danger in it, not just the benefit; a reader who doesn't should still find it a good poem about a drained pool.
