I have been arguing about the definition of death with two friends — one a transplant surgeon, one a hospice nurse — and I have started writing down cases along with my own gut verdicts, in the hope of finding out whether the verdicts add up to a principle or whether I am simply inconsistent. Here is the list as it stands. A man whose entire brain, cortex and brainstem alike, has been destroyed by hemorrhage, who has no brainstem reflexes, makes no respiratory effort when the ventilator is briefly disconnected, and whose heart beats only because the machine keeps oxygenating him: dead, and I would not object to his organs being taken if he had consented in advance. A woman who destroyed her cortex but not her brainstem in an anoxic episode years ago, who breathes on her own and opens her eyes on a sleep-wake cycle but has shown no sign of awareness in five years: alive — not a person in any sense I care about, but alive, and I would be horrified if a surgeon took her heart. A man with locked-in syndrome who can communicate by moving one eyelid: alive, obviously, and no principle should be needed to see it. A woman whose heart has stopped in the emergency department and who is five minutes into chest compressions: not dead, or at any rate not dead yet, since the team may well get her back. A donor in a controlled circulatory-death protocol, whose heart has stopped and whose circulation has been absent for the few minutes the protocol requires before the team proceeds: dead, and I think the transplant team is right to go ahead. A child pulled from an icy river with no heartbeat and no brainstem reflexes for the better part of an hour, who is rewarmed and eventually walks out of the hospital: was never dead at all, I want to say, because what looked like death was reversible. And a pregnant woman who met whole-brain criteria and was ventilated for months so that the fetus could come to term: dead the whole time, in my view, whatever the ventilator was doing.

Several of these pull against one another, and I cannot tell whether the pull is superficial. The woman in the emergency department and the circulatory-death donor differ, as far as I can see, only in that someone has decided to stop; if the donor is dead at five minutes, it is hard to see why the arrest patient is not dead at five minutes too, except that we intend to keep trying. The hypothermia case and the donor case differ only in whether reversal is available, which makes irreversibility sound relative to the state of our technology — and I do not like the thought that people could be dead in one decade and alive in the next, or dead in a rich hospital and alive in a poor one. Meanwhile my verdicts seem to invoke three different criteria at once: circulation in the donor case, the whole brain in the hemorrhage case, and something about reversibility in the drowning case. My tentative principle is that death is the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to maintain itself as an integrated whole; but I cannot say whether the integration in question is the brainstem's business, which would keep the cortical patient alive as I want, or the capacity for consciousness, which would not — and if it is consciousness that matters, the locked-in man and the cortical patient end up on opposite sides of a line that I drew in the opposite direction.

What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself. For each clause, tell me which of my cases is doing the motivating work, and where a clause exists only to save one verdict at the cost of an otherwise uglier theory. Then apply the leading candidate to cases I have not listed — please invent two or three that would actually discriminate between the candidates, rather than illustrations of the one you favour — and say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, which I should abandon, and what abandoning it would cost me. I am especially interested in whether the reversibility problem can be solved without making death a matter of local resources and current medicine, and in whether the cortical patient and the whole-brain-dead pregnant woman can be kept on the same side of the line by anything other than a disjunction.
