Last term I chaired my department's hiring committee, and we lost the better part of an afternoon to a set of votes that were, taken one at a time, beyond reproach and, taken together, incoherent: a clear majority endorsed our first criterion, a clear majority endorsed our second, and a clear majority rejected the only candidate who satisfied both. We resolved the matter by fiat, in a way that left everyone faintly embarrassed. That afternoon has followed me into the graduate seminar on judgment aggregation and democratic theory that I have been asked to take over next spring, and I want the final assessment to make students confront what a committee is actually doing when it tries to repair its own inconsistency, rather than merely to name the phenomenon.

Please write me one exam question for that seminar, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question should be built around a rule I have been drafting and that I want students to take apart. Call it Small-Margin Repair. A body of voters each returns a complete and logically consistent set of yes/no verdicts on a fixed list of propositions, some of which entail others — the natural case being two premise propositions and a conclusion proposition that holds just in case both premises do. The rule runs as follows. (1) Take the majority verdict on each proposition separately. (2) If the resulting set of verdicts is logically consistent, that set is the collective judgment. (3) If it is inconsistent, take a minimal inconsistent subset of it — a subset that is inconsistent although every proper subset of it is consistent — and reverse the majority verdict on whichever member of that subset was carried by the smallest margin. (4) Repeat until the set is consistent. (5) Where two members of the relevant subset were carried by the same margin, reverse the one printed lower on the ballot.

The exam question should require students to do all of the following. First, apply the rule to a small case that the question itself states in full, small enough that the arithmetic can be checked by hand in a couple of minutes, and then exhibit a case, or a variant of the stated one, in which nothing about anyone's opinions changes and yet the collective outcome does — for instance because the propositions are reordered on the ballot — and say which property of aggregation rules that change violates. Second, assess which of anonymity, neutrality, independence of the collective verdict on each proposition from the individual verdicts on that proposition, and immunity to strategic misreporting the rule does and does not have, giving a short argument or a counterexample for each. Third, reach and defend a verdict on whether this is a defensible way for a real committee to resolve an inconsistent set of majority verdicts, engaging the strongest objection rather than merely listing objections.

Constraints. The paper must be self-contained: the definition of the rule is to be restated on the exam paper, and no part of the question may presuppose that students have read anything, so the question has to carry its own case and its own vocabulary. The question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar contrast between voting on premises and voting on the conclusion, or by naming the discursive dilemma; the marks must turn on what Small-Margin Repair does that neither of those does. No citations, no reading lists, no author names anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme. Aim the question at a well-prepared student working for about ninety minutes.

Deliver a model answer of roughly 700 words — the kind of answer that would earn full marks — and a marking scheme that assigns the marks for each must-hit point out of 25, states in a line what separates an A answer from a B answer, and adds, for every must-hit point, one line on how candidates characteristically miss it. The property-by-property assessment has definite right answers; the closing verdict does not, and the scheme should reward a well-argued verdict on either side.
