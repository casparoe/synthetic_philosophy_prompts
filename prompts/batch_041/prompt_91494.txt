I am on the advisory board of a small liberal arts college that is revising its core curriculum. The board has spent two years classifying practices and ideals as either expressions of “Western values” or not, and its track record is now long enough to constrain what it can consistently do next. Its decisions so far are these. It counted trial by jury as a Western value, even though the institution is medieval in origin and is not used in every Western country. It counted the separation of church and state as a Western value, even though several Western states have established churches and the doctrine is contested within the West. It counted human rights as a Western value, even though the phrase and the international instruments are twentieth-century and many of the rights are now affirmed globally. It counted democracy as a Western value, though it originated in a small Greek city-state and has taken illiberal forms in the West. It counted capitalism as a Western value, though it is now practiced worldwide. It did not count Confucian filial piety as a Western value, though several Western philosophers admired it and some Western families practice something like it. It did not count socialism as a Western value, though its major theorists were European and it has deep roots in Western labor movements. It did not count vegetarianism as a Western value, though Pythagoreans and some Christian ascetics practiced it. It did not count slavery as a Western value, though Western societies practiced it for centuries. It did not count absolute monarchy as a Western value, though it was the dominant form of government in Europe for a long period. It counted gender equality as a Western value, though it is a recent achievement and remains incomplete.

Next month the board must classify a proposed addition to the core: a module on “algorithmic due process,” understood as a set of procedural rights that people should have when automated systems make consequential decisions about them — notice, explanation, contestation, and human review. Some board members say this is just a particular application of the rule of law and therefore already covered. Others say it is a distinctively twenty-first-century, transnational technocratic norm with no clear home in the Western tradition. The board wants to be consistent with its own precedents, but its precedents do not wear their principle on their sleeve.

If you were advising the board, what should it decide, and why? Identify the closest earlier precedent and say what work it does. Then set out the competing rules that fit the board’s existing verdicts equally well — for example, a genealogical rule, a functional rule, a normative-ideal rule, or a distinctiveness rule — and say what each would predict about algorithmic due process. Give your best guess about which rule the board is actually running, and name one further case that would best tell those rules apart. Finally, if the board’s precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say what principle it would be adopting if it voted yes and what principle it would be adopting if it voted no.
