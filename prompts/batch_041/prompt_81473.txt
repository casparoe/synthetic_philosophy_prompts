I'm in a seminar on constitutional theory that keeps drifting into the philosophy of self-binding — Odysseus lashed to the mast, Ulysses contracts in psychiatry, that whole literature — and I've noticed that my gut about people who tie themselves down and my gut about constitutions that tie down future majorities don't seem to come from the same place, or at least I can't find the place they both come from. A friend of mine says all precommitment is just a bet that your future self is stupider than your present self, which is funny but I don't think it's right. In my notes I've started calling them hot and cold bindings: hot when the self you're binding is in some altered state — manic, craving, panicking, enchanted — and cold when the self you're binding is calm and informed and has simply changed their mind. Here's where my gut actually lands, case by case.

(1) Odysseus has himself lashed to the mast and orders the crew to ignore his begging. Fully legit, even admirable — the screaming later self gets no vote. (2) A friend with bipolar disorder, while stable, signs an advance agreement authorizing detention and treatment if she goes manic again. Legit, and I'd hold her to it even when she's manic, furious, and perfectly articulate about how fine she is. (3) Same friend signs the same agreement *during* a manic episode, believing she's been chosen for something. Not binding at all. (4) An advance directive refusing life support, written while healthy; after the accident she's awake, herself, cheerful, and says she wants to live. I say the directive wins, but I feel sick about it, and my friend says the woman in the bed wins. This is the one I keep flip-flopping on. (5) A man leaves his cards at home and takes $200 to the casino; when he phones his wife to wire more, she should refuse. Legit. (6) He signs a commitment contract — $500 to a friend if he smokes. Enforceable, sure, but if he decides the $500 is worth a cigarette he isn't doing anything *wrong* by smoking; the contract doesn't make it wrong. (7) A contract to work off a debt for ten years with no exit, signed freely. I don't think that's enforceable, even if the later self is content with it. (8) A parliament passes a statute with a clause saying no future parliament may repeal it. Void — no parliament can bind its successor. (9) A constitution entrenches certain rights as unamendable. Legitimate; I'd defend it against a later 80% majority that wants them gone. (10) But a constitution that entrenches a specific tax rate or a balanced-budget rule as unamendable — that's just a winning coalition freezing the game, and I say illegitimate. (11) A Ulysses contract containing a clause saying any attempt to revoke it is itself void and triggers the penalty. Something's off there — a contract can't eat its own revocation.

My hunch is that the binding is legitimate when it protects the bound self's capacity to keep deciding later — shields them from a state they'd disown — and illegitimate when it simply forecloses a later calm judgment on the same question; hot good, cold bad. That seems to get 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 10, 11, and it's why 9 feels different from 10, since entrenching rights protects the process of deciding while entrenching a tax rate freezes one decision. But then it looks like it kills case 4, where the later self is completely cold and informed and wants to live, so either 4 has to go or there's a distinction between binding your body and binding your vote that I'm missing. What I want: the principle, or the small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could actually test it against new cases; an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate; and what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed — a will, which binds your estate after you're gone and can't affect you, and a secession clause, where a whole people tries to entrench its own unity against a later majority in one region. If my verdicts are just incoherent, or if I've dressed up the same case twice in different clothes, or if "precommitment" isn't one thing and I'm lumping together two problems that need separate treatment, say that plainly instead of humoring me with a tidy principle. And please don't just report what some theorist said — I want the principle and I want to see where it breaks.
