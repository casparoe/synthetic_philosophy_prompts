You are a poet with a philosopher's training — the kind of writer who can produce a villanelle that a specialist in metaethics recognizes as exactly right and that a reader who has never heard of the debate still finds moving. Work slowly and deliberately. In this task the form is not decoration but the argument itself, so plan carefully before you draft and test your plan against the constraints before you commit to lines.

The occasion: I teach the final session of a graduate seminar on the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism — the difficulty that arises because moral vocabulary keeps its meaning when it appears in unasserted and embedded positions (as the antecedent of a conditional, as the object of wondering or doubting, under negation), even though the expressivist holds that the point of such vocabulary is to express an attitude rather than to state a fact. I want to close the seminar by reading a poem aloud and handing out copies. Write that poem.

Form: a villanelle. Nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, two refrains returning in the pattern the form requires and meeting as the final couplet, with a strict rhyme scheme and metrical control. Deliberate slant rhyme is acceptable; accidental rhyme and padded lines are not.

Hard constraints:

1. The poem must turn on one short evaluative clause of your choosing — the sort of thing a person says and thereby takes a stand — and that clause must appear with exactly the same wording at least twice: once as a complete assertion (someone saying it, or believing it, flat out) and once buried inside a larger sentence where nothing is being asserted, for instance under if, whether, or because, or under a verb of wondering, doubting, or reporting. The reader must feel both the sameness of the words and the difference in force.

2. At least one stanza must show the inference working: the clause as the antecedent of a conditional, then the clause again as a flat verdict, so that a reader who follows the steps feels the pressure — for the step to be valid, the antecedent has to carry the very content the expressivist denies is there.

3. The closing couplet, where the two refrains meet, must be the best reconciliation you can give the expressivist: the move that treats the embedded occurrence as expressing a higher-order attitude, such as disapproval of a combination of attitudes, or a constraint on what one can consistently approve or plan. But the poem must leave the seam visible. The fix should read as something won, not something waved away.

4. The poem must live in ordinary speech and in a scene — people talking about a third person, someone doubting, someone insisting — and must enact the problem rather than explain it. No technical vocabulary (nothing like express, attitude, proposition, embed, unasserted, truth-apt, normative, quasi-realism). No names of philosophers, no titles, no citations, no footnotes, no glossary. If the poem needs apparatus to work, it has failed.

5. Accuracy: someone who works on this problem should be able to point at each line and say what it is doing, while a reader who knows nothing about it should still be reading a poem. Do not resolve the problem; make the shape of it felt.

Process: begin with a brief plan — a few lines stating what the two refrains will be, where the embedded occurrence falls, where the turn comes, and how the closing couplet does its work. Then write the poem, with a title. Then reread it against constraints 1 through 5 and repair whatever slipped, checking in particular that the repeated clause is word-for-word identical in its two positions and that the refrains land where the form requires. Finish with a note of no more than 120 words, for the handout, identifying which line does which job, so that a reader can see the machinery after reading the poem.
