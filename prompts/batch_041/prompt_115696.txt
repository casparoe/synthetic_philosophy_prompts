Kavka's toxin puzzle has always bugged me — the one where the billionaire wires you the money if you can genuinely intend, tonight, to drink the vial of disgusting-but-harmless toxin in the morning, and you're totally free to change your mind after; the payoff's for the intention, not the act. I want a short story that takes that exact structure and drops it somewhere I haven't seen it.

My transplant: a parole board, near future. They've got a scanner that reads remorse perfectly — not the performance of it, the real thing — and whether you genuinely intend to go straight. No false positives, no false negatives, theyve checked it to death. Early release now goes only to people who pass, and you get scanned the morning of your hearing. The prisoners have all worked out the puzzle on thier own: you can't decide to feel it, and wanting the door open is precisely what stops you from feeling it.

Write it from the POV of a board member who helped build the program and is now sitting on the other side of the glass. Two things I want in there. One, the texture of a person trying to want something they don't want — get that on the page without making it clinical. Two, the moment she starts to suspect the ugly structural fact: that anyone who passes was going to go straight anyway, so the program is selecting people rather than doing anything to them. And then let her notice the crack in that — that the machine might still have a causal effect, if word of it gets out early enough that people have time to actually become the kind of person who'd pass, which is a different thing from intending it on the day. Don't solve it with a broken scanner, and don't let anyone fake it. Around 2000 words, and give me one short scene that's just the sound of the room while somebody waits for their result.
