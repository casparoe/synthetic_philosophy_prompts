Ok so I'm on this small annotation/reading group that's putting together a practical guide for people who have to make "is it alive?" calls on weird samples -- mostly exobiology-adjacent stuff, but it's also feeding into a biotech ethics thing I'm doing on the side. What I want from you is a decision tree plus a map of the position space, not an essay about vitalism.

Here's the setup. Picture a lab tech who gets a sample and has to answer a short list of yes/no questions about it, and the answers route them to a position on what life is, and therefore on whether the sample counts. The nodes have to be questions you could actually answer by looking at the sample and running a few tests -- does it grow, does it repair itself, does it have a boundary, does it vary heritably, is it descended from something already agreed to be alive, does it take in free energy -- and phrased so the tech has no idea which position a "yes" leads to. No node should be anything like "do you think life requires metabolism?"

The sample types you have to handle, at minimum. Some of these I'm making up, that's fine, treat them as stipulations:

- a self-replicating mineral assemblage on a vent: grows, heals its own cracks, seeds daughter assemblages, but the chemistry is silicate and every mutation gets corrected, so it never evolves
- a prion
- a virus
- a lineage of self-replicating programs on a laptop that mutate and get selected by their own memory limits, but the laptop is just hardware
- a bacterium frozen for 40 million years: zero metabolism, revives when you warm it up
- a sterile-but-busy cell: metabolises and maintains itself fine, replication machinery permanently broken
- an autocatalytic chemical network with real heredity but no membrane, it just diffuses through the surrounding water
- a whole pond that maintains its own chemistry and occasionally "splits" into two ponds after a flood, but nothing in it is alive by anyone's normal standard
- a chemically perfect simulation of a bacterium running in a closed simulation nothing can escape, so it can never evolve in our world
- a viral gene that's been sitting in a mammal genome for a few million years and has lost every gene for making a body

Then the positions. I don't want the leaves named after people -- I can't look anyone up and neither can you, so describe each view in a phrase instead. I need at least: a metabolism/self-maintenance-first view, a replication-and-evolution-first view, a "life is a property of lineages and processes rather than of individuals" view, a degrees-of-life gradualist view, a "strong emergence, there's something extra going on" view, a "the individual organism isn't alive, only the whole biosphere-ish system is" view, and a "this is a stipulation and we should split the word into two" view. Where a combination of answers in your tree corresponds to a position nobody actually holds, don't delete it -- mark it and say in one line whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

Also I want a section at the end on what the tree can't seperate. My guess is that it can't separate two views that agree on every testable question but disagree about whether "alive" names a real joint in nature or is just a useful label -- but tell me if there are others, and don't paper over them.

Keep it to maybe 12 leaves max. Each leaf gets the position's name (or "unnamed" if there isn't one) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. And coin me a term for the "borderline but we should keep watching it" bucket -- make something up, define it in a line, then say which of your leaves would and wouldn't use it.

Oh, one more thing: build the tree from the questions first and drop the positions in where they land, so that if two positions end up at the same leaf you tell me that instead of inventing a fake distinction to split them.
