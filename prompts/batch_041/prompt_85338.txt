Write a dialogue between two equally competent researchers who disagree about whether destroying a habitat is, on balance, good or bad for the wild animals living in it. One is a restoration ecologist who holds that habitat loss is a catastrophe for wild animals and that our near-total ignorance of their inner lives forbids us from gambling on welfare estimates; the other is a welfare-oriented biologist who suspects that many wild animals live lives that are, on average, not worth living, so that fewer such lives may be better than more.

Have them fight over two specific things. First, a distinction I want the dialogue itself to draw: standing-stock welfare, the welfare of the animals alive right now, whose deaths the destruction directly causes, versus flow welfare, the welfare of all the animal lives the habitat would have generated over the coming century if left alone. The biologist should press the ecologist on why losing the second counts as a loss at all if those lives would have gone badly. Second, the replacement problem: when a species-rich habitat is destroyed, its animals are not simply subtracted; they are replaced by generalists — rats, pigeons, cockroaches, parasites — whose lives may be better or worse than those they displace, so the destruction may alter the composition of sentient life without changing its total amount.

The ecologist must not concede easily: let them argue that our ignorance about wild welfare cuts against the biologist's confidence rather than for it, and that the biologist's premise, put into practice, would license any developer to bulldoze anything. Each reply should answer the specific point the other just made rather than restating a position. Close with the one piece of evidence each would most want and neither has.
