I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am preparing to interview a philosopher for a small internal podcast at my company. The guest defends a version of metaontological deflationism: she argues that many disputes about whether composite objects, numbers, or persons exist are merely verbal, because the disputants operate within different conceptual frameworks or attach different meanings to existence claims, and she treats existence questions as internal to a framework rather than as questions with a single framework-independent answer.

I want the interview to stay in the weeds and not let her simply restate the view. I have built a diagnostic test from my day job, which I call the interoperability test. It says a dispute is merely verbal when four conditions hold: (1) observational equivalence, meaning both sides agree on all observable or predictive consequences; (2) translational parity, meaning each side's vocabulary can be mapped onto the other's without losing explanatory or predictive power; (3) schema canonicalization, meaning the dispute persists only because each side treats its own ontology as the default or canonical description; and (4) decision invariance, meaning no practical, scientific, or normative decision changes when you switch from one ontology to the other. I want questions that press whether this test captures the metaontological notion of a verbal dispute, where it over-generates or under-generates, and what her view implies when the test fails.

Draft eight to ten interview questions in a sequence that makes her standard deflections progressively harder. For each question, include the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Press on: the difference between verbal and genuine disagreement; quantifier variance and whether it makes ontology a matter of language choice; the objection that if any disagreement can be redescribed as verbal by positing a new meaning, the view threatens to make every dispute verbal; whether deflationism leaves us unable to criticize frameworks that seem politically or morally harmful, especially for existence claims about race, gender, or social kinds; the distinction between questions internal to a framework and questions about the framework as a whole; and what argument or evidence could count against her view. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies, and do not ask generic questions about her career, influences, or advice for students.

Return a single JSON object with exactly these fields: `guest_position_summary` (string), `diagnostic_test_summary` (string), and `questions` (array). Each element of `questions` must be an object with fields: `order` (integer, starting at 1), `question` (string), `target` (string naming the specific claim or move in her position that the question tests), `likely_reply` (string, the answer she is most likely to give), `follow_up` (string, keyed to that likely reply), `pressure_type` (one of: clarification, counterexample, internal_tension, scope, meta_level), and `uses_software_analogy` (boolean). Ensure the sequence ends with a question about what could change her mind.
