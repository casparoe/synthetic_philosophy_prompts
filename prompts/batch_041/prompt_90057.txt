I cover mind and cognitive science for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the better part of a year circling a puzzle I cannot get to sit still. It begins with a piece of laboratory folklore that I have seen demonstrated many times: two small discs are flashed in quick succession, one slightly to the left of the other, and if the spacing and the timing are right the viewer sees not two discs but a single disc that travels from the first position to the second. The refinement that interests me is what happens when the two discs are different colours. The viewer reports watching the disc move and watching it change colour partway along its path — at a moment, crucially, that lies before the second disc was ever shown, and therefore before any light carrying the second colour could have reached the eye. I understand that Dennett pressed this case into service against a certain picture of how consciousness is organised, and that he framed the explanatory options as a choice between what he called a Stalinesque story, in which the perceptual content is fabricated after the fact and then delivered to the subject already edited, as a show trial is staged for an audience that never sees the rehearsals, and an Orwellian story, in which the experience genuinely occurs in the order reported and it is the memory that is subsequently rewritten. I can recite the distinction, but I have never been able to satisfy myself as to which account is actually correct, and I have a growing suspicion that the way the question is posed is doing some of the work.

What I want from you is an essay that takes a position rather than shrugging. Set out what is genuinely at stake between the two stories — what difference it could possibly make, to the subject or to us, whether the editing happens on the way in or on the way out — and then defend a verdict about which, if either, is the better account of the colour change. As you do, please confront a wrinkle that has been nagging me. Suppose we add a condition in which the viewer is asked to press a button at the instant the colour appears to change, and suppose that on some trials the presses cluster at times that fit neither story cleanly: too early for a genuine experience of the second colour to have been caused by the second disc, yet too late to be a memory laid down only after the fact. Does a case of that shape collapse the distinction, reveal it as a contrast between two things that might both be true at once, or show that the vocabulary of before and after is simply the wrong vocabulary for describing experience at all? Reach a conclusion. I am not after a literature review or a reading list, and I have no use for citations; I want the argument carried far enough that I can see where you land, and why someone who favoured the other story ought to feel the pressure.
