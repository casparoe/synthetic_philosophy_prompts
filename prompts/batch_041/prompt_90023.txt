Ok, so I need help with something and I'm honestly not sure I even know how to ask for it. Background: I spent about thirty-five years doing vision science, retinas, LGN, a fair bit of psychophysics at the end, and I retired two years ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy of mind more or less full time, and I've talked my way into a reading group at the local university. They've asked me to run a session next month and I've decided to do something dumb: I'm going to stand up and defend the view that the word qualia doesn't name anything, that the notion is an incoherent posit, and that we'd be better off just saying there are no qualia at all.

Most of the line is Dennett's, I'll admit it, reading Quining Qualia basically converted me. The target I want to hit is the package where qualia are supposed to be ineffable, intrinsic, private, and known completely just by having them. I want to argue that no single property does that work and that the package only stays upright because of a picture of introspection we have no reason to trust. Where I think I might have something of my own is an analogy from the old trade: reading off your own experience is like reading a needle on an uncalibrated gauge. The reading is real, it's repeatable, and it's often the best evidence you've got, but nothing follows from that about some special quantity out there that the needle is tracking. So when the report and the measurement come apart, my favourite case is chromatic adaptation, where I stare at a red patch long enough that a neutral grey starts looking green to me, while everything the retina is doing is perfectly ordinary adaptation. The realist move is to say the qualia changed and the reports are tracking something the measurement can't reach. I want to say that's not a hypothesis, it's a relabelling, and it's unfalsifiable in the ugly way, like aether. I also want to pull apart three things that I think get run together: the fine-grained discriminable character of experience, the what-it's-like locution, and the intrinsic-private-ineffable object of inner apprehension. I'll grant the first two cheerfully and claim only the third is rotten. I suspect that's where I get killed.

Now the group. Six regulars and it is definately not a friendly crowd for this.

Two card-carrying realists. One works on the hard problem and treats consciousness as a further fact about the world; he'll want zombies and the Mary case on the table and will accuse me of verificationism. The other is more of a phenomenal-concept person, she thinks I keep confusing the property with our way of representing it, and she'll say my gauge analogy begs the question, because there's no appearance-versus-reality gap for experience itself.

Then someone more eliminativist than I am, who thinks I'm being a coward by still talking about the character of experience at all and will want me to go all the way to talking about what people say rather than what they experience. Those two objections are seperate problems for me, I know.

Then a Wittgensteinian who thinks the whole debate is a grammatical illusion and will be annoyed that I'm still treating experience as a thing that has properties. Then a grad student who does empirical work on metacognition and perceptual adaptation and will want to know what my view predicts and whether any of my cases could have come out the other way. And the convener, who does history of philosophy, who I'm told will say my target is a strawman because nobody has held all four marks at once since about 1930.

They've given the session ninety minutes and I expect the last forty to be me being grilled. So write me the prep sheet. I want the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each of those people would phrase it and not the way a blog would, ranked by how likely and how damaging they are, each with about two minutes of reply from me and a note on where my reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so I don't look like I'm dodging. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, the one that actually has teeth rather than the one that's just rude. If one of their objections really does show I'm wrong rather than merely awkward, say so plainly, I'd rather find out now than in the room. I'm not a philosopher and I'll be using lab words where they expect jargon, so flag where I should just use their term instead of mine.
