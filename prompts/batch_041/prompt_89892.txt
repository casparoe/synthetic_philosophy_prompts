Two colleagues in my department got into an argument after a seminar, and I would like an outside adjudication. The subject was whether information can ever be bad for you.

1. A maintained that there is a theorem — usually called Good's theorem — to the effect that the expected value of free information is never negative: a Bayesian decision-maker who is offered a costless look at the data can only gain in expectation, because she is free to ignore whatever she sees and act exactly as she would have acted anyway. On that basis he said that any talk of ignorance being rational, or of information doing harm, is simply confused.

2. B objected that there are obvious counterexamples — someone who would be happier not knowing a grim prognosis, or a person who performs worse under the weight of a discouraging fact — and added that the negative value of information is itself a known result, so A's theorem must be smuggling in premises.

3. A replied that those are cases of regret after the fact, not of negative value before it, and that the results B half-remembers all turn on the information being costly or on the agent failing to be a genuine expected-utility maximiser; grant costless information and a consistent Bayesian, he said, and non-negativity is a theorem, full stop.

4. B came back that "she can always ignore it" is doing all the work: real people cannot un-know things, and, more importantly, if part of what I care about is being a certain kind of person — someone who trusts her partner, say — then information changes my preferences and not merely my probabilities, so the theorem's setup never gets off the ground; and in situations involving other agents, being better informed can leave me worse off because it narrows what I can credibly do.

Neither would concede. Please adjudicate move by move, rating each of the four moves on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say what, if anything, both parties are missing. Return your answer as a JSON object with the following fields: "moves", an array with one entry per move in the order given, each entry an object with "move" (the move's number), "speaker" ("A" or "B"), "rating" (one of "clearly mistaken", "mostly mistaken", "mixed", "mostly right", "clearly right") and "comment" (one or two sentences justifying the rating); "crucialDistinction", a short string naming the single distinction that does most to resolve the dispute; "whatBothMiss", a short string; and "bottomLine", one sentence stating who is more right overall and why. Write in British English.
