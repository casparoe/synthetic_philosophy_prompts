I have no background in economics or philosophy, but I follow prediction markets closely and read a good deal of popular writing about them, and there is a picture I keep encountering that I can no longer make sit right with something else I once read.

The picture is that a market is, at bottom, a great polling machine. On this view, prices exist to summarize what everyone collectively knows, so that the quoted price of a contract is simply the crowd's best estimate of some fact — who will win an election, whether a storm will make landfall, what a company will earn. The same picture appears whenever people say the stock market is voting on the future, or that bond yields reveal what traders believe about inflation.

The thing I cannot square with it is a claim I came across and have not been able to forget: that if traders were all perfectly rational, all began from the same starting probabilities, and all knew these things about one another, then there would be no trading at all. No one would accept the other side of a bet, the argument runs, because the price on offer would already be the price you yourself would have set, and you would have to wonder why anyone was willing to take the opposite position from you. If that is right, then the polling-machine picture appears to describe something that could never actually operate.

I would like you to treat the polling-machine picture as an analogy and audit it. Set out the correspondence as explicitly as you can — what plays the role of the voters, the ballot, the tally, the announced result, and the margin of error, if anything does — and then show me where the mapping breaks down. My suspicion is that the tension between the two claims turns on assumptions the polling picture quietly imports and never states: that everyone is answering the same question, that the individual answers are arrived at independently, and that there is a single fact being estimated. I want to know whether those assumptions are doing the real work, whether the idea that markets reveal anything survives once they are removed, and which of the disanalogies genuinely matter for that conclusion rather than being incidental. I am not asking for a review of the literature; I want the audit itself, and if the analogy is being asked to carry more than it can, I would like to know what a better picture would be.
