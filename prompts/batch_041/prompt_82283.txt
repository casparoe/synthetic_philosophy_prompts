Ok, so I'm a novelist — historical stuff, mostly — and I've spent the better part of a year in various reading rooms, and I've talked myself into a view I can't shake and I'd like you to try to kill it. Here's roughly what I've landed on, three claims and a sub-claim:

(1) The archive isn't a record of what happened, it's a record of who had the power and the habit of writing things down. Preservation is a power relation before it's anything else.

(2) Because of that, the silences aren't just gaps — they're evidence. The absence of a person, an event, a whole category of experience from the record tells you something positive about the machinery that decided what was worth keeping.

(3) It's not only what gets kept but the shape of the keeping. The form the records come in, the categories on the finding aid, the genre the clerk happened to be writing in — those formats don't just store history, they decide in advance what can be said inside them. So the archive sort of manufactures its subject rather than reflecting it.

And a sub-claim I'm fond of: a silence can be produced at several different moments. The thing can fail to get recorded when it happens, or get dropped when scattered documents are pulled together into a collection, or just never get called up by anyone, or get narrated into insignificance later on. Those are seperate kinds of silence and I suspect (2) is only really true of some of them.

What I want from you is defeaters — not a reading list, I can't use one, and please don't give me citations I can't check. What would count against this? Which observations, which historical cases, which counterexamples or arguments should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be?
