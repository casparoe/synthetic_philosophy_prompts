I am a high school student enrolled in a comparative-religion seminar, and for our unit on medieval philosophy I have been reading the explanation Maimonides offers in the Guide of the Perplexed for the laws of sacrifice. As I understand it, he does not treat sacrifice as something God wants for its own sake. Rather, he treats the sacrificial commandments as a kind of concession to the people who received them: the Israelites were already steeped in the sacrificial practices of the surrounding nations, and to have demanded that they abandon the whole institution at once would have been too violent a wrench, so the Law took up the practice and redirected it toward God instead of abolishing it outright. I find this explanation deeply persuasive, but a friend in the seminar pushed it much further than I was comfortable with, and I cannot locate the point at which the push goes wrong. Please take the role of a careful philosopher of religion with expertise in medieval Jewish thought, and reason slowly before you answer: begin by restating the argument below in your own words and saying what work each step is doing, and only then offer your diagnosis.

The argument my friend constructed runs as follows. (1) A commandment is a concession when its content was selected because of the pre-existing habits of the people who received it. (2) Maimonides holds that the sacrificial commandments were given because of the Israelites' pre-existing immersion in sacrificial religion. (3) Therefore the sacrificial commandments are a concession. (4) A concession accommodates a practice rather than requiring it, and so does not render that practice essential or permanently binding. (5) Therefore the sacrificial commandments are not essential or permanently binding; they were permitted rather than demanded. (6) But the same reasoning applies to any commandment whose content reflects the contingent circumstances of its recipients — laws shaped to a particular economy, a particular political arrangement, or a particular stage of civilization. (7) Many commandments reflect such circumstances. (8) So many commandments are, by Maimonides' own method, mere concessions rather than essentials. (9) If that is so, then the Law is not binding in its details in the way Maimonides insists it is — a conclusion he would surely reject. I would like to keep both his explanation of the sacrifices and his conviction that the Law is permanently binding, and I suspect they can be held together, but I cannot see which step is doing the illegitimate work. Is some step subtly wrong — an equivocation about what a concession is, or a slide from an explanation of why a law was given to a conclusion about whether it binds — or is the argument genuinely valid, so that the real question is which premise I must abandon? Please name the step or premise you would give up, explain why it looked so plausible in the first place, and tell me what it costs to reject it: in particular, whether I can retain the accommodation explanation of the sacrifices while blocking the slide to (9), or whether the very move that saves the sacrifices also drains the explanation of the appeal that made it attractive to me.
