I teach a second-year undergraduate course on moral change, and I am working through the final-exam scripts for the unit on moral revolutions. The question on the paper was this: "Entrenched practices such as Atlantic slavery and aristocratic dueling have been explained in three broadly different ways. One says the practice ended because the arguments against it were finally made and heard. A second says it ended because it stopped paying — the interest-based reading usually labelled the Williams thesis, on which British abolition is treated as a by-product of the slave economy's declining profitability and the rise of industrial capital. A third says it ended because honor changed — the reading associated with Appiah, who argues that the case against dueling, footbinding and slavery had been available for a long time before any of those practices ended, and that what actually moved people was a shift in what was held honorable and shameful. Set out the disagreement among these three accounts, and assess how far the honor-centered account can accommodate the considerations the other two appeal to." The script I am marking now is transcribed below exactly as the student wrote it, and I would like your assessment of it before I assign a mark.

Student's answer:

"Each of the three accounts is a claim about what does the causal work when a practice like dueling or slavery dies out, and they disagree because each is looking at a different stage of the process. The argument-centered account is the plainest: the practice survived because the arguments against it had not yet been made or not yet been heard, and it ended once they were — so the abolition of slavery is the belated triumph of the case the abolitionists had been pressing since the eighteenth century. The Williams thesis, which was advanced by the philosopher Bernard Williams, rejects that picture for slavery. On his account, British abolition was not a moral victory at all but an economic event: by the 1830s the slave economy had stopped being profitable, industrial capital had better uses for its money, and the moral case for abolition was at best a convenient rationalisation that let the beneficiaries of the change feel good about it. The interests came first, and the arguments arrived afterwards to dress them up. Appiah's The Honor Code is the third position. Appiah compares the duel in England, footbinding in China, Atlantic slavery, and honor killings in Pakistan, and observes that in each of these transitions the arguments against the practice were already familiar long before it ended; since they were sitting there unused, they cannot be what ended it. What changed was honor. Once dueling came to be seen as vulgar rather than gallant, gentlemen simply stopped issuing challenges, and once slaveholding became a source of shame rather than pride the institution lost the social support it depended on. Appiah's central distinction is between morality, which is external — the codes a community imposes on its members — and honor, which is internal, a matter of a person's own sense of what is due to them. Because honor works through pride and shame it can move people whom argument cannot reach. On the question of fit: the honor account can absorb the argument-centered view, since Appiah agrees the arguments had to be available, but it cannot really absorb the Williams thesis, because if the shift in honor was itself downstream of the declining profitability of slavery, then honor is doing no independent explanatory work. I take Appiah's account to be the strongest of the three, because he is the only one who explains why the arguments went unheard for so long. But it also shows that moral revolutions are not really about morality at all; they are about the reputational economy of a group, and the moral arguments that get remembered afterwards are simply the ones that happened to line up with the new code of honor. The abolitionists' arguments therefore did no causal work, and we should stop treating the end of slavery as a moral achievement."
