I am preparing a seminar in which J. B. Priestley's An Inspector Calls is read alongside Peter Singer's argument about the drowning child, and I would like a genuine philosophical reading of the play rather than the usual examination-style commentary on social responsibility. I do not need a bibliography or a reading list: I know the play and I know Singer's essay, and what I want from you is the reading itself, argued through the scenes.

The familiar move is to say that Priestley anticipates Singer — that the Birlings are bystanders who could have prevented a death at small cost, and that the Inspector exists to make them see it. I want you to take that suggestion seriously and then test it hard, because I suspect the play fits the drowning-child model much less well than the comparison suggests. Three things pull against it, and I should like your view on each.

First, Singer's argument turns on a bad thing that someone standing at the edge of the pond can prevent here and now. Eva Smith's death is not an emergency of that kind; it is what a class system produces through ordinary decisions — a sacking, a jealous complaint to a manager, a charity committee's refusal, an affair, a young man's drunken cruelty and the money he steals to make amends. Most of these are ways of treating someone badly rather than missed opportunities to rescue. If the play's claim is that they add up to a killing, does that support a positive duty to prevent harm, or does it displace it in favour of something closer to a duty not to take part in an unjust structure? I would rather have a straight answer than a both-and.

Second, the victim. The Inspector shows the photograph to one person at a time, and by the end the family can plausibly suppose that they were shown different women; the dead woman appears to them as Eva Smith, as Daisy Renton, and to Sybil's committee under the Birling name. Singer's case draws its force from an identifiable child in front of you, and there is a well-documented human tendency to act for identifiable victims while going numb before statistics. Priestley seems to withhold precisely that grip, and yet still expects us to feel the death. Is that a correction to Singer, or wishful thinking about how moral motivation works?

Third, causation is distributed. No single member of the family could have saved her. Singer insists that it makes no difference whether I am the only person who could act or one among millions in the same position; but I have been persuaded by the suggestion that where a harm can be prevented only by many acting together, each of us owes no more than a fair share of what would be required if everyone did their part. Which principle does Priestley's chain of responsibility actually vindicate? And what of the ending — the family's relief when the hoax is exposed, followed by the second telephone call? Is that the demandingness problem dramatised, the moral demand returning just as the family has talked itself out of it, or merely a lesson about the fragility of guilt? I am also interested in the gap between what Singer's argument implies and what he asks of people in his public advocacy.

Finally, tell me what the play's ground of obligation is. The Inspector appeals to a shared social body and to mutual responsibility, which is not obviously the same thing as Singer's impartial benevolence, and I want to know whether the difference matters or is merely rhetorical. And tell me what the play adds to the debate that argument on its own cannot — what it shows about how such obligations are learned, resisted and forgotten. Where the play resists the reading I am proposing, say so; do not force it into an illustration of a thesis.

Write in British English. Finish with a final line beginning Verdict: followed by one of the words SUPPORTS, COMPLICATES or UNDERMINES, a dash, and one sentence of justification, giving your overall judgement on whether the play, taken as a whole, vindicates Singer's rescue model.
