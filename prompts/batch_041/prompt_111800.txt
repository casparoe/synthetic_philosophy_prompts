I'm putting together a small writeup about cooperation between agents that don't trust each other — a person dealing with an AI assistant, or two AIs from different labs, where neither one can be sure how the other will actually play. The idea I want to build the example around is what I've been calling a safe Pareto improvement (SPI). Rough version: you change the game itself — the rules, the protocol, the menu of moves, whatever — and the change leaves every player better off than they'd have been in the original game, and it does this no matter how the players actually end up playing. Not just in equilibrium, not just assuming everyone is rational, not just assuming the other guy is modeling the game the way you are. So a normal Pareto improvement that only pays off if everybody coordinates on the new equilibrium does NOT count as safe.

What I need is a clean two-player thought experiment where (a) there's an obvious, tempting Pareto improvement — the kind a normal game theorist reaches for first — but it's not safe: under some plausible way the other player could respond, somebody ends up worse off than they would've been in the original game; and (b) there's a less obvious change that IS a safe Pareto improvement. Keep it small enough that I can draw the payoffs in a 2x2 or a tiny table. No superrationality, no acausal or updateless decision theory, no infinite games, and please don't lean on "both players are perfectly rational and this is common knowledge" — the whole point is that the other guy might be dumb, might be using a dumb heuristic, or might not even be playing the game you think he's playing.

After you lay the case out: say precisely why the tempting improvement flunks the safety test, why the other one passes, and name the one feature of the setup that's doing the work. Then tell me what happens if I strip that feature out — does the safe improvement stop being safe, or does it just quietly downgrade into an ordinary Pareto improvement? And finally, steelman the person who says my "unsafe" improvement is actually fine because in equilibrium nobody gets hurt — whats the strongest version of that objection, and how would I answer it?
