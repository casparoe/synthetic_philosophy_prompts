I'm a novelist — three books, all of them about people wanting things — and I've been sitting in on my brother's philosophy course this term, which was a mistake, because now I can't write a dinner scene without hearing him mutter that's just desire-satisfaction. What I'm actually trying to write is a novel whose whole engine is pleasure. Not the big stuff; the small ones. The first swallow of very cold water after a run. The click when a sentence thats been fighting you for a week finally gives in. The specific joy of gossip you aren't supposed to have. The smell of my daughter's head when she was small. So I went and read up on the hedonist claim that pleasure is the only thing good in itself, and I tried to write the passage that makes the case for it, and instead I built this thing, which I'm fairly sure is a reductio and I can't find the door out:

(1) Hedonism: an experience is good for you exactly to the extent that it's pleasurable, and nothing else is good in itself.
(2) For that to be a claim and not a mood, "pleasurable" has to pick out one property that all and only the good-making experiences share.
(3) It can't be how they feel. My four pleasures have nothing in common as feelings; put them in a blender and you'd get nothing. I even tried to rescue it with a theory of my own — that a pleasure is any moment in which nothing is being asked of you — and then I remembered how much I enjoy winning arguments, so that's dead.
(4) So the shared thing has to be my attitude rather than the experience: each of these is something I like, or want to go on having, or would choose again tomorrow.
(5) But then the content drops out. If the good-maker is my liking, then anything I like is good because I like it, and what the experience is an experience of is doing no work at all.
(6) Which is just desire-satisfaction theory with the satisfaction moved indoors.
(7) Hedonism and desire-satisfaction are supposed to be different views — one says what matters is how your life feels, the other says what matters is whether you get what you want, even if you never find out. That difference is the whole reason anyone bothers arguing about it.
(8) So either hedonism isn't a distinct view, or (3) is false and pleasures do share a felt quality.
(9) (3) isn't false. I've spent twenty years trying to put pleasure into prose and the honest report is that it has no signature.

So which step do I give up, and what does it cost me? I'd like to keep (3) — it's the only part I'm sure of — and I'd very much like to keep (7), because if hedonism isn't distinct then alot of what I've been reading this term is two people agreeing with each other in different accents. But something in there is working harder than it admits, and I suspect the honest fix costs me something I won't enjoy paying. Name the step, tell me why it looked so innocent, and price the alternatives against it — including the option where I keep the lot and live with the conclusion. My brother says the argument is "too greedy," which is his word for everything, including my last novel, so please don't just tell me that. Finish with a single line and nothing after it: Verdict: step (N) — three words for the price.
