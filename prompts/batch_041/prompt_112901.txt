I'm in the second term of a second-year course on ethical theory, and we've just finished the unit on hedonism as a theory of well-being. Every student has to run one fifty-minute tutorial for the rest of the group — about fifteen of us, most taking the course as a distribution requirement, so they're willing to argue but they haven't read much and they forget the reading by the following week. My session is on the heterogeneity problem: the objection that 'pleasure' picks out no single kind of state, so a hedonist who says well-being consists in pleasure has either named a mere family resemblance or is quietly relying on a disjunction of unrelated states, and in either case hasn't yet given you anything determinate to maximize. I understand the shape of the objection, but when I rehearse it out loud it comes out sounding like a verbal quibble, and I can already hear the room's response: sure, pleasures are different from one another, but they're all pleasures, so what exactly is the problem? That reaction is precisely what I need the session to move them past, and I haven't worked out how to get from where they'll start to where the objection actually bites.

So please don't write the explanation for me — write me the teaching plan. I want the order in which the pieces have to be introduced, and why the dependencies run that way: what has to be settled about what a theory of well-being is even supposed to deliver before the question 'what do pleasures have in common?' becomes urgent rather than fussy, and whether the objection should be staged as a problem for the hedonist's value-bearer, for her decision procedure, or for both. Then the single example that should carry the whole fifty minutes, and what it must have and lack — my instinct is to run several pleasures side by side (the taste of something sweet, the relief of scratching an itch, the satisfaction of finally finishing a proof, a sadistic gratification), but I suspect that a list is the wrong shape precisely because it concedes the heterogeneity at the outset and lets the group treat it as a survey rather than a problem; I want to know what one case would have to contain to do work that the list can't, and whether the obvious dramatic candidates (the sadist, the masochist) are traps, since they seem to smuggle in the separate question of whether bad pleasures count, which I'd rather keep out of this session. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface and with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off: I'm predicting 'hedonism is a theory about bodily pleasure', 'different pleasures just means different causes of the same inner feeling', 'so the objection is that hedonism is vague, and vague theories are perfectly usable', 'any common feature you name will be trivially true — they're all pleasant — so the objection refutes itself', and probably the Mill manoeuvre about higher and lower pleasures offered as though it were a solution rather than a restatement. For each, tell me where in the arc to intercept it and roughly what to say. I also want to know what to leave out at this level and announce that I'm leaving it out — I assume the question of whether pleasure is a felt quality or rather a propositional attitude (being pleased that something is so) is one of those things, but I'd like your view on whether it belongs, since it may pre-solve the heterogeneity problem by stipulation and rob the session of its tension.

One further thing. I'd like to bring in something the group definitely hasn't met in lecture and can't look up in the slides, as a way of keeping the discussion off the rails of the standard textbook exchange: in the reward literature, liking a thing and wanting it come apart — they can be dissociated experimentally, and it is the wanting rather than the liking that drives behaviour, so organisms can pursue what they no longer report enjoying and vice versa. That looks as though it might either sharpen the heterogeneity problem, by suggesting that 'pleasure' was doing two jobs all along (a felt quality and a motivational pull), or hand the hedonist a way out, by letting her separate the felt-quality role from the motivational one and give up only the latter. Tell me where in the sequence that could be dropped in without wrecking the arc, whether it helps or merely confuses at this level, and if you judge it a distraction, propose some other piece of outside material with the same function of making the room think rather than recall. Finally, give me a closing question I can put to the group that would show whether they've grasped the difference between 'pleasure is a heterogeneous class' as a harmless description and as an objection with teeth — and tell me what I should refuse to answer if someone asks, in the last five minutes, why the hedonist can't simply define pleasure as whatever the agent likes.
