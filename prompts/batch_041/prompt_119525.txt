Since I hung up my microscope and took up philosphy in retirement, I've been chewing over Popper's Poverty of Historicism. I spent my working life in palaentology, and I'm now suspicious that a lot of what my old field calls laws are either banal boundary conditions or disguised trends. Here's the distinction I've been faffing about with: a trajectory law says that any system of a given kind must pass through a fixed sequence of stages, so you can predict the next stage from the last; a ratchet law says only that once a system reaches a certain state it can't go back, without saying when or whether it will get there. Popper seems to think historicists need the first sort and can't have it. But plenty of historical science—the evolution of complexity, mass extinctions, the supposed stages of economic development—looks more like the second, or like a trend with no law behind it. Please describe the most important implications of Popper's critique for that distinction, for whether historical sciences can have genuine laws at all, and for how much predictive weight we should put on grand trends such as the idea that complexity necessarily increases or that history has a direction. I'd rather have the implications spelt out for actual practice than a summary of Popper.
