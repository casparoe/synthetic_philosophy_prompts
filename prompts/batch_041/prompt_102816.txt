Two colleagues of mine, Nadia and Owen, have been arguing for weeks about how moral revolutions actually happen, and they have asked me to referee. I am the only neutral party in the room and I would rather hand the transcript to someone who can rule on it move by move. Here it is, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Before the argument proper, both agreed on the same rough facts, so nothing turns on contested chronology: Britain abolished the transatlantic slave trade in 1807 and slavery in its colonies in 1833, paying the slaveholders a very large sum in compensation; dueling was already a settled object of public condemnation in the English press for decades before it actually died out; and footbinding in China survived repeated official denunciation and only collapsed under a concerted campaign in the early twentieth century.

Owen opened. What ends a norm, he said, is not winning the argument but the moment the people who enforce the norm can no longer make it pay. British West Indian slavery ended because the plantation economy had entered decline and the planters had lost their political grip; the moral case against slavery had been available for a century and had changed nothing. Dueling died when it became a legal and reputational liability for the class that practiced it. The arguments are the soundtrack; the interests are the cause.

Nadia answered that dueling was never materially ruinous for the men who dueled — it was cheap for them and costly to no one they cared about — and that it died because it became dishonorable: a man who declined a challenge stopped being thought a coward and started being thought a ruffian, and eventually the duel itself became the mark of a barbarian rather than a gentleman. Footbinding, she added, was sustained by a marriage market that rewarded it, so it had its own microeconomics, and yet it died when it became a badge of national shame. And the decline story about slavery is at best contested: abolition arrived with massive compensation to owners, which is not what a state does to a class whose asset has already become worthless. Arguments do not move people, she said; a change in what it means to be honorable does.

Owen replied that honor shifts are downstream of interest. Honor codes, he said, belong to whoever can keep the costs of a norm off their own books. Call this the insulation condition: a norm survives as long as its beneficiaries are insulated from its costs, and it dies when those costs are internalized — when the enforcers themselves start paying. On this view, honor is merely the vocabulary in which a newly costly practice gets relabeled. When the commercial and industrial interest displaced the planter aristocracy, it became useful to redefine the planter's way of life as dishonorable; the honor shift did not cause the abolition, it was the form the interest took.

Nadia granted the interest shift and said it still could not do the work without honor. Interests are always pressing somewhere; what is rare is the moment when an interest can be expressed as a stain. Call this honor-conversion: an argument ends a practice only when it succeeds in recoding the practice as the mark of a contemptible kind of person, so that continuing to do it costs you standing rather than merely costing you money. On Owen's view, she said, every costly norm should die as soon as it gets expensive, and they plainly do not; costly norms last for centuries. She then pressed what she took to be the decisive gap: abolition required extending honor to people who had been placed outside the honor community altogether, and no calculation of the planters' declining profits tells you that the enslaved were owed standing. That, she said, is not a transmission belt for interest; it is an independent input.

Owen offered a synthesis. Norm death, he said, happens when the coalition that enforces the norm fractures. Interest decides who defects and when; honor supplies the currency in which the defection is justified. She had shown that honor is the mechanism; he had shown that interest is the trigger; that is a division of labor, not a rivalry. Nadia's parting shot was that this division of labor gives honor all the causal work while letting interest mean whatever the outcome requires, and that if any interest can be expressed as a stain and any stain can be redescribed as an interest, then they have not got two theories but one theory with two vocabularies.

Please adjudicate. Rate each of the five moves — Owen's opening, Nadia's reply, Owen's insulation condition, Nadia's honor-conversion, and Owen's synthesis — on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Be specific about which particular claims within each move are sound and which are overreach, rather than scoring the speakers overall. Then answer three questions directly. First, is the synthesis in the final move a genuine reconciliation or a verbal fudge, and what would have to be true for it to be one or the other? Second, are the interest view and the honor view actually distinguishable in principle, or can each always be restated in the other's terms — and if they are distinguishable, what observation would separate them? Third, what are both parties missing about how such norms end — for instance about who counts as a member of the relevant honor community, about the difference between norms enforced on elites and norms enforced on the powerless, or about the role of formal state coercion as against informal social sanction?
