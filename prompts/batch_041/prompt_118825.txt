I retired three years ago after thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, and I have spent much of that retirement reading decision theory. I want to get clear on a family of arguments that runs roughly as follows: if the world is very large, there are very many other agents whose situations and reasoning resemble mine, many of them beyond any causal reach of me; and because our decisions are correlated — by common origin, common reasoning, or common structure — rather than by any signal passing between us, my choice is evidence about theirs, and my choosing to cooperate is evidence that they choose to cooperate. On such a view, cooperation can be rational where no causal interaction is possible, and a single decision of mine can carry weight far out of proportion to its causal footprint.

Before I read further into this, I want the specification. What must a theory of rational choice in such a setting deliver to count as adequate? Give me the requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse (between a decision's being evidence for another's and its causing it, say, or between one-shot and repeated interaction), the constraints from probability theory and from ordinary decision theory it cannot violate — with the consideration behind each requirement. Work my test case through it: I have one chance to commit a substantial share of my savings to help strangers I will never meet, and I believe that very many other people, most of them causally unreachable, are weighing the same question with the same arguments. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial tastes, say which single requirement does the most to exclude the candidate views you know of, and tell me whether any pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied.

At most 700 words, with the requirements in a table, one row each, and the consideration column kept to a single sentence per row.
