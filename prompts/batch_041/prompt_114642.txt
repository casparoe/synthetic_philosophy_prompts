I sit on the animal-welfare advisory committee of a mid-sized seafood certification body, and we are three weeks out from a board vote on a revision of our farmed-species standard that has turned unexpectedly nasty. Our current standard protects only vertebrates, which means the fastest-growing part of the industry we certify — crustacean aquaculture — sits entirely outside it; the revision would bring decapod crustaceans (shrimp, prawns, crabs, lobsters) and finfish fully inside. The flashpoint is one hatchery practice. The supplier that produces most of the postlarvae we certify induces spawning in its broodstock by cutting off one eyestalk of each female, a routine technique in shrimp hatcheries that no one on the committee can honestly describe as either obviously cruel or obviously harmless. Last night I argued about it for two hours with a friend who runs a small hatchery and who has been quietly furious about this revision for months. His position is roughly this: decapods have no centralised brain and no credible evidence of felt pain, so the committee is indulging in anthropomorphic projection; the eyestalk procedure is a five-second operation performed by trained staff on animals that show no prolonged behavioural deficit; and if we harden the standard, the producers most likely to drop out of certification are exactly the small operators in the countries where we have the least leverage, so the net effect of our scruple would be to move those animals into unregulated markets. I could not articulate, in the moment, why I still felt the revision was right, and I do not want to walk into the board meeting with only a feeling.

What I need from you is the written opinion I will circulate to the committee before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action on the eyestalk provision specifically and on the broader question of whether and how to extend the standard to crustaceans, with a time frame for implementation that the board can actually adopt or amend — a decision, not a survey of considerations. Please work within, and where necessary repair, the framework our drafting group has proposed, which I am not sure survives scrutiny: it sorts species and practices into three tiers according to how likely sentience is, attaches a higher evidentiary threshold for the upper tier, and then argues that where sentience is merely uncertain we should impose only negative duties (do not inflict noxious procedures, do not allow avoidable suffering) while reserving positive welfare provisions for the tier where sentience is well supported. I want you to say whether that asymmetry is defensible or whether it collapses into a way of doing nothing while sounding cautious, and to be explicit about the moral risk we run in each direction — the cost of wrongly extending protections versus the cost of wrongly withholding them.

The hardest thing, and the part I most need help with, is my friend's objection. Treat it as the serious position it is rather than a barrier to be cleared: if you conclude the eyestalk provision should be prohibited or phased out, you owe an account of why the epistemic and economic points he raises do not defeat that conclusion, and if you conclude it should be permitted, you owe an account of what that concession implies for the rest of the standard. Assume the board knows the biology reasonably well and does not need to be told that shrimp have neurons; the memo is about what we are entitled to do under uncertainty, not about whether invertebrates are interesting.
