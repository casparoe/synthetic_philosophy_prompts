I trained as a molecular biologist and spent forty years at the bench; since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology, and I have reached the point where I can follow the arguments without yet seeing their joints. I would like you to use the units-of-selection debate as a lens on one episode from my own field, and then tell me what the episode does to the debate.

The episode. Genome size in eukaryotes is almost uncorrelated with any plausible measure of organismal complexity: garlic and onions have larger genomes than we do, some salamanders vastly larger ones, while gene number barely moves. This is the C-value paradox. In 1980 two papers appeared back to back in Nature — one by Orgel and Crick, one by Doolittle and Sapienza — proposing that much of the surplus DNA persists because it is good at getting itself copied and reinserted, so that it is favoured by selection at the level of the sequence rather than because it does anything for the organism. Doolittle and Sapienza called the view they were attacking the phenotypic paradigm, and the proposal was widely read as a vindication of the gene's-eye view. The argument over how much of the genome is functional is still running: a consortium claimed that the great majority of the genome shows some biochemical activity, and critics replied that activity is not function.

I would like three things.

First, the mapping. Tell me which entities are in play — sequence, chromosome, genome, organism, population — and which is being selected, which is merely replicating, which benefits, and which is the vehicle; what the framework counts as evidence here, and what would count against it. Keep the replicator/vehicle (or interactor) distinction, and the difference between what a stretch of DNA does and what it was selected to do, explicit in the analysis, and say which of them this episode actually tests.

Second, an account of where the theory earns its keep and where it goes slack. If selection at the level of the sequence produces no adaptation at the level of the organism, has genic selectionism been vindicated by this case or emptied of content? Does the gene's-eye view predict or explain anything here that an organism-centred or multilevel account does not? And where is it silent rather than wrong?

Third, the objection that the whole dispute is bookkeeping — that any selection can be rewritten as selection among genes, so the levels question is a choice of accounting convention rather than an empirical matter. My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that this episode vindicates that objection: that what looks like a discovery about units of selection is really a discovery about how to keep the books. Tell me whether the suspicion survives contact with the case, and what evidence would have to exist for the levels question to be settled by data.

End with a verdict on a single question: does the selfish-DNA episode support genic selectionism, undermine it, or leave the dispute where it was? Put it as the last line, beginning Verdict:, followed by exactly one of the three words supports, undermines, or neither, and nothing after it.
