So I've been lurking on LessWrong-adjacent blogs for like a decade now and I feel like i've fully absorbed the standard Dutch book story — incoherent credences, money pump, therefore be a Bayesian, case closed. What I actually want is the most important implications of all the stuff *around* that story, the parts the intro posts definately skip.

Specifically the converse theorem and how leaky it is. Synchronic Dutch book immunity doesn't obviously carry over to diachronic coherence — the reflection principle and conditionalization cases — and even synchronically it only pins down finite additivity, not the countable additivity everyone actually uses in practice. And the whole thing assumes you're a bookie-able agent: someone who'll take any finite combination of bets, has linear utility in money, no transaction costs, and never just says no. Real agents have all of those outs, which means "you'd be money-pumped" might not actually be true of *you* even when your credences are a mess.

So: what are the most important implications of this for (a) how I should treat my own credences, (b) whether I still get to call my non-Bayesian friend irrational, and (c) what the Dutch book argument is really doing in the rationalist canon if it's this leaky? Be concrete about where the argument is doing genuine work and where it's just a nice story we tell to make probabilism feel inevitable.
