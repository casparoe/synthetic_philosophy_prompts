My friend and I spent three hours last night arguing about whether the practices we swim in — which side of the escalator you stand on, the handshake, the tip at the end of a meal, the tie at an interview, the queue, the fact that 'bank' means bank — are conventions in any theoretically load-bearing sense, or just whatever pattern repeated play happened to settle into. He kept naming cases and demanding that I say what each one is; I kept handing him a distinction, and he kept telling me the distinction was doing no work. I would rather end the argument by building the instrument than by out-talking him: a decision tree that takes a concrete practice as input and delivers a verdict about what kind of thing it is.

Please write that tree, and build it out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names — first the questions, then the positions they locate. Each branch node should be labeled with the question that separates its siblings, phrased so that someone can answer it about a case without already knowing which position the answer commits them to; no node may take the form 'do you accept the Lewis-style analysis?' The questions I want on the tree, because they are the ones we actually fought over, are: whether the practice has the structure of a coordination problem (would it be pointless, or worse, for you alone to switch, so that the benefit of conforming depends on others' conforming?); whether the practice is arbitrary with respect to the problem it solves, in the sense that another practice would have done the job equally well; whether what holds it in place is only the expectation that others will do it too, or also the expectation that others think one ought to and would blame one for not; whether the pattern requires common knowledge — everyone knowing, and knowing that everyone knows — or is sustained instead by precedent, imitation and local reinforcement, with nobody in a position to know anyone else's dispositions; and whether it rests on an explicit agreement or an authority's decree rather than on self-sustaining expectation. Leaves that must appear, however you arrange them: the Lewis-style analysis; a norm whose grip comes from normative expectations rather than from coordination, of the sort Bicchieri separates out; a convention that evolves and holds without common knowledge; a sanction-backed rule that is not a convention because it is not a coordination equilibrium at all; and a leaf for cases that are not conventions in any sense, covering mere habit and a practice simply dictated from above. Sort the escalator, the handshake, the tip, the interview tie, the queue and the meaning of a word onto leaves, and where two positions cannot be pulled apart by any later question — my suspicion is that the arbitrariness node and the common-knowledge node will do the same work on several of these cases, and that a norm resting on normative expectations will coincide with a sanction-backed rule wherever the sanction is informal — flag that in the item where it happens rather than papering over it.

Give me the tree as exactly five numbered items, one per branch node, in the order the tree asks them, each a sentence or two: the question in the form of a case someone could answer, and where each answer leads, either to a later item or to a named position. No introduction, no conclusion, and no separate scoring key.
