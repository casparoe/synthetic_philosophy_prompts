Ok, background: I'm a backend/infra engineer, never took a philosophy class in my life, but I keep having this exact arguement with a coworker and I want to actually settle it instead of rehashing it every sprint retro.

The arguement is this. When we build some system, is the system *itself* political -- does the design have a politics baked into it -- or is it just a neutral tool whose politics only show up once people use it? One camp says a design can be political in its own right: some systems basically require, or heavily push toward, a certain social arrangement, and you can't wave that away with "well it just depends how it's used." The other camp says the politics lives entirely in the people, the deployment, the laws, the business model -- the artifact is just a lump of code and silicon, and calling it political is either a category mistake or at best a sloppy way of talking.

I want you to write the joint statement that these two camps could both actually sign after a real, good-faith collaboration. Not a summary of the debate, not a "here's who's right" verdict, not a dialogue -- a single document both sides would put their names on.

What I need in it:

A numbered list of agreed claims. And I mean real, non-vacuous claims -- not "we agree technology matters" garbage. Stuff like what "political" is even being predicated of, what counts as the artifact (the design? the deployed instance? the whole sociotechnical setup?), whether the same object can be political in one context and not another, and what it would even take for a design to *force* an outcome versus merely make it likely.

Then the disagreements, each one written in wording that its own side would actually accept. So the neutral-tool people sign their own paragraph and the thing-itself-is-political people sign theirs, and neither feels strawmanned. Give me this as a table: the question being disputed, camp A's wording, camp B's wording.

Then the cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling it would actually move at least one side. I have a hunch a couple of these are secretly word fights (like, does "political" mean "has political consequences" or "embodies a political choice" or "constrains the space of possible social arrangements"?) and a couple are the real thing (like whether the relevant object is the design or the deployment, and how much repurposing a system genuinely allows). For each crux, give me the case or argument both sides agree would bear on it, plus what each side predicts about it, written down in advance before anyone gets to move the goalposts.

Use these cases as the material -- they're my own examples, don't worry about where they come from, just reason about them:
- an engagement-optimized ranking/recommendation system,
- a messaging app that ships end-to-end encryption on by default with no server-side plaintext,
- an internal API that only lets you represent records one way (say, a single canonical ID per person), so whole classes of use cases just can't be expressed,
- a content moderation/takedown pipeline with a fixed escalation policy,
- and a consumer scheduling app that assumes every user has one calendar and one timezone.

A few rules. No reading lists, no citations, no "as so-and-so argued" -- I can't look any of that up and honestly neither can you, so don't lean on it. If you're not sure who said what first, just don't. And please be committed: where you think one of the supposed disagreements is just people using a word differently, say so and name the word; where you think it's a real disagreement, take a side and give your reasons, even if you're not 100% sure. I'd much rather have a definite draft I can fight with than a survey that lists the options and leaves me to pick. If you genuinely can't decide a crux, say that explicitly and tell me what would tip you.

Format it as: numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with their tests and predictions.
