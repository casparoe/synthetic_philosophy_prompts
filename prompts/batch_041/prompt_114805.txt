I am a high-school student preparing a formal essay for a philosophy club, and I would like you to argue for the following position: the symbol grounding problem is not solved by attaching language models to images, audio, or robotic bodies, because those additions can supply causal contact with the world without supplying the kind of corrective risk that makes a symbol mean something for the system itself. To make the argument precise, introduce and use a distinction between referential credit and referential collateral. Referential credit is the degree to which a system's use of a term is licensed by the surrounding linguistic and social practice; referential collateral is what the system would have to put at stake if the term were misapplied, such as some expectation, action, or prediction that could fail in a way the system can register as its own error and revise from. Argue that current systems can accumulate enormous referential credit while posting almost no referential collateral, and that this is why their apparent reference remains borrowed rather than owned. Then state and answer the strongest objection: that human beings are also mostly running on referential credit from other people, and that collateral is either unnecessary or is just more prediction error. Use a concrete example, such as a system that correctly labels a cat in a photograph but cannot be wrong about the cat in the way a person can be wrong about whether the animal before them is a cat. Distinguish this from the claim that machines cannot have experiences or consciousness; the argument should be about reference and meaning, not about inner feel. Do not cite sources, do not give a reading list, and do not rely on claims about what any particular philosopher or scientist has written. The essay should be self-contained, and it should end by saying exactly what would count as a system posting referential collateral, and why that is more than a technical upgrade.
