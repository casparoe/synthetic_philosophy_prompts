I serve on the scientific review board of the Meridian Institute for Advanced Computation, a small independent funder of high-risk work in formal methods and machine learning. At our next meeting we must vote on a two-year proposal from a group that wants to build what it calls a 'Gödelian safety envelope' for a reinforcement-learning agent. The proposal's central claim is that if we train an agent to prove, in a formal system S strong enough for arithmetic, that its own policy is safe, then by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem S cannot prove its own consistency; therefore the agent will inevitably have 'unprovable safety obligations.' The group proposes to detect those obligations at runtime with a 'reflection monitor' that treats any safety statement the agent cannot prove as a stop condition, and they argue this yields a mathematically guaranteed shutdown mechanism. The principal investigator adds that the same result shows no sufficiently powerful AI can ever be verified, so the only responsible course is to fund partial, unprovable safety. Two board members want to reject the proposal as a misuse of Gödel; a third says the objection is pedantic because the engineering may be useful regardless. Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete decision -- fund, reject, or fund with specified conditions -- with a time frame for implementation, and it should give the strongest honest case for the position it does not adopt. In doing so, separate what the incompleteness theorems actually establish from what the proposal needs them to establish, and say whether the 'reflection monitor' survives once that distinction is drawn.
