I have just spent four hours arguing with a friend about an argument that two other friends had the night before, and neither of us could settle it. That is why I am writing. The original dispute was between Marcus and Delia, and it concerned whether work on computational complexity has any real bearing on how we should think about rationality. I have reconstructed it below as faithfully as I can, and I am deliberately not telling you which of them I found more convincing, because by the end I genuinely could not tell.

What set it off was the ideal of the perfectly rational agent — the imaginary being that never makes a mistake — together with the observation that so many of the questions such a being would have to answer turn out to be intractable: the shortest route through a few dozen cities, the best explanation of a body of evidence, the exact probability of a hypothesis in a moderately complicated causal model. Every known method for problems of that kind has a running time that explodes as the problem grows, and almost everyone expects that no efficient method exists.

1. Marcus: If the best thing to do is a fact, then a perfectly rational agent would have to find it, and the problems of that kind are exactly the ones no efficient method can solve. So the perfectly rational agent is not a distant ideal but a fiction; and a norm that no possible being could follow is not a norm at all.

2. Delia: That is a category error. Those results are theorems about algorithms, about worst-case inputs, and about how running times grow. The ideal agent is not an algorithm; it is a mapping from evidence to action, and nothing in its definition says it must run on a machine in real time. You have also slid from 'no efficient method' to 'no method'. There is a method; it is simply unimaginably slow. Facts do not stop being facts because they are expensive to discover.

3. Marcus: Then the ideal is idle. Any actual agent must run something, and whatever it runs will be either wrong or too slow. And 'ought implies can' is not a controversial principle: if nothing can do it, nothing ought to. An ideal that cannot tell a real agent what to do in the cases where it matters is not guiding anything; it is a roundabout way of saying that we are bad at thinking.

4. Delia: You are reading 'can' as 'can in the worst case, on every input, in the time available'. But the theorems quantify over all instances. The instances a particular agent meets may be easy, and an agent may be able to do its heavy computing long before it has to act. Your argument needs the premise that the hard instances are both reachable and unavoidable, which is far stronger than anything the theorems give you. And an agent's own habits change which problems it meets: one that is bad at a kind of problem tends to arrange its life so as to avoid it, so hardness measured over a fixed range of cases is not the same as hardness for an agent that partly chooses its own inputs.

5. Marcus: Some of these problems are not merely slow to solve exactly; you cannot even get close. For those there is no useful approximation to converge to. And when the hard case does arrive, the ideal falls silent exactly when it is needed. Worse, the ideal agent's state cannot even be written down: to be ideally rational it would have to carry a probability over every hypothesis, including ones nobody has thought of.

6. Delia: Falling silent is still information; it tells you the problem is hard, which is what you would want to know before spending a life on it. You keep assuming the agent's job is to compute the answer, but a standard of correctness is not the same thing as a procedure for reaching it. No drawn circle has a perfectly constant radius; that does not make the definition of a circle false or useless. And your last point is a separate argument, which you should not run together with the first, because it would go through even if every problem were easy.

7. Marcus: Take the one you like, then. Suppose someone proved tomorrow that every problem whose solution can be checked quickly can also be solved quickly — P = NP, as the slogan goes. Would the ideal agent then be achievable? If the answer depends on an open mathematical question, that is a strange place for a norm to live.

8. Delia: No, and that is a point against your framing rather than mine. A method whose running time grows as the hundredth power of the input is useless in a world where an agent has seconds to act. Complexity theory deliberately discards the constants and the concrete resource limits, which are exactly what matter for whether an agent can do something. 'Is ideal rationality feasible?' is not the question 'is P = NP?'

9. Marcus: Then here is the real problem. The ideal agent would have to decide how long to deliberate, and deciding that is the same kind of question one level up: it must work out whether thinking longer is worth more than acting now, and that is a harder problem than the one it was trying to solve. The regress never bottoms out, so the ideal has no first step.

10. Delia: That regress is about implementation, not about the standard, and it applies to every account of deliberation, including yours. Deliberation is a bounded agent's problem; the ideal agent is not pictured as sitting there deliberating. If you build deliberation costs into the ideal, you have not shown that it is incoherent — you have simply chosen a different ideal.

That is roughly where the evening ended, and my own argument today went no better. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the ten moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating; where a move bundles two claims that should be assessed separately, separate them; and where a move can be repaired, say how. Then say which of the two had the better of the overall dispute, and what both of them between them failed to see. I am not asking for pointers to further reading — I want to know what is actually true at each step.
