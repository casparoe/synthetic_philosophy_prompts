I have been trying to pin down what, if anything, is morally different between lying to someone and merely misleading them, and in the course of it I built the following argument. It is either valid with a conclusion I should accept, or broken in a way I cannot see, and I would like you to decide which.

By a lie I mean an assertion of something the speaker believes to be false. By a misleading statement I mean an assertion of something the speaker believes to be true that nevertheless leads the hearer, through the hearer's own inference, to a false belief. A stock case: asked whether a colleague is trustworthy, I reply that the colleague is an excellent cook and a devoted parent. Every word is true and believed; the questioner concludes that the colleague is not to be trusted, and that is exactly what I intended.

The argument:

(1) The wrong of deception, wherever it occurs, consists in this: the hearer is left with a false belief that she would not have had but for the speaker's contribution.

(2) A hearer who is lied to and a hearer who is misled by true statements end up in the same position: each holds a false belief that the speaker's contribution produced.

(3) The only difference between the two cases lies in the form of what the speaker uttered -- whether the falsehood was asserted outright or arrived at by the hearer -- and this difference does not bear on the wrong done to the hearer or on the belief she is left with.

(4) If two actions share every wrong-making feature and differ only in a respect that is not wrong-making, they are morally on a par.

(5) Therefore lying and misleading are morally on a par, and the widespread view that lying is the graver wrong is a prejudice about surface grammar.

I cannot bring myself to accept (5), yet each step looks true to me. I also notice that the argument seems to prove too much in a second direction: because (1) locates the wrong in the outcome alone and says nothing about what the speaker intended or could have foreseen, the argument would also put a deliberate lie on a par with a true statement that the speaker had no reason to think would mislead anyone. I cannot accept that either, and if that is what (1) buys, I want to know what else it buys.

So: identify the premise or step I should give up, explain why it looked compelling, and tell me what giving it up costs -- in particular, whether I can keep some moral difference between lying and misleading without also keeping the unwanted equivalence just noted, and if I must choose, which loss is the lesser. I would like a definite verdict, not a survey of the options.

After the analysis, give me a brief structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "rejected_step" (the number of the premise or step you give up, as a string), "diagnosis" (one sentence naming the flaw or the costly commitment), "why_it_looked_fine" (one or two sentences), "cost_of_rejecting" (one or two sentences), and "residual_difference" (your verdict, as a string, on whether any moral difference between lying and misleading survives, and if so which one). Keep each field to at most three sentences.
