I am a high school student who has lived in several countries, and I am trying to determine what I actually believe about everyday conventions. I have set out my verdicts on a number of cases below, and I would like to know whether a single principle can capture them, what that principle implies for cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts, if any, I should abandon.

1. Tipping a server at a sit-down restaurant in the United States: morally obligatory, not merely generous. A diner who leaves nothing has done something wrong, even if the service was only adequate.
2. Tipping a barista at a coffee counter in the United States: optional. Leaving nothing is not wrong; leaving a dollar is generous.
3. Tipping a server in Japan: not required, and often awkward or mildly rude. A visitor who tips because it is customary at home has not done something morally wrong, but has misread the local practice.
4. Queueing for a bus in Britain: obligatory. Joining the back and waiting one's turn is required; cutting is wrong.
5. Queueing for a bus in a crowded city where queueing is not the norm and everyone presses toward the door: not wrong to press forward, though it is unpleasant. A visitor who insists on an orderly queue and lets others board ahead is not wrong either, only ineffective.
6. Jaywalking at 3 a.m. on an empty street in a city where jaywalking is illegal but universally done: not wrong. The law is not doing moral work here.
7. Jaywalking in front of a small child in a city where jaywalking is illegal and rare: wrong, or at least blameworthy, because it teaches the child to take a risk.
8. A visitor from a country where jaywalking is normal who jaywalks in a country where it is illegal and rare: wrong, because visitors should follow local conventions when the convention protects a genuine local practice.
9. A local who does not tip in a country where tipping is customary but the worker is paid a living wage and the custom is dying out: I am unsure. My gut says not wrong, but I feel I should still tip if I am in the minority who knows the custom.

I notice a tension. In cases 3 and 8 I want to say that visitors should follow local conventions, but in case 5 I do not think a visitor who follows a home convention of orderly queueing has done anything wrong, even though it is not the local convention. I also notice that in case 1 I treat a convention as morally obligatory, while in case 6 I treat a legal rule as morally inert. I have been calling the idea I am after the host's prerogative: a visitor must follow local conventions when they are public, coordinated, and not morally objectionable, but locals may ignore conventions that are merely customary when no one is harmed. Is that principle defensible? Does it get all nine verdicts? If it cannot, which verdict should I give up, and what does the revised principle predict for a tenth case I have not listed: a visitor who is invited to a local home and is expected to remove their shoes, but has a medical condition that makes it painful to do so? Please state the principle or principles precisely, show which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Please do not simply say that conventions are relative; I want to know whether my verdicts can be unified and what the cost of unifying them is.
