Some friends and I have been circling the same argument for months now, and I've gradually come to think the argument is itself the thing to be diagnosed rather than settled. The topic is what a utility function is supposed to be, and the two camps are easy to state. One camp — nobody in the group uses the word, but the operationalist one — holds that utility is nothing over and above a summary of choice: to assign someone a utility function just is to represent a pattern of choices, and the representation results tell you which consistency conditions the pattern must satisfy for the summary to exist at all. On that view, talk of what a person 'really wants' beyond what they would choose, under some suitably specified circumstances, is either a handy fiction or a muddled way of gesturing at counterfactual choices. The other camp treats utility as a genuine psychological quantity — a degree of desire, or a measure of experienced satisfaction — of which choices are fallible evidence, and which can diverge from choice when people are inconsistent, self-deceived, or ill-informed. What strikes me is that nothing moves. Everyone agrees on the same behavioral findings: that people reverse preferences depending on how options are framed, that their choices can depend on what else is on the menu, that stated and revealed attitudes come apart. The operationalist says these simply show the choice pattern is inconsistent and hence admits no clean summary; the other side says they show choice is a noisy proxy for something that still has a determinate value. Then both restate their positions and we order another round.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute, the two sides attaching different senses to 'preference' and 'utility' and talking past each other? Is it substantive — a real disagreement about what exists, with one side committed to an ontology the other rejects? Or is it two groups answering different questions under one word: one about what representation best predicts and explains behavior, and one about what is actually good for a person? Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: that once you taboo the word and separate the predictive project from the prudential one, most of the heat dissipates, because the camps are not competing over a single slot — but that a genuine residue remains, namely whether the mental quantity is doing any causal or explanatory work or is merely an epiphenomenal relabeling of the choice pattern. I'd like to know whether that residue is real or whether it too dissolves. Two things I keep noticing and can't place: first, the reinforcement-learning world seems to run the same dispute in new clothes — is a reward function the agent's true objective or just the training signal that shaped it — and I want to know if that is the same disagreement or a different one wearing similar vocabulary; second, whenever someone in the group tries to broker peace by switching to talk of what the person would choose if fully informed and thinking clearly, both camps suddenly seem to agree, and I can't tell whether that is a real bridge or a fudge that quietly builds one side's assumptions into the setup. So: diagnose the structure of this impasse using what each side actually commits to, tell me which of the possibilities above it really is, and say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result pinning down exactly which consistency conditions separate the two projects, an experiment that would show preference reversals tracking some independent measure of welfare, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why.
