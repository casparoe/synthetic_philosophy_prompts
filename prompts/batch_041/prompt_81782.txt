I keep getting dragged into the same arguement about whether Buddhist ethics is really a form of consequentialism, and it's gone nowhere in about a year. Here's roughly where it sits. One side says: look at what the texts actually reward and punish — everything turns on outcomes, the precepts are rules of thumb that a bodhisattva is allowed to break when breaking them saves somebody, there's even that story (I think it's in one of the skill-in-means sutras, don't hold me to the source) about the ship's captain who kills a would-be mass murderer to save the passengers and takes the karmic hit himself. The other side says: but none of that amounts to a theory of right action, it's a training manual for character, the whole thing is about what kind of person you're becoming, and the 'consequences' being tracked are karmic consequences for the agent rather than aggregate welfare. Neither side has moved an inch.

My own hunch, which I'd like you to test rather than just agree with: the disagreement might be a level confusion. Consequentialism as normally stated is about persons and aggregate welfare, but the texts that look most consequentialist are operating at the conventional level, while the no-self doctrine dissolves the very persons and patients the theory is supposed to be maximising over — so the two camps are each reading the same corpus at a different level of truth, and neither reading can be refuted at the level the other is working at. My friend's competing hunch is that the real problem is arithmetic: if sentient beings are beginningless and endless in number, then 'maximise total welfare' has no well-defined value, so the consequentialist reading would have to be a threshold or satisficing view instead, and nobody in the debate has noticed.

So diagnose the stall. Is it a verbal dispute about the word 'consequentialism', two sides answering different questions under one heading, a hidden disagreement about which cases count as data (the precept-breaking stories versus the virtue literature), or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Tell me what each side is actually committed to, not what it would like to be committed to, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result about infinite aggregates, a change of question — or whether we should just stop.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no wrap-up. American English please. And don't just survey the positions, Ive read them.
