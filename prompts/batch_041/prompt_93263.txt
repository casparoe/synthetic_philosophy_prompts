I am a high school junior and the student coordinator of my school's debate club, and this term our reading group is working through John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (1859). I have been asked to open our discussion of Chapter II, "Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion," and while I can follow the general shape of Mill's argument, there is one sentence early in the chapter that I cannot get to sit still, and I would rather have a careful reading of it than a summary of the chapter, which I have already found in study guides.

The sentence comes in the paragraph where Mill explains what is lost when an opinion is silenced. He says the injury falls on the human race, on posterity, and on those who dissent from the silenced opinion, and then writes: "If the opinion is right, they are deprived of the opportunity of exchanging error for truth: if wrong, they lose, what is almost as great a benefit, the clearer perception and livelier impression of truth, produced by its collision with error." A little later in the chapter he adds: "All silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility."

What I cannot work out is whom the benefit in the second half of that first sentence belongs to, and what kind of benefit it is. If the opinion is wrong, the people who gain from hearing it argued are apparently the hearers rather than the person silenced, and the gain is described in two different ways at once — a clearer perception of the truth and a livelier impression of it. I suspect those come apart: one sounds like understanding an argument better, the other like feeling it more. But a collision, as I understand the word, requires someone who actually holds the error being collided with. So I cannot tell whether Mill's argument needs a sincere opponent or merely a competent one.

This matters to me because of two things in my own life. First, my school's debate format assigns sides by coin flip, so no one on either team argues what he or she actually believes; our coach says this is the best training there is, but a teammate insists that a disagreement nobody believes in cannot do the work Mill is describing. Second, a friend has proposed that our club use an AI chatbot to generate the strongest possible case against whatever position we are defending, so that we can test our views without waiting for a real opponent; she calls this Mill's method made convenient, and I am fairly sure she is wrong, but I cannot say precisely why. Our faculty advisor, meanwhile, keeps telling us that social media has turned us all into Mill's nightmare, even though it seems to me that we now run into opposing views constantly — just not in the way he has in mind.

Could you help me with four things, in order? First, identify who "they" are in the passage and explain why Mill rates the benefit lost in the second half of the sentence as almost as great as the one lost in the first. Second, say what the collision with error is supposed to accomplish, whether it is an epistemic good or a psychological one, and whether it requires the opponent to believe what he says or only to say it well. Third, tell me whether the sentence about infallibility is a separate argument from the collision argument, and whether my two cases bear on them differently. Fourth, say plainly whether either the coin-flip debate or the chatbot could satisfy what Mill is after, and if not, name exactly what is missing — belief, risk, the live possibility of being persuaded, or something else. I do not need a reading list; I need this passage unpacked. Please end with the single sentence I should use to open the meeting.
