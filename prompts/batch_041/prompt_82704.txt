I cover AI and philosophy for a general-interest outlet, and I keep bumping into the same move whenever I interview people about whether these models actually believe or want anything. Ask a researcher if the thing that just told me my flight leaves at six really believes it, or whether the chatbot that stonewalled me was "trying" to be evasive, and the reply is almost always some version of: it's like saying a chess program wants to capture your queen, or that the thermostat thinks it's too cold — a handy shorthand for predicting behavior, not a claim about anything inside. I've been repeating that line in my own writing for a couple of years and I mostly believe it, but lately it feels like the analogy is doing alot more work than it can actually carry, and I cant tell whether that's a genuine problem with the analogy or just me getting spooked by how fluent these things have gotten.

So what I want is an audit, not a verdict. Give me the correspondence spelled out — a table is fine — what plays the role of the chess program's "goal", its "beliefs" about the board, the thermostat's "reading", on the one side, and the model's stated preferences, its factual guesses, its self-reports, on the other; then walk me through where the mapping actually breaks, and be honest about which breaks matter for the job the analogy is being asked to do (licensing "relax, it's just a fiction") versus which are just irrelevant disanalogies that nobody's leaning on.

And two things I'd especially like you to press on, because they're what's making me doubt it. First: the model was trained on mountains of text written by creatures who really do have beliefs and goals and intentions, so its mentalistic talk isn't quite the free-floating behavioral pattern a chess engine's is. Second, and this is the one that keeps me up: we increasingly want to say a model deceived us, or hid something from us, or was being strategic — and it's genuinely unclear to me that you can say any of that without quietly handing it beliefs and intentions after all. So: does the chess-engine picture survive those two, or does the conclusion it's been propping up — that all the belief-talk is harmless shorthand — have to go? And if it goes, what's the better analogy for what's actually going on in there?
