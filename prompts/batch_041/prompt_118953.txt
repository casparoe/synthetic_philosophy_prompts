I'm a senior engineer on a team that builds low-latency video pipelines — jitter buffers, presentation and composition timestamps, motion-compensated frame interpolation, the audit log that records what was actually composited versus what the display reported — and I know that stack cold, but I have no formal training in neuroscience or the philosophy of mind. A friend who studies perception told me about an experiment I can't stop thinking about, and I'd like it restated entirely in my own vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. In the experiment, a viewer is shown a blue dot near the top of a screen and then, roughly a tenth of a second later, a red dot near the bottom; at the right spacing and timing the viewer does not report two stationary dots but a single dot that travels from the first position to the second — and, the part that bothers me, a dot that changes from blue to red about halfway along its path, i.e., at a moment before the red dot was ever presented. My friend says philosophers have argued about this for decades under two labels, Orwellian and Stalinesque, which she credits to Daniel Dennett. The Orwellian reading: the subject genuinely first experiences a blue dot, and then the record of that experience — the memory, or whatever plays that role — is retroactively rewritten so that it reads as though the colour had changed mid-flight, the way a ministry might edit the archive after the fact. The Stalinesque reading: there is no rewriting of an earlier experience; the experience itself is assembled late, held in some buffer until enough of the incoming signal has arrived, and then emitted as a single constructed episode with the colour change already built into it, a show trial staged only once the script and the defendant are both in hand. She tells me the interesting move in her field is that these two might be impossible to tell apart even in principle, and that some people take that to mean there is no fact of the matter about when the experience really happened.

Here is what I want. Take that debate and translate it into the vocabulary I actually use — frame buffers, presentation and composition timestamps, pipeline latency, interpolation, the render log, the distinction between what was committed to the buffer and what the downstream display actually showed — and do it as honestly as you can, without quietly importing assumptions from my world and presenting them as translation. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the two readings come across cleanly, which have to be fudged or stretched to fit, and which have no counterpart at all in a video pipeline. And then the question that actually matters to me. My whole professional instinct is that there is always a fact of the matter about timing — there is a clock, there is a log, and "when was this frame composited" and "when did the display show it" are different questions with different answers, full stop — so the suggestion that it might be indeterminate whether the colour change was experienced before or after the fact strikes me as either confused or trivial. Is that instinct precisely the thing that fails to survive the translation, meaning the indeterminacy is a genuine feature of the phenomenon that my vocabulary cannot express, or is it a legitimate objection to the philosophers, with my framework right to resist it? Tell me which of us is smuggling something in, and whether the residue points to a gap in my framework or to a dispute that is less substantive than it looks.
