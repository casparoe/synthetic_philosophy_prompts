I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, and for the past few months I've been sitting in on a small weekly argument group that two of my colleagues run. Last session turned into a long, unresolved fight over whether there is any genuine moral difference between lying to someone and merely misleading them, and since I'm the one who keeps the notes, they've asked me to write up what happened and get an outside verdict. Neither of them struck me as obviously wrong, and I don't want to hand the win to a side by accident, so I'd rather you adjudicate the exchange move by move than simply announce who won.

Here is the substance of it. Colleague A proposed a distinction she called fabrication versus cultivation. In fabrication, you plant a false belief directly: you assert something false and your hearer believes it because they took your word for it. In cultivation, you say only true things but arrange them so the hearer draws the false conclusion on their own; you never assert the falsehood, you merely leave the door open and decline to close it. Her claim was that cultivation is actually the graver wrong, because it makes the victim a co-author of their own error — the false belief is partly the hearer's own inference, so they cannot even resent you cleanly, and you have additionally exploited their willingness to reason charitably. Colleague B said this gets it backwards. On his view fabrication is worse because it is a direct violation of trust and of the hearer's standing to be told the truth, whereas in cultivation the hearer is at least working from true premises and retains the ability to catch the problem, so cultivation sits closer to a failure of helpfulness, which is sometimes permissible, than to an assault on trust. A replied that if cultivation is worse, then a company running literally true but systematically slanted advertising is worse than one running outright false ads — which she conceded sounded absurd, and which B immediately seized on as a reductio. B then offered a fix: what makes either act wrong is how much the hearer relies on the speaker's cooperativeness, so the two are wrong for the same reason and differ only in degree, with the degree set by the relationship. A countered that if that is right, then lying is just a label for the delivery mechanism and the whole distinction is morally idle — and that if it is idle, we should stop treating "I never said anything false" as any kind of defense. They went around this loop three times and neither moved.

Please do the following. For each distinct claim above — A's fabrication/cultivation asymmetry, her co-author-of-their-own-error argument, B's trust-violation argument, B's advertising reductio, his reliance-based unification, and A's closing morally-idle conclusion — tell me whether it is clearly mistaken, questionable, or clearly right, and give a brief justification for each rating. Then tell me which of the two, if either, holds the better overall position, and what both of them are missing. I'd especially like to know whether they are actually disagreeing about the moral facts or merely using "worse" in two different senses without noticing.
