Write a piece of sustained deadpan satire: an annual report from the chair of a sentencing commission in a fictional jurisdiction, addressed to its legislature, of about eight hundred words. It must be continuous prose throughout, the way one writes a letter to a body one must answer to — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered sections, no lists of any kind.

The jurisdiction has adopted an invented statute, whose name and central provision you should draft yourself. It holds that the sole justification for any sentence is the offender's culpability and the gravity of the wrong, and it forbids a sentence being raised or lowered on account of expected effects on future conduct — no deterrence, no rehabilitation, no incapacitation, no savings. The commission has also issued an internal drafting standard, which your narrator mentions in passing, discouraging the vocabulary of effectiveness in official documents, on the ground that describing punishment as a means concedes the argument to its critics. Your narrator is a thoroughgoing retributivist of the pure kind, for whom desert is both necessary and sufficient, and his sincerity is the engine of the piece. He must never once justify a decision by its consequences, and the strain of that discipline should be visible to a reader without ever being remarked on.

Three difficulties should occupy him. The first is measurement: desert tells him that one offence is graver than another, but a prison term requires a cardinal scale, and the commission's unit is anchored to an arbitrary legacy sentence inherited from the old code. He should defend that anchor candidly — conceding that it cannot be derived from anything — and insist that the objection is a category mistake. The second is a pending petition: a man sentenced decades ago for a grave offence, now terminally ill, who by every standard the previous regime cared about is neither dangerous nor even the same man; the doctrine permits release only if his desert has altered, and desert was fixed by the act, so your narrator must refuse, and must argue the refusal in the language of debt and balance rather than of cruelty, feeling all the while that the only reasons available to him are reasons his theory cannot own. The third is money: the legislature wants to know what the expenditure buys, and he must request it anyway, on the ground that an unpunished wrong is a standing injury to the political community rather than an inefficiency. At some point he should arrive at the thought that a consequence-based calibration would dissolve his difficulties at a stroke, and turn away from it.

Every absurdity in the piece must be a real implication of the doctrine rather than an invention of your own, and the narrator should be intelligent and articulate throughout — the comedy lies in the position, not in his stupidity. Someone who has thought hard about punishment theory should find the piece fair rather than a strawman, and someone who has not should finish it knowing what the doctrine commits its holder to. Do not signal the joke: no winking, no nudges in the narrator's voice. The target is the position, not any person, and no real statutes, scholars, or cases should appear; the only authorities cited should be ones you invent, along with the jurisdiction's name.
