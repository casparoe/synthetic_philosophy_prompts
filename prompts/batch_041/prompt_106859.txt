Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas — I've spent the last few months doing a series on how city governments are quietly importing behavioural-economics consultants to redesign public services — and last week I sat in the back of a budget meeting where one of these consultants was defending a proposal to means-test a new congestion charge and hand the revenue back to the poorest households as a flat annual rebate. A councillor objected that the scheme would leave a chunk of middle-income drivers definately worse off and would make the poorest wait years for a half-decent bus, and the consultant shrugged and said — and I wrote this down because it sounded so tidy — "any inequality is fine as long as it's to the benefit of the least well off, that's basically Rawls." The room nodded, the vote went through, and I've been chewing on it ever since.

Here's what I actually want, because I'm not a philosopher and I don't need Rawls 101. That one sentence strikes me as doing a lot of quiet work, and I suspect several quite different claims are hiding inside it. Can you lay them out as a menu — from the reading that's almost trivially true or definitional, through the ones that are substantive and genuinely defensible, to any that are just false — and then tell me which of them the consultant would actually need in order to get his conclusion that nobody should worry about the drivers who lose out? Somewhere in there I'd like the moving parts spelled out: "benefit" compared to what baseline, and to whom (real individuals, or some category like the unskilled), whether we're talking about a necessary condition that any inequality has to meet or a rule for choosing among whole arrangements, whether it's a constraint on institutions or on people, and whether the whole thing is meant as a test you could actually run on a policy or just a slogan about what fairness is. Someone also told me there's a real distinction between the difference principle and plain maximin, and between the general and special versions of the conception of justice, and maybe a lexical variant where you've first got to max out the worst off before anything else even counts — I honestly can't tell if that's just scholarly throat-clearing or if it changes what the sentence can mean, so say something about that too if it matters.

Keep it as a list I can actually bring back to the consultant, roughly ordered from toothless to radical, and flag for each reading whether it's the one people usually have in mind when they trot the line out to win an argument. No need for bibliographies or page numbers — I won't be able to check them anyway and neither will you, so just give me the conceptual map.
