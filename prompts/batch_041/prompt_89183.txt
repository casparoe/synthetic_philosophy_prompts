Background: I'm in a political theory / comparative politics reading group and we've been fighting about "democratic backsliding" for months with zero movement. Roughly three camps:

- Some of us want the concept kept minimal and procedural: if elections still happen and the opposition can still win, that isn't backsliding into authoritarianism, it's just bad politics. On this view Hungary, Poland, Turkey and the US are different animals and lumping them is the error.
- Others point at the same cases and say the interesting thing is exactly the grey zone: you don't need a coup. You pack the constitutional court, capture the media through friendly owners, rewrite the constitution and the cardinal laws that need supermajorities, purge the civil service after a failed coup attempt — and you're out of democracy without ever crossing a visible line. On this view the minimalists' threshold is precisely what the would-be autocrat exploits.
- A third group thinks the label is a partisan cudgel: applied to right-wing populists who win elections, not to centre-left governments that centralise power, and it quietly assumes liberal democracy is history's resting point, so any move away is "back" rather than just "different."

We also cant agree on who decides — the country's own voters, its courts, the EU, or the index-builders who turn it into a number.

Here's my hypothesis, and I want it tested rather than confirmed: the impasse isn't really about Hungary or Poland. It's that one side is answering a graded question (how much democratic quality was lost?), another a categorical one (did the regime type change?), and a third a normative one about what the concept is for — and we're all using one word for three questions.
