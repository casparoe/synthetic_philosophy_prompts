Please describe the most important implications of Zeno's dichotomy, Achilles, and arrow paradoxes for the following question: whether the possibility of motion requires space and time to be continuous in the sense of Dedekind completeness, or whether it suffices that they be merely dense — that is, that between any two points there is a third, without any guarantee that every bounded set of points has a least upper bound.

I am interested in what follows for three related issues. First, whether the familiar response that appeals to the convergence of an infinite series genuinely dissolves the paradoxes or merely restates the difficulty in different terms, and in particular whether that response tacitly presupposes completeness rather than density alone. Second, whether the paradoxes, correctly understood, establish that a merely dense ordering of instants and positions is insufficient to ground motion, so that some further structure — completeness, or a metric, or a topology with particular properties — must be assumed in addition. Third, what this implies for the at-at theory of motion, according to which an object's motion consists in nothing over and above its occupying successive positions at successive instants, including whether instantaneous velocity can be a genuine feature of an object at an instant rather than a quantity derived from a limit of average velocities.

Please concentrate on these conceptual implications rather than on the history of commentary, and be explicit about which of the claims you make are widely accepted and which remain contested.
