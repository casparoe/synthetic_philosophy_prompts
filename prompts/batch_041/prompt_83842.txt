For the better part of a year I have been writing a novel about a theoretical physicist who, late in her career, comes to suspect that the universe is indifferent to her equations — that the regularities she has spent a lifetime cataloguing are simply what keeps happening, with nothing behind them that could be said to insist on their continuing. My research for the book has drawn me into a long-running dinner-table argument with a friend who calls himself a Humean about laws of nature, and last week I described to him the scene I am trying to write: a character asks, of some law, what makes it hold — why it is not merely an enormous coincidence that has so far held without exception — and my friend cut the question off with one sentence, delivered as though it settled the matter: there are no necessary connections between distinct existences. I have since gathered that the line is a kind of watchword among philosophers of his stripe, and that it was doing a great deal of work for him in that moment, but I did not have the presence of mind to press him on what exactly it was doing. I would rather not put the sentence into my novel until I understand what I would be putting in it.

So what I want is a menu of the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered roughly from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each. What kind of necessity is being denied — logical, metaphysical, nomic, causal, or the merely psychological compulsion a thinker feels when one idea follows another? What are the "distinct existences" supposed to be: events, properties, facts, substances? Is the claim metaphysical, or epistemological, or semantic, or a claim about our modal concepts, or all four at once — and does it differ between them? Is it a thesis about possibility, to the effect that anything can be recombined with anything else, or a thesis about explanation, to the effect that the regularities admit of no further explanation, or a thesis about dependence, to the effect that the laws are fixed once the non-modal facts are fixed? Does it deny that laws are necessary, or only deny that they "govern" — and could someone accept the sentence while still insisting that laws are more than regularities, or reject it while still thinking that the picture of laws pushing things around is a bad metaphor? Which of these readings are platitudes no one would contest, which are substantive and defensible, which are outright false, and which is the one my friend most likely meant?

Finally, and this matters most for the book: which of those readings would actually license treating my character's question — what makes the law hold? — as confused rather than merely hard? My physicist's whole arc turns on whether the denial is an epistemological point (there is more, but we cannot reach it) or a metaphysical one (there is nothing more to reach), and on whether it leaves any room at all for the idea that the regularities are, in some sense, held in place. Please keep the answer self-contained: I want the claims themselves, laid out as a set of options I can weigh, not pointers to who said what or where to read about it.
