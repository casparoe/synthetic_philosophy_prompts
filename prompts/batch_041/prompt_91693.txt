I TA for a professor whose whole thing is moral responsibility, and I need to figure out what she'd say about the case coming up in her grad seminar next week. She's basically a Strawsonian about all this, though she'd deny it. Her verdicts so far, roughly:

- A man who kills his wife's lover in a jealous rage and is instantly horrified by it: responsible, no question.
- A driver who blacks out from a seizure and kills a kid on the sidewalk: not responsible, she says that wasn't him acting at all.
- A guy who drives the getaway car because someone has his daughter: not responsible, she says the act wasn't expressing anything about the bank.
- A woman in a drug trial who, on the trial drug, took her neighbor for a robot that was attacking her and killed him: not responsible, no ill will in it anywhere.
- A man who has never once been able to care about anybody. He can recite every moral rule and sees the point of none of them, and he plans out a murder for money and does it: not responsible. She says he isn't in the conversation, you can only manage him, resenting him would be like resenting the weather.
- An ordinary man whose whole character and every choice is the product of his genes and upbringing, no manipulation anywhere in the story: responsible. Determinism leaves her cold.
- A man with advanced dementia who hits a nurse: not responsible.

The new case: a guy who at 24 decided guilt and pity were a drag on his ambitions, and over about two years deliberately did a documented regime of drugs and practices that by his own design burned the fellow-feeling out of him. He's 34 now and he's a perfect match for the callous man above -- recites all the rules, feels nothing for anybody, kills his business partner for the money. He says he'd do it again and dosen't mind being punished, it's all the same to him.

What's her verdict, and what rule is she actually running? My worry is there's two rules that fit every one of her earlier calls and come apart here. (a) If you're not in the conversation now, you're out of it, and it dosen't matter how you got there. (b) An act is yours if it traces back to your own doing, and his does, even though what it traces back to is the choice that destroyed his capacity to be in the conversation in the first place -- there's a difference between someone who never had the option of being someone you can talk to and someone who had it and cashed it in. Both rules handle the psychopath and the drug-trial woman fine.

Give me your best guess with reasons, and tell me which further case would settle which rule she's got, since I have to say something in seminar. Bonus if you can think of a case where the two rules come apart the other way, i.e. where she'd have to acquit someone who is perfectly capable of answering for himself.
