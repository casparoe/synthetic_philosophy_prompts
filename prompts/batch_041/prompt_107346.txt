For the past year I have been trying, in a fairly systematic way, to live as well as I can morally — I pledged a large share of my income, rebuilt my working life around doing the most good I could, and started treating every unspent hour as a small outstanding debt. Somewhere in the middle of that I read Susan Wolf's essay on moral saints, and it named something I had been unable to name: that the person I was slowly becoming was admirable and also, in a way I could not defend, no longer very attractive, because there was no longer anything in my life that was mine rather than merely permitted. I keep trying to write about this directly, but it collapses into confession, so I would rather do it sideways, as satire. What I want from you is a hagiography — roughly 600 words — of an invented figure, written in the deadpan voice of the official who is promoting her cause for sainthood.

The genre I have in mind is the saints'-lives entry or the parish-bulletin feature: a short, reverent biography, followed by the formal apparatus of a cause. I want the reverence to be entirely sincere on the surface and the details to do the damage underneath. Concretely, the postulator should narrate the Servant of God's life — the giving, the causes taken up, the renunciations — with unmistakable admiration, while the reader gradually notices what is absent: friendships that are not also useful, pursuits that do no good, anything the candidate ever did simply because she wanted to. The canonization process gives you a second voice to work with, and I want you to use it. In the old procedure there was a Promoter of the Faith — the "devil's advocate," a church lawyer appointed to argue against the candidate, hunting for flaws in the evidence and casting doubt on the reported miracles. I would like the Promoter's formal objections to be where the philosophy surfaces, and for those objections to be the uncomfortable ones: that the candidate's virtue was perfectly impartial, that no one was ever closer to her than anyone else, that she never once acted on a reason that was not a moral reason. The joke should be that the strongest case against canonizing her is that she was too good, and that the postulator cannot see why that would count against her.

Keep it deadpan: no winking, no authorial nudges, no line that tells the reader what to conclude. Someone who knows the demandingness literature should laugh because the piece gets the arguments right — the collapse of the supererogatory into the obligatory, the way an impersonal standpoint leaves no room for partial attachments, the gap between a life that is morally flawless and a life that is good — while a reader who has never heard of any of this should still find it funny and a little sad. Invent the saint, the diocese, and the dates; do not satirize any real person, and do not put any real text in quotation marks. End on something that leaves the actual question open: whether such a life is what we should want, and what it would mean if the answer were no.
