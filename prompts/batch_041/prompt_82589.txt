I have been arguing with a colleague in economic history and have got myself into a corner I cannot argue my way out of. The quarrel is about Malthus. My colleague's line is that Malthus was not refuted, only early: that the checks he identified — famine, epidemic, war — did operate wherever the escape routes of emigration, agricultural improvement and, above all, the demographic transition were closed off, and that the theory's later career, through the development literature of the mid-twentieth century and the neo-Malthusian alarmism that followed, represents a series of legitimate refinements rather than a retreat dressed up as progress. I want to test that claim with a proper apparatus rather than trading intuitions, and the apparatus I want to use is Lakatos's account of scientific research programmes — hard core, protective belt, and the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem-shifts — run over the Malthusian episode, and then turned back on itself to see what the episode does to Lakatos.

Anchor the analysis on one dated episode rather than letting it float across two centuries. In 1980 Paul Ehrlich and Julian Simon formalised a wager: Ehrlich and his colleagues chose five industrial metals — chromium, copper, nickel, tin and tungsten — and, on paper, bought two hundred dollars' worth of each at the prices of 29 September 1980, with 29 September 1990 as the payoff date; if the inflation-adjusted value of the basket rose above the thousand dollars staked, Simon would pay the difference, and if it fell, Ehrlich would. Over that decade the world's population grew by more than eight hundred million, the largest decade of growth in recorded history, and every one of the five metals fell in real price; Ehrlich mailed Simon a cheque in October 1990. Treat that as the test case and do the mapping carefully. What exactly is the Malthusian hard core — the ratio of subsistence growth to population growth, the postulate of diminishing returns to a fixed factor, or the behavioural claim about procreation — and which of the auxiliary hypotheses (fixed agricultural technique, closed economy, absent or expensive contraception, the treatment of common-pool resources as unpriced) sit in the protective belt and were revised? Of those revisions, which increased the programme's content and which merely saved the phenomena? Then the awkward part: is 'right but early' a substantive claim with empirical content, or does an open-ended time horizon make the core immune to refutation by construction? Say precisely what Simon's victory falsified and what it left untouched, and adjudicate the standard post-mortems — the bet used the wrong commodities; prices do not measure ecological scarcity; depletion and externality never show up in a spot price; and, most interestingly, that the window was simply unlucky, since the same metals had risen sharply from 1950 to 1975 and would have risen again over a sufficiently long later period. Are these the ordinary Duhemian complications any research programme is entitled to absorb, or the signature of a degenerating shift?

Finally, run the lens the other way, because I suspect the case is not as tidy for Lakatos as it looks. If a programme's characteristic predictions are indexed to an unspecified future, the methodology may have no principled way to distinguish one that is early from one that is dead, and its verdict can only ever be delivered in retrospect — which is exactly what is in dispute. If you think a rival account handles the case better — Popperian falsification with the auxiliary assumptions made explicit, or a Bayesian treatment of a prediction so vague and self-adjusting that almost no observation could disconfirm it — then say so and say why, and be willing to conclude that Malthus's defenders are making a coherent move or that they are not. I do not want a reading list or citations; argue from the structure of the case. Give me one continuous argument of no more than nine hundred words, opening with a single sentence that states your verdict on the 'right but early' claim outright, and closing with the one feature of the episode that your chosen framework still cannot accommodate.
