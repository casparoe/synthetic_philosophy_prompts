A colleague of mine who has taught this material for years has settled into a stable pattern of verdicts on trolley cases. Diverting a runaway trolley away from five workers onto a siding where it will kill one: permissible. Pushing a passer-by off a footbridge into its path so that his body stops the trolley: impermissible. Detonating a charge that brings down a wall, burying one trapped worker who could not have been rescued in any case, where the rubble then blocks the trolley and saves the five: permissible. She permits the diversion even when the lone worker is a child, and the order in which I put the cases to her makes no difference.

Next case: the trolley is bearing down on five workers. A man is standing on a footbridge above the track. From a control booth a hundred yards away she can open a hatch beneath him; he will fall into the trolley's path, his body will stop the trolley, and he will be killed. She never touches him, need not be near him, and has no wish for him to die.

Predict her verdict. Then say which rule she is in fact running — a strict intention-based doctrine of double effect, a rule turning on whether the victim's body is used as a means whatever is intended, a rule turning on whether the killing is done with her own hands and at close range, or a rule-consequentialist test of which act-type generally does less harm — which you think it is and why, and which further case would best separate the candidates that remain. If no single rule fits her record, say which verdict is the outlier. Say how confident you are and single out the claim you are least sure of.
