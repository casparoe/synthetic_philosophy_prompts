I am assembling a counterexample to the rule of thumb that when an aggregate comparison and the subgroup comparisons disagree, the disaggregated comparison is the one to trust. Design a case of Simpson's paradox that runs in the opposite direction from the usual illustration: the pooled comparison is the causally correct one, and every subgroup comparison points the wrong way.

Specifications. Use a realistic cohort setting — a treatment, an outcome, and a variable used to partition the sample — and give concrete counts, so that the reversal falls out of the arithmetic rather than being asserted. All subgroup comparisons must favor one conclusion and the pooled comparison the other, with the pooled comparison being the one that reflects the treatment's actual effect. Do not reuse the kidney-stone treatment example, the Berkeley graduate-admissions example, or the low-birth-weight smoking paradox.

After the numbers, supply the rest of the analysis. Draw the causal structure the counts realize, name the single feature that generates the reversal and explain why it produces opposite signs at the two levels of analysis, and say what happens to the paradox if that feature is removed or if the sample is partitioned on a different variable. Close with the strongest objection available to someone who insists the subgroup comparisons must be right, and assess how much of the case survives it. The whole answer must be self-contained: no references, citations, or appeals to literature.
