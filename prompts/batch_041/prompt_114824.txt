Last night I lost a long argument with a friend who insists that anyone who can do arithmetic knows that voting is a waste of an afternoon, and I could not articulate a reply. It happens that next week I am recording a podcast episode with a decision theorist who argues that the paradox of voting is an artifact of modelling the act as a lottery on one's own decisive ballot: he says the relevant quantity is the probability that the margin of victory falls inside the band of votes a single ballot could plausibly move, which for any real electorate is far from negligible. I do not want him simply to restate this. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press him where the literature presses, with at least one each on (i) whether the band-of-votes quantity is genuinely the one that bears on the decision, and what fixes the width of the band, since if uncertainty widens it the argument threatens to swallow its own conclusion; (ii) why his reasoning does not prove too much, given that every one of my fellow citizens has, by his lights, the same reason to vote, and a reason that everyone holds equally cannot be the operative reason for any particular person's acting; and (iii) whether what he is offering is really an assurance-game or rule-consequentialist argument in disguise, in which case the rationality of my vote turns on what others are likely to do rather than on any calculation of mine. Add one question on why he thinks the expressive account of voting, on which one votes to affirm a commitment rather than to change an outcome, is the worse explanation, and one on whether his view implies I should stay home in a safe seat or spend the afternoon on get-out-the-vote work instead. For each question, supply the follow-up that answers the reply he is most likely to give.
