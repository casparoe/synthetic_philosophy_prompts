OK so I've somehow agreed to run a guest session in my friend's MFA seminar next month — fiction students, fifty minutes, a whiteboard, no slides. The topic I volunteered for is the problem of dirty hands in politics. My angle is that it'll help them write political characters who arent just saints or cardboard cynics, so the moral machinery actually has to land.

Dont explain dirty hands to me, I basically get it. What I want is a plan for how to teach it. So:

First, the order. What has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them, and what depends on what? I have a hunch the very first move is getting them to feel the difference between doing wrong and doing harm, but tell me if thats off or if something else has to come first.

Second, the carrying example. I want one case to build the whole thing on, and I do NOT want the ticking bomb — its been done to death and it drags in torture and emergencies and they'll fight about that instead of the actual issue. What I want the example to have: a specific person who gets wronged (not just an aggregate of harm), a genuinely good outcome that really does depend on the wrong, and no clean third option. Tell me what the example has to have and lack, and whether I should invent one on the spot or use something they'll half-recognize.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they'll show up, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. My guesses: they'll arrive thinking dirty hands just means politicians are liars; they'll think its the same as the lesser-evil / utilitarian math; and some of them will think the guilt is just hypocritical theater. Also I bet a few will collapse it into a moral dilemma or into moral luck, and I want to know how to keep those separate without delivering a lecture.

The thing I actually care about, and the reason I want to teach it at all, is the leftover — the bit where the good outcome doesnt wipe out the wrong, where theres a remainder the character carries and can never pay off. I want that to be the emotional spine of the session. So build the sequence so it lands, and tell me where in it the remainder should first appear. And I want to end up on the weird double-bind — that we seem to want the person to do the wrong thing AND to be damaged by it, so that feeling bad is somehow the proof theyre still worth trusting. Thats the part I find genuinely strange, and I want them to feel it too.

Then: one question or one tiny exercise at the end that shows me whether they got it, not just whether they nodded along.

And what should I openly refuse to cover at this level — and say so out loud — so I dont get sucked into the scholarly fight about whether the whole idea is even coherent?

Write it in American English, thanks.
