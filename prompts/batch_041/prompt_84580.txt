I teach a third-year seminar on philosophy of history and I'm writing the final this week, and the topic that's giving me grief is the covering-law model vs. the narrative view of explanation. The reason it's suddenly personal: I've spent the last month going through a box of my grandad's letters from the war for a family book, and I keep writing sentences I can't justify. "The letter of the 14th is the one where he starts lying to her." "That's the last cheerful one before he was captured." Nobody alive on the 14th could have known that letter was either of those things. What it *was* seems to depend on stuff that hadn't happened yet. Which seems to make a nonsense of the idea that there's an event sitting there fully formed, waiting for the historian to bring along a law and some initial conditions and deduce it.

So that's the question I want to set. Trouble is, every version I write turns into "compare and contrast Hempel with the narrativists," and the students hand back a memorised list of the four standard objections and I learn nothing about whether they understand any of it. I want the question to make them do the diagnosing instead of reciting the diagnosis.

Here's the shape I'm after, though make it better than this. The standard objections to the covering-law model are (a) the barometer that falls before the storm, where the regularity is real but the "explanation" obviously isn't one; (b) the symmetry of explanation and prediction, which fails for a slump or a revolution; (c) the fact that historians never state laws, and the ones you can supply are truisms — starving people normally do eventually revolt — so the force of the explanation seems to come from the deviation from a norm, not from the law. Are those objections to the covering-law model's account of how an explanation is *structured*, or to its account of *what it is that gets explained*? Then the thought experiment I want them to chew on: imagine a record that captures everything that happens at the instant it happens and nothing that happens later — a perfect chronicle. Does that record contain any history? If not, what does history have that the chronicle can't, and can the covering-law model accommodate that, or is it commited to saying the chronicle is the complete description of what there is to explain?

Then the crux I actually want them to decide, because it's what I'm stuck on: is the fight between the two views a fight about the world — about whether human affairs are law-governed — or a fight about texts, about what a piece of writing has to look like in order to explain something? If it's the second, is either side really disagreeing with the other, or are they answering two different questions and just calling the other's answer "not an explanation"? And the follow-up I think will sort the A's from the B's: can the narrative view get by with nothing law-like at all, or does it quietly need something like a norm ("people normally do X") to make a story followable? If it does need one, has the narrativist accidentally conceded the covering-law point, or has the covering-law model just been shown to be trivially true?

What I need from you: the exam question itself, pitched for a 90-minute final, then a model answer of about 700 words, then a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points with the marks for each, and for each point a one-liner on how students typically fluff it. I don't want the question answerable by someone who's just read a summary of Hempel, so have the marking scheme say explicitly what a B answer looks like and what has to be added to make it an A. Don't ask them for citations or further reading, and don't assume they know any texts by name — they only get what's in the question.
