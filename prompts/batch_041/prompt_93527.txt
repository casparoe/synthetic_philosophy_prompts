My uncle Ray died in March at 74, and somehow I got voluntered to give the eulogy at the memorial in two weeks. Ray spent most of his working life as a press officer and then as one of those crisis-communications consultants companies hire after they've done something bad, to say things that are true in a way that makes reporters write the story you want. He was genuinely proud of it. He had a rule he'd lay out at Thanksgiving after a couple glasses of wine: he never once, in thirty-odd years, said anything he believed to be false. But, he'd add, the truth is a big bag and you get to pick which rocks you pull out of it, and a person who hands you only the rocks you didn't ask for isn't doing you a favor. He said the second thing was the honest profession and the first was for cowards. My mother has always thought that was a rationalization. I honestly dont know if she's right.

Here's my problem. The room is going to split three ways. His sisters and my cousins, who loved him and couldn't care less about any of this. A pack of the reporters and flacks he worked with, who know exactly what he did and half of whom still can't stand him — including one woman in the third row whose career he ended with a single sentence that I'm fairly sure was true. And a little knot of people from the ethics seminar he audited for fun in his sixties, who are going to sit there auditing me, listening for whether I say something false or merely something misleading, because that was the fight he'd start at dinner. I want a eulogy that is, as far as I can manage, entirely true — Ray would have wanted that, and the reporters would smell it on me if it wasn't — but that still lets the room feel what we lost. So I need to actually understand the distinction he claimed between lying and misleading, and whether it survives contact with a real case: whether saying something true that you know will lead someone to a false belief is a genuinely different kind of act from saying something false, or the same act with better manners. And I need to know what to do about the fact that the one true sentence that would most honor the woman in the third row is the one that would wreck the room for everyone else, and the family doesn't even know he was the one who said it.

So please write the eulogy — about five minutes, meant to be spoken. No jargon for the cousins, no hagiography for the reporters, and enough substance that the seminar table can tell I took the lying-versus-misleading thing seriously instead of just quoting something warm. One of the seminar people used to call the middle case — the true statement aimed at a false belief — paltering, if that helps you name what I'm circling. And I'd like it to end in a way that's honest about him without me having to pretend the third row isn't there. If you think the honest thing to do is to mislead the room a little, say so and write it that way; I'd rather know.
