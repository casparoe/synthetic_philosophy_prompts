I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind, and I want a single essay question built on Hofstadter's careenium image. I'll gloss the image here so the question can stand on its own: imagine a large table covered in countless tiny magnetic balls that careen off one another and off the walls; their collisions throw up recurring higher-level clumps, and from a zoomed-out vantage point those clumps can be read as registering and storing patterns from the world outside the table, with the boundary's responsiveness to that world doing the input work and, in the extended version, the clumps' arrangements doing the output work. Hofstadter's own question is which level does the shoving — the balls, or the clumps they compose.

Most of my students can recite the standard mental-causation debate on command, so I don't want a question they can answer by summarizing the exclusion argument. I want one that forces them to say what would have to be true of the substrate dynamics for the clump-level description to earn genuine causal and explanatory standing rather than being a convenient redescription, and to say whether the careenium is simply the exclusion problem in billiard-ball dress or whether something about it changes the shape of the problem. Answerable in about 75 minutes.

Produce the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme totalling 20 marks that names the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific moves that should cost marks — and, for each penalty, the actual mistake it catches. The scheme must clearly separate an answer that merely says both descriptions are true so both levels are causes from one that distinguishes explanatory autonomy from causal autonomy and then defends a position.

Begin with the exam question itself. No preamble, no restating of this brief, no commentary on your design choices.
