I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and I'm at the reporting stage of a piece about how we talk when automated systems fail to act: the braking assist that doesn't brake, the triage tool that leaves a patient in the wrong queue, the monitoring service that stays quiet through an outage. Over a month of interviews I've lost count of the times someone has ended a conversation with me by saying, more or less verbatim, that you cannot cause harm by doing nothing. A robotics researcher said it at a conference reception when I asked whether his system had caused a death by defaulting to inaction; a hospital administrator offered it as a defense of a written policy of non-intervention; a commentator used it to wave away a wrongful-death suit. What interests me is that the people on both sides of these exchanges agree about the facts — they agree that had the system braked, had the patient been moved, had the alarm sounded, the harm would not have occurred — and then disagree about whether anything at all has been said. My editor wants the column to be about that sentence rather than about any single case, and I'd rather not write the usual piece in which a philosopher is quoted saying that causation is complicated.

So please act as a philosopher of causation — someone who works on the metaphysics of causation but is equally at home in the way causation is argued about in law, medicine and engineering — and give me a rigorous disambiguation. I don't want a verdict on my cases; I want the menu of distinct precise claims that the sentence could be expressing, arranged from the trivial to the radical. Begin with a short brainstorm of the space before you fix your taxonomy, and think carefully about the fact that the sentence looks like a claim about the world but may be doing something else entirely. Some readings will be about the ontology of omissions and absences; some about what our words mean; some about which of many true causal facts we single out to call the cause; some about norms, expectations and defaults, and whether an omission is even identifiable as an omission apart from some standard the agent failed to meet; some about legal duty, which may turn out to be a way of getting liability without causation rather than a claim about causation; and some about whether preventing someone else's prevention counts as causing anything. I've also been told, by someone visibly pleased with himself, that an omission is only an omission relative to some expectation — tell me whether that is a deep point or an empty one, and whether it belongs on the menu.

For each reading I'd like to know whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false; whether it is compatible with the but-for facts everyone in my interviews agrees on; and whether it would actually do the work it was being made to do in the exchanges I describe. I suspect at least one of the readings is true as a remark about language and is being used as though it were a claim about the world, and I'd like you to say which. Close with the two or three questions I should put to the next person who says it, and with your own view of which single reading best explains why the sentence is so durable. No citations, please — I can't check them and I won't print them — and no reading list.
