For the past year my closest friend and I have held a stable disagreement about something that matters greatly to both of us, and neither of us has moved. When I described the standoff to two friends with far more training in decision theory than I have, they ended up arguing with each other about what the theorem on agreeing to disagree actually implies for a case like mine. I could not follow all of it, and I would like a careful adjudication of who was right at each step. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

Anna: There is a theorem showing that two agents who are rational, and who have common knowledge of one another's rationality and beliefs, cannot persistently disagree. So if you and your friend have both reflected and still differ, one of you must be mishandling the evidence. Disagreement that survives reflection is a defect, not a normal state.

Ben: That is not what the theorem says. What it shows is narrower: if two agents share a prior, are both Bayesian, and their posteriors about a given event are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal. It does not say that rational people cannot disagree; agents with different information disagree constantly and both may be perfectly rational. You have inflated the result.

Anna: Granted. But the surviving core is still strong: once each of us knows the other's view, either the common-knowledge conditions fail or one of us is in error. Persistent disagreement after full disclosure is a signal that something has gone wrong.

Ben: The conditions fail so completely that the result is inert. It needs a shared prior, and nothing in individual rationality forces two people to share one; that is an added assumption, not a theorem. It needs common knowledge of each other's rationality, which neither of us can have about the other with certainty, let alone have as common knowledge. And it is formulated over a fixed set of possible states; it is silent when the disagreement concerns what the possibilities are, or what matters. So it cannot bear on your case.

Anna: The shared prior is defensible. Trace each person's beliefs back to their evidence, and any difference in prior should itself be traceable to a difference in information; absent a difference in information, there is no rational basis for different priors. That is why the shared prior can be treated as a constraint of rationality.

Ben: That is precisely the step I reject. It is a substantive thesis about the nature of evidence, not a consequence of Bayesian rationality, and many hold that two agents can rationally keep different priors while agreeing on everything they have observed. Without it, agreeing to disagree is not merely possible but ordinary. And even granting it, the theorem speaks only to the probability of a well-defined event; it says nothing if your disagreement is partly about which considerations count in the first place.

Anna: Still, as a diagnostic it earns its keep: it directs you to ask whether you truly have common knowledge, whether you truly have the same evidence, and whether what looks like a factual dispute is a difference in values in disguise.

Ben: That is a grand way of saying that you may have different information or different priors. I do not need a theorem for that, and casting it as one implies a precision the situation does not have.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each claim, say who is right and why, and flag any place where someone reaches the right conclusion for the wrong reason, or the wrong conclusion for a right reason. Close by saying what both of them overlook about how the theorem bears on a real, ongoing disagreement like mine, and who, if either, holds the stronger overall position. Keep the entire answer under 700 words.
