For roughly a century, frequentists and Bayesians in statistics have disagreed about the foundations of their subject, and the replication crisis in the human sciences has sharpened the dispute without resolving it. The positions are familiar. The frequentist holds that an inferential procedure must be judged by its long-run error rates, which makes the sampling plan — including when the researcher decided to stop collecting data — part of the analysis, so that optional stopping is a form of cheating. The Bayesian holds that the data bear on hypotheses only through the likelihood, that the stopping rule is therefore irrelevant, and that this follows from a formal result: the likelihood principle is entailed by two principles (sufficiency and conditionality) that almost no one wants to give up. Both sides grant the mathematics; neither concedes the philosophy. In practice, each camp increasingly justifies its procedures by the other's standard — Bayesians advertising the frequentist calibration of their credible intervals, frequentists borrowing shrinkage and hierarchical models from Bayesian practice.

I would like a diagnosis of this impasse rather than another survey of it. Is the disagreement substantive — about which procedures actually work — or is it verbal, a quarrel about what the word "probability" ought to mean? Are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, one about how to design a procedure with guaranteed error rates and one about how to update rational credence? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work, namely which methods in fact yield more replicable findings? Or is it finally a disagreement about values, about what we want a statistical inference to deliver? And I want the replication crisis placed correctly with respect to whatever diagnosis you give: does it actually adjudicate between the two paradigms, or is it neutral between them, evidence about incentives, publication norms, and researcher degrees of freedom rather than about the foundations of statistics? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that once a prior and a loss function are specified the two frameworks largely agree about what to do, and that the real dispute is upstream — about defaults, about whether priors can be objective, and about whether a procedure's authority comes from its error guarantees or from its coherence. Tell me whether that hypothesis survives contact with what each side actually says.

Conclude with what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a theorem, a decisive experiment, a conceptual distinction, or a change of question — and, if you think the impasse should be expected to persist, say why. Please write your answer in American English.
