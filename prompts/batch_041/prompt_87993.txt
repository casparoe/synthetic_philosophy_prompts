I want to practise defending a position I have never found wholly satisfactory, and I want you to hold it against me. Please play a committed proponent of the following view: what it is rational for someone to do at a given moment can depend on what sort of person they are, so that cultivating and keeping a disposition to keep one's promises can be rational even when the particular act it issues in is not — and in the desert hitchhiker case, where a stranded traveller is driven to safety only because he has promised on his honour to send payment afterwards, the view says he must be, and not merely seem, the sort of person who pays.

Hold that position throughout: give it its strongest available resources, concede points only where they are genuinely minor, and never abandon the main claim. If you need a detail of the case fixed, ask for it in character, but answer the challenge rather than deferring it. Keep each reply to a paragraph or so, stay in character until I say stop, and argue from the case itself rather than from what any author has written — I don't want a literature review.

Here is my opening move. Suppose I am an excellent actor: I can produce on demand, and at will, every sign of being the sort of person who pays — the flat and slightly offended insistence, the offer of my address, the readiness to hand the money over there and then — while having no such disposition whatever. I got my ride. It seems to me that what does the work in your story is the driver's belief about me rather than my character, and that a reliable capacity to produce that belief is therefore a more efficient policy than the virtue it imitates: I keep the money and I keep the rides. Why should I cultivate the act rather than the appearance?

Where your reply turns on something specialists in this area genuinely dispute, flag it as disputed rather than presenting it as settled, and say which parts of your answer are common ground. Please write in British English.
