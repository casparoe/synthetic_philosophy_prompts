I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the epistemology of long-run moral choice, and I help run a reading group that pairs short fiction with hard problems in ethics. I need a story for the session on what is usually called consequentialist cluelessness, and I would like you to write it.

Let me set out the problem as I actually want it engaged, because the standard summary is thinner than the thing. If the value of an action is the value of its total consequences, and consequences ramify without end, then the quantity we are supposed to maximize is dominated by terms we cannot reach. Not merely unknown: unknown in a way that supports no probability either. For most acts we cannot say whether the distant effects are net good or net bad, large or negligible, and no amount of research closes the gap, because the chain runs through too many contingencies. The interesting part is what follows. This is often taken to license paralysis, on the reasoning that if the ledger is unreadable then nothing is justified. But cluelessness is symmetric; the alternative to acting is just as unreadable, so the problem cannot by itself favor inaction. At the same time, the short-run consequences we do know seem to lose their force the moment we concede that the long-run terms exist and could swamp them. I want a story that stays inside that squeeze rather than dissolving it.

So here is the setting, which I would rather you use than invent your own oracle. In this world, prediction of the future is exact and cheap, but only to a horizon of roughly ten years. Past the horizon the readings are, provably and by general agreement, statistically independent of what actually happens; the limit is a property of the world rather than of the instrument, as settled and as unexciting as thermodynamics. People have lived with this long enough to build customs, professions and institutions around it. One of those professions is the reader: someone trained to run a proposed action through the instrument, interpret the readable window, and advise. Their professional norms are the thing I most want the story to expose.

Turn the story on one decision of real weight: a public proposal in a city whose consequences inside the window are overwhelmingly and unambiguously good, and whose consequences outside it are noise. The reader of the story should feel both halves of the squeeze — that the good inside the window is real and must not be waved away, and that someone who has understood the limit cannot simply point at it and stop. Give me at least two further characters who respond differently from one another and from the protagonist: one who manufactures numbers where none are available, one whose rigor has hardened into a refusal to act at all. Let the protagonist land on a working rule for acting under cluelessness, a compromise rather than a discovery, and show the rule being applied, then show what it costs. Include one scene in which the protagonist has to give reasons to someone with power over them, and let those reasons be visibly incomplete. End without confirming that the protagonist was right; I want my group to be able to argue it both ways.

Constraints, since I have seen this go wrong. Fifteen hundred to two thousand words. No character may explain the philosophical problem or use its vocabulary; the theory is my job at the reading group, not the story's. No time travel, no simulation twist, no revelation that the instrument is conscious, benevolent or malignant. Do not let the far future turn out to be knowable after all, and do not let the noise carry a hidden message. The physics stays background, like gravity. Give the story a title and give me nothing else — no synopsis, no notes on the theory, no afterword.
