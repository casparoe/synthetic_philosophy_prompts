I'm the TA for a third-year metaphysics course that grew out of a reading group which started life as an LW-adjacent book club, and this week I'm marking the counterpart-theory question on the problem set. The cover sheet says the question is out of ten, split 3/4/3 across parts (a)–(c); partial credit is given for correct moves even when the conclusion is wrong, and restating the question earns nothing. Here is the question as the students saw it:

'(a) [3] On Lewis's account, what does the counterpart relation relate, and what is it meant to replace? (b) [4] A classmate writes: "Counterpart theory and the standard transworld-identity semantics are notational variants; the counterpart relation is just identity wearing a similarity mask, since Lewis makes it reflexive and pairs each of us off with a counterpart in each world." Assess the claim. (You may find it useful to keep apart the three relations we distinguished in week 4: the accessibility relation between worlds, the similarity ordering that does the work in the semantics for counterfactuals, and the counterpart relation between individuals.) (c) [3] Consider a world W containing exactly one person, P, who resembles the actual Humphrey in all the respects that matter for being Humphrey's counterpart, and who also resembles the actual Nixon in all the respects that matter for being Nixon's counterpart. Is W admissible on Lewis's account, and what does the case show about the slogan that my counterpart is what I would have been had things gone otherwise?'

One answer, transcribed as written: 'Lewis's counterpart theory asks us to give up transworld identity — the idea that one and the same individual is part of more than one world — and to explain de re modal claims instead by a relation of similarity between numerically distinct individuals in different worlds. The counterpart relation is not a thing's relation to itself under another description: it holds between a thing and other things, and it is the relation the translation of quantified modal logic uses to handle the modal operators. So "Humphrey could have won" comes out true, relative to a world and an assignment, just in case some world accessible from ours contains a counterpart of Humphrey who wins. The payoff is that the theory avoids the problem of accidental intrinsics: since nothing is in two worlds, nothing has to be both tall and short, and the apparent contradiction is dissolved into a difference between distinct things rather than a clash of properties in one thing. On the classmate's claim, I think the classmate is essentially right and only the framing is off. Reflexivity is built in — nothing in a world is a counterpart of anything else in that world, but each thing is its own counterpart — and every thing in a world is paired off with its counterpart in the worlds we quantify over, so the relation behaves like an equivalence relation and pairs each thing with exactly one counterpart per world. The counterpart relation is not identity wearing a mask; identity is rather the limiting case of the counterpart relation when the similarity threshold is set to perfect resemblance, which is precisely what the transworld-identity picture tacitly assumes. Indeed, of the three relations we distinguished in week 4, all three are the same device applied to different arguments: the counterpart relation is the similarity ordering over worlds restricted to individuals, so "the nearest world where Humphrey wins" and "the nearest world where Humphrey's counterpart wins" come to the same thing. On the chameleon case, a single individual in W cannot be the counterpart of two different actual people. Because the relation is reflexive and one-to-one, a counterpart relation that ran one-to-many would not be a relation of identity at all, and Lewis needs it to behave like identity for the translation of modal logic to be a translation. So W is not admissible, and the questions about the truth of "Humphrey could have been the one in W who won" and "Nixon could have been the one in W who won" do not arise.'
