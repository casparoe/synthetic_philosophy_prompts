Every year I teach a graduate seminar that sits between metaphysics and the philosophy of science, and for several iterations now I have used the International Astronomical Union's August 2006 resolutions on the definition of a planet as a running case study. The facts are well enough known that I will not rehearse them: a body is to count as a planet only if it orbits the Sun, is massive enough for its own gravity to have made it nearly round, and has cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit; Pluto satisfies the first two conditions and fails the third; the definition is expressly confined to bodies orbiting the Sun and therefore says nothing about exoplanets; and the membership rejected the rival proposal that would have made the dwarf planets a subcategory of planets, which would have preserved Pluto and lengthened the list. Two smaller details matter for what I am about to ask, and my students reliably miss both. First, the definition names Mercury explicitly as a planet even though Mercury is not in hydrostatic equilibrium, so the second criterion is plainly not doing the work the definition advertises. Second, the affair replays an older one: Ceres was announced in 1801 as the missing planet, the tally of planets had reached twenty-three by the middle of the nineteenth century, and the community then reclassified the whole set as asteroids. What I want is not history but a philosophical analysis, run in both directions.

Take the apparatus of verbal disputes and metaontology and ask what it makes of this. Does the quarrel between the dynamical and the geophysical camps look like a verbal dispute in the technical sense — the parties attaching different senses to a shared word, so that the disagreement dissolves once the senses are separated — or is it a case the standard taxonomy handles badly, given that both camps appear to mean the same thing by planet and are arguing about which criterion the word ought to carry? Is that dispute merely verbal, broadly verbal, or a normative disagreement about concept choice that the verbal/non-verbal contrast cannot capture? Bring to bear Carnap's contrast between questions asked within a framework and questions about which framework to adopt, Hirsch's quantifier variance (the thought that rival ontologies may simply be speaking different languages, neither more correct than the other), and Sider's insistence that our best theory can render such questions determinate, and say which of these accounts best fits what actually happened in Prague — and, more revealingly, which of them the episode embarrasses.

Then run the analysis the other way. Metaontologists typically frame their problem around existence questions that no procedure could settle; here a committee settled a classificatory question by vote, and the vote carried costs — textbooks, museum exhibits, public indignation — which suggests that something was at stake that a purely verbal reading would have to explain away. Does the episode favour the view that these disputes are indeterminate and resolved by adopting a language, or the view that some classifications carve the world better than others and the vote was tracking that? Tell me, finally, what the case reveals about the limits of the metaontological framing itself, and name the kind of case — from the history of classification in some other science, or from ontology proper — that would best tell the competing positions apart. My students keep saying that the whole business is just semantics, and I want to be able to say precisely why that is both true and useless.
