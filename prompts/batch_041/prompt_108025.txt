After a career in experimental physics I have spent my retirement reading philosophy, and I have been struck by how little the textbook interpretations of probability resemble the reasoning that actually goes on in a laboratory. I would like you to use the OPERA affair — the September 2011 announcement that neutrinos had reached Gran Sasso roughly sixty nanoseconds sooner than light would have, quoted at six sigma when the discovery convention is five, with the collaboration openly asking the community to find its mistake, and the eventual explanation in a loose fibre-optic link between a GPS receiver and the master clock — as a case study of the three standard interpretations of probability. For frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity in turn, tell me what the quoted significance was actually a statement about, what the collaboration was entitled to conclude from it, and how the wider community's refusal to believe the result looks when reconstructed in that interpretation; then be explicit about where each reading runs out — the reference class for a propensity, the prior over a violation of Lorentz invariance, and the distinction between a random error and a misdescribed apparatus. Finally, reverse the direction: does the episode discriminate among the three, or does it show that they differ only in bookkeeping rather than in what anyone would have done? I followed the history closely at the time and do not need it retold; what I want is the mapping, and a plain account of the places the framework cannot reach.
