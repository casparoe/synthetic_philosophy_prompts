A fictional strategy consultancy, Harrow & Voss, has built a "civilizational resilience" practice and has just circulated a client-facing thought-leadership report titled The Collapse Dividend: Managed Simplification for Complex Organizations. Write that report.

The report is written by consultants for executives: confident, fluent in the vocabulary of frameworks, maturity models, and maturity curves, and entirely without self-awareness. It should not be a parody of bad writing — the prose should be good. The comedy comes from the gap between what the historical and archaeological record actually says and what the firm needs it to say in order to sell an engagement. Where the report states what happened in the past, it must be accurate; where it draws lessons, it should be wrong in precisely the ways a smart, motivated author would be wrong.

The report should contain, in roughly this order: (1) a title and a one-paragraph executive summary that overclaims; (2) a diagnostic instrument — a composite Collapse Index with at least four named indicators (loss of central authority, abandonment of monumental construction, loss of a writing system, demographic decline, or your own equivalents) and a four- or five-stage maturity model that a society is said to pass through; (3) three case studies of about 150 words each — the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya, and Rapa Nui — each scored against the Index and the stage model; (4) a "Myths, Corrected" section; (5) a pull-quote from a named partner at the firm; and (6) a closing list of five takeaways for the C-suite, at least two of which contradict each other, followed by a call to action in which the firm proposes to collapse one of its own practice areas in the name of resilience. The Index should generate at least one verdict that is inconvenient for the argument — a society that by the Index's own lights collapsed while its people prospered, or one that "survived" only in a form the firm would call failure — and the report should repair the anomaly by adding an indicator after the fact.

The material the report must engage with, digest accurately, and then misuse:

- The account on which societies collapse when their investments in complexity yield diminishing marginal returns: complexity as a problem-solving strategy whose costs rise, collapse as rapid simplification rather than extinction, and the implication that for many people living through it the simplification may be an improvement or at least no worse. The report should find this account inspiring and convert it into a recommendation to management.
- The Maya case, where "collapse" names the abandonment of the southern lowland centers in the Terminal Classic while the northern Yucatán and the highlands continued and prospered; where drought reconstructed from regional climate records is now a leading but not exclusive explanation; and where the Maya did not disappear, since millions of Maya people and their languages are still there. The report should use the Maya as a warning about failure to adapt without noticing what this does to its own definition.
- Rapa Nui, where the deforestation is real but the self-inflicted-ecocide story is contested — the Polynesian rat and other causes are implicated in the loss of the palm, and the catastrophic population decline followed European contact and nineteenth-century slave raids and disease rather than preceding them. The report should deploy the ecocide version as its most vivid morality tale, acknowledge the revisionist literature in a single hedging footnote, and then proceed exactly as before.
- The Late Bronze Age, where the destructions of roughly 1200–1150 BCE broke the Hittite empire, destroyed cities such as Ugarit, and burned Mycenaean palaces, while Egypt survived diminished and Assyria and Babylonia largely continued; where the "Sea Peoples" are plausibly as much a symptom as a cause; and where the leading accounts are multi-causal — drought, migration, earthquake, and the failure of an interconnected trade network. The report should extract from this a lesson about diversification, and should use the network-failure framing to argue for exactly the concentration of dependency it warns against.
- The argument, associated with the editors of Questioning Collapse, that "collapse" is often a category imposed from outside, that such narratives do moral work for the present, and that the societies in question mostly changed rather than failed. The report should cite this argument approvingly in its introduction and violate it on every subsequent page.

Do not invent quotations and attribute them to real scholars, and do not put invented words in the mouths of real people; the only direct quotations should come from fictional employees of Harrow & Voss. You may refer to published scholarship in passing, but do not attempt a reference list, and do not guess at dates or titles you are unsure of — if you cannot state a source accurately, describe the position without naming it. The piece should be legible to a reader who knows no archaeology and satisfying to one who does: a specialist should find the satire fair rather than lazy. Never signal the joke. There should be no wink, no ironic aside, no moment at which the firm notices its own incoherence. Roughly 1,000 words, formatted with headings.
