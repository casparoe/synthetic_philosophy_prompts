Two philosophers of probability have spent years arguing past each other about uncertain evidence, and have now agreed to write, jointly, the document their exchange should have produced. One holds that all genuine learning by a Bayesian agent is conditionalization on a proposition the agent comes to accept with certainty, so that an apparent redistribution of credence across a partition, with no proposition becoming certain, is either really conditioning on some higher-order proposition about the agent's own state, or else not learning at all but a change in the agent's input that falls outside the scope of any rule of learning. The other holds that uncertain evidence is a genuine and irreducible category: an agent may redistribute credence across the members of a partition without becoming certain of any of them, and the rule governing that shift takes her prior conditional probabilities given each member of the partition as fixed and reweights them by her new probabilities over the partition, recovering ordinary conditioning when the new distribution over the partition places all its weight on one member.

Draft their joint statement. It should contain the following.

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign: the two rules and the exact relation between them; what the rigidity requirement on the prior conditional probabilities says and what it does not say; the precise statement of what happens when two successive uncertain shifts are made with respect to different partitions, including whether and when the result depends on the order in which they are made; the correct formulation of the diachronic Dutch book argument that has been offered on behalf of the uncertain-evidence rule, stated only as far as its formal conclusion goes; and what the object of an uncertain shift is — a proposition, a partition, or a distribution over a partition. No agreed claim should be so weak that either side's view would survive its denial.

2. A disagreements table. Each row is a single sub-question on which the two still differ. For each row give: the sub-question in neutral wording; each side's own formulation of its answer, written so that the side in question would accept the wording as its own; and a note on whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal, with a reason.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give a case or argument that both sides accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case shows. Consider, and either adopt or replace with better ones, the following candidates: whether the rigidity requirement is a constraint that follows from the concept of evidence or an empirical assumption that can fail; whether avoiding a diachronic Dutch book is a requirement of rationality or merely a property of a coherent policy; and whether the certainty camp's reduction of uncertain evidence to conditioning on a higher-order proposition preserves the content of what is learned.

4. A short adjudication of three cases, each handled separately, with each side's verdict stated in its own terms. (a) A report: an agent's only change is that her credence in the content of a source's report rises, while her estimate of the source's reliability is unchanged and independent — is she updating on uncertain evidence about the report's content, or conditioning on the proposition that the source issued the report? (b) A drift: over an hour, with no proposition she would say she has newly come to believe, an agent's credence in a hypothesis about her immediate surroundings slides upward as a sensation changes — learning, or not? (c) A self-doubting channel: an agent treats a signal as uncertain evidence about a hypothesis, but also treats that same signal as evidence about whether the channel producing it is reliable, so the signal bears at once on the hypothesis and on the trustworthiness of the evidence about the hypothesis — state whether the rigidity requirement has a well-defined application here, what each side says the correct update is, and whether the case forces either side to revise a claim it made in section 1.

5. A closing paragraph identifying any part of the dispute that would dissolve if the two sides settled on a definition, and any part that would survive such agreement.

Write the whole document in the two philosophers' shared voice, as a text both could put their names to.
