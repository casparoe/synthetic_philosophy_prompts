I'm a software engineer by training — no formal philosophy background — and I've somehow ended up as the lay member of my hospital's ethics committee, which has spent the last several months arguing about advance directives. The argument is really about something I can't get my head around: when a competent person writes a directive saying that if she later develops dementia she wants no life-prolonging treatment, and then that later person — now demented, apparently cheerful, and insistent that she wants the antibiotics — refuses, whose word should win? Some of my colleagues talk as though the earlier self has a kind of sovereign authority over the later one, the way a person can bind herself with a Ulysses contract or a smoker can lock away her own cigarettes; others say autonomy is always the autonomy of whoever is here now, and that a person who no longer holds her old values is, for these purposes, someone else. What nags at me is an asymmetry I keep noticing: we let the earlier self bind the later self, but never the reverse, and I can't tell whether that's a principled feature of autonomy or just a bias toward the articulate. I've seen the same structure in self-binding contracts used in addiction treatment, and in arguments over whether a now-content patient can be "harmed" by being kept alive in defiance of what her earlier self wanted.

I'd like to turn this into a structured agenda-setting exercise rather than another round of argument. Suppose a foundation handed me a budget to support roughly four to six researchers for three years on exactly this cluster — precedent autonomy, self-binding, and the authority of the earlier self over the later one — and asked me to recommend which open questions they should take up and in what order. Which questions belong at the top, and why? I care less about the list than about the reasoning: for each, why it is upstream of the other questions rather than downstream of them; whether it's the kind of thing that can actually be resolved by argument in three years or is really a standoff between incompatible starting points; how much it would change clinical practice or the law if it came out one way rather than the other; and what argument or evidence over the next couple of years would move it up or down my ranking. I'd also like to know what you would deliberately deprioritize, including questions that sound weighty but whose answer wouldn't change anything downstream.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single question you'd fund first, and why — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
