I'm a postdoc with a foot in two camps, a theory group and a philosophy department, and next month I'm giving a joint seminar talk defending a claim that I expect to irritate both: that when philosophers of mind and epistemologists appeal to computational intractability in order to argue that Bayesian updating or expected-utility maximization cannot be a norm for finite agents, they are usually appealing to the wrong kind of hardness result, and that once one separates what I have been calling representation-hardness from instance-hardness, a narrower and more defensible thesis survives. The narrower thesis is that for the inference problems at issue, the difficulty is not an artifact of the algorithm, the encoding, or the machine model but is a feature of the problem itself, and that ideal rationality should therefore be defined relative to a resource class rather than as an unconstrained limit, with different resource classes yielding genuinely different normative theories rather than the same theory plus a footnote about running time. The argument has three moves. First, a taxonomy: a problem can be hard in the instance sense (this input defeats every algorithm we have), in the representation sense (hard under one encoding, easy under another), or in the class sense (complete for a class that no physically realizable device escapes). Second, a claim that the class-sense hardness of these inference problems is stable under the reductions and encodings anyone would accept as faithful, so that the second category collapses into the third for our purposes. Third, the normative conclusion: a theory that specifies what an agent ought to believe or do while remaining silent about resources is not an idealization but a category error, since its prescriptions are undefined for every agent that could exist.

The audience is about a dozen people. Six or seven are complexity theorists whose own work is on circuit lower bounds, derandomization, and average-case complexity; they are not hostile to philosophy but they have little patience for it, and their default suspicion is that anyone who says intractable in a philosophical register has confused no known algorithm with no algorithm, has ignored the gap between worst case and average case, or has smuggled in a claim about uniform machines that a non-uniform or interactive model would undercut. Two are philosophers of science who work on idealization and on what makes a model explanatory; they will care about whether my thesis is descriptive or normative and about whether a resource-indexed rationality is still recognizably a theory of rationality rather than a description of engineering practice. One is a statistician who will want to know why practical inference is not, in fact, intractable, given that people solve large instances of these problems every day with satisfiability solvers and sampling methods. I have forty-five minutes and then thirty minutes of questions, and a referee at a journal has already described the thesis as either trivially true or plainly false, so I know the triviality charge is coming.

Write my question-period prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from a philosophy-of-computing reading group, ordered by how likely each is combined with how much damage it does if I fumble it, each phrased the way the person who raises it would phrase it, with a two-minute reply for each, a note on what I should concede up front, and a note on where the reply is weakest. Flag which objections are really the same objection in different clothing. I do not need references or citations, just the arguments. And tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
