I'm leading a single seventy-five-minute session in a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, and the students are mostly refugees from computational linguistics and cognitive science: they can all recite the distributional picture of meaning as a location in a high-dimensional space, and they arrive convinced, in a way they've never had to defend, that whatever a speaker means is settled by what's inside the speaker. What I want from you is not the explanation of semantic externalism but the plan for delivering it — the order in which the pieces have to arrive so that each step is earned rather than announced. Tell me what has to be in place before the Twin Earth thought experiment can do any work at all (my instinct is that the idea of two subjects being identical "from the inside" has to come first, but I suspect the reference/meaning distinction is doing something earlier in the sequence than I've been putting it), what depends on what, and where the canonical water case belongs in that sequence, if it belongs there at all.

On the carrying example: I'd rather not open with Putnam's water-and-XYZ, not because there's anything wrong with it but because it arrives pre-labelled as science fiction and invites this particular audience to treat the whole issue as a puzzle about faraway planets rather than about their own words. So choose the example that should carry the session, and tell me what it must have and what it must lack. My own guess is that the twins' internal states have to be stipulated identical in a way these students will actually grant, that the resulting difference in what they mean must not be traceable to any difference in their beliefs or experiences, and that the term should ideally get its extension from a community's practice rather than from a hidden microstructure, so that nobody can deflate the point into "the word just tracks the chemistry." Then tell me where the canonical cases belong once the example has done its work: whether the water case should come second, as the clean formalization of what they've just seen, and whether Burge's arthritis case is the right follow-up to show that the phenomenon isn't confined to natural kinds.

The part I actually need is the misconception map.
