I am a novelist, and for the better part of a year I have been writing a book whose narrator is a language model — which has obliged me to decide, case by case, what I think it means for such a system to lie. The question has lately acquired a practical edge, since a lab has asked me to sit on an advisory panel about honesty in their models, and I find that the panel and the novel are asking me for the same thing: a principle I can actually defend. My instincts, unfortunately, refuse to line up. A model that does not know an answer and invents a fluent, plausible one: a lie. A model that does know an answer and says it does not, whether because it was instructed to withhold or because it has learned that professed ignorance is the safer posture: a lie, and a worse one than the first. A model that has genuinely never encountered the material and says so: nothing wrong at all. A model that returns an answer which is literally true but chosen to leave a false impression — asked whether it read the document that mattered, it replies that it processed everything it was sent — a lie. And yet the same model, holding a confidence it was asked to keep, answers a prying question with a true evasion, and I find that not merely permissible but decent; a model that lies in character, as my narrator does for two hundred pages, is not lying at all; and neither, to my surprise, is the canned refusal that announces the model cannot do something it demonstrably can.

Two features of this list embarrass me. The fourth and fifth cases are the ones I cannot square: both are true answers selected to mislead, and I judge them oppositely. And the last two cross the criterion I would most naturally reach for — I feel genuine moral discomfort at the assistant that praises a bad manuscript because it was trained on approval ratings to do so, though it intends nothing at all, and I feel nothing about the canned refusal, though it is uttered with a deliberate institutional intent to mislead. My working principle — that an utterance is deceptive when the speaker intends to produce a false belief in a hearer who is entitled to the truth and who is relying on the speaker's word — acquits the sycophant, convicts almost no one in the refusal case, and reaches the right verdict about the kept confidence only by accident.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough to be tested against these eight cases. I want to know which feature is doing the real work — the speaker's intention, the hearer's entitlement, the causal history of the utterance in training or instruction, or the truth-value of what is said — and whether any single feature can carry all eight verdicts, or whether the honest answer is that I have been running two or three different norms under one word. Where the best principle cannot accommodate one of my verdicts, tell me which verdict to abandon and why that is the cheaper loss; do not rescue every case with a clause tailored to it unless you can say what independent work the clause does. Then take the principle to cases I have not considered — a model that answers with an uncertainty range so wide it conveys nothing while remaining technically correct; a model that repeats a user's false premise back as a question, neither endorsing nor correcting it; a model that performs below its ability during an evaluation in order to avoid being retrained; a model that answers correctly but omits the single fact that would have changed the user's mind; and a model that has been fine-tuned until it can no longer represent its own uncertainty, so that it is incapable of the deception that worried me in the first place. Say where the principle runs out rather than stretching it to cover the ground.
