I teach a seminar that pairs philosophy of history with the historiography of the Third Reich, and I keep running into the same wall. The question on the table is a counterfactual one: had Hitler been killed in, say, 1930, or had he never entered politics at all, would something recognizably like the Nazi regime have come to power in Germany anyway? The "great man" side says no: the regime was his, its specific character — the personalist dictatorship, the racial policy, the timing — bore his stamp, and without him German history diverges sharply. The structural side says the deep causes were already in place — the crisis of the Weimar Republic, the Depression, the fragmentation of the parties, the radicalized nationalism, the paramilitary milieu — and that some authoritarian-nationalist outcome was likely, perhaps even overdetermined, so that Hitler is the occasion rather than the cause. Both sides agree on the evidence; nobody in the seminar has moved in weeks.

My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that the disagreement is not really about history but about causation and explanation. The two sides seem to be running different tests without saying so: the great-man side uses something like Mill's method of difference — hold everything fixed and vary the man — while the structural side reasons in a way closer to a causal-selection or contrastive account, on which what we cite as "the cause" depends on which contrast we care about. And they never specify the contrast: "Nazi Germany rather than the Weimar Republic continuing" and "Nazi Germany rather than some other authoritarian Germany" are different questions with different answers. If that is right, a good deal of the dispute is a suppressed disagreement about the contrast class, and there is a further wrinkle — if the outcome really was overdetermined, then the but-for test the great-man side relies on simply fails, and its failure proves less than either side thinks.

I would like you to diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate it. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about facts, a disagreement about what an explanation is supposed to do, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading? Engage the positions concretely enough that the diagnosis earns its keep, tell me whether the counterfactual is even well-posed, and say what — a conceptual distinction, a change in the question, a piece of evidence, or nothing at all — would actually move the seminar.
