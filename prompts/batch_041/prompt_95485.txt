I'm a graduate student in philosophy of language, and I need a short poem to close out the final meeting of a reading group that has spent the term on Grice — the intention-based account of speaker meaning, the cooperative principle, the maxims and the implicatures they generate, and the tests (calculability, cancellability, non-detachability) that are supposed to sort conversational implicature both from conventional implicature and from entailment. I'd like you to write it. Take the role of someone who has this apparatus entirely at their fingertips and also has a poet's ear; and please don't versify first and think later — begin by working out, for each stanza, exactly what is said, exactly what is implicated, and exactly which maxim is being flouted, and only then start drafting lines.

Here is the design I have in mind. The poem is spoken by a woman in a hospital corridor to her brother; they have just come from seeing their mother, and everything the woman says to him is literally true and, on the surface, beside the point — the vending machine that only takes coins, the parking validation, the weather, the fact that the cafeteria shuts at four. One line should recur word for word as a refrain, unchanged, and its implicature should be different each time it returns, because the context around it has shifted. (That's the point I care most about: same what-is-said, different what-is-implicated.) Each of the first four stanzas should turn on the flouting of a single maxim — Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner, one per stanza, in whatever order serves the poem — and a reader who knows the framework should be able to reconstruct the flouting from the poem alone, without being told what it is. The fifth stanza should turn on the hearer's failure or refusal to cooperate: he answers only what is said, treats the implicature as though it were an entailment, or contradicts something she never said; and at the end the speaker tries to cancel the implicature out loud — "I don't mean anything by that" — and the cancellation doesn't take. I want a reader to be able to say, after the last line, which side of the conversational/conventional line that failed cancellation falls on, and why.

Since the whole poem is ultimately a case of speaker meaning rather than sentence meaning, the reflexive intention should be visible somewhere in it: she needs him to understand her because he recognizes that she intends him to. Keep the diction plain — the more ordinary the words, the more work the maxims can do — and don't let the poem lecture or explain itself. Give me exactly five numbered stanzas of four to six lines each, with no title and no prefatory remarks. Then, in a single sentence after the poem, tell me how confident you are that the poem tracks Grice's distinctions accurately and name the one line you are least sure of, flagging plainly whether any of it leans on a contested reading (for instance, whether "what is said" is really as minimal as the poem pretends it is, or whether the implicature in the third stanza is conversational at all rather than conventional).
