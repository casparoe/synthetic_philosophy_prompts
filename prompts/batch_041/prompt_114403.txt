I've spent enough time in these arguments to notice that nearly everyone states the procreation asymmetry as a slogan — you needn't create a happy person, but you must not create an unhappy one — and then quietly switches to whatever machinery makes the slogan come out true. Before I pick a side, I want the spec. What would a theory of the asymmetry actually have to deliver?

Give me the requirements list: the cases it must get right (the child you may decline to create; the child you may not create; the choice that determines who exists at all; the life that is worth living but contains a harm that not existing would have avoided, and whether anyone has standing to complain about it; the existing sufferer whose condition the theory must not license you to ignore), the distinctions it must not collapse (creating a life versus continuing one; a comparative harm to a person versus a non-comparative badness in their coming to be; the badness of a life versus the badness of a birth), and the formal constraints it must respect, including the reversal test, under which a change and its inverse may not both count as for the worse, and the symmetry argument that treats the time before our birth and the time after our death as alike. Add the constraints that it not imply an obligation to create unlimited numbers of marginally happy lives, or a permission to create a miserable one as a means to others' benefit.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp, flag any subset you think cannot be satisfied jointly, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. I want a theory good enough to guide an actual decision about embryo selection and to survive being turned on a policy question, not one that only handles toy cases. My hunch is that the asymmetry is a constraint on agency rather than a fact about value; make the list sharp enough to tell me whether that hunch is forced.
