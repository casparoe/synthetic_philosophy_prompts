I have no academic background — I left school at seventeen and learned everything I know about music by playing it in bars — so I apologize in advance if I use a term of art loosely. For the past three years I have been the volunteer arranger for a community chorus of about twenty singers, ages nineteen to eighty, and lately I have been trying to think seriously about a picture of creativity I came across in a book about thinking: the claim that creative work is usually not invention out of nothing but variation on a theme, where a theme, or a style, can be thought of as living in a space of possibilities defined by parameters — call them knobs — that you can turn. On this picture, the everyday creative act is to turn a knob that has not been turned yet; the deeper acts are discovering that a knob everyone assumed was adjustable is actually welded shut, because turning it destroys the thing, and adding a knob to the space that was not there before. If I have that wrong, tell me so in a sentence or two, but then get on with the task, because what I want is not a lecture on the idea — it is the idea turned into a procedure I can actually run.

Here is what I have to work with. Our chorus sings from a book of eight songs — folk songs, spirituals, a couple of old hymns — and I write a new arrangement of one of them roughly every other week for a Saturday afternoon concert in the parish hall. The audience is mostly the same forty or fifty regulars, and they come because they like these songs; if I wander too far, they stop singing along and stop coming, and the singers notice that and lose heart. Rehearsal is one ninety-minute session on Wednesday. I read a lead sheet but not a full score, so anything I write has to be describable out loud to people who mostly do not read music at all. Our director has final say and will overrule me without ceremony if the group cannot learn a thing in a week, and she is usually right. I need to hand out parts on Tuesday night, so whatever procedure you give me has to be runnable in one evening at my kitchen table, not over a month of study.

Please write me that procedure: a checklist or ordered set of tests I can run on a given song before I start writing. I want it to say how to inventory the knobs of a particular song; how to sort them into knobs that are merely customary and knobs that are load-bearing, where turning them stops the song being that song; the order in which to turn things, so that I am not fiddling with five knobs at once and learning nothing; a test for telling a real variation from mere decoration, since I am vain enough to confuse the two; what to do when I have turned a knob and the song has dissolved into something else — when that is a failure and when it is the whole point; how to recognize the moment when the honest move is to add a new knob rather than turn an old one; and a stopping rule, so I know when the arrangement is done rather than merely different. Then run it on two of our songs. The first is a slow, sad ballad in three-quarter time with a tune so familiar that half the audience hums along, a simple three-chord harmony, and a verse I could sing in my sleep. The second is a fast call-and-response work song with a one-line chorus repeated eight times and a harmony that the tenors can barely hold. Include one case where your procedure gives me an answer I will not like, and mark clearly the points where the procedure runs out and a person has to decide — where the question is taste, or where I am making a call on the director's behalf.

I want you to commit. Do not lay out three schools of thought and leave me to choose; name the knobs you think are welded shut in each of these two songs, say which one you would turn first and why, and say where you expect to be wrong. Tell me what the procedure cannot do — in particular, what it will misfire on, and what it will tempt me to mistake for creativity when it is only novelty. If the honest answer is that no procedure of this kind can tell me whether a variation is good, say so plainly rather than dressing up taste as technique.

Write in American English, and keep the whole thing to no more than 1,200 words, with the procedure itself in no more than ten steps.
