A colleague keeps pressing the following argument, and I want to know how much of it the analogy can actually carry. A person who has spent years and a great deal of money on a graduate degree, a business, or a relationship that now plainly will not deliver should walk away, because continuing is like sitting through the rest of a movie you have already paid for and are not enjoying: the ticket price is gone either way, the only live question is whether the remaining time is worth spending, and if it is not, staying is pure waste. From this he concludes that honoring a sunk cost is never rational, and adds that it is the same mistake as the gambler who believes the dice owe him a win after so much loss.

I want an audit of how far the movie analogy holds. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the ticket price, the remaining runtime, the expected enjoyment, the cost of walking out, and the identity and reasons of the person deciding — then say where the mapping breaks and which breaks actually matter for the conclusion he draws. Two things interest me in particular. My suspicion is that the analogy quietly assumes the value of a long undertaking can be split into past and future segments and then summed, and I want to know whether that assumption survives for goods whose value seems to be constituted by their history: a decade-long apprenticeship, a marriage, a promise, a research program, a political commitment. I also want to know whether the comparison to the gambler's fallacy helps him or hurts him — in the gambler's case the past really is causally inert, whereas a sunk expenditure may have changed the world by building capacities, creating commitments, and foreclosing options — so is the past in the sunk-cost case as inert as the comparison needs it to be?

Conclude by saying whether the claim that honoring sunk costs is never rational survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along; if there is a better analogy for the cases in which continuing is in fact rational, give it and say what it captures that the movie does not. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
