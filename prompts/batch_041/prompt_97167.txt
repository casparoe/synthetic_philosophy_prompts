Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about why anyone should maximize expected utility, and we kept circling back to the same historical episode: Allais's choice problems and what the profession did with them. I would like you to take that episode as a case study and run the analysis in both directions — theory onto episode, episode back onto theory.

Here are the facts, so that we are working from the same ground. In the early 1950s Maurice Allais put two pairs of choices to people: first, a sure million versus a lottery with an 89 percent chance of a million, a 1 percent chance of nothing, and a 10 percent chance of five million; second, an 11 percent chance of a million versus a 10 percent chance of five million. Most people take the sure million in the first pair and the ten percent shot at five million in the second, and no assignment of utilities makes both of those choices maximize expected utility; the pattern violates the independence, or common-consequence, axiom, which is precisely what Allais intended. Some theorists treated the pattern as a mistake of intuition rather than a defect in the axiom; others built rival axiom systems, among them prospect theory and rank-dependent utility, that accommodate it.

The lens I want you to use is the coherence argument for expected utility maximization: the family of Dutch book, money pump, and representation-theorem arguments holding that an agent whose preferences violate the axioms is exposed to a sure loss, that such exposure is what irrationality consists in, and that a rational agent must therefore be representable as an expected utility maximizer. Do the mapping honestly. Who are the players in this episode, what are the options, what is being offered as evidence of irrationality, and does the coherence argument genuinely engage the Allais respondents or slide past them? Say where the mapping strains: whether the argument establishes exploitability rather than irrationality; whether it presupposes a chooser who meets each trade singly and cannot see the sequence coming; and whether it can even identify which set of axioms is the coherent one, given that any axiom system excluding the Allais pattern can be defended in the same terms. Then run it backwards. What does the episode show about the coherence argument — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the argument never anticipated? Does the fact that the argument's defenders treated a robust intuition as the error, rather than the axiom, reveal an asymmetry the argument cannot license?

Finally, the interpretive question my friend and I could not settle. He holds that the coherence argument is a genuine discovery about rationality, and that the Allais respondents were simply mistaken about their own preferences. I hold that it is at most a theorem about what a certain idealized chooser cannot be maneuvered into, and that calling those respondents irrational is a stipulation rather than a finding. Tell me who is right about what, and what we both missed. I am especially interested in whether an agent with incomplete preferences — one who never cycles and never violates transitivity, but simply declines to rank some pairs at all — escapes the money pump, and if so, whether that escape vindicates the coherence argument or refutes it. Be explicit about how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
