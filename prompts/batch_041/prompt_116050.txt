My friend says the language analogy settles the moral objectivity debate. His version: there's a ton of cross-cultural variation in moral norms, but there's just as much variation between languages, and nobody concludes from that that there's no fact about whether a sentence is well-formed — so moral variation is surface-level, the deep structure is shared, and diversity doesn't threaten objectivity. I think the analogy is being made to carry more than it can but I cant put my finger on where. Can you audit it?

Give me the correspondence table first: what's supposed to map onto universal grammar, onto parameters, onto the competence/performance split, onto the kid who picks up the rules without ever being taught. Then the places it breaks, and which of those breaks matter for what he's using it for versus which are just idle disanalogies.

But here's the thing that bugs me. The linguistic universals get their bite from how tightly constrained the space of possible languages is — the interesting claims are about what no language does. If the moral space isn't constrained like that, and it looks like cultures fill in nearly every slot, does the analogy still support his conclusion, or does it actually cut against it? And there's a version of this I find more interesting: both fields built their universals out of a narrow band of samples — a handful of languages on one side, undergrads plus a few small-scale societies picked for contrast on the other. Is that a point in his favour, since the bias is symmetric and so the comparison is fair, or does it mean he's comparing two artifacts of the same sampling habit?

Then tell me what would be a better analogy. I've seen morality compared to cuisine — universal need to eat, universal taste receptors, no true cuisine — and to legal codes, where one function gets implemented a dozen different ways. Pick whichever is strongest and say what it actually supports: objectivity, anti-realism, or neither.

And keep it tied to the actual cross-cultural work on folk moral judgement, not just the armchair stuff. My suspicion is that most of what gets called a moral universal is universal among people who fill in questionnaires, and that the real variation shows up exactly where the WEIRD samples are the odd ones out. If that's right, does it break the analogy, or is it just a methods complaint that leaves the argument untouched?
