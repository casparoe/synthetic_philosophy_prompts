My mother has advanced dementia, and for the past year my brother and I have been arguing about the house. She still has good days. On one of them, unprompted, she told me she did not want it sold. My brother's reply — and I have since heard the same move from my sister about the funeral and from my aunt about her letters — is that this is not what she really wants. What she really wants, he says, is what she would have wanted back when she could still see the whole situation clearly; and that woman, he is confident, would sell, because selling is the sensible thing and our mother was always a sensible woman. What interests me is not who is right but the shape of the move. The person being consulted is a version of my mother improved along certain lines — better informed, steadier, less frightened, able to hold the whole picture in view at once — while her values are left exactly as they were, so that the improvement is supposed to stand neutral between my brother and me. And yet in every actual use of it I have witnessed, the improved woman turns out to want precisely what the person invoking her wants, and there is no way to check, because she never says anything her own advocate has not already said on her behalf. When I try the same trick on myself it behaves the same way: the me who has thought it all through agrees with whatever I already lean toward, only more calmly. The oddity is sharpest where the two of us agree completely about the facts — we both know what the money would do, and for whom — so the appeal to her being better informed cannot be doing the work it is asked to do, since better information settles questions about what is the case and this is a disagreement about what matters.

I suspect I am looking at a general move rather than a family failing, and I would like to know whether it has a name. I am aware of something in law and medicine about deciding for people who cannot decide for themselves, but as I understand it that applies precisely where the person cannot speak at all, whereas here she does speak and is overruled by an appeal to the woman she would be if she could think straight. I have a vague sense that political philosophers do something similar when they justify arrangements by what people would agree to under improved conditions, and that economists and philosophers of welfare have a related notion about preferences a person would have if fully informed — but I cannot tell whether either of those is the same thing, or whether the thing I am describing is a distinct and nameless variant of them.

So: if there is an established term for this move, tell me what it is. I will not be satisfied with the bare label. I want the carving — what exactly the move is, how it relates to the nearest established concepts and where it parts company with each of them, why the improvement is made along some dimensions of a person and not others, and whether the resulting verdict can ever have authority over the actual person's expressed wish. Give me the boundary cases: does the move become legitimate when the person is wholly unable to speak, or when the improvement is confined to information alone, or when the person herself has previously endorsed the improved version? If nothing established fits, say so plainly and coin a term, define it, and tell me which of the cases I have described fall inside it and which fall outside.
