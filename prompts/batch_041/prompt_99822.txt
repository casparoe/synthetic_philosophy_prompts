I am working through Aquinas's treatment of sedition in the Secunda Secundae (II-II, q. 42), for a seminar on medieval political thought, and I keep running aground on the reply to the third objection in the article where he asks whether sedition is always a mortal sin. The reply reads: "A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely; for this is tyranny, being conducive to the private good of the ruler, and to the injury of the multitude." What I want is not a paraphrase of this but an account of the argumentative work it is doing. In the preceding article Aquinas defines sedition rather narrowly — as discord among the parts of one people, one part rising against another — and I cannot tell whether a single assassin, or a small conspiracy with no popular following, would fall under that sin at all. If it would not, then this reply may be clearing away a charge that was never properly laid, and the sentence about disturbing a tyrannical government may be carrying less weight than histories of resistance theory typically ask it to carry. I am also unsure what "disturbing" is meant to cover: deposition, armed revolt, assassination, or merely the refusal of obedience.

Second, I am trying to square this passage with the discussion of tyranny in Aquinas's unfinished treatise on kingship, the one addressed to the king of Cyprus. As I recall that discussion — and I may be flattening it — the counsel there is far more cautious: a tyranny that has not become extreme is to be endured, because the attempt to unseat it commonly produces something worse, and private persons who take it upon themselves to kill a tyrant are playing a perilous game, since it is the boldest rather than the best who tend to come out on top; where no human remedy is available, one is to have recourse to God. I want to know whether the two texts genuinely conflict, or whether the Summa's claim that there is no sedition in disturbing such a government is compatible with those warnings, perhaps because the Summa is settling a question about the species of a sin rather than about who may lawfully act, and by what authority. I am also puzzled by the conditional clause about greater harm: is that a real consequentialist proviso capable of making a revolt unlawful even against a genuine tyrant, and if so, who is competent to make that judgment — the subjects at large, some public authority, or no one short of God? And I am struck by the reversal at the close, where the tyrant rather than the rebel is named the seditious party; I want to know how hard that reversal can be pressed, whether it makes tyrannical rule itself an act of sedition or is only a re-description with rhetorical force.
