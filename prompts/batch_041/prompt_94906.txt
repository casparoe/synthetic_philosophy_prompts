You are a philosopher of science who works on causation; think carefully before you write, and treat this as a teaching instrument rather than an essay. I run a graduate seminar on the counterfactual and interventionist traditions, and I want a single decision tree that takes a student from the one commitment nearly everyone in the debate shares — that a cause is at least a difference-maker — down to a named position. Every branch node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case or a claim that the student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to: early and late preemption, overdetermination, double prevention, omissions and other absences, a putative cause on which no one could ever intervene, an intervention that is itself caused by the very thing it is supposed to intervene on. The fault lines I care about are whether the relata of causation are events or variables, whether a causal claim requires that some intervention be possible at least in principle, whether intervention may itself be treated as a causal notion, whether absences can be causes, and whether a single concept of causation is being stretched to cover two different jobs — making a difference and producing an effect. Give each leaf a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment: counterfactual dependence among events, interventionist counterfactuals over variables, the structural-equation approach, mechanistic accounts, primitivism, pluralism, and any others you judge to earn a leaf. Mark every combination of answers that turns out to be unoccupied and say whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has explored it, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. Aim for eight to twelve branch nodes, and do not give me a reading list. Finish with a single machine-readable line of the form Verdict: leaves=<n>; unoccupied=<n>; inseparable=<list>.
