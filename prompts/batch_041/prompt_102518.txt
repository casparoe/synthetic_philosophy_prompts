I'm a software engineer on a team that builds tool-using agents for back-office operations — issuing refunds, making account changes, drafting customer communications — and I have no formal background in philosophy, so what I need is a decision procedure, not an exposition. Our design reviews keep relitigating the same question: should a given capability be built as a goal-directed agent that optimizes a stated objective (a scoring rubric, a success metric, a reward we hand-tune), or as an approval-directed agent that at each step selects the action it predicts a designated human approver would most approve of, given a description of that action? We currently settle this by whoever argues longest, and I'd like to replace that with something a review can actually run.

Please write me an ordered procedure: the questions to ask, in sequence, for each new capability; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure terminates in a recommendation for one design over the other; and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values or risk appetite rather than engineering. It has to be usable by an engineer who has never read anything about this, and it should be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a procedure.

Three distinctions from the underlying idea need to survive the translation, because they are the ones that keep biting us, and I want the procedure to say explicitly how each is handled rather than assuming it away. First, the difference between the approver's approval of the act as it is described to them, their approval of the act under its actual downstream consequences, and their approval of the policy that generated the act — the procedure should state which of these the agent is to be pointed at in a given case, and how to tell. Second, the gap between the human approver in the loop and the model of the approver that our system actually queries at runtime, including what to do when the two diverge and how to notice that they have. Third, the cases where approval is being used as a stand-in for something else we care about — customer welfare, legal exposure, a fairness commitment — and the conditions under which that substitution quietly breaks.

Then run the procedure on three capabilities: an agent that decides refund exceptions up to a fixed dollar ceiling, with a support lead as approver; an agent that drafts and sends a re-engagement email to the whole customer base; and an agent that proposes edits to its own regression and evaluation suite, with the metrics owner as approver. I expect at least one of these to come out in a way I won't like; say so plainly rather than smoothing it over. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do, and where you expect it to misfire in practice.

Constraints: it must be runnable inside a thirty-minute design review by people who are not specialists, it should name the role that has to sign off on each outcome, and it should not include reading lists, citations, or references to papers — anything of that sort is useless to me. Close by saying how confident you are in the procedure as a whole and in each load-bearing step, and single out the one step you are least sure of.
