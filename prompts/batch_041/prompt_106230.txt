I am a final-year PhD student in a complex-systems group, and as part of a cross-departmental seminar I have to submit a short position paper setting out my research philosophy for the quantitative study of long-run history. Before I commit to it, I would like a candid audit of whether the commitments below actually cohere. I have a nagging sense that not all of them can be held together, but I cannot tell which one is doing work the others cannot pay for, and I would rather find out now than in front of an audience of historians. Here is the set.

(i) Long-run agrarian history exhibits genuine recurrent regularities, most conspicuously the multi-century oscillations in population, elite numbers, state finance and internal violence that are sometimes called secular cycles. I take these to be properties of the social systems themselves rather than artifacts of how historians periodize the record.

(ii) Those regularities are best accounted for by a short list of mechanisms: population growth pressing on a bounded resource base; elite overproduction, in which the number of people with elite aspirations, whether credentialed, armed or politically connected, outruns the number of elite positions available; competition among those aspirants for patronage; and the fiscal strain this imposes on the state, which raises the returns to factional violence. Call this the structural-demographic account.

(iii) To explain a historical pattern is to exhibit the mechanism that generates it. If my model, when run, produces oscillations of roughly the right period and amplitude, I have explained the oscillations, and I do not think anything over and above generative success, such as a covering law or a reconstruction of the actors' reasons, is required.

(iv) I also hold that the interesting macrohistorical episodes are path-dependent: which faction prevails, when the crisis breaks, and whether a state reforms or collapses all turn on contingency, such as the harvest, a single battle, or the competence of a ruler. The system is chaotic in the technical sense, so nearby trajectories diverge rapidly.

(v) I nonetheless think we can assert that the risk of serious political instability in the 2020s is elevated relative to the 1990s, and I count such assertions as genuine, testable forecasts rather than rhetoric.

(vi) I am untroubled by the fact that models of this kind are usually calibrated case by case after the outcome is known, with parameters chosen to fit the episode under explanation. A flexible model that can be tuned to fit is simply a flexible model.

(vii) Nor am I troubled that the programme mostly examines cases in which the predicted outcome occurred, the collapses and revolutions, rather than the near-misses and the episodes in which elites were absorbed and nothing broke.

Please tell me which of these hang together, which pairs are in genuine trouble, and which apparent conflicts are in fact harmless. I would rather have the two or three most serious problems diagnosed properly than a list of every conceivable friction. For each, say what has to give and what that concession would cost me. Structure your answer as follows: begin with a two- or three-sentence verdict; then give a table with exactly these columns, Tensions (the commitments involved, by number), Verdict (Genuine or Apparent only), Severity (1 being the most damaging), Diagnosis (two or three sentences on what actually conflicts), Cheapest repair, and Cost of repair (what I lose by making it); and finish with a single sentence naming the one change that buys the most coherence at once.
