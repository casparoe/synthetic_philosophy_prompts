A recurring argument on a forum I lurk on flared up again over the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I'd like a move-by-move adjudication rather than a verdict on the whole dispute. The running example was an invented one: a gene therapy that corrects a rare mutation responsible for mild intellectual disability, but that raises measured cognitive ability by a roughly fixed increment in anyone who receives it, affected or not. Two posters, Ines and Oskar, went at it.

Ines: the distinction is dead on arrival. The same molecule both treats and enhances, so "therapy" versus "enhancement" is a fact about the recipient's baseline rather than about the act; medicine already allocates by expected benefit, so we should drop the labels and ask only whether the outcome is worth it.

Oskar: that proves too much. One molecule's double duty no more collapses the concepts than water's ability both to slake thirst and to drown collapses drinking and drowning. The distinction lives in the baseline: therapy restores a system that is malfunctioning, enhancement pushes a functioning system beyond the range it was selected to occupy. Therapy is therefore presumptively required and enhancement presumptively optional — not because I have smuggled in a value, but because removing a malfunction and adding a capability are different acts.

Ines: your "range it was selected to occupy" is a statistical average dressed up as a telos; on your view a short man is malfunctioning. And you cannot rest the normative conclusion (required versus optional) on the descriptive one and then call the descriptive one neutral — the presumption is precisely what is in dispute. Try the hard case: a drug that prevents age-related cognitive decline. Everyone calls prevention therapy, yet nothing is malfunctioning yet, so your account must call it enhancement.

Oskar: prevention is therapy aimed at a counterfactual future malfunction, so it fits. What the hard case really shows is that the line is contested wherever we disagree about whether something counts as a malfunction — aging being the obvious example. That is a dispute about the facts, not a collapse of the distinction; a sorting rule was never supposed to settle every case by itself.

Ines: a sorting rule that needs a prior answer to every contested case is doing no work, and if "malfunction" is whatever we decide to call it, the distinction is conventional — which was my original point.

Please rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say where the two are talking past each other, and identify what each is missing. Don't just name a winner.
