Right, so I've somehow ended up as the person who has to write the reconciliation document for our privacy reading group, and I need your help drafting it properly rather than doing what I did last time, which was to write "they basically agree" in an email and get shouted at for a fortnight.

The setup: two of our regulars have been arguing since January. One of them (she calls herself an instrumentalist and is happy with the label) thinks privacy is only ever valuable downstream — it's a means to autonomy, intimacy, a life you can revise, protection from discrimination, room to be embarrassing in. Strip out all the downstream conseqences and there's nothing left to lose. The other one (she says the value is constitutive — her word, I'm not sure it's the right one) thinks that's backwards: part of the value is internal to being a person at all, so being observed can be a loss even in a world where no consequence ever follows from the observation, nobody judges you, nobody acts on it, and you never find out.

I don't want a verdict on who's right. I want the document they should have produced months ago, that both of them could actually sign. So:

1. The agreed claims. What can both put their names to about what privacy is (a right? a condition? a bundle of claims against other people? a social good? a relation between a person and an audience?), about what counts as a privacy loss at all, about the cases they keep re-litigating, and about what would count as evidence one way or the other. Watch out for the fudge where 'privacy' quietly starts meaning 'the good things privacy brings' — if the instrumentalist can't say her thing without begging the question against the other one, say so rather than papering over it.

2. Where they actually part. Each side's position stated in wording the other side would accept as fair, even though they reject it. This is the bit I most need, because they are both terrible strawmanner, and half the year's argument has been about positions neither of them holds.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling it would move at least one of them. My guesses, tell me if I'm off: whether the kind-observer case (perfectly discreet, never judges, never acts, subject never learns) is a loss or a non-event; whether an exposure nobody ever exploits is a harm or merely a risk of harm; whether the thing of value is control over the information or something that control is itself downstream of; and whether they're even talking about the same object, given that one of them pictures a teenager's bedroom and the other pictures a data broker. One of them thinks privacy is mainly a shield and the other thinks it's mainly a hoard, and if that's a verbal dispute I want it named as one rather than dressed up as a substantive crux.

4. For each crux, a case or consideration both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Invent cases freely, as long as both camps would accept them as fair. If you can invent one that separates 'the information exists and is held' from 'anyone uses it or could use it', please do — I've tried and I keep smuggling consequences back in through the side door. Two sharp cases beat six soggy ones.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then a table for the disagreements and cruxes. Please use British spelling throughout, the journal we're sending it to is fussy and I've already been told of once. And no citations or reading lists — we've had enough of that, and neither of them is going to be won over by a footnote anyway.
