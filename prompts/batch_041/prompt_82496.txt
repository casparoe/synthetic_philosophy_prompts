I am drafting a chapter on diachronic rationality for a collection aimed at graduate students, and before I commit to the central argument I would like your critical comments on it. The chapter is organised around the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences, and my complaint is deliberately not the usual one — not that the pump requires a cooperative adversary, nor that the sequence of trades is contrived. My complaint is that the argument trades on a conflation between two properties that are constantly treated as a single property: being exploitable by an idealised adversary, and being self-undermining in deliberation. The pump turns only because the agent is assumed to be myopic at each step — it compares the two holdings then on offer and takes the preferred one — and because its preferences are assumed to range over terminal holdings rather than over courses of action. I want to argue that once either assumption is relaxed the construction loses its normative force, and that what it really establishes is a disjunction, and a more interesting one than the conclusion usually drawn from it.

The shape of the argument is this. Take the cycle A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A, with the agent holding A and an adversary offering in sequence: C for A plus a small payment, then B for C plus a small payment, then A for B plus a small payment. What the sequence shows is that an agent who (i) ranks holdings cyclically and (ii) chooses at each step by pairwise comparison of the holdings then on offer will end up where it started, poorer, having been worse off at every step. But (ii) is a premise about the agent's decision procedure, not about its preferences, and (i) is silent on whether the agent also ranks courses of action — whole sequences, or policies. If it does, and does so acyclically, then the cyclic ranking of holdings is perfectly consistent with a ranking of policies on which refusing the first trade is best, and the pump stalls. So the money pump is at bottom a representability result: you cannot combine cyclic rankings of outcomes with the absence of any ranking of policies. That is a theorem about the structure of a preference state space, and my contention is that it has been read as a verdict on rationality. I am aware that this is close to the familiar move of letting the agent adopt a plan, and that the plan is then treated as authoritative; what interests me is what that move silently presupposes.

Where I take the philosophical action to be, and where I would most value your scepticism, is the residual question the disjunction leaves open: whose ranking of policies is it? If the agent itself ranks policies, the immunity is an exercise of ordinary practical reasoning, and the questions become how such a ranking is formed and how stable it is under pressure. If instead the ranking is installed by something external — a precommitment, a rule with a cost of exit, a trusted other, or, in the cases that interest me most, scaffolding around a bounded agent such as a checklist or a professional role — then the agent is unexploitable in the sense that matters for markets without possessing the coherence the argument was supposed to vindicate. I want to say that these two cases come apart, that exploitability arguments are constraints on market participants rather than on rational agents, and that this relocates the money pump from the theory of coherence to the theory of institutions. A colleague I have been arguing with for weeks thinks this proves too much: any exploited agent can be described after the fact as having a policy-level ranking on which its conduct was optimal, so my repair threatens to make the pump vacuous rather than precise. I am not yet sure whether the fix is to demand that the policy-level ranking be the agent's own in some substantive sense, or to drop the appeal to policies altogether and locate the defect in a mismatch between the agent's rankings at different times.

What I would like from you, then, is comment of three kinds. First, on accuracy: is the representability claim correct as stated, and does the myopia premise really carry the weight I put on it, or is there a version of the pump that survives without it? Second, on interest: is the disjunction substantive, or is it a redescription of the standard reply dressed up as a result? Third, on exposure: construct for me the strongest objection an unsympathetic referee would raise, in your own voice — I want a reconstruction, not a survey of who has said what, and I am not asking for references. Please tell me how confident you are in each of the claims you make, and single out the one point at which you are least sure you have it right.
