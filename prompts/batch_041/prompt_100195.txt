Over the past year or so I've been reading my way through the popular literature on moral foundations theory — the idea that political disagreement is partly a disagreement about which moral intuitions get privileged, with care and fairness doing most of the work on the left and loyalty, authority and purity (and, in some tellings, a dislike of being pushed around) doing a lot of the work on the right — and I've noticed that almost every explanation of it ends with the same move. The psychology is supposed to show that each side is being moral by its own lights, and from there we are told, more or less gently, that we should stop treating one side's morality as the correct one. That conclusion seems to come from nowhere, and I'd rather see the joint than be lectured about it.

So: write me a parody of a corporate values handbook — the glossy onboarding document a mid-sized company produces after hiring a consulting firm to redo its ethics program — as written by a fictional consultancy that has rebuilt the whole thing on moral foundations theory. Around 700 words, laid out as numbered sections with headings, deadpan from first line to last: no winking, no narrator stepping out to explain the joke, no real people or real firms named. Each foundation should surface as a concrete and faintly absurd HR policy — a leave policy, an expenses rule, a clean-desk rule — and somewhere past the halfway point the memo should introduce an additional foundation, presented as a routine update following an employee complaint, without ever acknowledging how odd it is that one can simply be added. The joke I'm after is that the document treats the enumeration of everyone's moral intuitions as though it had already settled the question of what the company ought to do, and never notices the gap; so please don't have anyone inside the memo point at the gap either. Someone who has never heard of the theory should be able to follow the document and find it funny purely as a piece of corporate prose, and someone who has read the literature should be able to tell that the author understands what the theory actually claims and finds the satire fair.
