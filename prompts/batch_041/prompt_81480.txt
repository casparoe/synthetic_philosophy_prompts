I'm a postdoc in philosophy with roughly eighteen months left on a fellowship and a background in decision theory and microeconomics, and I'm trying to decide which two or three papers to write on money pumps and diachronic rationality. This is the only stretch of protected time I'm likely to have for a while, and I'd rather spend it on questions that move the area than on another survey of the standard argument, so what I want from you is a defended research agenda: which open questions here most deserve a paper, in what order, and why that order rather than another. Please take seriously that importance is relative to something — here, to a single philosopher with a formal decision-theory toolkit, a limited budget of time, and an interest in changing how people in the field argue rather than in cataloguing what they've said.

The questions I keep circling, in no particular order, are these. First, whether the sequential money-pump argument against cyclic preferences establishes anything at all, given how heavily it leans on assumptions about what the agent will do at each stage — the familiar trichotomy between naive, sophisticated, and resolute choosers, and the contrast between pumps presented all at once and pumps that unfold over time. Second, whether the pump is best read as an argument about preference rationality or as something narrower, about exploitability or the existence of a certain kind of arbitrage opportunity, and whether those two can come apart. Third, what the argument is supposed to say about agents who are intransitive or incomplete but not cyclic — the self-torturer being the obvious case — and whether such agents are pumpable in the same sense. Fourth, whether the whole dialectic can be recast synchronically, as a point about representation and the axioms of expected utility, so that the diachronic framing is doing less work than its proponents think. And fifth, a suspicion I'd like stress-tested: I've been calling it the one-trade pump, a single pair of offers presented simultaneously such that accepting both leaves the agent strictly worse off although she prefers each to the status quo in the relevant pairwise comparison, and I suspect a good deal of the debate quietly assumes the agent's valuations are risk-neutral over money and stable across the sequence, so that any pump registers as a pure loss; relax either assumption and some pumps dissolve while others become considerably more interesting.

Please rank the candidate questions, adding any I've missed or wrongly framed, and say which are upstream of which, which are tractable with conceptual and formal tools rather than experimental work, which look neglected, and which I should drop because settling them wouldn't change anything downstream. I'd also like you to state explicitly how confident you are in the ranking as a whole, and to single out the one point in it you're least sure of and why.
