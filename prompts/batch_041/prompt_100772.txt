I am the knowledge engineer on a small team building a task-planning module for warehouse robots. Every week or two I hand the planner a new action specification — pick, stack, charge, open, and so on — and the planner treats any fluent I do not explicitly list as an effect as though it persists unchanged across the action. That default is what makes the specifications writable at all, and it is also where we get burned: the planner will assume a box stays on the shelf while the robot carries the shelf away, or that a battery stays charged while a heater we forgot to model drains it. I have no logician on the team, no time to prove my specifications consistent, and one sign-off from a senior engineer; a bad specification costs us a day of downtime, and a bad one in the wrong place costs a collision.

Turn what the frame problem literature has actually settled into a procedure I can run on each new specification. What I want from you: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to decide whether a fluent may be left to the persistence default or needs an explicit rule of its own, when to add a causal axiom rather than trust the default, and the points where the procedure must stop and hand the specification to the senior engineer because the remaining question is a modeling judgment rather than something a procedure can settle. Draw on the common-sense assumption that things stay as they are unless something changes them — and the law-of-inertia metaphor used to formalize it — on the strategies that were proposed to keep specifications small, and on the reasons those strategies were shown to be incomplete: the qualification problem, the ramification problem, and the counterexamples that defeated the naive minimization of change. Do not give me a survey; give me the procedure.

Then run it on three specifications: (1) a robot moving a box from one shelf to another; (2) a robot opening a valve on a tank, where opening the valve drains the tank, the draining changes the tank's weight, and a scale reports that weight; (3) two actions that are harmless in isolation but can run concurrently and together defeat the default — construct the pair, for instance a robot that moves a support while another places a load on it. For at least one of the three, the procedure should force an answer I will not like, and you should say so plainly. End by stating what the procedure cannot do.
