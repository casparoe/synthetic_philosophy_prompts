I'm a second-year PhD student in a cog-sci lab and our reading group has been stuck on the side-effect effect for over a year now, to the point where I've started to suspect we're not even having one argument. I'll lay out the shape of it and then ask you for a diagnosis instead of a verdict. The basic case everyone in the group accepts: a chairman says he doesn't care about anything except profit, and the new program will increase profits and, depending on the version, either harm or help the environment; asked whether he harmed it intentionally or helped it intentionally, most people say yes in the harm version and no in the help version. Nobody disputes the result, we've rerun it on our own undergrads twice. What we fight about is what it shows. One camp says it shows the ordinary notion of intentional action is partly normative: whether something counts as intentional depends on whether it's the kind of thing we'd blame someone for, so the asymmetry is evidence about the concept itself. A second camp says it's a pragmatic artifact — the vignette makes the side effect the point of the story and basically invites a verdict, so people answer the question they think is on the table rather than the one printed, and nothing follows about the concept. A third camp says both of those are chasing a phantom, because there's no single folk concept to have and the interesting question is what work the word does in our practices of blaming and excusing. We've now invented maybe fifteen variants between us and every one of them gets explained after the fact by all three camps, which is what got me wondering whether the camps are even answering the same question.

My hunch, and I'd like you to tell me if it's wrong: the two main camps disagree about no fact at all — they're answering different questions (what the word means to speakers, versus what is going on in the head of someone who answers), and the stalemate is really about what counts as the thing to be explained, plus a background split over whether a concept is a natural kind to be discovered or a norm-laden practice to be described. To test that I tried a variant I haven't seen anywhere: same structure, but I replaced the harm with a pure rule violation — the program breaks an absurd internal bylaw, harms nobody, offends nobody, changes nothing anyone cares about. If the asymmetry survives, harm-based explanations look shaky; if it dies, that suggests the asymmetry is about harm or blame rather than norms generally. The group split three ways on what my variant even shows, which I think is itself data about our problem.

So: diagnose us. Verbal dispute, substantive dispute about facts or mechanisms, two different questions under one heading, or a sign the whole thing is badly posed? Tell me what each side is actually committed to and which of those commitments the other side can't accept, and then, given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal model with predictions we don't already have, or dropping the question — and what each camp would have to give up for it. I don't need a reading list, I've read the main papers; I want the diagnosis with reasons, and an honest answer about whether this one can be settled at all or whether the honest move is to stop expecting it to be.
