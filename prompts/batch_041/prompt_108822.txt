In a reading group I attend, one member keeps invoking the organic view of civilizations to argue that the present troubles of the liberal-democratic West are not a policy failure but a life-stage. His claim, in outline, is this: a civilization is an organism; organisms pass through youth, maturity and decline; decline in an organism is not a correctable defect but the working-out of its nature; therefore the reformist impulse — rebuild the institutions, restore the trust, reverse the demographic and fiscal drift — is roughly as sensible as telling an old man to be young again. He appeals to Spengler's comparative morphology of cultures and, more temperately, to Toynbee's account of growth and breakdown, and treats the analogy as established enough that the argument can be run on it directly.

I want an audit rather than a verdict on whether the West is in fact declining. Set out the mapping the analogy requires — what is supposed to play the role of the organism, its species, its genome, its metabolism, its development, its death — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. In particular, I would like you to say what the analogy quietly imports: whether treating a civilization as a single organism commits him to a hidden teleology or a hidden determinism; whether it presupposes that the whole has interests, or a good of its own, over and above those of the people inside it; and whether the naturalness of death is being smuggled in as a reason to accept it.

Then weigh a rival picture I have been toying with, in case it does the work better. Rather than an organism, treat a civilization as an ecological community undergoing succession: it is assembled by a particular sequence of disturbances and colonizations, it can become locked into a configuration that persists for a long time, and what looks like senescence may in fact be a stable degraded state that endures precisely because the species that would replace the incumbent ones cannot establish themselves. On this picture there is no lifespan and no scheduled death — only path-dependence, thresholds and hysteresis — and 'winter' would be a basin of attraction rather than a season. Does this alternative preserve what the organism analogy was capturing (the sense that the decline is self-reinforcing rather than a string of correctable errors) while dropping the parts that do the illicit work? And where does it predict differently from the organism picture — what would each lead you to expect about whether, and by what means, a society can exit the decline?

Fold in the older cyclical theories only insofar as they sharpen this. Polybius on the rotation of constitutions and Ibn Khaldun on the rise and decay of dynasties each encode a cycle without a biological clock; I would like to know whether their version of cyclicity is closer to the organism picture or to the succession picture, and what that tells us about how much of the conclusion that decline is inevitable was ever carried by the analogy itself rather than by the theories that borrowed it.

I am not asking for sources or reading lists; argue from the concepts. Finish by telling me which conclusion, if either, the organism analogy can actually support, and what a better analogy would be if neither of these is right.
