I am designing a comparative unit for a graduate seminar on the cosmological argument, and I would like you to carry out a ranking exercise within a framework of my own devising. Please reason inside that framework rather than rehearsing the standard verdicts of the secondary literature, since the point of the exercise is to see whether the arguments sort differently once they are scored this way.

The framework turns on what I will call the theistic yield of a cosmological argument. To estimate the theistic yield of an argument, proceed in two steps. First, construct its minimal terminus: the weakest entity that satisfies every demand the argument's premises actually make, while possessing none of the attributes of classical theism that the premises do not themselves entail. Second, ask how tightly those premises, taken on their own, constrain the space of admissible termini in the direction of a being that is personal, omniscient, omnipotent, and morally perfect. An argument whose premises leave the minimal terminus looking like a mere abstract ground or an impersonal cause has a low theistic yield; an argument whose premises cannot be satisfied by anything short of a personal creator has a high one.

Please assign each of the following formulations a theistic yield expressed as a decimal between 0 and 1, and then rank the formulations from highest to lowest yield.

(a) The temporal kalam argument: whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause.

(b) The Leibnizian argument from the principle of sufficient reason: every contingent fact has an explanation; the universe is a contingent fact; therefore the universe has an explanation outside itself, ultimately in a necessary being.

(c) The modal argument from contingency: a being that exists necessarily is possible; whatever is possibly necessary is necessary; therefore a necessary being exists.

(d) The Thomistic argument from motion or change: whatever is in potentiality is actualized by something already in actuality; an essentially ordered series of actualizers cannot regress infinitely; therefore there is a first actualizer that is itself unmoved.

(e) The argument from the impossibility of an infinite regress of dependent causes, run without any premise about temporal beginnings.

For each formulation, give the number, a one- or two-sentence justification that names the specific premise doing the constraining work, and — wherever you assign a value below 1 — a brief description of the minimal terminus your score presupposes. Conclude with the overall ranking and a single sentence identifying which premise, across all five formulations, contributes most to theistic yield.
