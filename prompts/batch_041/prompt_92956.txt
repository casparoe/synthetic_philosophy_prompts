Ok, I'm finishing up a piece for a volume on philosophy of history and I could use a second pair of eyes. It's short — the working title is something like "Cycles, Loads, and the Limits of Quantitative History" — and the whole thing turns on a distinction I've been chewing on for months. I've pasted the draft below. What I want from you is a hard read, not encouragement. Specifically: (a) anything in it that is just factually wrong about what Turchin actually claims, (b) whether my central distinction is doing real work or is just the old prediction-vs-explanation distinction in new clothes, (c) whether my small-n objection is fair or whether I'm ignoring how the models are actually tested, and (d) any place where I'm being uncharitable in a way a referee would catch. Bullet points are fine, and please seperate the things you think are flatly wrong from the things that are merely contestable — I need to know which objections I have to answer and which I can just acknowledge. DRAFT

Cliodynamics asks whether history can be made a science, and its flagship result is the secular cycle: societies that oscillate over centuries between integrative and disintegrative phases, driven by the coupled dynamics of population, elite competition, and the fiscal capacity of the state. The claim is not that history repeats. It's that history has a dynamical structure, and that structure can be modelled, measured, and in principle forecast.

I want to argue that this debate has been badly framed on both sides. The critics say societies are too meaningful, too singular, too interpretive to be reduced to variables. The defenders say look at the fit statistics. Both are arguing about whether quantification is legitimate, and that's not where the action is. The interesting question is what kind of explanation a secular cycle is supposed to be.

So here is the distinction I want to run with. Call something a clock theory if it posits an endogenous oscillator with a characteristic period, such that phase is a real property of the system and knowing the phase buys you predictive leverage — you can say not just that a crisis is coming but roughly when, because the mechanism itself is timing the thing. Call something a load theory if it posits no period at all: strain accumulates, and release happens when some trigger arrives, and the trigger is not part of the theory. Under a load theory, the appearance of periodicity is an artifact of two facts — that recovery from a big release takes a long time, and that we observe only a handful of release events in any given record. Under a load theory, the question where in the cycle are we is a misleading question. The right question is how much slack is left.

Now: Turchin's formal apparatus is unmistakably clock-ish. Dynamical systems, phase space, sustained oscillation, the whole kit. But the mechanism as he describes it is load-ish. Elite overproduction accumulates, popular immiseration accumulates, fiscal distress accumulates, and then a trigger converts accumulated strain into crisis — and the trigger is explicitly not modelled, because it can't be. He even reaches for the image of a forest filling up with deadwood and some spark setting it off. That is a load picture, not a clock picture. There's no oscillator there. There's a pile and a match.

Which means the framework gets to have it both ways. It borrows the epistemic prestige of a clock theory — this is a science, there are phases, there are predictions — while retaining the flexibility of a load theory, where nothing has to happen at any particular time and a failed forecast is really just a trigger that hasn't arrived yet.

Three consequences I want to draw out.

First, falsifiability. A clock theory makes phase-and-timing predictions and can be wrong in a way that costs it something. A load theory makes conditional risk claims — if strain is high, then when a trigger comes, expect trouble — and those claims are almost impossible to falsify, because the antecedent is always satisfiable and the trigger is unspecified. If you can retreat from the first to the second whenever a prediction misfires, you have bought yourself immunity. I don't think that's dishonest, I think it's an unacknowledged slide, and it's the slide that does the work.

Second, the celebrated prediction of unrest in the 2020s. It's usually cited as a hit. But read under the load interpretation, it wasn't a forecast at all. It was a statement that the strain indicators were high and rising. That's a much weaker claim, and it's the one the mechanism actually supports.

Third, the reference-class problem. If the theory is a clock theory, the period is a property of the system, and you should be able to estimate it. But we have a handful of well-documented cases — a couple of English ones, Rome, Han and Tang China — and each case is an entire society with its own historiography, its own data quality, its own boundary problems. With an n that small, and with the cases not being independent draws in any obvious sense, the period isn't estimated, it's stipulated. There's a version of this that bites even on the load theory: elite overproduction is a ratio, and both numerator and denominator are things you have to decide how to count. Who is an elite? Counting rules are not innocent, they encode a politics.

One more, briefly. Turchin takes over Ibn Khaldun's asabiyya and glosses it as collective solidarity. But for Khaldun the concept is bound up with lineage, with religion, with the specific generational dynamics of dynasties founded out of tribal peripheries. Strip those out and what remains is a very thin variable — social cohesion, or group feeling, measured by whatever proxies are to hand and available to be invoked after the fact to explain whichever outcome occurred. Concept-stretching isn't automatically a sin. But it has to be paid for. What does the thin concept explain that the thick one didn't, and what did we lose in the trade?

Let me be clear about what I'm not saying. I'm not saying quantification is illegitimate, or that the mechanisms are unreal, or that the whole programme is ideology dressed as maths. Population pressure, elite competition, and fiscal strain are real and they really do interact. My claim is narrower and, I think, more annoying: cliodynamics is a load theory wearing a clock theory's clothes, and the ambiguity between the two is exactly what makes it look at once rigorous and unfalsifiable. If it came out and declared itself a load theory — if it said plainly that we are modelling the accumulation of strain and not the timing of its release — it would be a better research programme, and a considerably less exciting one.
