Forty years I spent as an analytical chemist — mass specs mostly, dirt and drinking water, the kind of work where if you can't put it in the machine you don't get to have an opinion about it. I retired two years ago and since then I've been reading philosophy of science like it's a job. Me and two other retired guys run a tiny podcast out of my basement, maybe forty listeners, and next month's guest is a philosopher of physics I've spent all summer reading. He defends string theory and the multiverse, and not in the usual shrug-y way — his line is that string theory is confirmed, just not by data in the ordinary sense. Three legs to it, as I understand him. One, the theory has nothing to tune: no free parameters to dial, so it can't be a curve fit. Two, it makes the fine-tuning of the constants stop looking like a coincidence, and he says that's a real explanatory gain and not just a restatement of the problem. Three, it explains why its rivals looked so good to us at the time — why the alternatives were attractive, for reasons the theory itself accounts for. He's got a phrase he leans on hard, "the theory pays for itself by making coincidences go away," and I've noticed he reaches for it whenever an objection gets sharp.

I want 9 or 10 questions that get into the actual machinery, and under each one a follow-up for the answer he's most likely to give — he's a smooth interviewee, so I want the follow-up pre-loaded rather than improvised. No soft stuff please, nothing like what got you into physics. At minimum I need one question each on:

- the measure problem — if the multiverse is infinite, what does it even mean to say our observed values are typical, and what happens when he says the theory supplies the measure itself;
- whether anything could make him lose: if the LHC keeps finding nothing, does that count against, and if the relevant scale is forever out of reach, is there any result that would;
- the difference between a theory being likely true and a theory being worth ten years of a person's life, since I suspect he slides between those two when he says "confirmed."

Then I've got three specific things I'd like him pressed on, and I'd like you to build questions around them:

1. I heard a story about a computer that searches the space of parameter-free theories and prints out the one that fits all current data — parameter-free by construction, and it makes the fine-tuning go away too, because it just says the constants are random draws. Does his argument confirm that thing? If not, what extra ingredient does string theory have that it lacks?

2. Fine-tuning talk seems to need a prior over the constants, and a flat prior over the constant isn't flat over its logarithm or its reciprocal, so which one is he using and does he think the choice is doing any work. I'd like to know what stops this from being the whole argument.

3. If a measure makes observers like us astronomically rarer than disembodied brains that hallucinate our data, does his own explanatory story turn around and bite itself.

Two more things I keep wondering about, work them in if they fit. There seem to be at least three different multiverses floating around — the branching-quantum one, the one from eternal expansion, and the string landscape — and his argument seems to work equally well for all three, which makes me nervous, because I'd like to know what a piece of evidence looks like that can't confirm everything shaped the right way. And there's the recent line of work where people use string theory to rule out whole families of low-energy theories as unable to be completed in a theory of quantum gravity at all; if that shrinking is what gives the theory its teeth, I want to know whether the original praise — nothing to tune — was ever doing any work, or whether he's quietly switched to a different and better theory halfway through.

End on the question I actually care about: name the result that would lower your credence. And if nothing can, I want the follow-up asking whether "confirmation" is the right word for what's left.
