I teach a 200-level ethics course on distributive justice -- 18 students, one 80-minute session -- and I want an activity that makes the levelling-down objection land as a feeling rather than a bullet point. You're a philosophy teacher who also designs games, so take the rough shape below as a starting point and tell me where its wrong.

Rough shape: students play a Ministry of Wellbeing with 12 units to allocate across five districts whose baseline levels are fixed and public (something like 1, 2, 3, 4, 6). Before they see any scorecard they have to commit to an allocation *and* to one sentence stating the principle they're following. Only then do I reveal that three different panels are scoring them: (a) plain sum of wellbeing, (b) sum of square roots of wellbeing, and (c) a rule I'm inventing on purpose because nobody's memorised it -- score = the median district's level × the number of districts within 2 units of the median, so what's being valued is solidarity/clustering, not equality as such. Then the twist: a vial becomes available that lowers the best-off district by one unit and does nothing for anyone else. Under (c) that's a straight improvement (with those baselines, 12 goes to 15); under (a) and (b) it's just damage. I want them to have to decide, rule by rule, whether to administer it, and to run straight into their own stage-one principle.

Give me: setup and materials, minute-by-minute timing, how the three rules get revealed so there's a reversal instead of a lecture, the debrief mapping each moment of the game onto the piece of the idea it's meant to deliver, what predictably goes wrong the first time and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. Don't let them hear the term 'levelling down' until the debrief.

Two things to think hard about. First, brainstorm a couple of alternative relational rules before you settle on (c) -- is a clustering rule the cleanest way to make the point that the objection isn't really about equality, or would something else bite harder? Second, I suspect prioritarianism dodges the objection for free (it's monotone in everyone's level, so it can never want the vial) and that the dodge is paid for elsewhere -- so give me a third option that makes rule (b) look bad too, and say whether my suspicion is right or whether I'm framing the whole thing wrongly.
