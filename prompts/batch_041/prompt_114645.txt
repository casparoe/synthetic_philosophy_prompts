I'm a third-year PhD student working on the epistemology of inference, and for the past several months I've been bothered by what looks to me like two literatures slowly collapsing into one another without either noticing. The first is the old regress about justifying deduction: an interlocutor grants every premise, grants the conditional that connects premises to conclusion, and still declines to draw the conclusion; when you hand him the further conditional that would license the step, the same refusal simply reappears one level up, and no finite stock of added conditionals ever gets the inference off the ground. The second is the more recent anxiety about whether logic is normative at all — whether the fact that a conclusion follows from some premises gives anyone a reason to believe it, or whether validity is a purely formal relation that is silent on what to think. Both literatures are decades old, both have a small repertoire of stock moves, and both seem to me to run in place: the participants keep restating their positions in marginally better notation, and the same objections are met with the same rebuttals, season after season. I'm not asking for a survey of who said what; I know the texts well enough, and I don't need a reading list. What I want is a diagnosis of the impasse itself.

My working hypothesis — which I'd like you to test rather than assume — is that the two impasses share a source, and that the source is not the regress but a disagreement about what kind of thing a reason to infer is. On one construal, a reason to infer is a further proposition one can believe (so the regress is real and threatens to be vicious); on another, it is a rule one follows rather than a premise one believes, in which case the demand for an added conditional is simply the wrong demand; on a third, it is a disposition or practice one merely has, in which case no justification at the level of content was ever on offer. If that taxonomy is right, then the disagreement is neither cleanly substantive nor cleanly verbal: it may be two groups answering different questions under a single ambiguous heading — one asking what makes an inference correct, the other asking what makes it rational to perform — with the apparent conflict generated by a word doing double duty. But I hold that loosely, and I can see the opposite verdict being correct: that there is a genuine, hidden disagreement about standards of justification (propositional versus doxastic, or the autonomy of inference) that the parties have never brought into the open, or that the whole thing is an ill-posed question that dissolves once we stop asking for a justification of basic rules. Please engage the actual positions concretely enough that the diagnosis bites — say whether this is a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, a case of different questions under one name, or a difference in starting intuitions that neither side can argue for; identify whether some covert empirical or formal question (about how reasoners in fact operate, say) is doing the real work; and, given your diagnosis, tell me what if anything would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and why. I'd rather have a defended diagnosis with reasons than a verdict, and I'd like you to flag the point at which your own diagnosis would be falsified.
