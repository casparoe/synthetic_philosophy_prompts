I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, about halfway through a chapter on sortition, and I've hit a snag that's more conceptual than historical so I figured I'd just ask it flat out. The thing that keeps bugging me is that the citizens' assembly template — the one Ireland used in its assembly experiment, the French climate convention, the permanent citizens' council in the German-speaking community of Belgium, the Gdańsk city assemblies, and basically every how-to manual and OECD report that's been written off the back of them — is the same design in its bones, and it's a strange design. You recruit by lottery, but never a plain lottery: you stratify the sample so it mirrors the population on age, sex, region, education, sometimes migration background, sometimes urban vs rural. You pay people, or at least cover their costs, often a per diem. The design is supposed to avoid the people who put themselves forward — the whole point is that you didn't choose to be there and can't be there because you want something out of it. You sit them in a room for a few weekends with trained facilitators and expert witnesses, and the voting rule varies (bare majority in some cases, supermajority in others, which I think is itself telling). And then the output is *recommendations*: the assembly proposes, and the parliament, or the government, or the whole electorate in a referendum, decides. Meanwhile the one institution where we've let a randomly selected group actually decide — no ratification step, no veto, no sending it back for another round — is the criminal jury: drawn by a plain unstratified lottery off the rolls, paid a token fee, given no facilitators, and required to return a verdict rather than a suggestion. And even there we don't fully trust the lottery, since each side gets to strike a few people for no stated reason.

What I want isn't a defense or a critique of lottocracy, I've read plenty of those. I want a reconstruction of what this actual practice — the template rather than the theory behind it — has to believe. Concretely: what view of political legitimacy does the two-stage design presuppose, that a random body is good enough to constitute an authoritative voice but not good enough to decide? Does stratifying by demographic category quietly commit the thing to a mirror or descriptive theory of representation, and if so is that consistent with the stock sortitionist argument that the point of the lottery is precisely to dissolve interest and faction and get people reasoning about a common good? What do the payment and the screening-out of volunteers presuppose about motivation, and about whose political participation is trustworthy? Is the practice strictly committed to any of this, or does it just sit comfortably with several incompatible accounts — a Condorcet-style epistemic story, a pure fair-procedure story, a consent or fair-play story, something about the division of labour between deliberation and decision? Which rivals does it actually rule out, as opposed to merely not needing? And if you swapped the presupposed view for one of the rivals, what would have to change in the design — what's load-bearing and what's decoration? I'd much rather be told the practice is underdetermined between two named theories than that it's underdetermined, full stop.
