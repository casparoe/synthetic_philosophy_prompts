I retired from a chemistry department about six years ago and since then I've been doing a kind of self-directed philosophy degree, mostly reading and sitting in on a reading group that meets above the bookshop on Thursdays. In March its my turn to lead, and I've picked risk-weighted expected utility — Lara Buchak's thing, from her 2013 book Risk and Rationality. Eight people, all comfortable with probability (two retired engineers, one actuary), none of them have done any decision theory. Fifty minutes with a whiteboard, but I've been told I have to leave room for arguing, so call it thirty-five minutes of explaining.

Don't explain REU to me, I've read the book. Plan how I should teach it. What has to be in place before the risk function even makes sense — my hunch is they first need to see expected utility as weighting the gaps between ranked outcomes by cumulative probabilities rather than weighting the outcomes themselves, but I don't know if that's the right first brick or if it should come later. What single case should carry the whole thing, and what does it need? I want them to feel the rank-dependence in their bones — that the same 20% slice of probability counts for more or less depending on what sits above it — and I suspect a three-outcome case does that better than the Allais matrix everyone reaches for, but you tell me. The misconceptions: I'm already worried about three. One, that r is just a distortion of each probability on its own, rather than something that depends on where the outcome sits in the ranking. Two, that risk-averse and risk-seeking are the only two settings, when I gather r can be convex in one stretch and concave in another. Three, that this is a descriptive theory of how people actually choose rather than a claim about what's rational. Where in the sequence does each get planted, and where do I head it off? What question do I put to the room at the end that would tell me whether they've got it, rather than whether they can recite the formula? And what should I openly refuse to cover in thirty-five minutes — I assume the representation theorem, but maybe I'm wrong about that. One more thing: the actuary will ask, inside the first five minutes, how this differs from rank-dependent utility, and I don't have a crisp answer. Whatever order you give me, make sure that answer falls out of it naturally instead of being bolted on at the end.
