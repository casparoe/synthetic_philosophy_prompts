For the better part of a year a reading group I belong to has been working through the literature on the norm of assertion, and we have reached the stage where we can recite one another's moves in advance. The positions are familiar enough: that one may assert only what one knows; that truth is the operative standard and that belief, justification, and adequate evidence either drop out or enter as separate conditions; that the real standard is belief, or justified belief, with the knowledge requirement an artefact of how the examples are described; and the sceptical view that there is no single constitutive norm, since 'assertion' may not name a single act. The dialectic is equally familiar. The knowledge camp produces cases in which a speaker says something true and well supported but plainly does not know it — the lottery ticket, the confident but premature diagnosis, the claim about a match result read half an hour ago — and reports that the assertion is improper; the opposition replies that the impropriety is conversational or prudential, or that those cases are really about belief, or that the judgement is tracking the speaker's liability to criticism rather than the standard the act itself answers to. Each side then accuses the other of explaining the data away rather than accounting for it, and we adjourn. Our group is mixed — two epistemologists, a linguist who keeps observing that 'assert' is not a natural kind, and me — and I suspect that is part of why the impasse is so vivid to us. What I want from you is not another round of the dispute but a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and one I would be pleased to see refuted, has two parts. First, I suspect that 'norm' is doing at least three different jobs in this literature and that the parties are often not disagreeing about the standard at all but about which of the three the phrase 'the norm of assertion' ought to name: a rule that partly constitutes the act, so that a breach yields a defective instance of the act rather than an impermissible one; a requirement of permission or warrant, so that a breach yields something the speaker was not entitled to do; and a rule of uptake, governing what an audience may take for granted once the act has been performed. These come apart, and a standard that is plausible for one may be hopeless for another — truth, for instance, looks like a serviceable candidate for the first and a poor one for the third, since a hearer who learns that a speaker was merely accurate acquires less than a hearer who learns that she knew. Second, I suspect the vignettes that supply nearly all the evidence are systematically confounded: they vary the speaker's epistemic position while holding fixed the audience, the stakes, the speaker's alternatives, and the availability of excuses, so that what informants report may be closer to a verdict about blameworthiness than about the standard the act answers to.

Please assess this. Set out each side as it actually argues, then say which portions of the impasse are verbal and which are substantive, and whether the two camps are in fact answering different questions under one heading — one about the constitutive standard of a technical act-type, the other about when it is permissible to tell someone something. Where the disagreement is substantive, say what it turns on: facts, standards of evidence, which cases count as data, or starting intuitions that neither side can argue for. Where it is verbal, say what the dispute is nevertheless about, since a choice of terminology with theoretical consequences is not nothing. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result about which candidate norms can bear which explanatory load, a different kind of evidence, or a change of question — or an argument that none of these is available and the standoff should be expected to persist. To keep the diagnosis honest, test it against a few cases the literature rarely uses: a forecaster's public prediction, a hedged claim in a scientific paper, a diagnosis delivered to a patient at the edge of the evidence, an entry in a private diary, a step asserted inside a proof, and a deliberate simplification told to a child. I am not asking you to settle which norm is correct, and I do not need references; I need to know what we have actually been disagreeing about.
