I'm a postdoctoral fellow working on constitutional theory and the intellectual history of Catholic social teaching, and in a few weeks I'll present a paper at a small, deliberately heterogeneous workshop on subsidiarity. My audience will fall into roughly four clusters, and I already know what each of them hears when I open my mouth: (1) EU law specialists, who work with subsidiarity as a ground of judicial review of legislative acts, are accustomed to a standard of review so deferential that they can barely recall a successful challenge, and can recite the early-warning machinery and the ordinary proportionality test in their sleep; (2) two German public lawyers, who will tell me that their constitution does contain an explicit necessity condition on federal legislation but that their constitutional court has made it practically impossible to fail, and who are professionally invested in the thought that European integration must remain answerable to something like a national responsibility for integration; (3) a Swiss participant or two, for whom subsidiarity is a constitutional directive about how tasks are allocated among confederation, cantons and communes, and pointedly not a test that any court applies; and (4) an American federalism scholar, who arrives via enumerated powers and the anti-commandeering cases and is allergic to natural-law vocabulary on principle.

The thesis I'm defending is narrower than the room expects and, I think, uncomfortable for all four clusters: that we run together two logically independent principles under one word — a principle for allocating competences in the first place, and a principle governing how a competence, once allocated, may be exercised — and that most of the apparent disagreement over whether subsidiarity is a legal norm, a political aspiration, or a piece of rhetoric dissolves once you say which of the two you mean. My further claim is that only the second reading is adjudicable anywhere, because it takes the allocation of competences as given and asks only about necessity, while the first is where the real rhetorical work is done and is, as usually stated, not justiciable in any system. It follows that anyone who invokes subsidiarity to argue for devolution while defending the principle in the vocabulary of the second reading has quietly changed the subject — and I intend to say so out loud, which is why I need to be ready.

Please write me a Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from this room, not from some general readership of the literature, ordered by how likely each is multiplied by how much damage it does, with each objection attributed to the cluster that will raise it and phrased the way that cluster would phrase it — I want the EU lawyer's version of an objection and the Swiss version of what looks like the same objection as separate entries, since they will land differently. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first thirty seconds, before anyone has to ask; which two or three of the objections are really one objection wearing different clothes; which single exchange the paper's credibility will rest on; and finally, the objection I am hoping nobody raises. No citations, no reading list, no survey of the literature — I know the literature; what I need is the question period.
