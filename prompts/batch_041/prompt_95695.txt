For thirty years I was a fisheries biologist; since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more appetite than system, and I would like to put the two together. Please work out for me what our actual treatment of farmed aquatic animals presupposes, philosophically speaking, and write it up as the substance of a paper of roughly four thousand words.

The practice I have in mind is the one I spent my career inside, and it contains a discrepancy I have never been able to resolve. On the finfish side, the codes of practice and the assurance schemes I worked under expect a fish to be rendered insensible before it is bled — percussive stunning, electrical stunning, and in some operations carbon dioxide narcosis, which immobilizes an animal within a few minutes while giving no reason to think it has lost consciousness. Meanwhile several methods remain in wide commercial use that the scientific advisory bodies have faulted for years: asphyxiation in air, live chilling in ice slurry, the two together. On the crustacean side the movement has been in the opposite direction. Decapod crustaceans and cephalopods have been formally recognized as sentient for the purposes of welfare policy in some jurisdictions, and almost nothing has followed from the recognition: crabs and lobsters are still sold alive and boiled at the point of sale, and the slaughter standards that govern the finfish side of the industry do not reach them at all. There is a second discrepancy that I notice more as a working scientist than as a reader of philosophy. The very assurance schemes that require stunning before slaughter assess the welfare of the animals under their care by mortality rates, growth, fin condition, lesion counts and similar indicators of health and functioning. So one part of the regime appears to treat welfare as a matter of how an animal feels, and another part as a matter of how well its body is doing.

What I want is a reconstruction of the theory this practice presupposes, not a survey of what others have written. In particular: what criterion of moral standing does the practice assume — a nervous system of a particular kind, a set of functional capacities such as avoidance learning, analgesic responsiveness and motivational trade-offs, or something else — and does a single criterion explain both the protection extended to fish and the near-total absence of protection for crustaceans, or does the practice quietly need two? What does the whole apparatus of stun-then-kill assume about the harm of death? It looks to me as though it assumes that what is wrong is the suffering and not the dying, since rendering an animal insensible first would otherwise achieve nothing; tell me whether that really is presupposed, or whether an account on which death itself is a harm could make sense of the same rules. Which of these commitments is the practice genuinely bound to, and which is merely a comfortable fit that several incompatible theories would equally license? And what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced — by a graded rather than threshold conception of sentience, for instance, or by a precautionary rule that treats a capacity to suffer as something to be assumed until disproven rather than proven before it is respected?

I will supply my own references, so please do not gesture at literature I cannot check. Where the empirical evidence is genuinely unsettled — whether crustaceans feel pain, how long a fish in ice slurry remains conscious — say so plainly, and distinguish what turns on the science from what the philosophy decides regardless. Introduce the vocabulary as you go; I am still learning it. And tell me where the practice is underdetermined, that is, where I should stop saying that a rule presupposes a given view and say instead that the view is one among several that would leave the rule untouched.
