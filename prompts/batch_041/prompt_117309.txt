I want to understand a maneuver that circulates in discussions of acausal threat, and then to hear whether you think it works.

Set the situation up as follows. Two agents, X and Y, are causally isolated: nothing either does can make a causal difference to the other. They are, however, computational processes, and Y's process contains a model of X's decision procedure; Y's policy is a function of what that model reports, and X's action is the output of that very same procedure. So although no signal passes between them, X's choice of decision procedure and Y's conduct are linked by the fact that both are functions of one and the same algorithm. Y's policy: where the model reports COMPLY, Y takes the concession it wants and leaves X alone; where the model reports REFUSE, Y harms X. X knows this, and Y knows that X knows it.

Now the maneuver. Rather than merely resolving to refuse, X is said to adopt a surrogate goal: an additional preference, general rather than tailored to this particular threatener, whose satisfaction is advanced precisely when threats of this kind fail. The thought is that once X holds such a goal, the harm Y inflicts on refusal is no longer a pure loss. It becomes the price of something X wants, and perhaps evidence that X has got it. Anticipating this, Y computes that threatening X yields no concession, and so never threatens.

Explain this to me. In particular:

(a) What is the surrogate goal supposed to accomplish that a bare resolution to refuse cannot, given that in both cases the operative thing is the procedure X runs rather than any episode of resolve?

(b) What work is the temporal language — adopting the goal in advance — doing, given that X and Y are not situated in a shared temporal order, and that X's procedure is what it is regardless of when it was fixed?

(c) Must the goal be genuinely held rather than strategically professed? And can its content be specified precisely enough to be a goal at all — what state of the world is it for a threatener to be frustrated, and can an agent be said to bring that state about by having refused?

(d) The interaction problem: if Y can also adopt a general surrogate goal — say, one advanced whenever an agent that adopts anti-threat surrogate goals is harmed — what settles the outcome, and is there any equilibrium here that does not simply reduce to which party is willing to absorb the larger loss?

Then commit. Say whether you regard the surrogate-goal maneuver as a genuine solution to acausal threat or as a redescription of the problem in a different vocabulary, and give your reasons. If you think it is genuine, state precisely what must be true of X's and Y's decision theories for it to work; if you think it is not, say what the maneuver is actually accomplishing on the occasions when it appears to work.
