I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the duty to remember and the case for forgetting, by going through concrete cases and trusting my reactions rather than any theory, and I would like you to tell me what principle — or small set of principles — best captures the pattern, what it implies for cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts I would have to give up.

Here is where I come out. A woman whose brother was killed by a drunk driver decides, without pretending it did not happen, to stop dwelling on it: she skips the sentencing, never raises it with her family, and asks that others not bring it up. I do not think this is a failure; I would call it admirable. A country emerging from a dictatorship grants its torturers amnesty in exchange for full testimony before a public commission whose records are preserved. I find the amnesty distasteful and would still accept it. The same country, by contrast, seals the records, forbids the subject in schools, and demolishes the detention center. I think that is wrong even if it buys a calmer politics. A man's father was a guard at a camp and is long dead; the son tells his own children nothing, saying the past is the past. I think that is a betrayal — of the victims, not of the father. A friend once witnessed something humiliating about me and never mentions it; I would be glad if he genuinely forgot it. A town keeps a permanent public archive listing, by name, the residents who informed on their neighbors during an occupation, long after everyone named is dead. I think keeping it is right, though I cannot say it does anyone any good. The same town renames its streets after the victims but also removes the only plaque that recorded the perpetrators' names; I feel that is a loss, though not an outrage. And a museum of a massacre proposes to let some of its records decay, because the descendants of the victims have asked to be allowed to stop being defined by it. I am torn, but I lean toward honoring the request.

My tentative principle is this: we owe remembrance to those who were wronged, and forgetting is permitted only when it is the wronged themselves who ask for it; where the wrong was one we committed, remembering it is owed to the wronged. Please test that against the cases, repair it where it fails, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which you think I should abandon. If it helps to distinguish remembering from merely knowing, or commemoration from acknowledgment, or personal from institutional memory, do so — but each distinction should earn its keep by deciding a case.
