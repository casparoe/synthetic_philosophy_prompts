For a while now I have been circling a particular vertigo that I first met in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, in the passages where he builds typogenetics, his little formal world of strands and the enzymes that cut, splice, and rearrange them. What stays with me is not any single rule but the shape of the whole contraption: a strand of nucleotides is read as a message, and what it spells out is machinery that reads and writes messages, and in the right arrangement that machinery turns back on the strand that produced it, and the loop closes. Self-replication and self-reference, I keep thinking, are the same trick in two costumes; the strand stands to the enzyme it encodes the way a sentence that declares its own unprovability stands to the proof it talks about. I would like a story that lets a reader feel that rather than be told it.

So: write me a short story built on that isomorphism. I picture a setting where it can breathe, and you should choose the one that lets the doubling be the plot rather than the backdrop. It could be a near-future laboratory where a researcher has been running a typogenetic artificial-life system for years and one lineage of strands has begun to do something she cannot classify; it could be a far stranger world in which such formal strands simply are how life is written and the inhabitants have never known anything else. What I do not want is a lecture in costume. No scene where a patient senior scientist explains the central dogma or Gödel numbering to a novice, no tidy allegory in which the biology merely illustrates a theorem, no closing paragraph that steps back to tell me what it all meant. I want the self-reference to be something the reader watches happen and only afterward recognizes as self-reference, the way one suddenly sees the figure in a drawing that had looked like mere shading.

One more thing, and it is the part I care most about. The isomorphism is not perfect, and I would like the story to brush against the place where it strains. A sentence that points at itself does so through a code that was built precisely to let it point; a strand does nothing so direct. It is read, and the machinery it yields acts on strands in general, and only the entire system, looping back through its chemistry, manages anything we would dare call reference. If your story can carry a character to the edge of that difference, the difference between a self that is a single pointing finger and a self that is a loop distributed across a whole metabolism, I will consider it a success. End on an image rather than a moral. Keep it to roughly fifteen hundred to twenty-five hundred words, and write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables, just the story told the way one would tell it aloud.
