Some months ago I left a secure position to work on a long project of my own, and I deliberately arranged it in two stages: an early stretch in which pressing on is plainly the right thing to do, and a later stretch after which pressing on is a mistake and I should stop and salvage what I can. I assumed I would always know which stretch I was in. In practice the two feel identical from the inside — same desk, same routine, the same slow progress — and I have come to think I am in something very close to the position the absent-minded driver problem describes: a driver who passes two look-alike exits and cannot tell which one has been reached, so that what is rational before setting out and what seems rational from behind the wheel come apart.

What I want is the most important implications of that problem for someone actually in my position. Rank them, give the reasoning behind each, and state the practical consequence. Three features of my case are not part of the usual telling, and I suspect they matter. First, my confusion is not even-handed: the later stretch is far more repetitive, and I lose track of where I am much more often there, so the fact that I am unsure is itself weak evidence about which stage I am in. Second, I can leave myself a written instruction — but writing it costs me a working day, and reading it does not by itself make me follow it. Third, the standard answer appears to have the driver randomise and then not remember having randomised, which strikes me as a strange thing to require of someone who owns a pen.

I am not after a survey of the puzzle or a reading list. I want to know what follows for how I should decide when I catch myself at a checkpoint, for how I should have set the thing up before I began, and for how much it would be worth paying to remove the uncertainty about which stage I am in. Where the usual treatment depends on assumptions my case violates, say which, and what takes their place.
