So I've somehow been roped into running a 90-minute session for the volunteer guides at a small local museum - smart adults, no formal history training, and one of them keeps announcing that King Arthur obviously wasnt real because he's a 'legend'. My brief is narrow: by the end they should be able to field the front-desk question 'was X real?' without either the credulous 'well the old stories say so' or the smug debunking thing.

Don't explain the topic to me, plan the session.

I want the whole thing hung on a device I've been kicking around, which is to treat 'is this history yet?' as three seperate dials that people always collapse into one: (a) how costly the story is to the people telling it - does it make their own side look bad, (b) whether the corroboration is genuinely independent or just the same story wearing a different hat, and (c) how it sits with what we know from outside the story altogether (pottery, tax records, some Egyptian victory stela boasting about wiping out Israel, that kind of thing). My hunch is that the Troy / Arthur / Exodus fights are mostly people turning different dials without realising it. Tell me if the device is actually any good, and if so where in the sequence it belongs - I have a feeling it can't go first, but I don't know what it has to come after.

Then: the order the pieces have to go in and why, i.e. what genuinely depends on what. Can you talk about independent attestation before they've stopped treating 'myth' as a synonym for false? Can you talk about external corroboration before they understand why a story being old isn't itself evidence?

Pick which of the three cases should carry the whole thing, and defend that pick against the obvious objection that it's the one everyone already does. Say what the other two are for.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, with exactly where in the sequence I head each off: myth means it didn't happen, absence of evidence is evidence of absence, they found a burnt layer so the war happened, the kernel-of-truth fallacy, all-or-nothing thinking (either the whole exodus happened or none of it did). And one closing question I can ask that tells me whether they've picked up the dependency structure rather than just the vocabulary.

Last thing - tell me what to cut at this level, and tell me to say out loud that I'm cutting it.
