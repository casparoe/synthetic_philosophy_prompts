Read H. G. Wells's The Island of Doctor Moreau through the species problem in philosophy of biology. I am less interested in the familiar playing-God theme than in what the novel assumes about the nature of species. Moreau's project of transforming animals into human-like beings seems to presuppose that species boundaries are both real and violable, while the Beast Folk's reversion to animality suggests that the animal nature cannot be erased. Does the novel therefore depend on a pre-Darwinian essentialism about species, or does it dramatize the instability of species concepts? Bring in the distinction between species as natural kinds with essences and species as historical individuals or populations. If species are individuals—particular lineages without essential traits—what are the Beast Folk? A new species, a hybrid, a failed artifact, or something the categories cannot capture? Does the horror of the novel survive a thoroughly Darwinian, non-essentialist view of species, or does it require the reader to believe that crossing species boundaries is a metaphysical violation? I want a reading that takes both the novel and the species problem seriously, identifies which scenes do the philosophical work (the recitation of the Law, the Leopard Man's reversion, the narrator's final inability to see human faces without seeing the Beast Folk), and says where the novel resists the reading. Take a definite position on whether Wells is an essentialist, a proto-population thinker, or something else, and defend it. Also address whether the novel conflates a metaphysical claim about species boundaries with a moral claim about human superiority, and whether that conflation is a flaw or the point. The response should be committed, not a survey of possible readings.
