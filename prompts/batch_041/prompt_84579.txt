I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group this term, and we've spent two sessions on epiphenomenalism — the view that phenomenal properties (the felt redness of an afterimage, the sting of a pain) are caused by physical processes in the brain but themselves cause nothing. I can state the position abstractly well enough, but I keep losing the thread at the point where it is supposed to undercut itself. As I reconstruct it: our judgments and reports about experience are themselves events in the world, so if epiphenomenalism is true, my judgment that I am having a reddish experience must be caused by whatever physical state underlies the experience rather than by the experience itself. But then the judgment would have occurred whether or not the phenomenal property was there, which seems to strip it of evidential force — the epiphenomenalist ends up like a witness who concedes her testimony would have come out word-for-word the same had the events she is testifying to never happened. I want to understand exactly where the pressure comes from, how much of it is genuine, and what it would take to defuse it.

To keep this concrete, I have been working with two cases. First, imagine a brain-reading device that, from cortical activity alone, predicts a subject's verbal report of what a stimulus looked like half a second before she speaks, and can also trigger an indistinguishable report by direct stimulation with no stimulus present at all. Second, imagine separating two flavors of epiphenomenalism: one on which qualia are inert but their physical bases cause our judgments, so that judgments covary reliably with qualia without being caused by them; another on which qualia are inert and the judgments are caused by states that merely happen to accompany them. I suspect the first flavor is the only defensible one, but I cannot tell whether covarying reliably without being caused buys the judgments any epistemic standing, and I also worry the first flavor smuggles causation back in — if the physical base necessitates the quale, does the quale not end up in the causal history of the judgment after all, the way a shadow is in the history of what casts it? I would also like to know what work the evolutionary version of the worry does: if phenomenal properties do no causal work, selection cannot have shaped them, so their reliable co-occurrence with the neural states that do the work starts to look like an unexplained coincidence.

Please don't hand me a bibliography or survey who has said what; I'm asking you to reason this through using the cases above, and I would rather you argue in your own terms than lean on named positions I have no way to check. Structure the response under these headings, in this order: "The Position and the Puzzle" (roughly 150 words, restating epiphenomenalism and the paradox in your own words); "Where the Pressure Comes From" (roughly 300 words, laying the argument out in steps and saying which premise a critic should attack); "Escape Routes and What They Cost" (roughly 500 words, at least three distinct responses, each with its price, including at minimum the covariation or structural-coherence route, the grounding or constitution route, and the illusionist route that denies the datum); and "My Verdict" (roughly 250 words). In that last section, commit: tell me which escape route you think actually works and why the others fail, rather than laying out the options for me to choose among, and name the single assumption your preferred route rests on that you would most expect a critic to attack.
