I'm a retired chemist — 35 years in atmospheric measurement, mostly building and babysitting instruments — and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy and decision theory, stuff I never had time for. Something's been nagging at me and I'd like an actual principle out of it, not a bumper sticker.

Here's what set it off. I read somewhere (a statistican, I forget who) that if you're a Bayesian — degrees of belief, update on evidence, pick whatever action looks best given what you believe — then getting extra information for free before you decide can never leave you worse off in expectation. Worst case you ignore it and do what you'd have done anyway. On its face that makes 'ignorance is bliss' impossible for a rational agent. Seems airtight when I write it out. And yet:

- In my last year at the lab a colleague offered to re-run my main dataset on the new spectrometer before I submitted. It might well have sunk my pet hypothesis. I said yes without thinking twice, and I still think that was obviously the right call.
- A few years back a scan turned up something benign — nothing to be done about it either way — and my GP told me anyway. I'd have been happier not knowing; the worry cost me a couple of months. I think she shouldn't have mentioned it.
- I won't look at my pension balance in a bad month. I know I won't sell — I never do — and looking just wrecks the week. Not looking feels correct.
- A mate of mine is trying to stop drinking. He's refusing the genetic test that would tell him his risk, and I think he's right: he says knowing he was high-risk would make him fatalistic and he'd drink more. So for him the information would literally make his behaviour worse.
- A judge tosses evidence that's plainly relevant because the police got it illegally. I think that's right, and I don't think it's the same flavour of 'better not to know' as the others — that feels like a seperate question — but I couldn't tell you why.
- Same year as the pension business: I had a test for something that came back fine, and even though the result couldn't have changed any treatment I'd have wanted the number anyway. I just wanted to know. Still do.

My own stab at a principle is that it's all about whether I can *do* something with it — worth having if it changes what I'd do, pointless if not. But that gets me maybe half of them. The pension case has an action sitting right there (sell) that I know I won't take, and the last case has no action at all and I still wanted the number, while the scan case also had no action and I'd rather have stayed in the dark. So 'actionable' isn't it, at least not by itself.

Is there one principle, or a small set, that gets all six, or close? Put it precisely, and tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed — a doctor wondering whether to tell a patient about a risk nobody can do anything about, when the patient has already said they'd rather not be told. Then be straight with me about which of my six it has to throw overboard. And at the end, say how confident you are in the whole thing and point to the single step you're least sure of.
