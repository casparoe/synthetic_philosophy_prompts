I have recently moved into a role that runs paired teams in Copenhagen and in Chicago, and the contrast has become something of an obsession for me. The same piece of good work is announced and applauded in Chicago and quietly absorbed, almost apologized for, in Copenhagen; a Danish colleague eventually explained to me the Law of Jante, the ten-rule code of conduct that Aksel Sandemose placed in his 1933 satirical novel and that Scandinavians now invoke, half in jest and half in earnest, to name the local allergy to anyone who stands out. An Australian friend tells me her country has its own term for the same reflex, the tall poppy syndrome, though she insists it cuts differently, being more about resenting the one who rose than about protecting the group. All of this has landed on me while I am working through a course on nineteenth-century political thought, where I keep reading Tocqueville on the democratic passion for equality, on the tyranny of the majority, and on self-interest rightly understood. The phrase the American Dream, I have learned, is itself a twentieth-century coinage, so none of these three labels was available to him; the phenomena they name, however, seem to me to sit squarely on the questions he cared about most.

What I would like from you is a careful reconstruction of the position Tocqueville would most plausibly have taken on these three things, argued from his own premises rather than from anything he actually said, since none of it existed in his lifetime. I am especially interested in whether he would read the Law of Jante as the natural terminus of the democratic passion for equality, that is, as the point at which the leveling impulse turns on any individual who rises above the rest, or instead as a survival of something older and pre-democratic, a small-town remnant rather than a democratic product. I want the same treatment for the tall poppy syndrome, which I suspect he would have diagnosed quite differently, and for the American Dream, which I am inclined to see as a fragile counter-current to the very egalitarianism he thought irresistible, though I am prepared to be told I have misread him. Along the way, tell me which of his actual doctrines the reconstruction leans on most heavily, where it has to be stretched or supplemented before it will carry the weight, and, importantly, what observations or arguments would count against the reconstruction and suggest he would in fact have gone the other way. Distinguish throughout between what follows tightly from things he plainly held and what is mere extrapolation, and be honest about whether the exercise is underdetermined to the point that several incompatible readings are equally defensible.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to someone, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
