For the last several months I have been convening a small reading group on explanation in biology and cognitive science, and two of its regulars have settled into positions that strike me as genuinely opposed yet almost impossible for either of them to state in terms the other will accept. The first, a philosopher of mind, holds what I have come to call the crane-only view: every instance of apparent design, order, or novelty in the living world — minds and languages included — is in principle the product of cumulative selection acting on small, selectable variations, so any explanation that bottoms out in "and then the right kind of organization arose" is a skyhook in disguise, and the recurring pull toward such explanations is precisely the vice we ought to name as the mirror image of greedy reductionism. The second, a graduate student in evolutionary developmental biology, holds the structuralist view: some properties belong to the arrangement rather than to the parts, higher-level regularities can be explanatorily autonomous and can even constrain the behaviour of the components, and the crane/skyhook vocabulary is rigged, because a crane is simply whatever turns out to decompose into selectable steps and a skyhook whatever does not, so the distinction can never be falsified by any case and merely redescribes each outcome after the fact. When they argue, each accuses the other of begging the question, and each ends up paraphrasing the other's position in a way the other rejects outright.

I would like you to draft the joint statement they could both sign if they committed to a genuine adversarial collaboration. Give me, first, a numbered list of claims both can affirm — about what selection may and may not be assumed to accomplish, about what it would take to have "reduced" some phenomenon, and about the status of higher-level regularities — each phrased so that neither party concedes anything merely by signing it. Then state the residual disagreements precisely, with each side's formulation written in wording that side would accept for itself, and set them out in a table. Identify the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them; I expect these to include whether any criterion for crane versus skyhook is available independently of whether a reduction has actually been carried out; whether the bare possibility of a crane for some phenomenon is itself any reason to think a crane exists for it; whether appeals to organization or to downward constraint do explanatory work or merely restate the phenomenon to be explained; whether "greedy reductionism" picks out a determinate error or collapses into a label for whatever reduction one happens not to like; and whether self-organization, evolvability, or the origin of life constitute in-principle limits on selectionist explanation or merely gaps that further cranes will eventually fill.

For each crux, give the case, experiment, or argument that both parties would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that case or argument shows — phrased, again, so that each side can accept its own prediction as a fair statement of its view. Where you judge that part of their dispute is really verbal, say which part and why; and where one side's position turns out to be unfalsifiable as stated, say what minimal revision would make it testable.
