I am preparing a long-form interview for a small podcast on self-reference and cognition. My guest is a philosopher of cognitive science who defends what she calls the inviolate-level thesis: roughly, that any system able to represent its own representational states must contain at least one level of description that is causally efficacious in producing those states but is never itself represented within the system. She treats the tangled hierarchy of two hands each drawing the other as an intuitive illustration, but I want the conversation to be about the thesis rather than about self-reference in general.

I have been trying to think of a case that I have not seen discussed in this connection, and I would like the interview to use it as a running example. Imagine a compiler that can rewrite its own source code, including the module that decides which rewrites to perform. On the face of it, the system represents and modifies the very rules that govern its own self-modification, so it looks like a system with no inviolate level. But one might reply that the inviolate level has simply moved one meta-level up, to whatever fixed semantics allows the rewriting language to be interpreted. I want questions that press her on whether the inviolate-level thesis is a conceptual necessity or merely an engineering contingency, and that make her say what would count as a genuine counterexample.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. They should go beyond having her restate the thesis. At least one question should ask how she individuates levels in the first place, so that the claim that there must be an inviolate level is not trivially true. At least one question should address the standard objection that the inviolate level is just the implementation level and carries no independent explanatory weight; include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. At least one question should draw a distinction I have been mulling over: between a level that is inviolate in the sense of being unrepresented and a level that is merely unrepresented at a given time but could in principle be represented later. Ask her whether that distinction does any work for her thesis, and include a follow-up. At least one question should ask whether the thesis has any testable consequences for AI interpretability, or whether it is compatible with every architecture we can imagine. Another should ask how the thesis handles a system that can modify its own inviolate level, if such a thing is possible. A final question should ask what she would give up first if the thesis turned out to be false.

For each question, supply a brief follow-up that anticipates the answer she is most likely to give, so that I can keep the conversation moving without repeating myself. Do not ask generic questions about how she became interested in the topic. Avoid asking for reading lists or citations; I want the questions themselves to carry the philosophical weight.

At the end, add a short note in which you state how confident you are that these are the questions the thesis most needs to face, and identify the single point in the sequence about which you are least sure. Write in British English.
