I'm a doctoral candidate in political theory, and I'm applying to a research centre's visiting fellowship for one semester. The application requires a two-page research proposal, and I'd like you to draft it.

My half-formed idea, which I'd like you to take seriously enough to argue with rather than simply adopt: the debate over welfare conditionality has been conducted almost entirely as a dispute about desert. Does someone who can work but doesn't deserve support? Does reciprocity justify work requirements? Do sanctions express contempt for claimants, or respect them? I think that framing mislocates the work conditionality actually does. Entitlement to most means-tested benefits turns on facts the state cannot observe: whether someone is genuinely unable to work, genuinely searching, genuinely living apart from a partner, genuinely caring for a relative. On the reading I want to develop, conditionality is a screening device. It manufactures a signal by making the benefit costly to obtain, so that those with alternatives select themselves out. On that reading, the moral vocabulary of deservingness is not the justification of the policy at all but a public-facing description of the mechanism, and its work is epistemic rather than retributive.

If that is right, the interesting question is not whether claimants deserve support but this: when a policy deliberately makes a benefit unattractive in order to deter the ineligible, it knowingly makes it unattractive to the eligible as well, and the eligible bear the screening cost. What makes that permissible, and how much of it is permissible? I want a proposal that puts that question at the centre and treats the desert literature as the foil rather than the subject.

The proposal should do the following.

State the question so that it has a determinate answer, and say what kind of answer it is: a constraint on permissible policy design, a threshold, a set of conditions, a decision procedure.

Say why the existing positions leave it open, and argue the point rather than restating it. My own suspicion, which you should test: the reciprocity and desert literature settles the question only by assuming that the eligible are identifiable independently of the screening, which is precisely what is in doubt; and the standard least-restrictive-means style limits imported from rights adjudication likewise presuppose a determinate class of rights-bearers. If both suspicions are right, the question has been left open by the shape of the assumptions rather than by oversight.

Give the method, as concretely as the genre allows, with a work plan. I expect the project to move through something like these stages: first, separate screening justifications from desert justifications and say what each can and cannot support, including whether the desert vocabulary is doing any independent work once the screening rationale is on the table; second, work through the mechanics of an actual scheme, since the screening story is only as good as its empirical premise, and I want to test that premise against the fact that sanctions in practice attach far more often to procedural failures, such as missed appointments and unreturned forms, than to established deception, and that a substantial share of overpayment is attributed to administrative error rather than claimant fraud — I will check the figures myself, but the proposal should explain why those two facts matter if the screening reading is correct, namely that the signal is noisy and the cost is borne by people who were never the target; third, construct a candidate constraint, in the neighbourhood of a requirement that the state show the deterrence gain could not be bought more cheaply in the coin of the claimants' own interests, or that those screened out by mistake be compensated, or that the burden be shown to fall on the class whose exclusion was the point; fourth, test that constraint against cases where it appears to give the wrong answer, including cases where I would want the scheme to survive.

Say what would count as the project failing. Candidates I have in mind: the screening reading turns out to be eliminable in favour of a straightforward desert reading after all; or every candidate constraint either collapses into ordinary cost-benefit reasoning, in which case there is no distinct philosophical question here, or forbids schemes I am not willing to give up, in which case the constraint is not credible.

Be honest about scope for a single semester, and say what the deliverable would be if the argument only half works.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I will supply the references, and I would rather you refer to positions and arguments in your own words than to named works, since a named work I cannot check is worse than none. Two pages, continuous prose with a few headings, formal in register but not stiff. End the document with a single final line in exactly this form and nothing after it:

\proposal{question: <the central question in one sentence>; falsifier: <the one finding that would show the project fails>}
