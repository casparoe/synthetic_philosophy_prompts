Question: "Voting is said to be paradoxical in at least three distinct senses. Identify them, state for each the precise result and the assumptions on which it rests, and say how far each bears on the claim that majority rule is the natural or correct way to aggregate preferences. Where a result is commonly over-read, say what it does not show. Assume preferences are ordinal and that no interpersonal comparisons are available."

Student's answer:

"Three senses are usually distinguished.

First, Condorcet's paradox. With three or more alternatives, majority rule can generate a cycle: A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. No alternative is then a Condorcet winner, and there is no majority will waiting to be discovered. The result requires only ordinal preferences over at least three options, and it requires no irrationality on anyone's part. It shows that majority rule cannot always deliver a transitive social ordering, and Arrow's theorem generalises the point: no aggregation rule over three or more alternatives can satisfy unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives and non-dictatorship at once.

Second, the paradox of not voting. In a large electorate a single vote is almost never pivotal, so the expected instrumental return to voting is negligible and a purely instrumentally rational citizen should abstain. Turnout is far higher than this predicts. In practice the puzzle is resolved by noting that many elections are in fact decided by a handful of votes, so the chance of being pivotal is not as small as it looks; expressive and civic benefits account for the rest.

Third, the jury theorem. If each voter is more likely than not to be right, and votes are independent, the probability that the majority is right rises with the size of the electorate and tends to one. This is the deepest of the three, because it shows that majority rule is not merely fair but truth-tracking, and it underwrites the case for extending the franchise as widely as possible. Because it is a theorem, the case for democracy does not rest on any empirical claim about voters' competence.

May's theorem is the formal statement of what is attractive about majority rule: between two alternatives it is the only rule that is anonymous, neutral and positively responsive. Unlike Arrow's result, May's is a characterisation rather than an impossibility, but it too assumes that we are choosing between just two alternatives.

Taken together, the three support the familiar conclusion that no voting system is perfect, and that the choice among systems is a matter of trade-offs."
