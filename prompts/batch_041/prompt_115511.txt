I have spent fifteen years as a commercial insurance underwriter. I think in risk pools, experience rating, moral hazard, adverse selection, loss ratios, and claims adjudication; I am fluent in the actuarial machinery and comfortable with the mathematics, but I have no formal training in moral or political philosophy. A friend who works in social policy keeps talking about the welfare state's fixation on separating the deserving from the undeserving poor — conditionality, work requirements, benefit sanctions, periodic crackdowns on fraud — and I keep having the feeling that I almost understand the whole dispute through my own vocabulary, but that the translation is doing something quietly dishonest. So I would like you to take the moral debate about desert and conditionality in welfare provision and restate it as faithfully as you can in the language of insurance: entitlement conditions as policy terms, the work requirement as a deductible or co-payment, benefit fraud as misrepresentation at the claims stage, the deserving/undeserving distinction as risk classification and experience rating, sanctions as the denial or rescission of a claim, and so on. Where a mapping is genuinely apt, say so and explain what makes it apt; where it is merely suggestive, do not paper over the gap.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. My industry has elaborate machinery for predicting who will claim and how often, for pricing risk, and for shaping the behavior of the insured through incentives and verification — but it seems to have no place at all for the question of whether a claimant deserves the money. A policy pays out or it does not, and the claimant's character matters only insofar as it predicts loss. Is the moral debate, then, just an incentive-design problem wearing a moral costume, or does the part that fails to translate — desert, the distinction between the unfortunate and the culpable, the sense that some recipients are owed what they get and others merely tolerated — mark a genuine blind spot in my framework? Please be honest in both directions: do not smuggle in actuarial assumptions and present them as the moral view, and do not flatter my suspicion that the moralists are simply confused. Tell me which claims survive translation intact, which survive only mutilated, and which cannot be said in my language at all, and finish with your own view on whether the residue is a defect of the insurance vocabulary or evidence that the original debate is less clear than it pretends to be.
