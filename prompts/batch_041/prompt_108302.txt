I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence and I am preparing the final assessment for the unit on the intentional stance and the attribution of beliefs to AI systems. I need a second reader for one student's script, and I would like the grade plus the comments that justify it. I have not formed a settled view of this script and want an independent read.

The examination question was as follows.

(a) Set out what an interpreter commits herself to in adopting the intentional stance towards a system, and explain how the assumption of rationality is supposed to function within it.

(b) Consider a large language model that, when asked about its own states, consistently produces sentences such as "I have no beliefs; I only predict the next token", while its behaviour is reliably anticipated by an interpreter who ascribes beliefs and desires to it (that it wants to be helpful, that it takes the user to be asking for X, and so on). Using the two-ledger distinction developed in the second half of the course — the prediction ledger, on which an ascription is assessed by whether it lets you anticipate the system's behaviour better than a design-stance or physical-stance description, and the entitlement ledger, on which an ascription is assessed by whether the system stands in the right relation to the content for the ascription to be literally true of it — say what, if anything, the model's self-reports settle, and what the intentional stance's predictive success does and does not establish.

(c) Does anything change if the model is deployed as an adviser whose recommendations users act on, so that its "beliefs" become the basis of decisions taken by others? Answer with reference to the distinction between original and derived intentionality, and say whether that distinction, or the intentional stance itself, can support holding the system answerable for what it says.

The student's answer was as follows.

Dennett's claim is that we can predict and explain a system's behaviour by treating it as a rational agent: we ascribe beliefs and desires to it, work out what it would be rational for something with those states to do, and treat that as our prediction. He distinguishes this from the physical stance, which predicts from the laws governing the system's constituents, and the design stance, which predicts from what the system was designed to do. For an artefact the two non-physical stances are often close together, since what the model was designed to do and what it "wants" tend to coincide; the difference is that the design stance is keyed to the designer's intentions and the intentional stance to the system's own. Adopting the intentional stance carries no commitment to the system's having experiences or feelings, and that is the point of calling it a strategy rather than a thesis about consciousness.

The rationality assumption is the engine of the strategy: we predict the behaviour an agent with those beliefs and desires would produce if it reasoned correctly. Because it is an assumption, it is also a vulnerability. If the interpreter's ascription says the model believes p, and the model then asserts not-p, the assumption has been falsified for that case, and the ascription should be withdrawn rather than repaired.

The model's own disclaimer should not be treated as decisive. Dennett's question is which interpretive strategy earns its keep, not what the system says about itself, and a verbal report is just more behaviour to be predicted. On the prediction ledger the intentional stance plainly earns its keep here, since the interpreter's ascriptions anticipate the model's outputs across a wide range of prompts.

On the entitlement ledger I take Dennett's view to be permissive. He is a pragmatist about these ascriptions: belief-talk is licensed by its predictive utility, and there is no further fact about intrinsic intentionality that could defeat a useful ascription. The distinction between original and derived intentionality — borrowed from Searle, and, on my reading, one that Dennett thinks is less sharp than it looks, since all intentionality is a matter of what an interpreter can make sense of — therefore does not block the entitlement. If anything the adviser case strengthens the position. Once the model's ascriptions are guiding other people's decisions, it is doing the work that ascriptions to people do, and it is answerable for its "beliefs" in the way any agent whose beliefs guide action is: the entitlement ledger is satisfied along with the prediction ledger.
