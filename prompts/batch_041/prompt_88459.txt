Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words, set over a single working day at a small maritime-insurance underwriting house. The protagonist, a signing actuary, must personally certify a portfolio of several thousand individual cargo-voyage risks. For each voyage she holds a high, evidence-based confidence — well above the threshold her profession treats as sufficient for assertion — that it will not be the one to fail; yet the same evidence, aggregated, tells her with near-certainty that at least one voyage in a portfolio this size will fail, and the certificate she is required to sign opens with a standard clause conceding exactly that. The story should make the reader feel the pull of both commitments at once: that she is entitled to each individual confidence, and that she cannot coherently be entitled to all of them together.

Two further elements can carry the dispute: a junior colleague who proposes that the firm simply lower its per-voyage threshold until the whole conjunction clears it, and the actuary's own suspicion that this cure is worse than the disease. Do not let any character name the paradoxes or cite any literature; the tension should be shown through the work rather than announced. Render the underlying logic accurately enough that a specialist would recognize it, and let the story commit — through what the actuary actually does when she signs — to one resolution of the tension, rather than leaving every option equally open.

After the story, add a short note of roughly 200 words that states which resolution the story commits to and what that commitment takes for granted, identifies where its handling of the ideas is most vulnerable, and says what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to tell the story toward a different ending.
