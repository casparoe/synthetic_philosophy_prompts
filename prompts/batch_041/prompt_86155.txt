I'm a retired marine biologist — thirty years on fish behaviour, mostly cleaner fish and reef stuff — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy, enthusiasticly but not well. I've got a question I keep failing to answer for myself and I'd like you to have a go at it.

It's about when it's fair to say that something understands. I read a lot of the writing on whether LLMs understand anything and I nod along, but then I check my gut against cases from my old field and it stops being tidy.

Here's what I think, case by case.

A thermostat: doesn't understand the temperature. Not close.

An E. coli swimming up a sugar gradient: doesn't understand where the sugar is. It's a mechanism, a beautiful one, but no.

A honeybee that's never visited a particular patch, flies out, finds it, comes home and dances the direction and distance to the hive: I want to say yes, she knows where the food is. "Understands" might be the wrong word, but she isn't a thermostat.

A cleaner wrasse deciding whether to nibble its client or groom it, and changing its behaviour depending on whether a bigger fish is watching: yes, it's tracking something. It has a working model of what the other fish is likely to do.

A chess engine that beats every human alive: no, it doesn't understand chess. I'd bet my pension on that, though I can't say exactly why. (My son says it's just species chauvinism with extra steps.)

A model trained only to predict the next token — and let me stipulate the finding, since the details don't matter for my question — where if you poke around inside it you can read off a coherent representation of whatever situation the text describes, and it answers questions about that situation that were never in its training data: no, it doesn't understand. That's the one everybody argues about and I'm the one saying no. I can see how that looks sitting next to the wrasse.

A person with blindsight who can catch a ball while sincerely denying they can see it: their hand seems to understand something that their person doesn't. I suspect that case is instructive but I can't get it to tell me what it's instructive about.

And the one I flip-flop on: a jumping spider, brain the size of a poppy seed, that takes a long detour around an obstacle to reach prey it can no longer see. People in my old department called that planning; I once wrote in a grant proposal that the spider "represents" the prey's location, which embarrasses me slightly now. Is that understanding? Some days yes, some days I think im just using the word because it's shorter than the mechanism.

My best attempt at a principle, which I'd like you to break: something understands iff (a) its internal states are about something in the world, (b) they're usable in flexible, novel ways in the service of the thing's own ends, and (c) the system isn't just a lookup table keyed to the situation. I think that lets the bee and the wrasse in, keeps the thermostat and the bacteria out, and keeps the LLM out. But I have a nasty feeling that (b) and (c) are doing all the work and would let the LLM in if I applied them honestly, and that (a) is either empty or else assumes the very thing I'm trying to explain.

So what principle actually captures my verdicts, or something close to them? Which of my verdicts has to go? And tell me what your principle does with a case I haven't listed. If the honest answer is that understanding isn't one thing and I'm asking for a single line where there's only a messy cluster, please say that to my face rather than inventing a tidy principle to make me feel better — and if my setup is confused some other way, tell me that too. I'd much rather be told the question is bad than get a fluent answer that papers over it.
