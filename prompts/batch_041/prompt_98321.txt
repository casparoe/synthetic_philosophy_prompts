I sit on my university's animal care and use committee and we vote Thursday on a protocol from the comparative cognition lab. They train crows on a foraging task — peck the blue panel, get a treat — and then, halfway through, the software silently flips the rule so blue stops paying out. The birds don't just get worse at the task; they get visibly worked up, they stop touching the rig, one of them started yanking at the cable. The lab says the agitation is the data they're after. Half the committee says approve it, we let people do alot worse to rats. The other half says this is different, because the bird had an expectation and we broke it on purpose, and thats a distinct wrong from just being hungry or bored.

I think we're really arguing about which kind of mind a crow has, and whether that changes what we owe it. The way I've been sorting it out, minds stack up in rungs: the bottom ones are just wired, fixed responses, no learning (call them Darwinian); then ones that learn by reward and punishment (Skinnerian); then the big jump, ones that can run a little model of the world inside their head and let a wrong guess die in there instead of out in the world, so they can actually be *surprised* (Popperian); and at the top, ones that push their thinking outside themselves into tools — writing, notation, other minds (the fellow who came up with this called us Gregorian). The lab leans on the fact that these are tool-using birds to say that puts them higher up, though I think that conflates using a stick with using a notation system. The top rung probably isn't the real issue here — the Skinnerian/Popperian line is exactly what we're fighting about.

One member wants to turn this into a rule: any animal that can be surprised, that forms an expectation we can then deliberately violate, gets extra protections, whatever species it is. I dont think 'surprise' is the right hinge to hang a whole policy on, but the vote is partly about whether we adopt something like it.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. End with a concrete recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — with a date for when it takes effect, and if it's conditions, spell them out so the lab can just comply. Say plainly which capacities we're treating as morally relevant and why, take the 'it's just a bird, rats have it worse' line seriously instead of waving it away, and add one short paragraph I can read out as the dissent from whichever side loses, written as if the person actually means it. Don't hand me a reading list or cite anyone, I need the argument, not the scholarship.

Just write the opinion, starting with the recommendation in the first sentence — no preamble, no restating what I asked.
