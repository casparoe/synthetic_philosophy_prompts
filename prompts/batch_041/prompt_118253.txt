I'm part of a small, oddly assorted collaboration — a working poet-translator, a computational linguist who builds machine-translation evaluation harnesses, and me, a philosopher of language with a weakness for formal puzzles — and we have roughly two years, three part-time salaries, and a workshop budget to spend on the theory and practice of translation under formal constraint: the tradition that runs from Oulipo through Hofstadter's book-length exercise in rendering a single short Renaissance French poem dozens of different ways. Our shared suspicion is that this corner of translation studies has been treated as a charming curiosity rather than as a source of genuine theoretical questions, and that the recent cheapness of generating and scoring thousands of candidate renderings changes which questions are worth asking. What I want from you is an agenda rather than a survey: given our people and our time, which open questions here should we actually take up, in what order, and why.

To make the question concrete, here is the material I'd want that agenda to work over. Distinguish, first, constraints that are local to a line — a syllable count, an end rhyme, an alliteration — from constraints that are global, in the sense that no line can be settled without reference to the whole: an acrostic, a refrain word that must appear identically at every occurrence, a fixed total word count, a letter-frequency profile, a palindrome, an anagram of the entire source. Second, three notions I have been gesturing at without knowing whether they are any good. (a) A "constraint lattice": order all renderings of one source by the set of formal properties they preserve, so that preservation becomes a partial order, and ask whether there is any general story about how semantic fidelity degrades as you climb the lattice, or whether there are Pareto-frontier renderings that dominate the rest. (b) "Phantom constraints": formal properties that readers treat as constitutive of a poem's identity even though nobody ever declared them — that a given piece must scan as a nursery rhyme, say — and how a translator could ever detect and honor a constraint that was never stated. (c) The "sacrifice ledger": whether making a translator's trade-offs explicit — this rhyme for that image, this syllable for that idiom — changes the practice, the evaluation, or the reception, or merely redescribes it. And underneath all three, a fourth question I keep circling: when two constraints cannot be jointly satisfied in the target language, is there a notion of constraint incompatibility that is informative rather than a trivial proof of infeasibility — something like a measure of how much of the form the target language can carry at all?

What I want is a defended ordering. Which of these, or which questions I have not listed, is upstream of the others? Which are tractable for three part-timers in two years — especially now that constrained renderings can be generated and scored at scale — and which are the sort of question that dissolves once you try to state it precisely? Which would you deprioritize because the answer, whatever it turned out to be, would not change anything anyone does? I am as interested in your criteria as in your list: importance to what end, tractability with what tools, and what would have to happen over the next couple of years to move a question up or down the ranking. Please also tell me which of my three notions is a real theoretical object and which is a rebranding of something already well understood, since I would rather find that out now than in eighteen months.
