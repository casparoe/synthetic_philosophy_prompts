I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of language, and I've been trying to write a short essay for a workshop on slurs that doesnt just rehash the usual truth-conditional vs. pragmatic split. I've got more notes than polished prose, sorry. The core move: I want to distinguish three things a slur can do in a context, and claim that a lot of the standard puzzle cases only look like conflicts because people slide between them. First, contempt signaling: the word carries a conventional attitude of contempt or hostility toward the target group. Second, coalitional indexing: using it or not marks where the speaker stands relative to the target group and to other speakers; it's a badge, and badges can be worn ironically, reclaimed, or coerced. Third, taboo transmission: the word is a kind of social hot potato, so merely reporting or mentioning it can transfer risk or liability to the speaker, independently of what it signals about their attitudes. I then argue that in-group uses, e.g. between members of the target group, are best explained by coalitional indexing overriding contempt signaling in some contexts, while the weirdness of certain reports and negations is best explained by taboo transmission, and that the standard debate keeps trying to make one channel do all the work. I also try to use this to say something about why some slurs feel worse than others even when the contempt content seems similar: maybe because their taboo-transmission profile differs, due to historical use, legal exposure, or associational load, not because their semantics differ.
