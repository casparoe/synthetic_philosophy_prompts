I'm a third-year philosophy student writing an essay for a module on moral and political obligation, and a running argument with my flatmate about whether I owe him a tip-sized apology for something or other has somehow ended up in the draft. The essay is called 'Route and Tribute: Everyday Conventions and the Obligations of Visitors', and its central move is to split the conventions I care about — queueing, jaywalking, tipping — into two families that I claim behave very differently. Conventions of route govern how we share space and time: queues, which side of the pavement to walk on, whether to cross against the signal. Conventions of tribute govern the transfer of value as acknowledgement: tips, round-buying, the bottle of wine you take to a dinner. Route conventions, I argue, are transparent in their point — anyone can see from the inside why a queue works — whereas tribute conventions are opaque: the amount, the recipient and the occasion are arbitrary, and the whole function is expressive rather than coordinating. From this I build a three-part test for when a convention generates a genuine obligation rather than mere social pressure. The rationale must be in play (the convention's point must actually be at stake in the case at hand); the convention must be legible (its point graspable by the agent without having been socialised into it); and it must be relied upon (others must be adjusting their conduct in the expectation that you will conform). Route conventions pass all three almost automatically; tribute conventions typically pass only the third. The upshot for visitors is meant to be deflationary in one direction and inflationary in the other: the visitor who fails to tip is not thereby a moral delinquent, since the convention is neither legible to her nor doing its work in her case, but she is bound by the queue and by local conventions of route generally, because those are legible and load-bearing wherever she comes from. There is a section on jaywalking that I am fond of. In Britain there is no jaywalking offence to speak of, yet the informal norm is remarkably sticky, and I take this to show that the norm is doing something other than solving a safety problem: it is a vestigial convention whose rationale is in play only in some circumstances, which is why we feel its pull strongly at a busy junction and not at all on an empty residential street at midnight.

What I would like from you is a critical read rather than a summary. First, on accuracy and originality: is the route/tribute distinction doing real work, or is it the familiar coordination-versus-expression contrast in fancier dress, and if the latter, does anything in the argument survive the reduction? Second, on the three-part test: the rationale-in-play condition worries me, because it seems to license whatever the agent herself judges to be at stake, which would make the whole account self-serving; I would like you to press on whether there is a way to state that condition objectively, or whether it should be dropped. Third, on the visitor section: does the legibility condition earn its place, or is it just a roundabout way of saying that outsiders are excused when compliance would cost them and benefit no one? And is my conclusion — that a visitor's obligations sort by convention-family — a real thesis, or merely a redescription of the fact that we are lenient with strangers? Finally, I would be grateful if you would flag anywhere I have quietly assumed a substantive position I have not argued for, and tell me honestly whether the jaywalking section is an argument or only atmosphere.
