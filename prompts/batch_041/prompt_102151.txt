In our reading group we've spent like four sessions on the one-shot prisoner's dilemma and whether it's ever rational to cooperate, and I swear nobody has moved an inch. On one side you've got the straight game-theory line: in a genuinely one-shot PD, defecting strictly dominates, cooperating is just leaving money on the table, end of story. On the other side you've got the people who say that's only true if you think of "rational" as picking the best act against a fixed view of what the other player does — and that once you ask what a rational agent, or a rational policy, does, cooperating starts to look right, because the kind of agent who cooperates in symmetric one-shot PDs is the kind who does better against their own kind. The superrationality stuff, the team-reasoning stuff, the evidential-decision-theory stuff, all of it.

The weird part is I don't think anyone in the room actually disagrees about teh math. They agree what the payoff matrix says, they agree what happens under each decision rule. And yet it feels like they're having two different arguments at once. My hypothesis — and please stress-test it rather than just nodding along — is that this is really a disagreement about what "rational" is a property of: acts versus agents (or decision procedures), and that once you nail that down most of the heat goes away and what's left is a small, real disagreement about which notion of rationality we should actually care about. But I might be wrong. Maybe it's a genuinely substantive fight about decision theory itself, maybe the two sides are answering different questions that just happen to share the word "rational," maybe one side is quietly confused about something.
