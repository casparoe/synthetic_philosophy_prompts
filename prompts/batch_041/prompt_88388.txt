I have been collecting cases of a pattern I can feel but cannot name, and I would like to know whether it has an established name — and, more than the name, whether the cases I have been grouping together really do belong together.

The pattern is this. A question of the form "how likely is it that such-and-such?" appears to be perfectly well formed, yet the answer depends on something the question itself leaves open: the way the possibilities are carved up. In one sort of case the carving is over people or events. A friend of mine is an actuary; when he prices a policy for a particular fifty-year-old man who smokes and runs marathons, the figure depends on which table he consults — men of that age, smokers, distance runners — and the man genuinely belongs to all three classes. There is only one of him and the event is unrepeatable, so no frequency can adjudicate between the tables; the question "what is the chance that *he* dies this year?" seems to have no answer until we say which class he is being drawn from, and nothing in the case says which.

In a second sort of case there are no people at all, only geometry. Suppose a dart is thrown so that it lands "at random" on a circular board. The chance that it lands nearer the centre than the rim is a half if we choose the distance from the centre uniformly and then a random direction, and a quarter if we choose the point uniformly over the area of the board. Neither procedure is more random than the other, and I cannot see what would make one of them the correct reading of "at random". I am told there is a classical puzzle of this shape about chords in a circle which admits three equally defensible answers; I half-remember it and could not reproduce it.

A third sort of case concerns what we should believe before we have any evidence. Two colleagues looking at the same three heads in a row disagree about the next toss — one says a half, the other says four fifths — and their disagreement is entirely about what the space of possibilities looked like before the first toss.

What I want to know is whether there is one name for this or whether I am conflating several things. My own suspicion, which I cannot defend, is that the first case and the second are different in kind: the first seems to be about which class a particular case belongs to, the second about which measure on a space is the natural one. Yet they feel like the same failure, and I do not know whether one is a special case of the other or whether they are two species of something more general. I have come across the phrase "principle of indifference" for the rule that we should treat possibilities as equally likely when we have no reason to prefer one, but if that names the rule I keep wanting to apply, it does not name the thing that goes wrong when I apply it.

Since the label is not the point, please give me the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is; whether the cases above are one thing or several; which established concepts come nearest, and how each differs from what I have described — in particular, why this is not merely a question that is ambiguous or underspecified, and why it is not the same as a biased sample; and whether the standard remedies (insisting that the randomising procedure be stated in full, appealing to symmetry, restricting the principle to cases in which some symmetry group acts transitively) remove the difficulty or only relocate it. If a fully specified procedure can still be condemned as arbitrary, I should like to know on what grounds, because that strikes me as the crux, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuine problem or a confusion of mine.
