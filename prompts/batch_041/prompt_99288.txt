I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a funeral. One of my characters — a widower who has spent his retirement reading the Greek and Roman moralists — tries to console the mourners with the old Epicurean line that "death is nothing to us," insisting that their grief and their dread have been misdirected all along. I want the scene to be intellectually serious rather than a caricature, and I would rather know exactly what that sentence can bear before I put it in his mouth.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that "death is nothing to us" might be asserting — ranging from the nearly trivial to the genuinely ambitious — and for each say what it commits its holder to, whether it is defensible, and, crucially, whether it would actually underwrite the consolation my character is offering. I would like the readings sorted by what they take death to be (an event, a state, the absence of any subject, a deprivation of goods) and by what work the word "us" is doing in the sentence (whether it ranges over the living, the dying, the dead, or those who no longer exist at all). Where a reading hinges on whether something must be experienced in order to be bad for someone, make that hinge explicit, and tell me which readings collapse into that question and which somehow escape it.

I also want to know which reading my scene actually needs. If the strongest defensible reading would still fail to make the mourners' grief irrational — or would license only the claim that death is not an evil to be feared, rather than that it is no loss at all — then say so plainly, because in that case my character is overreaching and the scene ought to show it.

Throughout, distinguish what is settled and uncontroversial among people who study this material from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
