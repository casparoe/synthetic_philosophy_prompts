I'm a novelist — the book I'm finishing is about three generations of physicists in one family — and there's a scene where my main character, a retired statistical mechanic, gives a public lecture explaining Maxwell's demon to a room of undergrads. I wrote the speech myself, out of a couple of popular physics books I read years ago and a podcast I only half remember, so the words are mine but the physics is secondhand. Here it is:

"Maxwell came up with the demon in the 1860s to show that the second law of thermodynamics isn't really a law at all. Picture a box of gas split in two by a wall with a tiny door in it, and a little being who watches the molecules. When a fast one comes up from the left he opens the door and lets it through to the right; when a slow one comes up from the right, he lets it through to the left. Pretty soon the right half is hot and the left half is cold, and you've conjured a temperature difference out of nothing, which you can run an engine on. Free energy out of thin air.

Physicists spent a hundred years trying to kill the demon. Szilard's version, with a single molecule in a box, was the first real dent, and then Landauer and Bennett finished the job: the demon has to write down what he sees, and every bit of that record he later erases costs kT ln 2 of entropy, so the books balance in the end. The other half of the answer is measurement — you can't look at a molecule without disturbing it, that's Heisenberg's uncertainty principle doing the work, and that's where the entropy goes back into the system. Shannon's information theory is what ties the two halves together, since information turns out to be just another form of entropy. So the demon is impossible, thermodynamics survives, and that's why nobody has ever built a perpetual motion machine of the second kind."

I like the speech and dont want to gut it, but I definately don't want a physicist in the audience laughing at me. Go through it piece by piece and tell me what a working thermodynamicist would nod at, what they'd let slide because it's a public lecture, and what would make them wince or reach for a pen. And be honest about the ending — does "so the demon is impossible" actually follow from the erasure part, or is that a jump? My hunch is that the erasure accounting is the real answer and that the measurement business is just the same idea wearing a different hat, but I have no idea if that's right.
