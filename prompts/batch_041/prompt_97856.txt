My employer runs a fabrication plant, and I recently sat in on a meeting where two colleagues, Dana and Marcus, argued about which of two coating procedures the plant should adopt. I have no stake in the outcome and honestly cannot tell who had the better of it, so what I want is an adjudication of the exchange as it actually happened, move by move, rather than a recommendation about the procedure.

The evidence they were working from was a year of production records, summarized as pass rates on final inspection:

- Old procedure: 96 of 100 units passed on newer machines (96 percent); 30 of 100 passed on older machines (30 percent); 126 of 200 overall (63 percent).
- New procedure: 190 of 200 units passed on newer machines (95 percent); 28 of 100 passed on older machines (28 percent); 218 of 300 overall (72.7 percent).

The exchange went as follows.

(1) Dana: The bottom line is the pooled figures. The new procedure passes 72.7 percent against 63 percent for the old, which is a gain of nearly ten points, so we should adopt it.

(2) Marcus: That pooled figure is an artifact. Look inside the table. On newer machines the old procedure passes 96 percent against 95 percent for the new one, and on older machines 30 percent against 28 percent. The old procedure wins on both kinds of machine. It looks worse overall only because we ran the new procedure mostly on the newer machines, which pass inspection at a far higher rate no matter what we do. Compared like with like, the new procedure is worse.

(3) Dana: You have shown that the mix of machines differs between the two columns. That is not yet an argument for reading the table by rows instead of by columns. Reversals of this kind turn up both when the stratifying variable is a common cause of procedure and outcome, and when it is a common effect of them. Machine age is at least plausibly a common cause, since it can affect pass rates and may have influenced which machines got the new procedure. But imagine we had stratified instead by whether a unit was routed through the rework line, something that happens partly because of the procedure and partly because of the unit's quality. Adjusting for that would manufacture exactly the sort of reversal you are pointing to, and it would be a mistake. So the fact that the old procedure wins within each stratum is decisive only once you have said what kind of variable machine age is.

(4) Marcus: Machine age is a fixed property of the machine, recorded before anyone chose a procedure, and management decided which machines to pilot the new procedure on. So it cannot be downstream of the procedure, and it plainly influenced assignment. It is a common cause, and adjusting for it is the right thing to do.

(5) Dana: Granted. But the pooled number is not noise. It answers a real question, namely what defect rate to expect if we keep assigning procedures the way we have been, which is what the plant manager asked about. And in any case, if we switch every machine over to the new procedure, then everyone gets it, the machine-age mix no longer differs between the two groups, and the pooled comparison is the right guide.

(6) Marcus: That last step is the very mistake you accused me of. If we switch every machine, the mix changes, so the trial's pooled figure is not the rate we would get. What we need is the within-stratum comparison applied to our own mix of machines, not a number that came out of a mix that will no longer exist.

A third colleague, who was not part of the argument, said afterward that the whole thing was a textbook case of Simpson's paradox and that the name settles it in Marcus's favor, but I could not see how a name settles anything.

Please go through the six moves and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. For each move, say whether it establishes what its speaker claims for it, and identify any premise a speaker needs but does not state. Then say what both of them are missing, who has the better of the argument overall, and what the disagreement actually turns on.
