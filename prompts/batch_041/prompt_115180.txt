I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy, working on population ethics, and I serve as the ethics member of the technology appraisal committee that recommends what our public health plan will cover; I write the memos the committee circulates. Next month's file has split us, and I'd like you to draft the memo for me.

Take the role of a philosopher with deep expertise in population ethics who is also practised at writing for coverage and appraisal bodies. Think carefully before you write: in a short preliminary section, work out what is genuinely at stake in this file and state the strongest version of each side's position. Then write the memo itself, for members who are not philosophers.

The committee's coverage criteria contain a clause, added some years ago after two recommendations that had been defended almost entirely by projected savings and by the relief of informal caregiving. It provides that we may recommend coverage only where the persons who will benefit can be identified, and that system-wide savings, gains in workforce productivity, and relief of caregiving burden are not to be counted.

The file: a manufacturer asks us to cover a package of expanded carrier screening together with the prenatal and preimplantation options that follow from it, on the ground that the package will reduce the number of children born with a heritable condition that causes significant impairment from childhood onward. Read literally, the clause seems to forbid recommending it. The children who would be spared the condition are not the children who would otherwise have existed, so no one is made better off; and once caregiving relief is excluded, nothing countable remains. Our chair calls this the non-identity problem and would rather it stayed out of the memo. The disability advocacy groups in our consultation process argue that the package expresses the judgment that lives like theirs should not be lived, and two members have said they will vote against it on that ground alone. Our clinical advisers say the condition's effects are far more variable than the manufacturer's dossier implies.

What I need: a bottom line — recommend, decline, or recommend with conditions — with a time frame for implementation and review, and reasoning that the manufacturer and the advocacy groups could actually respond to. I also need a specific recommendation about the clause itself: apply it as written, reinterpret it, or amend it, and an honest account of what an amendment would let back in. Take the strongest objection to your own recommendation seriously rather than gesturing at it. Roughly 900 words, with headings and a one-paragraph bottom line at the top.
