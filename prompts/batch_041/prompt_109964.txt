I am a retired experimental physiologist — thirty-eight years at the bench, and four years now of reading philosophy in the evenings, without much guidance and with no illusion that I am anything but an amateur. I have just finished Franklin's Autobiography, and I want you to write me a dialogue about the project of moral perfection described in it.

I set out the material below as I understand it, because I want the dialogue to stay close to the text rather than to the general idea of self-improvement. Franklin calls his undertaking "the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection": thirteen virtues, each with a short precept; a page for each virtue ruled with red ink and seven columns for the days of the week; a week of strict attention to each virtue in turn, thirteen weeks to a course and four courses to a year; and a little black spot entered against every fault found upon examination. He says he chose "rather more names, with fewer ideas annexed to each" than the moralists he had read, "for the sake of clearness" — that is, he deliberately narrowed each virtue so that it could be tracked. He began with twelve and added Humility as the thirteenth after a Quaker friend informed him that he was generally thought proud. He reports that his scheme of Order "gave me the most trouble," and gives as his reason that Order was practicable for a man who had the disposition of his own time, such as a journeyman printer, but could not be exactly observed by a master who had to receive people of business at their own hours. Of Humility he says that he "cannot boast of much success in acquiring the reality of this virtue, but I had a good deal with regard to the appearance of it" — and the appearance, produced chiefly by a change in his manner of speaking, served him well, since his opinions met with a readier reception. He also records what his own reason kept urging against the project: that such nicety "might be a kind of foppery in morals, which, if it were known, would make me ridiculous"; that a perfect character might be "envied and hated"; and that "a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, to keep his friends in countenance." His verdict is that he never arrived at the perfection he aimed at, but that he was "by the endeavor, a better and a happier man" than he would otherwise have been; and he meant to publish the method as a book called "The Art of Virtue," stripped of the distinguishing tenets of any sect so that it might be "serviceable to people in all religions."

Please write a dialogue — ten to fourteen exchanges, two people at a table, no moderator — between one who holds that Franklin's project is a coherent and even admirable piece of moral engineering and one who holds that it is confused, or self-undermining, or aimed at the wrong target. Neither should be a strawman, and each should answer the other's actual argument rather than restating his or her own. I would like them to press on the following, though not as a checklist: whether a virtue consisting in the fit between a person and his circumstances — and Franklin's Order looks like precisely that, since the journeyman printer can keep it and the master cannot — belongs on the same list as Temperance or Justice, given that he narrowed each virtue for the sake of trackability; whether nightly self-examination measures a character or alters it (in my own trade we are wary of instruments that become part of the system they are measuring); whether the Humility episode shows the project failing or succeeding, given that the appearance of the virtue appears to have delivered its effects; and whether "a better and a happier man" is evidence or rationalization. Neither should put invented words into Franklin's mouth; where they attribute a view to him, let them paraphrase. I would rather the dialogue end with the disagreement standing than with a comfortable synthesis.

Finally, add a short note after the dialogue, clearly separated from it, telling me which of the claims the two of them make are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who know this material — what a careful reader would take as plain in the text, as against what is one speaker's reading — so that I am not left supposing that a contested interpretation is the consensus. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I am asking for a candid statement of the standing of the claims.
