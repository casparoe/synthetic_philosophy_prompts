I have been defending a version of Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge for several years, and I want to know what would count against it. As I hold it: S knows that p only if p is true, S believes p, S would not have believed p if p had been false, and S would have believed p if p had been true — with both counterfactuals assessed relative to the method S actually used in forming the belief. I accept the closure failure that comes with it. I know I have hands; I know that having hands entails that I am not a handless brain in a vat; and I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat. I regard that combination as the theory's selling point rather than its embarrassment, since it is what lets the theory dismiss the skeptic's premise without either conceding that we know nothing or claiming that we can know the denials of skeptical hypotheses.

Tell me what the position risks. I want the defeaters sorted by what they cost: which would be fatal, which merely awkward, which a defender can absorb with a qualification and which cannot be absorbed at all. Distinguish the objections that defenders of tracking concede from those that only critics press. And I want the targets to be the tracking conditions themselves, not just the closure failure in general: cases where the conditions are met and the belief plainly is not knowledge, cases where they fail and the belief plainly is knowledge, and results showing that the conditions cannot do the work they are being asked to do.

Two soft spots I already know about, which I would like you to assess rather than merely restate. First, the method-relative clause: describe the belief-forming method narrowly and almost nothing is sensitive, describe it broadly and almost everything is. Is that a fatal indeterminacy, a fixable one, or a problem the theory simply inherits from any reliabilist account — and what would a principled fix cost? Second, necessary truths, where the sensitivity counterfactual appears to have no possible antecedent, so that the condition is either vacuously satisfied or fails for want of a world to evaluate. Does that make knowledge of mathematics and modality a free gift of the theory, and does it matter?

I am also troubled by a case I have not been able to place. A subject forms a true belief by a method that would have delivered the opposite belief had things been otherwise; but a third party, whose interventions are counterfactually robust, would have stepped in to ensure that she believed it anyway. The belief seems as knowledgeable as before, yet the sensitivity counterfactual now fails. Is that a genuine counterexample to sensitivity as a necessary condition, or does it show only that the counterfactual must be restricted to what the subject's own method would have delivered? If the latter, say what the restriction costs.

Finally, be honest about whether the position is insulated. Has tracking, in its live formulations, been qualified — method relativity, defeater clauses, restrictions to ordinary contexts — to the point where nothing could count against it? If so, is that a defect of this theory or the price of any theory that has to accommodate the skeptic?

Answer without dissolving everything into a wash: say which objections you think a reasonable defender cannot survive, which they can absorb, and where your own confidence is low. I would be most moved by a case in which the tracking conditions hold and the belief nonetheless falls short of knowledge, or by a demonstration that the closure failure cannot be stated consistently with the rest of the theory. I would be least moved by an appeal to the intuitiveness of closure, which is precisely what is at issue. And if I have misdescribed the view — if, for instance, it cannot be formulated without the closure failure, or if the method-relative clause makes the counterfactuals unstable rather than merely hard to apply — say so plainly instead of answering the question as I posed it.
