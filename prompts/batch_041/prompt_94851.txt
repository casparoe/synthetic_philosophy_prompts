I run a philosophy department's weekly research seminar, and next term I have one 75-minute slot to teach the graduate students something about the limits of self-knowledge — specifically the idea that there can be facts about your own mind that you cannot know you are in, and the related thought that even if you know something, you may not be in a position to know that you know it. I want the session built around a game, not a lecture. Design it.

Setting and materials. Fifteen to twenty participants, one room, no computers required. You may assume a whiteboard, a stack of index cards, and ordinary office supplies. Money is not allowed; points or tokens are fine. The activity must be runnable by a facilitator who has read your brief once, with no special preparation beyond printing a page or two.

What the participants must come to see. I want them to feel, in their own choices, a structure that I can only describe to you abstractly here. Suppose each participant repeatedly receives a private, noisy report about some quantity, and must stake a claim about that quantity at one of several escalating levels: not merely that the quantity is such-and-such, but that they know it is, and that they know that they know it, and so on up a ladder. The point I want them to run into is that the set of situations in which they are entitled to each successive claim strictly shrinks as they climb, so that the ladder has a ceiling that is fixed by how coarse their information is, not by how confident they feel. I want them to notice that they are systematically tempted to claim one rung higher than the evidence licenses, and that the temptation does not come from bravado but from a genuine feature of what it takes to know: the same report that guarantees the underlying fact could, in a slightly worse world, have been produced by a situation in which the fact fails, and there is nothing in the report itself that rules that world out. The debrief should connect this to the general claim that no interesting fact about your own situation — including the fact that you are in a position to know something — is guaranteed to be knowable whenever it obtains.

Constraints on the design.

The idea has to show up in the mechanics, not in what the facilitator announces. Participants should be making real choices with real (small) consequences before anyone names the phenomenon, and the reveal should come from the scoring or the board, not from a speech. Design the payoff scheme so that over-claiming is punished in a way that makes the ceiling visible, but so that under-claiming is also costly — I do not want the safe strategy to be to claim nothing.

Because this is for people who cannot look anything up, do not build the activity around the history of the puzzle, the names of the people who posed it, or any particular published version of it. Do not ask them to read or recall any text, and do not include citations, references, or a reading list. The philosophical content must be reconstructible from the activity plus the debrief. Where you need a specific numerical example, invent one and make it internally consistent; do not rely on remembered examples from the literature.

Deliverables.

1. The rules, in enough detail that a facilitator can run it cold: what each participant holds, what they see, what they must decide, in what order, how many rounds, and how a round is resolved.
2. The payoff scheme, with the actual numbers, and a short account of why those numbers produce the pressure you want. Include the resolution table a facilitator would consult to score a round, or a clean rule for generating one.
3. A timeline for the 75 minutes, including where the activity pauses and where the debrief goes.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical structure: the point where confidence and entitlement come apart, the point where the ladder's ceiling appears, and the point where participants will want to say that they "really did know." Be explicit about which rung the ceiling sits at and why it does not depend on the details you happened to choose.
5. What will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, and how the facilitator should handle each failure. I am particularly worried about two things: participants treating the exercise as a test of risk tolerance or as a gambling game rather than as an epistemic one, and participants deciding that the whole thing is an artifact of the arbitrary numbers and that a sharper report would let them climb forever.
6. An honest statement of what the activity cannot reproduce. Tell me which feature of the underlying idea I will have to supply myself in words, and why the game, as a game, is bound to leave it out.
