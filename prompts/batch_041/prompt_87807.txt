I am an economist whose working vocabulary is the economics of knowledge: the division of labour and comparative advantage, make-or-buy and transaction costs, human capital and its depreciation, option value, principal–agent problems, path dependence and lock-in, stocks versus flows. I have no training in epistemology and no wish to acquire its technical vocabulary; I want a philosophical position translated into mine, not the reverse.

The position I want translated is the contemporary worry about epistemic dependence on AI: roughly, that there is a distinctive epistemic good — understanding — which is not reducible to holding true beliefs or receiving correct outputs, and that delegating one's cognitive work to AI systems tends to erode it. Set this position out first, in its own terms, on the assumption that I know nothing of it; then translate it.

Give me the translation in three passes. First, the clean mappings: for each element of the position, name the economic construct that carries it and say why the fit is genuinely good rather than merely suggestive. Second, the distorted mappings: where the fit requires bending the construct, tell me exactly what is bent and what is lost in the bending. Third, the residue report: which claims in the original have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, so that the best I can do is a strained metaphor or nothing.

For each item of residue, adjudicate the direction of the failure. Is it economics that is blind — a real feature of the phenomenon it cannot represent — or is it the original claim that is doing less work than its rhetoric suggests, or quietly relying on a distinction it cannot state precisely? Note that my own discipline has a long tradition of celebrating the division of knowledge, so I expect it to be sympathetic to outsourcing cognition; I want to know whether that tradition supports the philosophical worry, neutralises it, or is itself part of what the worry is about.

I am especially interested in one test case: the difference between genuinely understanding why something is so and merely being disposed to assert it correctly. Tell me whether that distinction survives translation, or whether economics can only ever recast it as a preference for one kind of mental state over another — and, if the latter, whether that is a defect of economics or evidence that the distinction was never as sharp as it was made to sound.
