I run the analytics desk at a foundation that funds high-variance work — biosecurity, AI, pandemic preparedness — and our trustees have adopted a rule that we never recommend a grant whose expected value is undefined. In practice that rule bites far more often than anyone expected: analysts keep bringing me proposals whose payoff model is unbounded and whose probability-weighted series is only conditionally convergent, so the same proposal can be made to look worth any amount depending on how the outcomes are grouped. That is the structure of the Pasadena game: flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first head falls on flip n, you win 2^n/n when n is odd and lose 2^n/n when n is even, and the series has no well-defined sum, which leaves any procedure that simply maximizes expected value with nothing to say.

Write me a decision procedure my analysts can run on each of the roughly eight proposals we see weekly, in under an hour, without a decision theorist in the room. Give the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to the trustees because the remaining question is one of values rather than arithmetic. It has to catch the Pasadena-style failure directly: the recommendation should be re-derived under at least one alternative but equally legitimate partitioning of the outcome space, and the procedure should say what to do when the two derivations disagree. Include rules for claims of infinite value, for probabilities no one can estimate, and for payoffs that are real but not monetary. Then run it on three proposals — a portfolio of long-shot research grants whose modeled benefits are unbounded, a monitoring program justified by a claimed chance of averting an infinite loss, and our flagship program, which everyone wants funded and whose expected value our own analyst reports as undefined. Mark plainly which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot decide.
