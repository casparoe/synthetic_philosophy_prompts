I teach a graduate seminar that pairs early modern moral philosophy with contemporary end-of-life policy, and I am putting together a speculative exercise on David Hume. Hume died long before any modern statutory scheme of medical aid in dying, and certainly before the specific problem of a patient who signed an advance directive requesting assistance at a later stage of dementia. Imagine that Hume could be brought forward to advise on a case now before a hospital ethics committee. The patient is a seventy-eight-year-old woman with moderate dementia. Years earlier, while competent, she completed a legal advance directive asking that she receive medical aid in dying once she reached what the directive calls a severe stage of cognitive decline. She now seems cheerful, does not ask to die, and sometimes says she wants to live; her daughter insists the earlier directive should be honored, while her son argues that her present contentment shows she has changed her mind. The jurisdiction permits advance-directive requests for dementia, but the statute is silent on how to weigh present expressions of contentment against a prior competent request. I would like you to act as a philosopher-historian with deep expertise in Hume's moral psychology, his account of the passions and sympathy, his distinction between natural and artificial virtues, and his critique of religious arguments in ethics. Reconstruct the position Hume would most likely take on whether the directive should be honored. Do not reduce the answer to a slogan such as autonomy or sanctity of life; instead, show how his commitments about sentiment, utility, character, and the limits of promising would shape his verdict. Also address whether he would distinguish what he would defend as a philosopher from what he would recommend as a matter of public policy. I am not asking for a summary of his views on suicide; I want the reconstruction itself.

Start by brainstorming the relevant Humean materials and any tensions among them, then give your best reconstruction in a clear, organized answer. Because this is a counterfactual exercise, be explicit about how confident you are in each major step and single out the one point in your reconstruction about which you are least sure. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the reasoning, not a survey of secondary literature. Write in American English.
