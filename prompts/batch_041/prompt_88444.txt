ok so im a 4th-year in political theory, writing on the ethics of war, and theres a cluster of legal practices i keep tripping over and i want to actually think it through instead of just rehashing Walzer at myself.

heres the package. under the law of armed conflict, a soldier fighting for an aggressor state who shoots an enemy soldier commits no crime: hes a lawful combatant, hes entitled to POW status if captured, and he cant be prosecuted for the killing, however unjust the war he happens to be fighting in. but the people who launched that same war can be prosecuted — the crime of aggression is, by construction, a leaders-only crime; only people who effectively control or direct a states political or military action can commit it. and then a civilian in that same war who picks up a rifle and shoots an enemy soldier has no such immunity and can be tried for murder. three causally similar acts, three different legal treatments. and it gets stranger: the law also wont let the soldier opt out on the ground that *this* war is the unjust one — im pretty sure you can get conscientious objector status in the US if you object to all war but not if you object selectively to a particular war. so his own judgment about the wars justice is legally inert in both directions: it doesnt make him liable if he fights, and it doesnt excuse him if he refuses.

what i want: reconstruct the theory of responsibility (and maybe the theory of what a state is, or what a soldier is) that this whole package presupposes. does it presuppose the moral equality of combatants, or is that a red herring — could you get combatant immunity out of something much thinner, like a reciprocal convention both sides have an interest in keeping (more duelling code than moral claim), or out of a division-of-labour picture where the justice of the war is the leaders business and conduct is the soldiers? separate carefully what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit. say which rival views it quietly rules out. say where the three treatments pull against each other — my gut says immunity for the killer + liability for the leader + no right of refusal for the dissenter cant all fall out of one principle, but i want to know if thats right. and say what would have to change in the law if you took the revisionist line seriously, the one where unjust combatants are doing something seriously wrong, and whether the practice could survive that.
