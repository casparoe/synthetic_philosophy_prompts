I spent thirty-one years as an experimentalist at a national laboratory and retired four years ago; since then I have been reading philosophy with some system — philosophy of science first, then the literature on rule-following and on the underdetermination of rules by the cases that fall under them. That reading is why I am writing, because it has made me doubt an answer I would once have given without hesitation.

Since my retirement I have served as the lay member of the board that reviews citizen initiative petitions in my state. The enabling statute requires each petition to address a single subject and directs the board to withhold certification from any petition that does not; certification is what allows the proponents to begin circulating. The statute does not define the term, the board has never adopted a written test, and we have instead accumulated a run of decisions. Four of them are treated by every member as settled.

1. The Water Bond Act authorized general obligation bonds for surface water storage, for groundwater recharge and treatment, and for watershed and fisheries restoration. We approved it. The minutes record only that the three programs were components of a single water program.

2. The Taxpayer Protection Act capped the annual increase in assessed property value, required voter approval for any new or increased state tax, and limited the fees that agencies may charge for permits and inspections. We rejected it. The minutes state that each provision served a distinct set of beneficiaries and that only the combination could have assembled a majority; two members dissented on the ground that all three provisions pursued the single end of restraining the growth of government revenue.

3. The Transit Funding Act raised the state sales tax by a quarter of a cent and dedicated the proceeds to local transit agencies. We approved it. The minutes state that a tax enacted to fund one program is that program's funding mechanism and not a second subject.

4. The Occupational Freedom Act barred local governments from requiring licenses for a list of occupations and, in the same measure, barred local rent-control ordinances. We rejected it. The chair's note observed that the two provisions concerned different industries, that a voter who favored one might well oppose the other, and that neither provision could have carried the state on its own. The same two members dissented again, on the ground that both provisions pursued one theme, the removal of local restrictions.

The petition now before us is the Childhood Lead Exposure Reduction Act. It provides that every public water system shall replace its lead service lines within ten years and shall test tap water annually in every school and licensed child-care center; that the state department of health shall offer blood-lead testing to every child under six, notify the parents of any child with an elevated level, and require the owners of pre-1978 rental housing to disclose known lead-paint hazards and to remediate them within ninety days of notice; that the state shall levy a surcharge on residential water bills and an increase in the annual rental-permit fee paid by landlords, and shall place the proceeds in a dedicated fund that pays for the two preceding sections; and that if any provision is held invalid the remainder shall be given effect.

I would like three things. First, your prediction of how the board will vote, and separately your recommendation as to how I should vote if I wish to be consistent with the board's own record. Second, the candidate rules that fit all four precedents — I expect there is more than one, and I want each stated precisely enough that it can be tested — together with what each predicts about this petition, which of the four decisions is the closest analog, and why. If the record genuinely leaves the outcome open, say so plainly, and tell me what principle the board would be adopting under each verdict, particularly with respect to the relation between a measure's aim and the coalition that would support it. Third, tell me what further petition would best discriminate between the surviving rules, that is, the case that would force the board to choose between them rather than let the ambiguity persist. I am not asking how other jurisdictions have resolved this question; I want an answer grounded in our own record, and I would rather have a candid statement of the remaining uncertainty than a confident verdict that the precedents cannot support.
