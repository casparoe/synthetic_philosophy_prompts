I am a novelist by profession, though for the past three years I have been living inside a book about a field linguist who goes mad trying to translate a language isolate, and the research has pulled me into Quine's radical-translation scenario and the gavagai example. Next month I have been asked to give a colloquium talk to a philosophy department that is mostly philosophers of language in the Davidsonian tradition, with two Chomskyan linguists and one cultural anthropologist who has done actual fieldwork; they are friendly, but they will not let a novelist get away with hand-waving. My thesis is that the gavagai case has been misread as a lesson about the indeterminacy of translation. What it really shows, I will argue, is that the native's language may be holophrastic where ours is compositional: gavagai is not a referring term but an occasion-bound predicate, and the indeterminacy arises because our own grammar forces us to parse it as a term. I will also argue that this reading survives even if the translator is granted a rich inferential-role semantics, so the behaviorist framing is doing less work than either Quine's defenders or his critics suppose.
