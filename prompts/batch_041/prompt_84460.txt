I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy and I've somehow been roped into drafting one of the questions for our MA comprehensive in comparative ethics -- three hours, closed book, students answer two of five, 25 marks each. Mine is the Buddhist ethics slot. The trouble is that the standard debate over whether Buddhist ethics is best read as a form of consequentialism has been chewed over so thoroughly that a student can pass by just listing the positions: the it's-consequentialist reading, the it's-really-virtue-ethics reading, the it's-a-distinct-fourth-thing reading. I want a question that can't be answered that way, so here's the crux I want it built on. There's a move that shows up constantly in this literature: the no-self doctrine is supposed to underwrite agent-neutrality, because if there's no enduring self then my interests can't carry the kind of special weight that partiality needs, so we get pushed toward impartial concern for all sentient beings. Fine as far as it goes. But a student in my section flipped it on me last term and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. If persons are ultimately fictions, why does the person stay the unit of aggregation? Ordinary consequentialism needs a plurality of separate loci of welfare to sum across, and if the self is a convenient fiction then the line between my suffering and yours looks about as metaphysically robust as the line between my Tuesday and my Wednesday. So either the sum is over something other than persons, or the view stops being consequentialist in any recognizable sense, or the whole disagreement turns out to be verbal. I've been calling this the boundary problem in my notes, though I'm not attached to the name.

The case I want students to work with (phrase it more elegantly if you like): a healer in a plague year can either spend her remaining stock of medicine curing thirty people now, or spend it on a slower treatment that will save five people who, as a side effect of the treatment itself, will lose all their memories and end up psychologicaly discontinuous with the people who walked in. Ask whether the no-self doctrine changes how a consequentialist should count the five, whether it changes the thirty, and whether those two answers can come apart without the view falling apart. I want the question to force a verdict on that verbal-dispute escape hatch rather than just mentioning it -- if someone wants to say the two readings aren't really disagreeing about anything, they should have to pay for it.

So: give me the exam question itself (under 120 words, it has to fit the booklet), a model answer of around 600 to 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built on five must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how candidates typically lose that mark. Bear in mind they can't look anything up, and I don't want them rewarded for name-dropping, so please don't build the answer or the scheme around anyones particular paper -- no citations anywhere, and if the model answer wants to gesture at a position it should just describe it. I'd also like the scheme to say what separates an A from a B, especially on the last point about the escape hatch, since I suspect that's where all the weak answers will cluster.
