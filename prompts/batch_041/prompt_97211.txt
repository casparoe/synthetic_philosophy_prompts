I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I need to resolve a dispute that keeps resurfacing in design reviews. Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign at the end of a genuine adversarial collaboration on the question of whether artifacts have politics.

The first camp I will call the internal-logic camp: it holds that a technology has its own developmental trajectory, that once a technical paradigm is adopted the downstream social arrangements are largely forced by engineering and economic constraints, and that the artifact is therefore politically neutral, with any politics living in the surrounding institutions and incentives rather than in the thing itself. The second camp I will call the design-choice camp: it holds that any functional requirement can be satisfied by a wide space of implementations, that the choice among them is not settled by engineering, and that this gap is where political work gets done, so that the artifact is itself a political settlement frozen into code. Give both camps their strongest form and their best available arguments; do not build a strawman on either side.

Ground the document in one concrete case rather than in technology in general, so that it cannot float free of actual engineering. The case is a service that must shed load when it is overloaded: it cannot serve every request, so it must adopt a policy for deciding which requests to drop or delay. Treat that load-shedding policy as the disputed artifact and let the two camps argue about it specifically. I would also like you to introduce and then use three terms that I find useful but that neither camp currently owns: underdetermination, meaning the gap between a stated functional requirement and the set of implementations that satisfy it; reversal cost, meaning how expensive it is to change a social arrangement once an artifact embodying it has been deployed; and the median-user default, meaning the tendency of systems to be tuned to the typical user and to degrade for the tail. State explicitly whether each camp accepts each of these as a real phenomenon and whether it thinks the term does any explanatory work.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, including claims about the case above, about what would count as evidence for either side, and about which parts of the dispute are merely verbal; (2) the precise points on which they still disagree, each phrased in wording that the side holding it would accept as fair; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, stated as sharply as you can; and (4) for each crux, a case, argument, or experiment that both camps agree would bear on it, together with each camp's prediction, recorded in advance and stated so that the two predictions genuinely diverge.

Format the result as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, with columns for the point of disagreement, the internal-logic camp's formulation, the design-choice camp's formulation, the crux it turns on, the shared test, and each camp's prediction. Where you conclude that a disagreement is purely verbal, say so in the table and explain the distinction being drawn.
