I'm a senior backend engineer, and for the last couple of years I've been running a fortnightly seminar at my company on value disagreement in product and policy decisions. The audience is experienced engineers — people who are very good at finding the crux of a technical disagreement and quite bad at noticing when they've smuggled an economic metaphor into a moral one — and none of them has any formal philosophy training, which is rather the point of the seminar. We've decided to close the next cycle with a graded written exercise, and I've been asked to draft the single essay item. What I need from you is the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. The item has to defeat the candidate who has read the assigned material and can recite that compromise means each side gives up something, or that trade is mutually beneficial because the parties value things differently, but who hasn't actually thought about whether the analogy holds. The thing I keep circling, and would like the question to force into the open, is this: people constantly reach for the idea that a compromise between holders of different value systems is a trade, and that if each side concedes something it cares less about in exchange for something it cares more about, the result is an improvement for both — nobody worse off by their own lights. I find this compelling and I suspect it's smuggling something in. In an ordinary trade each party is exchanging things that are theirs to exchange, and the gain has to be measured in some common currency, and it may matter a great deal whether the two parties take their moral views to be preferences they happen to have or beliefs about how things really ought to go — a concession looks like one thing if you're giving up a preference and quite another if you're doing what you believe to be wrong. The case that bothers me most is the one where two parties strike a swap that both regard as an improvement and that leaves a third party, who was never at the table, worse off; I genuinely cannot tell whether that shows the arrangement is defective or shows the analogy is. The running example in the seminar has been a product team split between engineers who treat a data-sharing feature as a privacy violation and engineers who treat not shipping it as a safety failure, so something in that neighbourhood would land well, though I'd rather you didn't let the question collapse into a generic stakeholder-negotiation exercise.

Please write the essay question for a 60-minute closed-book exercise, pitched at that audience, where a good answer runs to roughly 800 words. The question should require the candidate to state what has to be true for a moral compromise to count as a genuine gain from trade, to commit to a verdict on at least one case where the analogy strains, and to say what turns on getting that verdict right — it should not be answerable by summarising the metaphor or by listing considerations on both sides without deciding. Then give me a model answer of about 800 to 900 words, written as a strong candidate would write it, and a marking scheme out of 30 built from five or six must-hit moves, each with the marks it is worth and a line on what a merely competent answer does in its place. Finish the scheme with a short list of the specific errors that should keep a candidate below a pass, and with a note on the single move that separates a distinction-level answer from a solid one. Two constraints: the candidates cannot look anything up, so the item must be answerable by reasoning about the cases rather than by name-dropping, and I don't want it to depend on any particular author, book, or article — I'll add reading suggestions myself later. Please don't include references or citations anywhere in the model answer or the scheme.
