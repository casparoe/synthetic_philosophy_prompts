The essay:

Agency-Matching and the Limits of the Transparency Test

Two accounts of manipulation dominate the discussion, and I think both fail.

The first is the deception account: manipulation is influence that induces false beliefs, or at least beliefs the target would reject if better informed. It fails on cases of true but misleading influence. If I present a set of options in an order chosen to exploit the target's tendency to favour the first, I need not have said anything false; the target's beliefs may all be true, and yet the influence is manipulative. The account also struggles with non-doxastic influence — pressure, flattery, the manufacture of urgency — where nothing is believed at all.

The second is the bypass account: manipulation is influence that works around or subverts the target's rational agency, exploiting bias, emotion, or habit rather than addressing reasons. This is closer, but it is caught between two failures. Read causally, it is too broad: all influence, including a good argument, works through causal processes in the target's cognitive machinery, and no line between addressing reasons and pulling levers survives inspection. Read normatively, it is too narrow: it tells us which influences count as manipulation only once we already know which psychological routes are respectable, which is the thing we wanted to know.

I propose instead an agency-matching condition. Manipulation is influence in which the level of agency the influencer recruits diverges from the level the target takes themselves to be exercising. When I exploit an order effect, I recruit an automatic process while the target believes they are deliberating over options; when I argue, I recruit the target's deliberative capacities and they know it. On this view the mark of manipulation is not falsity or irrationality but a kind of category error in the transaction: the target is doing one thing and being used for another.

The condition needs a supplement, because education and therapy also recruit automatic processes — repetition, imitation, habit — while the learner or patient takes themselves to be reasoning. What distinguishes the therapist from the manipulator is not the mechanism but the attitude toward the target's eventual recognition of it. So I add a recognition condition: the influence is manipulative when the influencer is indifferent to, or would be undermined by, the target's coming to see the mechanism at work.

But the recognition condition threatens to collapse the account into a transparency test — influence is legitimate just in case it would survive disclosure of the mechanism — and that test is too strong. Much legitimate persuasion would not survive disclosure: a syllabus that arranges readings to produce a gradual conversion, a friend who makes a well-chosen example land by not explaining why they chose it. Disclosure would not refute these influences so much as destroy them, and destroying them is not the same as showing them to be manipulative.

I therefore want to distinguish method-transparency from aim-transparency. What must survive the target's reflective scrutiny is not the method but the influencer's aim. The friend's method — the unannounced choice of example — need not survive disclosure; the friend's aim — that I come to see something true — must, and does. The manipulator fails the test not because the method is hidden but because the aim, once seen, would be rejected: what is wanted is not my seeing the truth but my behaving a certain way, or my continuing to hold a belief convenient to the influencer.

The conclusion is that the locus of manipulation is in the aim, not the mechanism. Manipulation is influence whose aim, and not merely whose method, would fail the target's reflective endorsement.
