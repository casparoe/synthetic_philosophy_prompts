I am a high-school student and a member of my school's debate team, and I would like to know whether there is an established name for a pattern I keep running into when people argue about a present decision by invoking the past.

The pattern is easiest to state with the two examples I hear most often: someone says that the lesson of Munich is that you never negotiate with a dictator, or that the lesson of Vietnam is that you cannot win a land war in Asia. I am not asking about those episodes themselves. What I noticed is the shape of the move. The "lesson" is almost never stated as a rule that could be carried over to a new situation and then turn out wrong; it is stored as a description of the old situation, and the phrase "the lesson of X" is just a way of pointing at that description. So when a new situation arises, the question people actually dispute is not "does the rule apply here?" but "is this another Munich?" — a question about resemblance. The resemblance is then settled by listing features of the new situation that were never part of the original lesson, with nobody explaining why those are the features that matter. Sometimes the move is made with no content at all — "we have seen this movie before" — which I take to be the purest version, since it claims the guidance comes from resemblance and supplies no rule.

I have tried asking, in these arguments, what would have to be different about the present situation for someone to agree that the lesson does not apply. I have never gotten a condition; I only get more features. So two people who agree completely about the facts of the present case can still disagree about whether the lesson applies, because they disagree about which features of the old case are the important ones, and the old case has as many features as you care to list. The argument feels decisive while being, as far as I can tell, unchecked by anything.

I have been told this is just the availability heuristic, or just hindsight bias, or just confirmation bias, and none of those seems quite right, because my thing is not about how easily a case comes to mind or about how people remember their own predictions; it is about where the guidance is stored and how its scope gets fixed. I have also been told that this is simply what all reasoning from experience looks like, since even a rule has to be applied to cases, and I would like a straight answer on that too.

Please commit to a position. Tell me whether an established concept covers this; if one does, name it and say precisely how the closest rivals — analogical reasoning, the availability heuristic, hasty generalization, hindsight bias — differ from it. If none does, coin a term, define it, and tell me what lies outside it, including whether it is a fallacy at all or merely the unavoidable shape of any argument from precedent. I have no way to look things up, so please do not send me to sources. Start your reply with a one-sentence verdict, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question.
