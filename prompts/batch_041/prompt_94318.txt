Ok so I'm writing a seminar paper on Galileo's hermeneutics and I want to use that famous line — scripture teaches us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go — as my epigraph, since he quotes it approvingly in the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (he credits some cardinal with it, I forget which). But every time I try to write one sentence saying what the line actually claims, I end up with either a platitude or a thesis I'm pretty sure Galileo would've rejected.

Like if I say "scripture makes no claims about nature," that's way stronger than anything he argues — he spends pages offering alternate readings of specific passages, Joshua telling the sun to stand still and so on, and you don't bother reinterpreting a passage unless you think it was asserting something in the first place. But if I say "scripture does make claims about nature, just accommodated ones," then I've lost the clean division of labor and I have to explain how you're supposed to tell an accommodated claim from a literal one, which seems to be exactly the thing under dispute.

So can you seperate out for me, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that line could be expressing — from the ones that are basically truisms about genre and audience, up to the ones that are real substantive claims about divine authorship and what the Holy Spirit intended — and for each one say whether it's actually defensible, whether Galileo needs it for the argument he's making in the letter, and whether it's compatible with the others? I especially want to know which reading does the work people want it to do when they trot it out today to settle a science-and-religion fight, and which reading Galileo is actually committed to once you press on him. My suspicion is that the readings that are most useful for the modern non-overlapping-magisteria move are exactly the ones that make his own exegetical labor pointless, but I can't tell if that's a real tension or just me running two different questions together. Bonus if you can say something about the difference between "the Holy Spirit didn't intend to teach astronomy" and "the Holy Spirit taught astronomy wrongly on purpose" — those sound close but I have a feeling they come apart badly for him.
