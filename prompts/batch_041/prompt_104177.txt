I chair the evidence-standards review board at a public health research funder. At next week's meeting we must vote on a proposal that its authors present as a methodological breakthrough and its critics call a waste of public money. The team wants to test the causal universal that every exposure to solvent Z produces peripheral neuropathy. Instead of following exposed workers forward and counting neuropathy cases, they propose to sample people who do not have neuropathy and verify that each was not exposed to solvent Z. Their argument is that the two claims are logically equivalent, so every neuropathy-free person with no solvent-Z exposure is a confirming instance of the universal; a large enough sample, they say, would raise its probability and do so more cheaply than a prospective cohort. One board member has called this a textbook fallacy; another says the logic is unassailable and only the epidemiology is questionable. I need a written opinion I can circulate before the vote. Please draft it.

It should recommend a concrete disposition -- approve, reject, or approve only with specified conditions and a timetable -- and give the board reasons it can act on. It should not simply explain the raven paradox to us; assume we know the basic shape. Instead, engage the crux: what the equivalence condition actually licenses, why a positive instance can confirm a universal in the formal sense while still being nearly worthless as evidence for a causal claim, how background assumptions, base rates, selection effects, and the contrast class of cases the study ignores bear on whether the proposal should be funded, and whether the applicant's reasoning proves too much -- if every non-black non-raven confirms that all ravens are black, what stops every observation from confirming every universal under some description? Give an honest account of the strongest point on the side you come down against. The memo should be about 900 to 1200 words, in prose with a clear bottom line and a short numbered list of conditions if you recommend conditional approval. Do not include citations or reading lists; argue from the concepts themselves.
