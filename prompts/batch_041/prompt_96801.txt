My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Aristotle's sea-battle problem, and I came away unsure how much I had conceded. He is better read in this area than I am, and he set out his reading with a confidence I could not match. His account went roughly like this:

"It is not complicated. Aristotle asks whether a prediction about the future can already be true. If it is true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the battle is already settled; nothing the commanders do tonight can make any difference, and the fatalist wins. Aristotle sees that this would make deliberation and responsibility pointless, so his way out is to deny that a prediction about a contingent future is true or false yet. It becomes true when the battle occurs and false if it does not. That is how he gives up the law of excluded middle for statements about the future, and that is what leaves the future genuinely open. He is therefore the first philosopher of free will, and the reason logicians have been worrying about future contingents ever since."

I suspect he is nearer the truth than I wanted to admit, and I do not know the text well enough to find the seam on my own. Please audit his account the way someone who works on this material would. Take it sentence by sentence and tell me, for each, whether it is accurate, whether it is the sort of simplification a careful teacher would let stand, or whether it is a misrepresentation; where a specialist sympathetic to Aristotle would object, give me the objection in a form I could actually put back to my friend; and tell me whether his conclusion — that Aristotle leaves the future open and founds the free will debate — survives once anything inaccurate earlier in the summary is corrected. If, on inspection, his reading turns out to be defensible rather than confused, say so plainly; I would rather be told that I lost the argument than be handed a correction invented to flatter me. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list. I want to know which of his sentences I can trust, and what work the doubtful ones are quietly doing.
