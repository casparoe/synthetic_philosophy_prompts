Next month I'm giving the toast at the wedding of two close friends who met eight years ago in a persistent online world and knew each other only as avatars for the first four years. I'm the groom's friend from before he ever logged in: I've never played the game, and I've never been inside the place where half this wedding met. That half, the ones who know the couple only as avatars, will be sharing tables with the family, including the bride's grandmother, who has said, without malice, that she doesn't see how you can love someone you met on a computer. Write the five-minute toast: warm, funny, recognizably a wedding toast, but doing real work. It has to find what is right in the grandmother's objection — something in it is true, and papering over that will lose her and the family half of the room — while refusing her conclusion, and it has to say what makes a place or a friendship real without turning into a lecture. The couple have asked that no in-joke be explained, but the family should still be able to follow the shape of every joke whose reference they miss. The groom has a line he uses whenever someone says it was just a game — a place is real if you can be homesick for it — and I want the toast to earn that line rather than quote it.
