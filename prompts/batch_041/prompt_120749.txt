I'm teaching a grad seminar on pain this term — half philosophers, half med students, plus a pain-clinic nurse who keeps us honest — and I keep getting stuck on the same thing, so I figured I'd just ask. The unit is on why pain is bad in the first place, and the case we've been chewing over is pain asymbolia. As I understand it, these patients — typically after damage to the insula, sometimes after a cingulotomy done for intractable pain — will tell you quite calmly that yes, they feel it, they can point to where it is, they can give you a number for how strong it is, but it just doesn't bother them. No distress, no suffering, by some reports they don't even flinch and some of them laugh. So the sensation is intact and the awfulness has come off, and everyone in the room suddenly notices they don't know which of the two they'd been calling the pain. Meanwhile the standard definition of pain has unpleasantness baked right into it — pain is meant to be an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience — so on the book definition these patients aren't in pain at all, which is exactly the sort of thing that makes the clinic nervous.

Here's the puzzle, and I want the practices, not a theory of pain. Think about how pain is actually handled. On the wards, in the quality metrics, in how an analgesic gets approved, pain is a number: you score it 0 to 10, the number is the target, moving the average score down is the endpoint, and the patient's own report is treated as the last word — pain is whatever they say it is. That's the whole fifth-vital-sign way of doing things. Now send the same patient down the hall to a chronic pain rehab program and you get the opposite teaching: the hurt isn't the harm, intensity and suffering and disability are separate things that come apart, and the goal is to get somebody living again even if the number never budges. And then there's the vet, who has no report to go on at all and has to infer that it's bad from the wiring plus the behavior — which is a third position again.

So: what do these practices presuppose? Does score-it-and-treat-the-number presuppose that the badness of pain is a function of how intense it feels — one magnitude — and therefore that a pain which doesn't bother you is either impossible or not really pain? Does the rehab program presuppose instead that the badness sits in the affective, aversive component, so that intensity on its own is close to inert? Which of those does each practice actually need, and which does it merely happen to sit well with? Where do they genuinely contradict each other, and where is the whole thing underdetermined — several different accounts of pain's badness licensing the very same protocol? Then take the asymbolic patient, who reports a 9 out of 10 and genuinely isn't distressed and never once asks for relief: what does the ward protocol tell you to do, what does the trial endpoint tell you, what does the vet's rule tell you, and could any of them give up the view of badness they seem to rest on and still be recognizably the same practice? And if you decided intensity was simply the wrong thing to target, what would concretely have to change — the scale, the metrics, the consent conversation, the approval endpoint? I'd rather not get a bibliography, I've got alot of those already.
