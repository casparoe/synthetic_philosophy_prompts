I run a 90-minute workshop on data and power — about 16 people, mixed bag (a few law students, some civil-liberties volunteers, a couple of engineers). I want an activity where they run three versions of the same little society and feel the difference in their bodies: that privacy means three quite different things depending on which regime you're standing in. Not a lecture about Germany vs America vs China — they should definately bump into the differences through the rules themselves.

So here's roughly what I'm picturing. Everyone holds cards with facts about them, some private, some already public, and there's a central register. Round one runs on the German idea of informational self-determination: purpose binding, so the register may only use a card for the purpose it was collected for, you can inspect the access log, you can demand deletion — and the deep point, which I want them to hit without me saying it, is that the protection is supposed to work by keeping data scattered across unconnected agencies rather than by keeping it secret (German writers sometimes call this a separation of informational powers). Round two runs on the American default: notice and consent, publicity as the norm, once you've handed a card over you've no standing to complain, and whoever receives a card may pass it on — so give them a market, with a price on sharing. Round three is the social-credit one: everything goes on the public board and the group scores each other against stated norms, with privileges for the high scorers.

Payoffs should be small and real — biscuits, first pick of seats, a coffee voucher for the top score — not imaginary money.

What they should come away seeing: the German model protects control over the terms on which you appear, not secrecy, and its real enemy is the complete profile rather than the disclosure; the American one protects against intrusion, which is why recombining public data feels like no violation at all; and the third turns visibility into an instrument of governance aimed at the group rather than the individual. Also the nasty bit — that round three is roughly what Westerners already live under via platforms, so nobody should leave thinking it's something foreign. And I'd like the debrief to get right that the actual Chinese system is a patchwork of blacklists and red lists and local pilot schemes rather than one national score; if they build round three as a single unified number, they've built a strawman.

Give me rules, materials, a timeline, the debrief mapping each moment onto the underlying idea, what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it (I'm expecting at least one person to refuse round three, and the scoring to get contaminated by whos popular), and an honest note on which feature of all this the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
