Last week a friend of mine, a population ecologist, and I spent most of a long drive arguing about the units of selection, and the argument ended where it always ends, with his claim that every case anyone has ever called group selection can be rewritten as selection among individuals or among genes, so that the whole controversy is a dispute about bookkeeping rather than about nature. I could not say precisely what would be lost if he were right, and on Thursday I have to teach the topic.

The setting: twelve first-year doctoral students in an interdisciplinary seminar, mostly ecologists and molecular biologists, none of whom has thought about levels of selection since an undergraduate lecture on kin selection, and two or three of whom suspect the whole subject is a hangover from the 1960s. I have eighty minutes, a whiteboard, and no set text. Do not explain the debate to me; plan how I should teach it.

What I need first is the order of ideas, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be in place before the question of the level at which selection acts can be asked in a way that has an answer, and why each later piece is unintelligible without the earlier ones. Tell me where the distinction between a replicator and a vehicle belongs in that sequence, and whether it should arrive before or after the students have seen that a single population can be described correctly at more than one level.

For the example that carries the session I want to use something I have invented rather than the usual yeasts and siderophores, half of which they will have met already and will nod at without thinking. Mine is this: larvae from many different parents settle on one rock and fuse into a single colony with a shared water canal; a fraction of the colony's cells become stinging defenders that cannot reproduce at all and die with the colony; the colony reproduces only when a fragment breaks off and settles elsewhere. The defenders therefore have no individual fitness of their own, their neighbours are not their relatives, and the entity that reproduces is the colony. Tell me whether this example can bear the weight of the session: what it has that the session needs, what it lacks, whether the lack is fatal or instructive, and, if fatal, what a replacement would have to contain.

Then the misconceptions: what they will bring in with them, what they will form during the session, the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, and the move that heads it off. I expect at least that a gene's-eye view will be taken to mean that there is no group selection, the inference from groups differing in productivity to group selection having been demonstrated, and the converse error of treating group selection as the theory that rescues altruism; I would like the rest. I also want one question to put to them at the end that would show whether they can distinguish a trait that merely benefits a group from a trait selected at the level of groups, and that a student who has learned only the vocabulary would fail. Finally, tell me what I should refuse to cover in eighty minutes and how to say so out loud, and whether the equivalence my friend insists on should be the destination of the session or a live dispute left open -- and if part of what he and I disagreed about is merely verbal, which part, so that I do not stage for the students a disagreement that is not there.
