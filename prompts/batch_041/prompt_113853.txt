Write a municipal statutory instrument — a Heap Regulation Ordinance — for a city council that has undertaken to settle, by legislation, the question of where a pile of grains stops being a pile and starts being a heap. The document should be about 700 words and should consist only of documentary forms: recitals, numbered sections, schedules, an explanatory note, and a short dissenting appendix. The register is deadpan bureaucratic. Nothing in it should acknowledge that it is funny, and no voice other than the documents should be heard.

The ordinance must do each of the following, and each item should be handled so that a reader who has thought seriously about vagueness finds it technically apt rather than merely approximate:

1. The recitals state, as a finding of fact rather than a concession, that the addition or removal of a single grain never changes the classification of a pile.
2. A schedule then fixes an exact number of grains at which a pile becomes a heap. The same schedule records that this number is a fact about the world which no inspection, measurement, or census can disclose, and it provides that a citizen who asserts knowledge of the number commits an offence; if the assertion later proves to have been correct, the penalty is doubled.
3. A section criminalises the march: it is an offence to argue in any proceeding that because n grains is a heap, n+1 grains must be, or that because n grains is not a heap, n-1 grains cannot be.
4. A tribunal procedure governs cases that are neither clearly heaps nor clearly non-heaps. The tribunal is to decide such a case by asking what the ordinance would have said under every reading of the schedule it could permissibly have adopted, and to find for the citizen only if the pile is a heap under all of them. The tribunal is forbidden to publish which reading it adopted, on the ground that publication would create a precedent, and a precedent would be a boundary.
5. A sub-committee is established to determine whether a given case is definitely borderline or merely borderline-borderline, with an appeal from that sub-committee to a further sub-committee of the same description.
6. A fine schedule is graduated by grain count, so that a pile of five hundred grains is half a heap and owes half the fee. The explanatory note should observe, without comment, that on this schedule the sentence stating that if five hundred grains is a heap then five hundred and one is comes out half true.
7. A companion schedule addresses baldness, adopted by reference.
8. A commencement clause provides that the ordinance enters into force once the city contains a sufficient number of heaps, no number being specified.

The document should never use the vocabulary of the philosophical literature — no talk of vagueness, tolerance, gaps, degrees of truth, or higher-order anything. The content must arrive entirely through the procedure.

The target is the demand for exact boundaries, and the wish to keep tolerance and precision at the same time, which is the temptation running through every standard response to the problem. No named philosopher, no real statute, and no living person should appear. The piece should never state the objections; a specialist should be able to identify each clause as a position in legal dress, and a reader who knows nothing of the literature should still find the whole thing quietly menacing. End with the dissenting appendix: three or four sentences from a single councillor, who states the difficulty in plain language without realising what he has said, and is overruled.
