I run the long-horizon country risk desk at a mid-sized institutional investor. Twice a year we refresh a political-instability score, 0 to 100, for roughly forty sovereigns; the score sets position limits, so a move of a few points is expensive and a miss in either direction costs us money and credibility. The desk is two analysts. We get one week per country, we have no historians on staff, we buy standard macroeconomic and governance data and nothing more specialised, and every score must survive sign-off by our head of risk, who reads no theory and will not accept a number he cannot trace. Our present method, a weighted checklist of governance indicators, has been useless for two years: it rates a dozen countries as safe that our portfolio managers no longer believe in, and it has never told us anything before it was in the newspapers.

I want to replace it with a procedure built on the structural-demographic account of secular cycles, in the tradition of Turchin's work: the claim that long-run political instability is driven by the joint and interacting deterioration of popular wellbeing (falling real wages, rising costs of the basic means of life), elite overproduction (more aspirants to elite positions than the society can absorb, producing factional competition), and the state's fiscal and legitimacy capacity (revenue, debt service, and the public's willingness to accept the state's authority), with societies passing through long integrative and disintegrative phases rather than fluctuating randomly.

Write me that procedure. Assume the analyst who runs it knows the framework only from a one-page briefing note, so it must be a sequence of steps with decision rules rather than a discussion. In particular:

1. The inputs. What must be measured, what proxies you will accept when the ideal indicator does not exist for a country, and how each proxy is to be discounted for being a proxy. Then the rule for the case we actually face every cycle: a country we are obliged to score but for which a core indicator simply does not exist. Say when the honest output is a refusal to score, and what minimum data would lift the refusal.
2. The steps, in order, with what counts as sufficient at each and where the procedure stops.
3. Diagnosis and forecast kept apart: where a country sits in the cycle versus when and how hard the turn comes. Say what the analyst should output when the first is confident and the second is not, and resist, explicitly, the temptation to let a confident diagnosis smuggle in a dated forecast.
4. A falsification field: what observable development in the next twenty-four months would show the reading was wrong, and what would count as the framework failing rather than the reading.
5. Two lists I care about more than the rest: the points where the analyst is exercising judgment dressed up as measurement, and the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person. Do not pad these with trivialities.
6. A closing statement of what the procedure cannot do, including how the fact that the framework is contested, since historians and modellers dispute whether this kind of macrohistory can be quantified at all, should show up inside the score and its stated uncertainty rather than being hidden behind a single number.

Then run it on the two countries in the note below. Do not soften either result. Our house view is that Ambria is a safe anchor and Reznia an obvious trouble spot; I have no attachment to those numbers, and I would rather learn now that the procedure refuses to score one of them, or rates the anchor worse than the trouble spot, than learn it from a loss.

Case 1 — Ambria. Upper-middle-income democracy, population 48m. Real median wage flat for twelve years while GDP per capita rose about a fifth. Share of 25–34-year-olds with tertiary qualifications up from 18% to 41% within a generation; youth unemployment 27%. House price to median income 9.5, up from 4.1. Nine parties now in the legislature, up from three; largest bloc 22% of seats. Government debt 84% of GDP; interest 9% of revenue. Trust in parliament 19%, down from 44%. Press freedom mid-table. No data on elite composition, elite aspirant numbers, or factional competition. Our current score: 18.

Case 2 — Reznia. Lower-income petro-state, population 11m. Oil and gas are 71% of fiscal revenue. Non-oil GDP per capita down 6% over five years. Urbanisation 38%; secondary completion 44%; Gini 0.52. Ruling party in power since 1994, no independent legislature, no free press. Reserves cover three months of spending. No data on wages, employment, elite networks, or public opinion. Our current score: 72.
