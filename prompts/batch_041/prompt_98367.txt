I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm at work on a long feature about a dispute in metaphysics that I suspect matters more than its subject matter suggests: whether a thing's dispositions — its fragility, solubility, flammability — can be analyzed in terms of what would happen to it under certain conditions, or whether they must be taken as primitive features of the world. The naive conditional analysis, on which x is disposed to M when S just in case x would M if S, is broken by three familiar kinds of case: the fink, in which the stimulus itself destroys the disposition before it can manifest; the mask, in which the manifestation is blocked while the disposition remains; and the mimic, in which something produces the manifestation without the disposition. The repair I keep encountering in the literature is to add a clause to the effect that the thing would manifest if its causal basis were intact and unmasked, and my question is whether that clause is doing genuine analytical work or merely helping itself to the very notion it was meant to analyze.

I have been turning over a variant of my own, and I would like you to reason about it rather than look it up. Imagine a lock disposed to open when the correct key is inserted. The lock is fitted with a single mechanism that, at the instant the key turns, does something to the lock that can be described with equal accuracy either as destroying the internal structure that makes opening possible or as leaving that structure intact and merely interposing an obstacle; by stipulation there is no further fact that decides between the two descriptions. After the key turns, the lock does not open. Is the disposition gone, so that the case is a fink, or is it merely blocked, so that the case is a mask? I cannot get a clean answer, and I have begun to suspect that the fink/mask distinction may be less a joint in nature than a distinction in how we choose to describe a case.

Here is what I would like from you. Give me a single credence — a number between 0 and 1 — that the conditional analysis of dispositions can be repaired, non-circularly and without smuggling in dispositional notions, by clauses about the causal basis being intact and unmasked. Then decompose it: how much of your number rides on whether finks and masks are genuinely possible states of affairs rather than artifacts of description; how much on whether intact and unmasked can be cashed out in categorical, non-dispositional terms; how much on whether the causal basis of a disposition can be identified independently of the disposition itself; how much on whether a circular analysis might still be a correct metaphysical account even if it fails as a conceptual one; and any further terms you think belong, each with a rough probability and one line of justification. Combine them into your final number, tell me which sub-question it is most sensitive to, and say what argument or finding would move it most. For calibration: I would put myself at about 0.35, and the philosopher I interviewed last week puts herself at about 0.8; if you can, tell me which term in the decomposition she and I are most likely actually disagreeing about. No citations or reading list, please — I am interested in how you weigh it, not in who said what.
