In my philosophy of mind seminar we have spent several weeks on the idea that a pattern is real when a description of a system in the pattern's terms is more economical than a description in lower-level terms, and that the physical, design, and intentional stances are three such descriptions, ordered by how much each presupposes about the system. To test whether I had understood the view, I worked out the argument below. Its conclusion is far too strong to be true, but I cannot find the step that fails, and I would like you to identify it and tell me what repairing it costs.

(1) A pattern is real just in case some description of a system is more compact than the system's raw behavioral record and, from that compact description, the rest of the record can be predicted.

(2) A stance is a vocabulary in which such descriptions are written; to adopt a stance toward a system is to commit to describing it in that vocabulary.

(3) The intentional stance describes a system as having beliefs and desires and predicts that it will act so as to satisfy those desires in the light of those beliefs.

(4) For any system and any finite record of its behavior there exists a compact description from which the record can be recovered: the shortest program that outputs the record.

(5) Hence every system is predictable under some stance, and therefore contains some real pattern.

(6) The criterion ranks descriptions only by how much they compress the record, and many different vocabularies compress the same record equally well.

(7) In particular, the description that says the system behaves as though it aimed to produce exactly the record it in fact produced compresses that record as well as any intentional description does.

(8) So the intentional stance is not privileged: every system contains indefinitely many equally real patterns, one for each vocabulary that compresses it.

(9) It follows that beliefs and desires are no more real than any of those contrived patterns.

(10) That cannot be right.

My suspicion is that the trouble lies in how I move from (4) to (5), though I cannot say what the trouble is. I am also unsure whether I should give up the criterion or give up the claim that the intentional stance is special. If I had to choose, I would rather keep (1) and lose the specialness than keep the specialness and lose the criterion, but I want to know what that preference commits me to. Please identify the step to reject, explain why it looked convincing, and set out what rejecting it costs — for instance, whether saving the view forces the reality of a pattern to depend on facts about us rather than on the system, and whether the three stances can still be ordered at all.

Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though writing me a letter.
