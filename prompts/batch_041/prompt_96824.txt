I have been trying to state my own view about who is answerable when an autonomous system causes serious harm, and I have a set of verdicts that seem to me to cohere without my being able to say what the coherence is. I would like the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that gets them, along with an honest account of which verdicts it cannot keep.

My verdicts:

1. A loitering munition in fully autonomous mode kills a child it has classified as a combatant. The operator who launched it had no channel to intervene once it was away, and the munition behaved within the accuracy envelope its manual stated. Verdict: the operator is not answerable for the death. I would rather see the family compensated from a standing fund than see the operator carry it.

2. Same munition, same launch, except that the officer who ordered it had been shown the classifier's false-positive rate on children in cluttered terrain and had a crewed alternative sitting on the ramp. Verdict: the officer is answerable, fully and without remainder.

3. The engineer who trained the classifier knew the training set underrepresented children in cluttered terrain, wrote a memo saying so, was overruled, and had no authority to hold the release. Verdict: she is not answerable. But I also think the manufacturer owes the family something, and I cannot say what that is if it is not her responsibility.

4. A trading firm hands a reinforcement-learning system the whole strategy space, with nothing but risk limits as constraints. It finds a strategy nobody at the firm anticipated and could not have anticipated, and that strategy crashes a market. Verdict: the firm is answerable, and not merely liable — blameworthy.

5. A hospital adopts a triage model that is less accurate for one patient group than another, which the hospital's clinical leads knew. The only alternative on offer was the previous, worse protocol for everyone, and the model saves more lives overall. Verdict: the hospital is not answerable for the deaths of the patients it treated worse.

6. A human analyst sits at the end of a targeting pipeline with three seconds to accept or reject the system's recommendation, on an interface that presents acceptance as the default and rejection as a five-step exception. She accepts; the target was a wedding party. Verdict: she is not answerable — the arrangement was built to produce exactly her deference.

7. The person who specified the three-second window and that interface. Verdict: answerable, fully, though they touched nothing, ordered nothing, and are not a but-for cause of the deaths in any ordinary sense.

8. A firm that cannot make its system safe arranges its development pipeline across four subcontractors in three jurisdictions, drawing the interfaces precisely so that no party has authority over the model's constraints. Verdict: whoever drew those interfaces is answerable, though nobody has authority and nobody intended any harm.

The case I cannot settle: a system that has been learning in the field for years, so that at the moment of the harm no living person had an accurate picture of what it would do, and at every earlier point each person involved did what a reasonable person would have done with the information then available. My instinct is that somebody is answerable, and I cannot say who without the answer collapsing into the name of the firm, which feels like a slogan rather than a name.

What I have so far, and do not trust: answerability tracks whoever held the authority to change the constraints under which the system ran, and who knew or should have known that the harm lay inside the envelope those constraints left open. That seems to get 2, 4, 7 and 8. It seems to overreach on 3, since she had no authority yet I want the manufacturer to owe something, and on 5, since the hospital had both the authority and the knowledge; and it says nothing useful about 1 or 6.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, actually captures these verdicts? State it precisely, tie each clause to the case that forces it, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed, and say which of my verdicts it cannot keep. If you have to overturn one, name it and price the loss. I would rather have a principle with a clean account of one broken verdict than an elaborate one that saves them all, and I am not asking for a survey of what others have said — I want your position.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my cases. Take a definite position: commit to a principle and defend it, rather than laying the candidates side by side and leaving the choice to me.
