Last night my friend Devi and I got into one of those 2am arguments that ruins the next day, and it was about Frederick Douglass, of all people — specifically the moment in the early 1850s when he broke with Garrison and announced that he no longer thought the Constitution was a proslavery document. Devi's take, which I have not been able to shake since: Douglass didn't discover anything. He didn't find a clause he'd overlooked, he didn't unearth new evidence about the founding, he just changed what he thought it takes to interpret a constitution. The Garrisonians read the thing the way a historian would — what did the people who wrote it and ratified it actually intend and expect? — and by that light it comes out as a compact with slaveholders: the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, the whole arrangement. Douglass switched to reading it the way a lawyer reads a contract: the text never uses the words slave or slavery, it gestures at the enslaved only obliquely, and its stated purposes are ones that slavery contradicts, so the document is on the side of freedom. Devi says that isn't an argument, it's a switch of methods, and it got switched because the politics of the decade demanded it — you can't work inside the Union or run for anything if you've already declared the founding charter a slaveholders' pact, so the charter got re-read until it stopped being one. She calls it the 'switcheroo' reading. I said that's cheap: Douglass had an actual argument that intent-based reading is simply the wrong method for a constitution, and a method can be adopted for good reasons even when the timing is politically convenient. We went back and forth for hours, I cited things I half-remembered, she cited things she actually knows, and neither of us landed a punch.

I'm applying for a small summer research grant in my department and I want to write the proposal on this, because I think theres a real question buried in our fight that neither of us managed to state properly. What I want the proposal to be about: when Douglass changed his mind, did he change his mind about a fact — what the Constitution says and requires — or about a method — what it is to read a constitution at all? And is that distinction even stable, or does every substantive conclusion about a document smuggle in a method, and every method smuggle in a substantive conclusion? Then the bigger version: is the claim that the Constitution was proslavery a determinate claim at all, or does it only get an answer relative to an interpretive theory, so that Douglass and Garrison weren't really disagreeing about the document but about what counts as the document in the first place?

I want the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, an explanation of why the obvious positions don't settle it — textualism, originalism, the living-constitution stuff, and the idea that a text just means whatever a community ends up using it to mean — then the actual method, like what I'd do month by month, what a successful result would look like versus what would count as the project failing, and an honest read on whether this is definately a semester's worth of work or whether I'm kidding myself.
