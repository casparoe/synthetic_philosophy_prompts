I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind and cognitive science, with about 18 students. I want to run an activity that makes them feel the force of the idea that Darwin and Turing each invert the usual order of explanation: instead of explaining a competent process by the comprehension behind it, we explain the comprehension by a process that has none. I do not want them to hear the names Darwin or Turing, or any label for the idea, until the debrief.

Design the activity so that it stages a double dissociation. In the first phase, the group should produce an outcome that is recognisably competent — something that looks as though it required understanding, planning, or judgement — even though no individual participant understands how the outcome was reached and no participant could explain it afterwards. In the second phase, the group should be given a rule, description, or procedure that they can understand perfectly well, and they should discover that understanding it does not enable them to produce the competent output; the comprehension is real but inert. The two phases should be different enough that students do not immediately see them as the same trick, but similar enough that the debrief can show them the shared structure. The point should show up in what happens, not in what the facilitator says.

The rules, roles, materials, and incentives should do the work. Do not rely on a lecture to introduce the idea, and do not have the facilitator explain the point mid-activity. Where the activity needs a payoff or a scoring rule, use something that does not require real money. Include a timeline for the 90 minutes, including set-up and debrief.

In the debrief, map specific moments in the activity onto the two inversions, and be honest about the disanalogy between them: in the first phase, something outside the participants — a rule, an environment, a scoring function — is doing the selecting or shaping, and in the second phase, something outside the participants — the procedure's designer — is doing the designing. The students should leave feeling both that comprehension was absent from the competent process and that they are tempted to reinsert a comprehender somewhere; they should feel the cost of each place where they might reinsert it.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it. Also tell me which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief.
