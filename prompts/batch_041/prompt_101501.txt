In 1971–1973, the government of Salvador Allende in Chile built, with the British management cybernetician Stafford Beer, a system called Cybersyn: a network of telex machines linking nationalized factories to a central computer in Santiago, statistical software that flagged when production indicators drifted outside acceptable ranges, an economic simulator for testing policy options, and an operations room where the resulting data were displayed for decision-makers. The design was deliberately recursive — Beer's Viable System Model assigned self-regulation to lower levels and reserved the center for exceptions, which were meant to travel upward as algedonic alerts. The system was reportedly used to help coordinate distribution during the truckers' strike of October 1972, though the project was never fully operational before the 1973 coup, and the operations room was never used in earnest.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the socialist calculation debate: Mises's argument that a socialist commonwealth cannot rationally allocate capital goods because it lacks the prices that a market for the means of production generates; Hayek's argument that the decisive economic knowledge is dispersed, local, and often tacit; and Lange's reply that a central planning board could set prices by trial and error, instructing managers to treat those prices as parameters and to expand or contract output accordingly. I am not asking for a history of the project or for a verdict on Allende's government. I am asking what the debate says the actors faced, and what the episode does to the debate.

Be disciplined about the mapping. Who or what occupies the position of the planning board? What plays the role of prices, and are they genuine prices in the sense Lange needed — parametric signals that managers can respond to without needing to understand the whole system — or are they something weaker, such as production targets, quotas, and physical indicators? Where does trial and error actually occur: at the center, in the factory, or nowhere? What kind of knowledge does the telex network collect, and how does that knowledge compare with the knowledge Hayek claimed markets transmit through prices — is the difference one of bandwidth and speed, or one of kind?

Then run the analysis in the other direction. A common reaction to Cybersyn is that it shows the calculation debate to have been a debate about computational capacity, and therefore obsolete: give the planners enough sensors and enough processing power, and the objection dissolves. Assess that reaction. In particular, consider whether Mises's and Hayek's arguments were claims about computational limits at all, and whether better technology could address them if they were not. Consider whether the system's recursive, self-managing design is best read as a vindication of Lange, as a tacit concession to Hayek, or as neither. Consider what it means that the one episode in which the system is said to have been useful was a logistics and distribution crisis rather than a problem of deciding what to produce — allocation of known goods toward a known goal, rather than discovery of what ought to be produced and at what relative cost.

Finally, be honest about what the episode cannot settle. The project was interrupted, the central computer was a single machine running software that was never fully trusted, and the surrounding economy still contained a large private sector, black markets, and inflation, so the prices the system worked with were not the parametric prices of the Langean model. Say what a decisive test of the debate would have looked like, whether Cybersyn could in principle have been one, and what the case nonetheless shows about how the debate was framed — including whether the framing, rather than the arguments, is what most needs revision.
