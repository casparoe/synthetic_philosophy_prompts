Last night I had a long and increasingly bad-tempered argument with an old friend, a historian by training, about who — if anyone — is to be blamed for the First World War, and I should like the disagreement restated in a vocabulary I actually command. I am a practising barrister, and I spend my working life in tort and regulatory crime, so I think in terms of but-for causation, legal causation and remoteness, intervening acts, joint and several liability, contributory fault, actus reus and mens rea, accessory and command liability, and the standard of proof. My friend's position is the revisionist one: that the war was nobody's fault in particular, or everyone's in general, and that the very question of national guilt is a hangover from 1919; Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers (2012) is the version of this he kept citing. My own instinct kept returning to the older claim associated with Fritz Fischer, whose Griff nach der Weltmacht appeared in 1961, that Imperial Germany bore the primary responsibility. We got nowhere, partly because I suspect we were using the word 'responsibility' in two different senses and neither of us could say which.

Please do the translation. Take the principal positions in the Fischer controversy — Fischer's thesis of German primary responsibility and the continuity it draws from 1914 to 1939, and the revisionist rejoinder that responsibility was broadly shared and that no single power willed the war — and restate each of them, as faithfully as you can, in the language of English civil and criminal liability. Do not smuggle in the assumptions of my framework (individual agency, mens rea, the discrete act) and then call the result a translation; where you have to import them to make the restatement go through, flag it.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about. Which claims translate cleanly — is the July Crisis simply a chain of intervening acts; is the Septemberprogramm of September 1914 an admission of prior intent or merely after-the-event evidence; does the blank cheque to Austria-Hungary read as authorisation or as recklessness? Which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — collective or national responsibility, 'primary' as a scalar notion distinct from mere but-for contribution, the overdetermined causation that seems to characterise the whole business? For each residue, tell me whether it marks a genuine gap in the legal framework or a genuine unclarity in the historians' claim, and say which of the historians' assertions become trivial or vacuous once translated.

Finally, two loose ends from the argument. First, Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, which I understand never used the word 'guilt' and was drafted chiefly to give a legal basis for reparations: were the Allied drafters doing something coherent in legal terms, or were they, as my friend insists, confusing a claim about liability with a verdict about blame? Second, if the translation does leave a residue, say what it would take for the historians' dispute to be stated in terms that survive it — or whether the dispute is better understood, as I began to suspect, as a disagreement about the standard of proof rather than about the facts.

Please write your answer in British English.
