I just got back from a three-hour arguement with my friend Dana — she's finishing a PhD in political science, I'm in my second year of a theory program — about the boundary problem, i.e. the fact that any democratic procedure for deciding who belongs to the demos already presupposes an answer to who belongs to the demos. Her line was basically: sure, you can't constitute a demos democratically all the way down, but that's just a fact about institutions, not a deep paradox. Every club, union and state starts with somebody drawing a line, and then the members take it from there; calling that the boundary problem is dressing up a truism as a puzzle. I said that can't be the end of it, because on most accounts democratic legitimacy has something to do with coercion being justified to the governed, and if the governed get picked by whoever happens to be holding power, the justification is circular in a way that "well, that's just how clubs work" doesn't touch. We went in circles and I've been stewing about it all week. It also made me realize that I don't actually know which parts of this are live questions and which have been talked to death, and I'd rather not spend a year rediscovering the obvious.

So here's what I actually need. I have roughly a year of fairly free time before my prospectus is due, no empirical methods training to speak of, and I want to spend it on one corner of this. Which open questions in the area matter most, and in what order? The candidates I keep circling are: whether the boundary problem is a genuine incoherence in democratic theory or collapses into a much weaker claim about institutional beginnings; whether the fix is some principle like all-affected versus all-subjected-to-coercion, and which of those can do real work without smuggling a demos back in through the definition; whether the problem looks different for non-state bodies — co-ops, unions, firms, platform governance, whatever body ends up overseeing AI systems — than it does for states, since those at least have a story about how their members came to be members; secession and who gets to vote on it; and whether this is a special problem for procedural accounts of legitimacy or whether it infects theories of justice generally. Please tell me which of these is upstream of the others, which are genuinely tractable for someone with a year of reading and no fieldwork, and which I should drop because the answer wouldn't change much downstream. I don't want a survey of who has said what — I can read the literature — I want a defended ordering: what makes a question worth the year, what the tradeoffs are between importance, tractability, and how many other questions hang on it, and what would move something up or down that list. And if you had the year, what's the first thing you'd try to settle?
