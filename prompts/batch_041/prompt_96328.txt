A friend in my game-theory reading group produced the following argument. He says it is the standard strategy-stealing argument, that it is entirely general, and that its generality is precisely the point. I have set it out as closely to his version as I can.

1. Any finite two-player game of perfect information, with no chance moves and no draws, has a winner: one of the two players has a strategy that guarantees a win.
2. Call such a game symmetric when the rules are colour-blind, so that at any position either player has exactly the same moves available, and a position poses the same problem to whoever is to move.
3. Suppose, for reductio, that in a symmetric game without draws the second player has a winning strategy, S.
4. The first player opens with any legal move.
5. Thereafter the first player plays as though he were the second player, following S and treating his own pieces as the pieces of the second player in the imagined game.
6. If S ever directs him to a point already occupied by his own opening piece, he ignores that piece and plays any other legal move instead; an extra piece of his own cannot turn a win into a loss, so the opening move costs him nothing.
7. So the first player wins, contradicting 3.
8. Therefore the second player has no winning strategy in such a game, and by 1 the first player has one.
9. So the first player wins at Hex, at Chomp on any rectangle larger than 1x1, at m,n,k-games generally, and at the misère form of Nim.
10. But at misère Nim played with a single counter the first player loses: he must take the last counter, and taking the last counter loses.

He draws two further morals: that finiteness is a dispensable assumption, since nothing in the argument uses it; and that the argument's non-constructive character is a virtue, because it establishes that the first player wins without anyone's having to find the winning strategy.
