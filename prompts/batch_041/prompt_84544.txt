I have recently been made chair of the committee that decides whether to keep or abolish wine at the weekly dinners of the graduate residence where I live, and the arguments on both sides have driven me back into Plato's Laws, which I last read as an undergraduate and which I now find far stranger than I remembered. I want to apply for a one-semester research fellowship in order to turn this into a small, well-defined piece of work, and I need you to write the proposal.

My half-formed idea is this. In the first two books of the Laws, the Athenian proposes the regulated drinking party as an institution of the ideal city, and he seems to want it to do two different jobs at once. It is to be a test that reveals a man's character, since wine loosens the grip of shame and shows what he is really like; and it is to be a training in self-control, since the drinker who learns to resist the pull of pleasure in company acquires a settled virtue he could not acquire sober. The usual treatment takes these two jobs to be complementary — the test is also the occasion for practice. I suspect they are not, at least not without a good deal of further argument, and that the dialogue's own resources for reconciling them, such as the image of the human being as a puppet pulled by several cords, one of them the gentle golden cord of law, and the notion of shame as the safeguard of the party, may only paper over the difficulty. If the party is a test, the drinker must not know he is being tested, or what we get is performance; if it is a training, he must know exactly what he is practising, or he cannot improve. A single institution cannot obviously be both.

Turn this into a two-page proposal for a one-semester fellowship. State the question so that it has a determinate answer — something like: does the Athenian's drinking party constitute one coherent institution, or does it equivocate between a diagnostic and a habilitating function, such that the text cannot consistently deliver both? Explain why the standard readings leave this open rather than settling it, and be honest about which parts of the Laws pull in which direction. Set out the method: an internal reconstruction of the argument of Laws I–II, with attention to the puppet image and to the role of shame, plus a comparison with the objections of the Cretan and Spartan interlocutors and with the sober regime the Athenian reserves for those who must rule. Say why this is the right approach rather than a developmental or chronological story about Plato's changing attitude to drink, and rather than a survey of Greek drinking practices. Describe what a result would look like, including what would count as the project failing — for instance if the two functions turn out to be separable under the text's own account of shame, or if the institution is meant to be transparently a test and the objection about performance simply does not arise. Finally, give a realistic month-by-month scope for a single semester. I will add the references myself; do not give me a bibliography or a literature review by citation.
