I am a novelist, and for the better part of six years I have been writing a long book that begins in the fourteenth century and ends in the seventeenth, and for the convenience of drafting I divided it into three parts, headed simply The Middle Ages, The Renaissance, and The Scientific Revolution. The headings were meant only to keep my drafts from bleeding into one another. But a historian I drink with has spent two evenings arguing that I have organized a thousand pages around three things that do not exist, and his argument — reproduced below as he gave it — has lodged in me like a splinter. (1) A general term names something real only if there is a fact of the matter, independent of our convenience, about which things fall under it and which do not. (2) The boundaries of the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are matters of convenience: the opening and closing dates shift from book to book (the fall of Rome, the fall of Constantinople, the Columbian voyages, the appearance of the Principia), no two historians cut the centuries in the same place, and the names themselves are much younger than the events they name, having been given by writers who came afterward and had their own reasons for wanting the past divided so. (3) Where membership is settled by convenience rather than discovered, there is no fact of the matter about membership. (4) So there is no fact of the matter as to whether a given decade, or altarpiece, or treatise on motion belongs to the Renaissance. (5) A term that answers to no fact is not the name of anything; at best it is a label. (6) Therefore the Renaissance is not real but invented — and by the same steps, so are the Middle Ages, the Scientific Revolution, the Enlightenment, the Industrial Revolution, the Victorian age, the Cold War, and the nineteenth century.

I can feel that each step is doing some work, and that step (6) is where the thing overreaches, but I cannot find the joint that gives. Two of the premises I am unwilling to surrender. I am fairly sure of (2): I have read enough history to know that the dates are conventions, that the boundaries are ours, and that nobody discovered the Renaissance the way one discovers a river. And I find (6) intolerable in its full generality, not out of sentiment but because the book cannot be written without such divisions — four centuries do not narrate themselves as one undifferentiated wash — and because the argument, followed through, would dissolve the word afterward along with medieval and modern, including the sense of my friend's own claim that the names were given later. He is untroubled; he says historians may go on using fictions so long as they know they are fictions, the way one uses a convenient map. A philosopher in the same circle told me that periods are vague rather than fictional, like a heap or a bald man, and that this makes all the difference; I could not see how, since a heap is at least made of grains and the Renaissance does not seem to be made of anything. Another simply said the argument proves too much, which I had already noticed, and which did not tell me where to strike.

So I would like you to tell me which step to reject, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what striking it costs me — what I lose elsewhere if I give up (1), or (3), or the criterion buried in (5). I would rather keep (2) than keep (6), but if the price of keeping (2) is that the Renaissance becomes a fiction that merely happens to be useful, say so plainly. I am also after a second thing, and it is the one that actually worries me as a writer: whether there is any principled line between a periodization that is merely conventional and one that is simply bad — the kind that flattens four centuries into a single mood, or quietly smuggles in a story of progress — or whether, once I concede (2), every cut through the past is as good as every other and the only remaining test is whether a given division sells books. Tell me what the right diagnosis implies for a case neither of us has mentioned, and whether my three headings should stay as they are, be renamed, or be abandoned.
