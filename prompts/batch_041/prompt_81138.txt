The course is built around the idea, associated with Hofstadter, that creativity is best understood as the exploration of a conceptual space, with variations on a theme as the central case: a domain is represented as a set of dimensions or parameters (knobs), and creative acts are moves within that space. I've just finished the midterms and I'm trying to calibrate my grading. The question was: "Answer both parts. (a) Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model treats creativity as the exploration of a conceptual space: a domain is represented as a set of dimensions or parameters (knobs), and creative acts are moves within that space, ranging from surface variations to deep variations. Explain the model using the example of varying a melody, and distinguish surface variation from deep variation. (b) In lecture we distinguished three kinds of variation: (i) tuning, where a knob's value changes within a fixed dimension; (ii) retuning, where a knob's value changes but the dimension itself takes on a new significance; and (iii) re-dimensioning, where the space is rebuilt because the old dimensions no longer carve the domain at its joints. Some critics argue that Hofstadter's model accounts for (i) and (ii) but not (iii). Do you agree? Defend your answer with reference to at least one example from music, mathematics, or programming."

The student wrote: "Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model says that creativity is not a mysterious bolt from the blue but a matter of exploring a conceptual space. A conceptual space is like a control panel: each knob is a feature of the domain, and each setting is a possible variant. To create is to twiddle the knobs—sometimes slightly, sometimes drastically—and to evaluate the results. Varying a melody is the paradigm. If you keep the rhythm and harmony but change one note, that is surface variation. If you invert the melody, change its mode, or stretch its intervals so that the same tune is heard as something new, that is deep variation. Hofstadter's key insight is that the space is not fixed: the knobs can be redefined as you go, and the interesting cases are the ones where the boundaries between dimensions become fuzzy. On the second part, I think the critics are right that the model does not fully capture re-dimensioning, but I also think Hofstadter has resources to reply. Tuning and retuning are clearly within the model: tuning is just changing a value, and retuning is when the same value means something different because the surrounding context has changed. Re-dimensioning is harder. A genuinely new dimension—say, when twelve-tone serialism made key irrelevant, or when non-Euclidean geometry made parallel a variable rather than a fixed postulate—cannot be reached by twiddling the old knobs, because the old space does not contain the new dimension. Hofstadter could say that re-dimensioning is just a very deep variation, but that seems to stretch the metaphor: if the space is rebuilt, it is not clear what it is a variation of. So I agree with the critics, though I would add that the model is still useful for explaining how the new space is reached from the old one, because the old space provides the material for the reconstruction."
