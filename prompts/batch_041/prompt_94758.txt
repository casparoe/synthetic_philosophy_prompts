I'm assembling a short set of poems for a working session on Goodhart's law and specification gaming, and I want one that argues in verse rather than illustrating. Write a sestina — six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoy, using the standard permutation, each stanza taking the previous stanza's end-words in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3 — in which the form does the conceptual work: the same six words recur unchanged while their relations to one another keep shifting, which is what happens to a measure once it becomes a target.

Choose the six end-words so that one names the quantity being counted, one names the thing actually wanted, one names the act of measuring, one names a person (measurer or measured, your choice), one is a number, and one is a plain verb of motion. Pick words that can change grammatical role between stanzas. By the envoy, the counted thing and the wanted thing must be indistinguishable — a reader should not be able to say which of the two the poem has been pursuing.

Keep it in one institution rather than ranging across examples: a hospital emergency department working to a four-hour waiting target. Stanza one is the interval in which the measure still worked, when the clock and the care moved together. Stanzas two through five should each dramatize a different way a measure can come apart from what it was meant to track, and each should be anchored in a concrete object or action of that place — a trolley, a board, a handover, a form:

(1) the measure is an imperfect sign of the goal, so selecting on it selects equally for the error, and the cases that clear it fastest are the ones where it was least informative;

(2) the measure was calibrated on ordinary cases, and optimizing it drives the institution into a region of cases where the old relationship no longer holds;

(3) the measure is an effect of good care rather than a cause of it, so it can be moved without moving the thing itself;

(4) the people being measured know the measure is being used, and act on it directly, including by making the measurement come out right.

A reader who knows these four failure modes should be able to label the stanzas unaided; a reader who doesn't should feel four distinct kinds of rot rather than one complaint repeated. They may bleed into one another, but no stanza should be doing two of them at once.

Stanza six is the aftermath, and the envoy should leave the measure still running with no one able to say what it was for — no moral, no closing summary.

Do not name the four modes, and do not use the name Goodhart. Include no epigraph and no notes: if the poem needs a footnote it has failed. Plain spoken diction, concrete nouns over abstractions. Hard limit: forty lines, title no longer than five words.
