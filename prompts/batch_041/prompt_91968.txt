I'm putting together the final for an upper-level seminar I teach on using history as evidence for forecasting -- the students are policy types who've had exactly one stats course and a lot of confidence. I want one essay question for the exam (60 minutes, closed book) that they can't get through by reciting the outside-view stuff from Kahneman and the megaproject reference-class work. So I'm building it around a concrete artifact they have to actually audit, and I want the model answer and a marking scheme to go with it.

Here's the scenario I'm going to hand them, verbatim-ish, and I want you to treat it as the object of the question:

A consultancy (call it the Meridian Group) publishes a forecast that a certain major power's current political order has "a 40% probability of collapsing within 15 years." Their two-page method note says: they assembled a reference class of 23 historical polities they call "empires" -- it's a grab-bag, including a couple of city-state hegemonies, one or two colonial empires, a shogunate, some dynastic states, and at least one thing most historians would call a kingdom rather than an empire; they computed how long each lasted (the spread is roughly 50 to 900 years, median around 250); they located the current power at about the 60th percentile of elapsed duration; and that percentile is where the 40% comes from. They add that the class "controls for regime type" because every member was an empire. They also state that the model assumes the hazard of collapse is constant over a polity's life, so age doesn't need to be modeled separately.

The question I want them to answer is basically: is this forecast any good? I want it to force them through the following, and the marking scheme should make clear which of these a top answer hits:

- whether that reference class is defensible at all, and what's actually wrong with the claim that it "controls for regime type";
- the statistical problem with estimating a survival/lifespan distribution from a set of polities that all collapsed -- they should name it and say what it does to the 40%;
- the constant-hazard assumption -- what it implies and why it might be false for polities;
- the difference between forecasting whether and forecasting when, and which one Meridian actually did;
- the strongest steelman of the forecast, then the strongest objection to it;
- and the single change that would most improve it.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and then the marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want it as a table, columns exactly: "Criterion" | "What a full-credit answer must contain" | "Marks" | "How students typically lose marks here" -- total 25 marks, and you decide the allocation. Don't lean on any particular book, author, or date; if a real idea is useful, describe it rather than cite it, because I don't want students rewarded for name-dropping.
