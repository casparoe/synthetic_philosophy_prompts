Next month I am giving a paper on Thomson's lamp at a small workshop on idealization and limits, and I need a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a rehearsal of the paper. The audience will be: two set theorists, one of whom regards the philosophy of mathematics as chronically afflicted with physics envy; two philosophers of physics whose own work concerns whether general relativity permits an observer to complete infinitely many operations and then transmit a signal; a metaphysician who works on determinacy and indeterminacy; and a historian of mathematics who will want to know what a nineteenth-century analyst would have made of the problem.

The thesis I am defending is that the lamp's terminal state is not underdetermined but mechanism-relative. The familiar deflationary reply holds that the toggling schedule can be redescribed so as to leave the lamp on at the limit, or off, and that the state is therefore simply not fixed by the description. I argue the reverse: any device that genuinely implements the schedule must be insensitive to the schedule's parametrization, and among such devices the terminal state is fixed by a continuity or conservation requirement that the lamp's description never specifies. On my view the puzzle is not about infinity at all but about the difference between a schedule of states and a mechanism that produces them, and the interesting residue is a question about which mechanism-types can do limit-fixing work.

Draft the questions this particular audience will actually put to me, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will ask it rather than in mine. For each, give me a reply of no more than two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is weakest, and a flag on whether the objection has real force or is merely a rhetorical posture. Tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks and what I must not concede at all. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
