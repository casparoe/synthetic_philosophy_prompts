My opening move: fine, priors. But I don't think properness is the thing doing the work, and here's a case I can't get out of my head. Someone picks n uniformly from 1 to 6, seals 2^n in one envelope and 2^(n+1) in the other, and tells me the rule. I open one and see 4. The other envelope is 8 if I'm holding the smaller of (4,8) and 2 if I'm holding the larger of (2,4), and conditional on seeing 4 those two are equally likely, so the expected value of the other envelope really is 5 and not 4 — the 5/4 rule is just true here, with a proper prior, finite expectations everywhere, no infinities to hide behind. Same story at 8, 16, 32 and 64. And the rule 'swap unless I'm holding 128' is the Bayes-optimal one, and it beats keeping by 16/3 on average, which I've now checked twice. So my hypothesis is that the whole 'you need a proper prior' line is a red herring: what actually matters is whether the swap rule is locally justified at every amount you might see, and when it is, the only escape hatch is diverging expectations — which my bounded version doesn't have. Where does that leave your dissolution? And if you tell me the 16/3 is just the value of information about the top boundary, I'll want to know why that same reply doesn't dissolve the original puzzle, since there my own amount is also information about the pair.
