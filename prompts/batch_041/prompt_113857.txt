I am in my final year of secondary school and spend one evening a week in a jurisprudence seminar run by doctoral students at a nearby law faculty, where we sit as a simulated constitutional chamber for an invented country (we named it the Republic of Valdoria) and work through a docket of cases we write ourselves. This term we read our way into the mid-century exchange between Hart and Fuller about whether an unjust enactment can still be law — Hart's insistence that legal validity and moral merit are separate questions, Fuller's suggestion that law has an inner morality of its own and that a system violating it badly enough forfeits its claim on our fidelity — and the instructor has asked me to prepare the bench memorandum for next week's hearing, which is why I am writing. Our chamber has already decided five cases, and I am meant to make the sixth decision consistent with them rather than with my own sympathies. The five: (1) a revenue measure passed by both houses but never published in the official gazette and then applied to conduct predating it, which we held was not law and quashed the convictions, reasoning that a command no one could have known of or complied with cannot claim legal authority; (2) a land-registration decree issued by a junta that had seized power unconstitutionally but whose commands were thereafter obeyed by every court and official for eleven years, which we held valid, on the ground that legality follows effective and generally obeyed authority rather than constitutional pedigree; (3) a licensing statute, properly enacted and published, that in operation destroyed the businesses of a religious minority, which we held valid and applied, adding that our revulsion did not bear on its validity though individuals might have no moral duty to comply with it; (4) a penal statute drafted so vaguely and self-contradictorily that no one could have told what conduct it required, which we held was not law; and (5) a formally impeccable but cruel statute whose defendant was an official who had refused to enforce it, where we upheld the statute yet held that he had no duty to obey it and should not be punished. In case (3) we expressly declined to decide whether a statute could ever be so unjust as to be void, saying the question was not presented.

Next week's case is a statute enacted by the restored parliament in full compliance with every formal requirement, duly published, unambiguous, prospective, and easy to obey, which withdraws legal personality from a named ethnic community, authorizes its members to be seized and held in forced labour for life, and makes it a crime to shelter or assist them. The defendant is a schoolteacher charged with hiding two children of that community; she does not deny it, and her advocate argues that the statute is not law at all and that the chamber must say so. What I want is not a verdict alone but the whole structure of the reasoning, in a structured answer with headings if that helps: first, the distinct rules our five decisions are jointly consistent with — I can see at least a rule that looks only to formal enactment, a rule that a valid statute may nevertheless impose no duty of obedience, the rule associated with Gustav Radbruch that unbearably unjust enactments are void from the outset, and some fourth rule that captures the reasoning we actually gave in cases (1) and (4) rather than merely their outcomes — and what each of those rules predicts for the teacher. Second, which of our precedents is the closest analogue and which rule I should adopt if I want to be consistent with our own record rather than with my own sympathies, and what principle the chamber would be committing itself to either way. Third, which further case would most cleanly separate the rules that survive; I have a suspicion that a case about whether someone already convicted under such a statute should now be released would do more work than yet another case about validity, but I would like you to say whether that is right and why. Please reason it through from the material I have given rather than supplying citations or further reading — the seminar has no library and I cannot look anything up.
