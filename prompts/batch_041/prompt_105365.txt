Here's the idea. Every money-pump argument I've come across targets *intransitive* preferences: the agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A all at the same time, so you lead them around the loop and relieve them of some money. The usual moral is that intransitivity is irrational, or at minimum a kind of incoherence. But it seems like you can build the same kind of pump out of an agent whose preferences are perfectly transitive at every moment and only *cycle across time*: on Monday they prefer A to B, on Tuesday B to C, on Wednesday C to A, and so on, with each day's ranking internally consistent. There's no intransitivity anywhere in sight, yet you can still walk them around the loop and extract money. So either the pump shows nothing about rationality in this case, or it shows that the real target of money-pump arguments was never transitivity but something about how preferences are supposed to hang together over time. I lean toward the second, and I suspect it forces a seperate between two things that get run together a lot: (a) being exploitable and (b) being irrational. My hunch is they can come apart, and that the literature mostly assumes they can't.
