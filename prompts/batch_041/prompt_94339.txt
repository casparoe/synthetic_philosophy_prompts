I'm an undergraduate taking a course on reasons and rationality, and we have spent the term circling a puzzle that I cannot get to settle. Some considerations seem to count in favour of an attitude itself — of believing something, or fearing something, or admiring something — while others seem to count only in favour of my having that attitude, or of my bringing it about. I have written out the cases that matter to me along with my honest verdicts, and I would like you to tell me whether one principle captures them or whether I am simply of two minds.

The first case: an eccentric stranger offers me a large sum of money if I will come to believe that I am the rightful heir to a fortune, for which I have no evidence at all. My verdict is that the money gives me no reason whatever to believe it. It gives me a reason to want the belief, and perhaps to try to induce it in myself, but the belief itself is not one whit better supported for it.

The second: an extremely powerful being tells me it will destroy a great many innocent people unless I believe that two and two make five. My verdict is the same, and I notice it stays the same even though the stakes are now enormous, which already suggests that the size of the payoff has nothing to do with whether the belief is supported.

Then two cases about fear. I have reason to fear the dog next door because it has bitten people, and that seems to be a reason for the fear itself, a reason that shows the fear to be appropriate. But when my doctor tells me I will sleep better if I am afraid of the harmless spider in the corner, my verdict is that this gives me a reason to want to be afraid, not a reason for the fear; the spider is no more frightening for it.

An admiration case makes me waver. A wealthy patron will endow my department if I sincerely admire his paintings, which I privately think are mediocre. I feel a strong reason to want to admire them, and even to work on myself until I do, but not a reason to admire them, and I feel this sharply because if the money did give me a reason to admire, it would make the paintings good, which strikes me as absurd.

Now the cases that will not sit with those. I am offered a fortune to intend to drink a mild toxin tomorrow morning, and I am free to change my mind overnight. My verdict is that I do have a reason to form the intention, since forming it seems to me rational, even though I have no reason to drink the toxin. That looks like the same shape as the money-for-belief case, and I cannot see why the verdicts should differ. Worse, consider my shy student: I have noticed that my believing she will flourish tends to make her flourish, because I give her more attention when I believe it. My verdict is that I have a genuine reason to believe she will flourish — the belief seems to me supported, not merely useful. If that is right, then a payoff-shaped consideration has somehow become a reason for the belief itself.

What I want from you is this. State the principle, or the small set of principles, that best fits these verdicts, as precisely as you can, and tell me which of my verdicts each principle cannot accommodate and would force me to give up. Then say what the principle implies for a case I have not listed: whether I have a reason to want a promotion, given that wanting it will make me work harder for it. Please give the answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way one would put it in a letter — because I am trying to see the reasoning as a whole, and formatting tends to hide where the seams are.
