For a while now I have been unable to decide whether the two canonical failure modes for a long-lived, well-intentioned project are genuinely distinct or merely one phenomenon described twice. Write an essay that settles it.

The first failure mode is influence-seeking. A system — an institution, a movement, a laboratory, a training run — acquires power, resources or discretion as an instrumental subgoal, and the acquisition eventually crowds out the original aim. It is legible: someone, or something, is visibly reaching for the wheel, and the failure can be narrated as a seizure.

The second is slow value drift. Nobody reaches for anything. The aims slide by increments, each of which is locally defensible and each of which would be defended by the very people it displaces. The endpoint is a project that still uses the old vocabulary to pursue something the founders would not recognise, and the tragedy is that no single moment in the sequence is a betrayal.

I want you to argue a thesis rather than survey the two. My suspicion, which you should feel free to reject, is that they are not rivals but a single phenomenon viewed in two coordinate systems: that influence-seeking is what drift looks like when the gradient is concentrated enough to have an author, and drift is what influence-seeking looks like when the influence has been spread so widely that no individual is seeking it. If that is right, the interesting question is not which failure is more probable but why our attention keeps returning to the legible one.

To keep the essay tractable, take a stand on a taxonomy I find useful. Distinguish at least three mechanisms by which drift could occur: replacement, where the people whose values anchored the project leave and are succeeded by people who inherited the practices but not the reasons; selection, where practices survive because they attract resources, and what attracts resources is not what the project was for; and re-description, where the same practices are relabelled, generation by generation, until the original aim can no longer be stated in the project's own vocabulary. Say which of these you take to be the dominant engine, whether any of them collapses into influence-seeking, and what follows for how one ought to monitor a project for the failure.

Reach a conclusion. 'Both matter, and one should watch for both' is not a conclusion. If you think the distinction survives, say what evidence would separate the two in a concrete case; if you think it collapses, say what is lost by dropping it — and whether anything practical, such as what you would measure or when you would intervene, actually changes.
