Two colleagues in my department have been arguing for weeks about whether the brain is a computer, and they have asked me to referee. Here is the exchange as fairly as I can set it out. (1) He says the brain is a computer: it takes in inputs, carries and transforms internal representations, and emits outputs, and each year neuroscience finds more algorithmic structure, so the thesis is a substantive empirical claim. (2) She says the claim is too cheap to be empirical, because any physical system at all can be described as implementing some computation — you can always map its states onto the states of a formal procedure. If "computer" only means "describable by an algorithm," then rivers and rocks are computers, and nothing is ruled out; she adds that a lookup table storing the brain's every input-output pair would pass any behavioral test while, in the interesting sense, computing nothing. (3) He says that is not the claim: the claim is that the brain's causal organization is of the right kind, and that this organization, not the wetware, does the explanatory work — which is why a simulation could in principle do what the brain does. (4) She says he has now surrendered the interesting half of his position: if only certain causal organizations count, then "computer" names a physical kind rather than a functional one, and he owes a principled account of which realizations count, one that does not simply say "the ones that behave like brains." (5) He replies that substrate-independence was never the claim that any substrate will do; it is the claim that the relevant level of description is functional, and functional descriptions can impose real constraints — stable memory, reliable state transitions — that not every lump of matter satisfies. (6) She says that if the content lives in those constraints, then "the brain is a computer" is either trivially true or does no explanatory work, and he should simply state the constraints and drop the slogan.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible only with a caveat, and justify each rating briefly. Then say what both of them are missing, and whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal.
