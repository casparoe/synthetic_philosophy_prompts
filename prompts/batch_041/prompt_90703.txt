I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, at work on a chapter about the function debate — specifically the standoff between selected-effects accounts in the tradition of Wright, Millikan, and Neander, and causal-role accounts in the tradition of Cummins, together with the system-relative readings of function those invite. I would like you to act as a philosopher of biology with real expertise in that literature and produce a sustained critical reading of Steven Spielberg's Jurassic Park (1993), with side-glances at Crichton's 1990 novel, through that debate. Please think carefully and begin with some brainstorming: set out the two accounts as you understand them, say precisely where they disagree and where the disagreement is merely verbal, then identify the scenes and plot facts in the film that bear on it, and only then commit to a reading.

Three things I want the reading to do. First, the film gives us de-extinct animals: assembled from a reconstructed genome, with gaps filled from frog DNA, deliberately made all female so that they cannot reproduce. On a strict selected-effects account, an organism with no selection lineage has no proper functions at all; its organs and dispositions can have only artifact functions fixed by designer intent. On a causal-role account, those same capacities have functions relative to whichever system you choose to analyse. The film's central reveal — that the animals are breeding anyway, and that the frog DNA is implicated — is therefore a case where the accounts come apart. I want you to say which account, if either, the film's own treatment presupposes, and where it muddles them.

Second, I want the problem run at two levels. The island is itself a designed system whose designed function is to entertain paying visitors; the ecosystem that emerges on it is nobody's design. A single event, say a large predator bringing down its prey, is thus a malfunction relative to the park-system and a functioning relative to the ecosystem. Does the film register that? Does it want the animals to be the genuine article, with species-typical functions — a fact about what a raptor's claw is for — and is that commitment compatible with either account, given that these animals have no evolutionary history and were built to specification?

Third, be honest where the reading strains. The film's rhetoric about life refusing to be contained, about nature finding a way, looks like a teleological vitalism that neither account endorses. Say whether that is a confusion the film is guilty of or an intuition the theories are failing to capture.

Deliverable: a structured piece of criticism of roughly two thousand words, faithful in both directions — it must not reduce the film to a teaching example, and it must not bend the function debate to fit the film. Close by saying what, if anything, the film contributes to the debate that argument alone does not. No citations or bibliography; I will supply those myself. Please paraphrase rather than quote dialogue.
