A colleague in the econ department -- good economist, but he talks about decision theory the way I talk about his field, i.e. confidently and from memory -- gave me his two-minute version of the Allais paradox over coffee, and I want to know how much of it I can safely repeat to my grad seminar next week. Here's roughly what he said, as best I can reconstruct it:

"Allais's whole point was to show that people are irrational. You give them two pairs of gambles. In the first pair, one option is a sure million, the other is an 89% shot at a million, a 10% shot at five million, and a 1% shot at nothing. Most people take the sure million. In the second pair you keep that same 89% shot at a million on one side and make it an 89% shot at nothing on the other, leaving an 11% shot at a million on the left and a 10% shot at five million on the right; now most people go for the five million. The trick is that the 89% is common to both pairs, so by the independence axiom -- which just says your preference between two options shouldn't be swayed by a chunk they have in common -- the same people should have picked the same way both times. They don't. That's the paradox. And since independence is the axiom that rules out risk aversion, a risk-averse chooser is bound to violate it. This killed expected utility as a description of how people actually choose and, because the axiom is constitutive of rational choice, as a normative theory too. The only way to keep expected utility is to insist that the majority of subjects are simply making a mistake. Allais himself wanted to replace the linear weighting of probabilities with a nonlinear one, which is more or less what Kahneman and Tversky formalized later."

I've taught this stuff for years and I have a nagging feeling that a couple of those sentences are subtly off rather than flatly wrong, but I can't put my finger on which ones, and I don't want to correct him in front of the students if I'm the one who's rusty. So go through it claim by claim: what would someone who actually works on this accept, what would they wince at, and what is just false? I'm inclined to think he's right that it's bad news for the descriptive side but wrong to treat that as bad news for the normative side, but tell me if even that is too tidy. And the part I care about most -- does his closing move, that the only defense left is to call everyone irrational, actually follow from anything true in the summary, or is it doing rhetorical work the arguement doesn't support? Finally, since I'm supposed to be the one teaching this: what is the real status of the independence axiom -- a requirement of rationality, a descriptive regularity, or something in between -- and do the standard reactions to Allais probaly differ over which of those it is?
