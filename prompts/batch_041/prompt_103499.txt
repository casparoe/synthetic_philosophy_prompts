I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on computability and its philosophical foundations, and I am currently grading the final examination. The examination was closed-book, and students had no internet access. One question was worth 25 points, and I am marking the script reproduced below. Please grade it.

The question:

(a) State what the Church–Turing thesis asserts and, just as importantly, what it does not assert; identify at least one neighbouring claim that is frequently confused with it. (b) Is the thesis the sort of claim that can be proved? Say what kind of claim it is, and say what would count as evidence for or against it. (c) A seminar participant objects that, since we are free to define 'effectively calculable' as 'Turing-computable', the thesis collapses into the trivial identity that Turing-computable functions are Turing-computable, and so asserts nothing. Assess this objection. (d) Describe one proposed challenge to the thesis and say whether it succeeds.

The student's answer:

(a) The thesis states that the class of functions a person could compute by following a finite set of exact, mechanical rules — no insight, no guessing, no appeal to intuition — is exactly the class of functions a Turing machine can compute. It does not state that every function is computable, and it does not say anything about efficiency: it is a claim about computability in principle, not about how fast or with how much memory a computation can be done. A neighbouring claim that is often confused with it is the physical version, according to which every process realisable in the physical world is Turing-computable; that is a stronger and empirical claim about physics, and it should not be conflated with the original thesis about effective calculability.

(b) The thesis cannot be proved in the way that a theorem of arithmetic can be proved. The reason is that it relates an informal, intuitive notion (effective procedure) to a formal one (Turing machine), and there is no formal system in which both notions can be expressed so that the relation between them could be derived. What Turing proved in his 1936 paper is that his machines, Church's lambda calculus, and the general recursive functions all single out the same class of functions. This convergence of independent formalisms is what gives the thesis its force, and it is in this sense that Turing established the thesis.

(c) The objection is mistaken. If we define 'effectively calculable' as 'Turing-computable', then the thesis does become a trivial identity, but the definition is not a free stipulation: 'effective procedure' already carries a pre-theoretic content — finite, deterministic, surveyable step by step — and the substantive question is whether that content is exhausted by the Turing model. The objection quietly changes the meaning of the term and so does not touch the thesis. The convergence of the formalisms does show, however, that the choice of Turing machines rather than lambda calculus is not arbitrary.

(d) One challenge is the oracle machine. An oracle machine equipped with an oracle for the halting set computes the halting function, which no Turing machine can compute. If such a machine counts as an effective procedure, then the thesis is false. I conclude that the thesis holds for ordinary computation but fails once we allow idealised machines with external sources of information.
