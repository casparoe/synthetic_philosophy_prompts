i'm trying to write down, once and for all, what a theory of vagueness actualy has to do before i'll take it seriously, and i keep going in circles. so help me build the spec. what does any adequate account of vague predicates have to deliver -- which cases it has to get right, which intuitions it has to honour or explain away, which results it can't contradict -- and give me the case or consideration behind each requirement, not just a label.

i want the borderline machinery up front. the sorites in both dress: the heap, and the forced march (one hair, then another, when did he go bald). genuine borderline cases, where we really don't know what to say and it isn't just ignorance of a hidden fact. penumbral connections -- if A is clearly red and B is clearly not red, then A is redder than B, whatever "redder" turns out to mean, and the theory can't let those pull apart. and higher-order vagueness: "borderline" is itself vague, so any theory that draws one clean line around the borderline cases has just relocated the problem one level up.

then two requirements i never see anyone state properly. first, a use case: i spend too much time around a rules engine and a building code, and sooner or later something has to decide whether a 12.4 metre building is "tall" and whether a 99.6 gram parcel crosses the 100g threshold. so i want a requirement about what the theory licences you to do with a borderline case in practice -- what verdict it forces, what it leaves open, what it tells an engineer to do -- not just what it says about truth. second, vague predicates have to stay usable: no theory on which "tall" is meaningless, or on which every application of it is a coin flip, or on which two people who agree on all the measurements can't communicate.

after the list, tell me which of these are real desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp -- i suspect the demand for a sharp cutoff, and the demand that classical logic be preserved come what may, are each doing more quiet work than people admit. but what i most want: flag any subset that can't be jointly satisfied. i have a feeling that tolerance (a difference too small to matter can't flip a predicate) plus bivalence plus "no sharp boundary" is three rails with nowhere to go, but say it properly, and tell me which rail each of the usual moves gives up -- degree theory, supervaluationism, epistemicism, contextualism. and finish by naming the single requirement that eliminates the most theories.
