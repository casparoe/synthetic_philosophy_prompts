Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Marcus: "I've been through it. It's a real hole, the fix is a two line patch, and people who know about it will patch it. Net positive, so I publish Monday. Waiting for the team to reach a consensus means nothing happens. (2) Dana: "I'm not disputing your read of the bug, I'm disputing how much weight your read should carry. Suppose every person who could publish this is right more often than wrong about whether publishing is a good idea, say 70% of the time. If ten people are in a position to publish, the chance that at least one of them does it on a mistaken read is 1 - 0.7^10, which is about 97%. So whenever a thing can be done unilaterally by alot of people, somebody doing it wrong is nearly certain. That's a known paradox, the unilateralist's curse. Which means the more people who could pull this trigger, the less you should trust your own pull. And notice that everyone else who looked at this and held back is evidence that you're the 30%."

(3) Marcus: "Your math proves way too much. If ten people can each do a thing, then by your logic someone is almost certainly wrong, so nobody should ever do anything alone, ever. Second, run the same numbers the other way. If nobody publishes, the bug never gets fixed, so the good outcome here needs at least one person to act, and the chance that at least one person gets it right is 1 - 0.3^10, which is basically 100%. Same arithmetic, opposite conclusion. Third, the 70% number is doing all the work and it doesn't describe me. I'm not a coin flip, I read the source, I have a reason. And if our judgments all come off the same bug report and the same vendor emails, they aren't independent, and your multiplication assumes they are. Fourth, your 97% is the chance that someone acts on a mistaken judgment, not the chance that anything bad happens. Once this is published it's out, and it doesn't get more out because more people published it."

(4) Dana: "You can't lean on the correlation point the way you want. If our judgments are correlated, then the people who looked and held back are correlated with you too, so their holding back is more informative about you, not less. You don't get to call the crowd noise when it disagrees and signal when it agrees. And your inverse case only works if publishing is good no matter how many people do it. It isn't. The harm saturates: the first writeup does nearly all the damage, and after that it's copy paste. The benefit is per user and drops off fast, because the tenth writeup adds nothing the first one didn't. So the value of your publishing depends on what everyone else does, which is exactly the fixed thing your whole argument assumes. Your math describes ten people each deciding about a thing with one fixed good or bad value. That's definately not this."
