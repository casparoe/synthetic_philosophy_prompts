I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy — the closest I came was a compulsory ethics module in undergrad — and lately a couple of colleagues and I have been arguing over lunch about whether markets are in some sense "natural" or whether money is just a convention we could redesign like an API. Wanting something more respectable than my own hunches, I picked up a well-reviewed popular history of economic thought, and it opened its chapter on antiquity with the following characterization of Aristotle, which I'll reproduce in full:

"Money, for Aristotle, is a purely artificial thing — a token people agree to use, with no root in nature. Its function is to be a neutral measuring rod for the value that goods already possess by nature, so that a shoemaker's wares and a farmer's grain can be traded in the correct proportions. Since human beings are political animals who need one another, exchange is itself natural, and any trade that moves goods around at their true proportional values is acceptable; but Aristotle condemns trade undertaken for profit, and usury above all, as a perversion of nature, because the whole point of an economy is to satisfy genuine need rather than to accumulate money for its own sake."

I have a vague sense that this is the kind of passage that sounds authoritative and is mostly fine, but that a classicist or a philosopher who actually works on Aristotle would keep wincing — particularly at the bit about goods having a value "by nature" that money merely measures, which smells to me like a later medieval idea being read back into him. I don't want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I can't look things up and neither, for present purposes, can the answer. What I want is an audit I can act on.

Please go through the characterization claim by claim and tell me, for each, what a specialist would accept as fair, what is a defensible simplification, and what is actually a misrepresentation — and where the misrepresentation comes from (a conflation of two things Aristotle keeps apart, an overstatement of a modest point, a technical term used in its modern everyday sense). In particular I'd like to know how the account stands on (i) whether and in what sense Aristotle treats money as conventional, (ii) whether he holds that goods are commensurable or instead raises a puzzle about it, and (iii) the basis of his objection to usury — is it really a blanket rejection of profit, or does it rest on something narrower? Where the scholarly situation is genuinely contested, say so plainly and don't present one reading as the consensus; where something is settled, don't hedge. Finally, tell me whether the practical upshot my colleagues and I keep reaching for — that Aristotle thought the market is a human invention we are free to remake — survives the correction. Please paraphrase rather than quote Aristotle, since I'd rather not trust a rendering I can't check.
