I'm taking a phil of mind and langauge course and writing a short paper on the Hofstadter-ish complaint that machine translation is shallow and doesn't understand anything, and I want to get at it sideways, by asking what the actual practice of MT already presupposes instead of arguing about definitions from scratch. What's bugging me is a tension: everyone in translation agrees theres no single correct translation, good translators routinely produce different but equally faithful versions, and in language classes we're taught to accept several renderings. Yet the standard way of scoring an MT system is to compare its output against one or two human reference translations and count how much of the wording overlaps, as if there were one target meaning the machine is supposed to hit. Meanwhile on the production side nobody asks whether the machine understood anything; they just measure post-editing effort, how many edits a human has to make to clean up the output, which seems to assume errors are local things you can fix in place rather than symptoms of the whole sentence being construed wrong. So what theory of meaning and understanding does this package presuppose — that meanings are determinate, language-independent things a system can recover without grasping them, or something deflationary where translating correctly is just whatever bilingual raters will accept? Which parts are strictly committed to a view and which are just engineering convenience, and if we swapped in something holistic like meaning as use, what would have to change about how we build and grade these systems? Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
