You are a philosopher working on decision theory and the ethics of AI, and I need a teaching instrument for a graduate seminar on idealized preferences. Please build a decision tree that begins from the single commitment that what a person actually prefers can come apart from what they would prefer if they knew more, reasoned better, and were more the person they wish they were, and that ends at the positions one can hold about what authority, if any, the idealized preference has over the actual one. Each branch node must be a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to; I want nodes on at least these distinctions: whether the idealization is purely epistemic or also rewrites what is desired; whether the idealized subject is still the same agent or merely a well-informed advisor whose preferences are evidence about the real one; whether idealization converges on a single answer or is relative to the procedure and to the order in which a person's inconsistencies get repaired; whether idealized preferences bind outright or only settle ties among incoherent actual ones; and whether moral constraints are built into the idealization or left out. Work the logical space first — the combinations of answers and which of them are actually occupied — before assigning names, and use as running test cases the smoker who endorses his own addiction, the person who under full reflection would abandon the project that gives her life meaning and says of that self that it is not her, and a pair of people whose idealizations diverge in opposite directions depending only on repair order. Aim for six to eight nodes and at most eight leaves; label a leaf with a standard name where one exists and mark the rest "unnamed", give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate.
