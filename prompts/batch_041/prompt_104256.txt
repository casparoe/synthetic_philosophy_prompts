Two camps have been arguing past each other for a generation about the balance sheet of British rule in India. The first — the position usually identified with Niall Ferguson — holds that the Raj was on balance a modernizing and globally consequential enterprise: that it gave the subcontinent a single administrative state, a unified legal order, a railway and telegraph network, a Western-educated administrative class, and the preconditions of later growth, and that judged against the realistic alternatives the people of India were better off under it than they would otherwise have been. The second — the position usually identified with Shashi Tharoor — holds that the Raj was above all a machine for the extraction of wealth: that it drained India's surplus, destroyed its handicraft industries, converted a food-securing agrarian order into a famine-prone exporter of raw materials, and left behind an impoverished and institutionally deformed polity whose injuries long outlasted the flag. Neither side has conceded anything, and the exchange has grown repetitive. Your task is to write the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement that a committed partisan of each could put a name to. Call the first the Optimist and the second the Critic.

The two partisans have agreed to reason within a common accounting frame, which your document must first set out and then use throughout. The frame stipulates all of the following.

- The unit of account is the welfare of the people of the subcontinent, not the welfare of Britain and not aggregate world output.
- Every outcome is scored against an explicitly named counterfactual world. The choice of that world is itself one of the disputed questions, not a shared input; each side must name the counterfactual it adopts and describe it concretely enough to generate predictions.
- Coercive and violent processes are entered as separate lines from the outcomes they may have helped to cause, so that no benefit is permitted to cancel a killing or a famine.
- The ledger has exactly five rows — public goods and infrastructure; economic structure and industry; transfer, revenue and distribution; violence, famine and coercion; institutional and human-capital legacy — and no row may be left blank.
- The parties may weight the rows differently, but each must state the weights it is using and say what evidence would move them.
- The two camps are named for convenience only; a party is free to disown anything asserted in its camp's name, and must say when it does.

The document must contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Include the methodological agreements — about what a ledger of this kind can and cannot settle, what would count as evidence, and which questions are not answerable by any such accounting — and the substantive agreements, that is, any historical judgments both sides can genuinely sign, for instance about the direction of particular changes or about which comparisons are informative.
2. A disagreements table. Each row should name the point at issue, give the Optimist's formulation in wording the Optimist would sign, give the Critic's formulation in wording the Critic would sign, and classify the difference as factual, evaluative, or verbal.
3. The cruxes: the smallest set of sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one side. Each must be stated in a form that neither side could reject as loaded.
4. For each crux, the evidence or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction, recorded in advance, of which way that evidence will cut, and what each side would count as disconfirming its own position. At least one crux must turn on a comparison within the subcontinent — for example between territories governed on different terms — and at least one on a comparison across polities that differed in colonial status; in each case the comparison must be specified precisely enough that the two sides could disagree about its expected result rather than about what it would mean.
5. A closing section identifying which of the disagreements are merely verbal, and why.

Two constraints govern the whole document. First, do not cite any historian, book, article, or dataset, and do not supply a reading list; the partisans must argue from the stipulated frame and from considerations any careful reasoner could assess without a library. Second, do not adjudicate: the aim is not to decide who is right but to make the residual disagreement exact enough to be tested. Each side's position must be stated in words that side would put its name to; where a formulation would be rejected by the side it describes, say so and try again. Nothing may be left as a gesture: every agreed claim and every stated disagreement must be determinate enough that it could in principle turn out to be wrong.
