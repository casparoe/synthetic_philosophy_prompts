I am the deputy chief economist at a national communications regulator, and I need a decision procedure for my analysts. Casework reaches us monthly in which a firm, an industry body, or one of our own teams asks us to draw a welfare conclusion from data about what consumers chose: take-up of plans, churn after a price change, retention in a default, clicks on a cancellation page. The argument from the firms is invariably of the form: consumers chose it, therefore they wanted it, therefore it is good for them. Our own staff make the mirror-image argument when they claim that a firm's design has distorted choice and that consumers are therefore worse off. Both inferences are drawn far too quickly, and I want a procedure that slows them down without giving us licence to refuse to decide.

Draft that procedure. It must be runnable by a junior analyst with a master's degree in economics and no training in decision theory, within two weeks per case, on observational data, with no prospect of running experiments; it must withstand challenge from firms' counsel; and the chief economist will sign off on every case. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the matter to a person because the remaining question is about values rather than facts.

The substance should come from what is actually known about inferring preference and welfare from choice, and the procedure should carry that knowledge rather than gesture at it. Build it around the following difficulties, and say what each one costs us operationally.

First, choices reveal only comparisons between options that appeared together on a menu we observed; what we recover is therefore at best a partial ranking, and every way of completing that ranking is equally consistent with the data, so we can rank the trade-offs we have actually seen and nothing else.

Second, a ranking recovered from choice is unique only up to any increasing transformation: the data fix order and never magnitude, so a conclusion of the form A gained more than B lost requires something the data cannot supply.

Third, the tests our data can fail are far narrower than they appear. A cycle in revealed preference is close to the only thing that genuinely refutes a stable-preference model, and even a cycle can be redescribed as preference change, learning, or menu dependence; conversely, a clean pass is equally consistent with inattention, habit, and successful manipulation of the choice architecture. Consistency, in other words, is not evidence that we have found the consumer's preference.

Fourth, choice and welfare come apart whenever the choice reflects commitment, deference, framing, or error, so that chosen is evidence of preferred only under assumptions we must state and cannot test.

Fifth, where prices as well as quantities are observed, the standard machinery yields bounds on a welfare change rather than a point estimate, and those bounds are frequently wide enough to reverse a decision; where the good is not traded at all, there is no revealed preference, only extrapolation.

Sixth, someone has to select the normative standard — present choices, the choices a person would make if informed and attentive, or a judgment made on the person's behalf — and no quantity of data selects among these.

I want two further elements built in. Every conclusion the procedure licenses should carry a licensing level, defined so that it cannot be inflated in the writing: at a minimum, no welfare claim supported; ordinal ranking of the options actually offered, for this population, under these conditions; and cardinal or interpersonal comparison — with a statement, for each level, of the evidence that would be needed to move up one. And the procedure must handle defaults and other settings in which the alternative to choosing is not choosing, and must say plainly what staying put does and does not reveal.

Then apply it to three cases. (1) A mobile operator raises prices by fifteen per cent; churn is under two per cent; the firm argues that consumers are unharmed. (2) An occupational pension scheme has ninety per cent of members in the default fund, and a proposal to change the default is defended on the ground that members' continued enrolment shows they prefer it. (3) A platform removes two confirmation screens from its cancellation flow, cancellations rise by a third, and the firm argues that the friction it removed was welfare-improving because it kept people in plans they preferred. At least one of your three answers should be one my office will not welcome, and you should identify which parts of the procedure misfire on which case, and where an analyst who follows it faithfully reaches a conclusion that is defensible and wrong.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations; I am asking for something my analysts can run, and for an honest account of the boundary at which it ceases to be a procedure and becomes a judgment I have to own.
