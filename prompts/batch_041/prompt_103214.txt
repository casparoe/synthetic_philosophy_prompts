I teach a seventy-five-minute session on the Browning–Goldhagen dispute over the perpetrators of the Holocaust to first-year students, and I want a plan for how to explain it rather than an explanation of it. My own hunch, which I want the students to reach on their own rather than be handed, is that the two books are not answering the same question: Goldhagen is after a motive, a belief that moved these particular men to kill, whereas Browning is explaining why men of unremarkable motives did not stop — which is how their evidence can overlap while their verdicts diverge. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. First, the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what, so that the motive-versus-enabling-condition distinction is earned rather than announced. Second, the single case the session should be built around, what it must contain and what it must lack, and whether the obvious candidate — the commander's offer to let his men stand aside from a mass shooting — is the right carrying example or the wrong one. Third, the misconceptions the students will bring or form (I expect "ordinary men means Browning lets them off," "situation explains, therefore excuses," and "eliminationist antisemitism means every German was a killer"), with the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Fourth, the one question I can ask at the end that shows whether they have grasped the structure of the disagreement rather than memorised two labels. Fifth, what I should leave out at this level and tell them I am leaving out. No readings, please, and nothing in the plan that a careful reader of either book would dispute.
