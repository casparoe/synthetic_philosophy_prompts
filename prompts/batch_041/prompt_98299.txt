I teach a graduate seminar on decision theory and rational choice, and I like to close the final session with a joke that actually lands — one that makes people who work on this material laugh because it turns on the real machinery, rather than a generic "economists are strange" gag that any first-year could tell. For the last meeting I'd like a joke about money pumps and the contrast between synchronic and diachronic rationality: the point being that an agent whose preferences cycle can have every pairwise choice look impeccable, while a clever counterparty walks her around the loop and takes a cut at each step, and the only escapes are the ones the literature actually argues about — a resolute policy of sticking to a plan once adopted, or a sophisticated anticipation of the trap that leads one to precommit in advance.

To keep it concrete, you might seed it with a trader at a market stall who will swap a banana for an apple, an apple for a cherry, and a cherry for a banana, charging a small fee on each swap. The humour should come from getting the mechanism right (each swap strictly preferred, each fee freely paid, no single step making the customer worse off) and from a punchline that turns on the escape route rather than on the bare existence of the cycle. Please don't just name-drop "intransitivity" and "money pump" as buzzwords — the joke has to be funny to someone who has genuinely thought about why the argument is supposed to be a problem and what it would take to defuse it.

Keep the whole thing to a single paragraph of no more than 130 words.
