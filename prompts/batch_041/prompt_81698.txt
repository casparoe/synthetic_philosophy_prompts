I am the research director of a state sentencing commission, and what I need from you is a decision procedure, not an essay. Our legislature has directed us to rewrite the felony guidelines so that every recommended range can be defended as what the offender deserves, and in the same bill has required us to build in an actuarial risk instrument and to hold every term under a forty-year ceiling. This puts two live philosophical positions in my lap. On one view, desert survives the denial of contra-causal free will: to deserve something is to stand in a certain relation to a shared scheme of cooperation, and we may go on treating offenders as answerable even if nothing about them is ultimately self-authored. On the other, "basic desert" — the idea that an offender deserves to suffer simply because of what he did, in a way that would justify the suffering even if no good came of it — presupposes precisely the kind of ultimate authorship a naturalistic picture of action cannot supply, so the state may restrain, incapacitate, and rehabilitate, but may not punish in the retributive sense. I have in mind the dispute associated with Dennett and Caruso; I would rather you engage the positions than the personalities.

Write me the procedure. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure issues a determinate number rather than a range, the points at which it stops with a no, and the points at which it must hand the decision to a human because what remains is a question of values and not of fact. The procedure has to keep culpability and risk from doing each other's work: a judge working through it must not be able to reach a desert verdict by way of a risk score, or a risk disposition by way of a desert finding. It should fit on a two-page worksheet, be completable in about twenty minutes, and be usable by judges with no philosophical training. If a judge departs from the recommended range, the worksheet should force him to name the step of the procedure at which the case falls out, so that our data tell us which step is doing the work.

Assume the stakes: roughly twelve thousand felony sentencings a year, each one a person's liberty, with the commission voting on the draft and the legislature able to reject it.

Then run the procedure on three cases, at least one of which returns an answer I will not like: (i) an offender of high culpability whose measured risk sits at the floor, forty years into a sentence, who will almost certainly never reoffend; (ii) an offender of high risk whose culpability is genuinely diminished by severe childhood deprivation and untreated psychosis; (iii) an offender for whom the desert calculation, honestly performed, exceeds the forty-year ceiling.

Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire, what it cannot do at all, and which single consideration, if it turned out to be right, would most undermine the whole thing — that is, what would change my mind about adopting it — and where in the translation from theory to worksheet you are most likely to have gone wrong. Assume I will supply my own citations: do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or references.
