I am preparing a seminar on thought experiments in science and want to assign the Victorian novella Flatland, but I am uneasy with the way it is usually taught — as a charming analogy for higher dimensions, a device for making the fourth dimension vivid. That seems to undersell and misdescribe what the book does. Read it as a thought experiment in its own right and tell me what epistemic work it actually performs.

Does Flatland establish a proposition — that a world of fewer dimensions is conceivable, say, or that its inhabitants could not represent a higher one — or does it merely produce an experience in the reader? Is it an argument with premises and a conclusion, or an intuition pump that concludes nothing? Take the moments when A. Square, having glimpsed the worlds of fewer and more dimensions, tries to preach the gospel of three dimensions to his countrymen and is punished for it. Consider, too, the book's self-referential structure: the reader is placed throughout in the position of the Sphere relative to A. Square, invited to feel superior to the Flatlanders even while being asked to imagine a fourth dimension she cannot picture. If the thought experiment's real target is the reader's own imaginative limit, then its payoff looks less like a discovery about geometry than a lesson in epistemic humility — and I want you to say whether that is knowledge or only a mood.

I also want the honest counterweight. Flatland is a satire as much as a thought experiment: the treatment of women as lines, the colour revolt, the rigid hierarchy, the persecution of A. Square for heresy. Do these satirical elements belong to the thought experiment or compete with it? Is the book's force as a thought experiment separable from its force as a Victorian satire, and if not, does that make it a weaker thought experiment or a richer one?

Engage the live philosophical question of how thought experiments can yield knowledge — whether they are disguised arguments, licensed appeals to intuition, or genuine instruments of a priori discovery — but argue with it rather than surveying it. I want a reading faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the novella to an illustration of a thesis about dimensions, and it should not bend the epistemology to fit the novella. Close by saying what, if anything, Flatland contributes to the debate about how thought experiments yield knowledge that a philosophical paper could not.
