I am a novelist at work on a collection of stories, each built around a single philosophical problem rather than about one, and I would like you to draft an entry in that collection at some length — roughly two thousand to three thousand words. The problem is the problem of other minds. I want it approached through an apparatus of my own invention rather than through the familiar thought experiments, which I find exhausted. No character should ever name the problem, and the vocabulary of the philosophical literature should not appear anywhere in the story.

The apparatus is this. Some years before the story opens, a bedside procedure entered routine clinical use: a scalp array coupled to a stimulus loop, measuring whether a nervous system responds to its own prior response, and how stably it does so. It returns a single number between zero and one. It is cheap, fast, and portable, and it is administered to the newborn, the dying, the anaesthetised, the animal, and, more recently, to engineered systems that no one quite knows how to classify. It was devised as a diagnostic and has become something else: a threshold, a verdict, an entry condition for being treated as a someone rather than a something. Take that drift for granted. It has already happened when the story begins, and no one in the story is surprised by it.

I want the following pressures dramatised rather than argued.

First, the difference between a marker and the thing marked. At least one character should be able to explain, fluently and correctly, why the number cannot be what it is now used as, and should nevertheless be unable to stop treating it as such. I want the reader to feel the gap between what she believes and what she does.

Second, the structure of the inference. Whatever the protagonist concludes about other people, she reaches it by way of her own case. Include a scene in which she notices this, and a colleague who denies that any inference is taking place — who holds that one simply sees another's state in the face and the voice, without evidence at all. Let both positions survive the scene intact, and let the plot proceed as though each of them were true, with costs attaching to each.

Third, the question of what has actually been asked. One character should press the point that even a flawless test would answer only how we come to know, and would leave untouched what we mean when we call something a mind; another should regard that distinction as a coward's dodge. Do not adjudicate between them.

Fourth, the asymmetry. She can never undergo the procedure in the way that matters, because she has her own case directly. Include an attempt at self-administration, and let its failure or inconclusiveness carry consequences rather than serve as a joke.

On craft: keep the prose concrete and unhurried, in a single close third person, past tense, realist in mode. No exposition, no lecture, no character delivering a position at length — the ideas must be carried by objects, procedures, and consequences. Avoid zombies, brains in vats, the Turing test, the Chinese room, and any quotation from a real text.

The story should turn on two administrations of the test: one whose result the protagonist needs, and one that leaves her indifferent. The order in which they occur should matter. The philosophical question is to remain open at the close; the practical question is to be settled; and the settling should be visibly arbitrary. Give the piece a title that does not contain the words mind or consciousness.
