I just got appointed to the zoning board of appeals in my city -- I'm the newest member and by far the youngest, and I have an undergrad degree in philosophy that I mostly keep to myself. We hear six to nine variance and special-permit applications a month, each one gets about fifteen minutes of discussion, and whatever we decide I have to state reasons for on the record that a lawyer can defend if someone appeals us to court. Our code gives four criteria for a variance, roughly: (1) the owner faces a practical difficulty or unnecessary hardship that isn't self-created, (2) granting it won't be substantially detrimental to neighboring property, (3) it's consistent with the comprehensive plan, and (4) it isn't contrary to the public interest. Last month the city attorney sent around a memo saying 'neighborhood character' on its own isn't a legal basis for denial, and the council just amended the comprehensive plan to add a clause telling us we may consider the effect of a decision on the supply of housing affordable to households below 60% of area median income. Nobody on the board, including the chair, seems to know what to do with that clause, and in practice most of our votes come down to how many people showed up to object.

What's bothering me is that these feel like philosophical questions wearing zoning clothes. I've been reading about the idea -- associated with Lefebvre and later taken up by Harvey -- that inhabitants have a collective claim on the city that isn't reducible to what property owners want, and about the critique that neighborhood veto power is often just a respectable way of excluding people. But I also sit there listening to a retired couple who genuinely will lose their afternoon sun, and I don't think the theory has anything to say to them that isn't insulting. So here's what I want. You're a philosopher who actually works with planning practitioners -- please think carefully and start with a short brainstorm of what you take the right to the city to require in a body like ours and what it can't mean (it obviously can't mean any resident gets to veto any change, but say why, and say what the collective character of the claim is actually doing), then turn it into a decision procedure I can run in fifteen minutes, out loud, in plain English, without sounding like I'm giving a seminar to the other comissioners. I need: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which steps are pure fact and which are values; where the procedure stops with a flat no; and where it hands the thing to my judgment, marked as such, so I can say honestly on the record that this is where I'm exercising discretion. Keep whatever distinctions from the theory are load-bearing for the hard cases and tell me which ones I can drop.

Then run it on three cases. First, a homeowner who wants a variance to convert her detached garage into a small rental unit; four neighbors have written in about parking and about the loss of the side yard's light. Second, a developer proposing sixty units, twelve of them deed-restricted affordable, on a lot with a shuttered dry cleaner, against an organized neighborhood group whose petition says the building 'doesn't fit' and will 'change the feel of the street' -- I'll admit I want to approve this one and I want to know if the procedure lets me. Third, a proposal to convert a single-family house into a sober-living home for eight people, where the objections at the hearing are about 'safety' and 'transient population,' and I genuinely can't tell how much of that is pretext. I'd rather you give me at least one case where the procedure says no to something I like than one where it just ratifies my instincts -- if the equity side of the theory implies denying a project that adds housing because it displaces the tenants who are already there, tell me that and make me live with it. Finally, tell me where this procedure will misfire, which steps are judgment dressed up as arithmetic, and what it simply can't decide -- because I'd rather know the failure modes than pretend I have a formula.
