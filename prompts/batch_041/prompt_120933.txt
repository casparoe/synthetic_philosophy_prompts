I'm a retired physicist (30-odd years of neutron scattering, hung it up in 2019) who's spent the last few years reading philosophy and history of science on the side. I've written a short essay on Galileo and scripture and I'd like you to tear into it.

The gist, roughly as it reads:

The Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615) is usually sold as the birth of the seperation of church and science. I dont think thats it at all. What Galileo actually did was hand the theologians a rule: where a demonstrated conclusion in natural philosophy conflicts with a scriptural passage, the passage has to be reinterpreted, because the Holy Spirit speaks to us in the language of ordinary people and never intended to teach astronomy. His example is Joshua telling the sun to stand still — the text talks as if the sun moves because that's how it looks from down here. So Galileo invented the doctrine of scriptural accommodation, and his rule is really a domain-of-validity claim: scripture is an effective description, good at the scales it was written for, and you shouldn't push it past its range. That analogy is the part I'm most attached to — I think accommodation just is what a physicist would call an effective theory, with the theologians mistaking a low-energy description for the fundamental one. And I claim Bellarmine basically agreed with him: his 1615 letter to Foscarini concedes that if Copernicanism were genuinely demonstrated, scripture would have to be reinterpreted.

What I want from you: (1) is the history right, especially the accommodation point and the Bellarmine bit — I'd much rather be corrected than flattered; (2) is the effective-theory analogy doing real work, or am I just dressing a truism up in physics clothes, and if it does work, where does it break down; (3) anything a historian would say I've read back into Galileo. Order the comments by how much they matter. No reading list please — I'm not going to chase down citations, so just tell me where a claim is shaky and what I should be saying instead.
