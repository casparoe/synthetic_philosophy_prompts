OK so this is a pattern I keep running into and I've never found a good word for it, and every time I go looking I end up at the genetic fallacy, which isn't quite it.

Here's what happens. Someone gives a naturalistic account of where a religious practice or belief came from -- say, that a set of fasting rules probably got started as practical food-safety habits in a hot climate and only later got ritualized, or that belief in an afterlife is a byproduct of the fact that we can't help imagining ourselves persisting. Fine, whatever, that's just a story about origins. The thing I keep noticing is that the SAME story gets grabbed by both sides as if it had settled the truth of the belief. One friend hears the fasting explanation and says, basically, isn't that wonderful, that's God quietly teaching us how to live, the science just confirms it. Another friend hears the exact same explanation and says, right, so it's just food safety dressed up, there's nothing to it. Both of them act like the origin story did the work for their side.

What bugs me is that neither inference follows. Knowing where a belief came from doesn't by itself tell you whether it's true, and it doesn't by itself tell you whether believing it is justified -- that part I get, that's the genetic fallacy. But that's not what's going on here, because these people aren't making a dumb mistake about origins vs. justification; they mostly know the difference if you press them. It's more like the explanation gets treated as a kind of switch you can flip either way, and whichever way you flip it feels like it settles something. And it seems to happen specifically when the explanation is of the naturalistic / evolutionary / cognitive kind, where the explanation is supposed to be neutral and yet never gets treated as neutral.

Is there an established name for this exact thing -- the double-use of an origin story, where the same explanation is recruited both to vindicate and to debunk, and where the recruitment itself is the phenomenon? I've heard "debunking argument," but that only names the debunking half. Is the vindicating half a recognized move with its own name, or does the literature just treat it as a misuse of the debunking machinery? And is there a term for the general thing -- the way an explanation of a practice's origin gets absorbed back into the practice and makes it feel more solid rather than less?

I don't want just a label. I want to know what the thing actually is: where the line is between this and the plain genetic fallacy, whether it's really two seperate phenomena (one per direction) or one, and what the boundary cases are -- does it happen with non-religious beliefs too, or is there something about religious ones that makes the origin story feel load-bearing in a way it isn't elsewhere?

Also, tell me how confident you are in what you say, and flag the bit you're least sure about.
