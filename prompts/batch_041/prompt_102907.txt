I am putting together a handout for a graduate seminar on machine ethics, and I want a decision tree that takes a system designer from a handful of starting commitments to a named position on how an artificial agent ought to reach moral decisions. The utilitarian-versus-deontological split should be the trunk of the tree, not the whole of it: by the leaves I want positions that a designer could actually implement, and that a reader could land on without having already picked a side in the textbook debate.

Build the tree from the logical space first and locate the names in it afterwards. Every internal node should be a question a designer can answer before knowing which position it leads to, and should be phrased as a concrete design or engineering decision rather than as a doctrinal label. The nodes I want the tree to turn on are at least these: whether the system is conceived as a moral agent in its own right, as a moral adviser whose verdicts a human endorses, or as a morally neutral tool whose output merely has moral effects; whether its moral reasoning must be intelligible to the humans it affects, or whether it may act on dispositions it cannot articulate; whether its moral constraints are compiled into the objective it is trained against or enforced architecturally at runtime; whether it is required to act under acknowledged moral uncertainty, and if so whether it resolves that uncertainty by maximizing expected choiceworthiness across a set of theories or by some rule that is robust to persistent disagreement among them; and whether, when the stakes are high and its governing theory is contested, the system may or must refuse to act at all.

The leaves should include, at a minimum: act utilitarianism; rule consequentialism; a two-level view on which ordinary deliberation runs on simple rules while the rules themselves answer to aggregate outcomes; hard-constraint deontology; threshold deontology, where constraints yield past some magnitude of stakes; a prima facie-duties view on which several considerations are each defeasibly binding; moral particularism, on which no finite set of principles is compiled in at all; an expected-choiceworthiness maximizer over a distribution of moral theories; a deliberative arrangement that treats the competing theories as a body that must reach some aggregate verdict; the obedience view, on which the system's task is to execute its principal's values however those values turn out; a virtue- or character-based design; and an abstentionist design that treats refusal as the correct output under moral risk. For each leaf give the name of the position and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Then do the two things a clean tree usually hides. First, mark any pairs of leaves the tree cannot actually separate, and say why the diagnostic questions fail to pull them apart. Second, list the combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the vacancy is there because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Keep the whole instrument to no more than twelve leaves, and make the branches do real sorting work rather than restating the labels.
