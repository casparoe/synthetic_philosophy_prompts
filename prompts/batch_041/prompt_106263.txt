I work on the machine translation team at a company that ships a production system, and I have no formal background in philosophy or linguistics, so I am hoping for a careful adjudication rather than a verdict on vibes. Two of my colleagues had an argument at lunch that I have been unable to resolve, and I would rather not take a side than take the wrong one.

The background: our system is a large neural model trained on parallel text. It has no hand-written grammar, no dictionary, and no explicit knowledge base. Dana, who works on the modeling side, thinks it is fair to say the system understands language. Ravi, who came over from computational linguistics, thinks that is a category mistake and that the system is doing something shallower. Here is how the exchange went.

(1) Dana: "Yesterday I gave it a sentence where the word 'bank' was ambiguous on its own, and the surrounding words made clear it was a riverbank. It picked the right sense, and not for a sentence that was in training. Nothing that only matched surface patterns could do that."

(2) Ravi: "It learned that 'bank' near 'water' and 'mud' tends to be the river sense. That is a correlation between words, not knowledge of what a bank is. You could get that from a large enough table of co-occurrences."

(3) Dana: "Then take an idiom. It renders 'he kicked the bucket' as the target-language expression for dying, not as an act involving a bucket. To do that it has to recognize the phrase is not meant literally."

(4) Ravi: "Idioms are fixed strings; memorizing them is what a lookup table does, and there are only so many. Show me it handling a metaphor invented last week and I will be impressed."

(5) Dana: "Fine — it also gets pronoun references right across a paragraph, resolving 'it' to something named three sentences earlier. That needs the whole passage, not the word."

(6) Ravi: "It resolves the pronoun by which reading is more probable given the rest of the text. That is still statistics over a paragraph instead of over a sentence. You keep sliding from 'more context' to 'understanding' as if they were the same thing."

(7) Dana: "At some point the behavior is indistinguishable from understanding, and insisting there is a hidden difference underneath is just refusing to call it what it is."

(8) Ravi: "And at some point calling every good prediction 'understanding' empties the word of content, since you would then have to say a weather model understands the atmosphere."

I reported this to a third colleague, who said we were talking past each other and that the real question is what "understanding" is supposed to add over "translating correctly." Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the eight, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or defensible-but-overstated, and why. Then state what the crux of the disagreement actually is, who is closer to right on that crux, and what both of them are missing — in particular, whether there is a defensible sense in which a system like ours understands and a defensible sense in which it does not, and how one would tell which sense is in play.
