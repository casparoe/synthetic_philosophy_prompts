For the past several months I have been reading popular accounts of computing and logic, and I have become fixated on a pattern of argument that I cannot find a name for, though I suspect it must have one. I have no training in the subject, so I may be describing something ordinary, but the more I look at it the more it seems to be its own thing. The pattern concerns a certain claim about what machines can do: roughly, that anything a person could carry out by following a definite set of rules, a machine could in principle carry out as well, and that nothing outside this class deserves to be called a genuine 'procedure'. What puzzles me is that the claim behaves like two different kinds of statement depending on who is pressing on it. When someone asks why we should believe the sweeping half of it — that no rule-governed process escapes the machines — the answer is empirical in form: here is a long list of ingenious schemes people have proposed, and not one has ever been shown to do anything the ordinary machines cannot. That is the language of a bold conjecture about the world, the sort of thing that could in principle be refuted tomorrow. But when someone actually exhibits a candidate counterexample — a process that seems to deliver answers with no step-by-step recipe behind it, or a device with so many interacting parts that no ordinary machine could mimic it — the reply is never 'then the conjecture was wrong'. The reply is that the process was not really a procedure, or that the device was not really computing. The counterexample is not defeated; it is reclassified, by adjusting what the word 'procedure' is allowed to cover. So the same sentence seems to be, at one moment, a risky claim about the limits of machinery and, at the next, a stipulation about the meaning of a word.

I would like to know whether there is an established name for a claim with this double life — one that is defended with evidence, as though it could turn out false, but is protected from refutation by being, in the end, a matter of definition. I want the answer to do more than supply a label. If such a claim has a name, I want to know how it is carved: is the whole thing one hybrid object, or is it really two claims stitched together — one a genuine, refutable hypothesis and one a definition or convention — and can those two be pulled apart cleanly, or does the useful part depend on their being run together? I also want to know how the pattern differs from its neighbours. It is not simply an unfalsifiable statement, because it is offered with evidence; it is not simply a definition, because it is treated as a discovery about the world; and it does not seem to be an ordinary case of someone quietly changing the subject, because the reclassification is done in good faith by people who believe they are defending the same claim throughout.

Perhaps the nearest thing I can think of is the old distinction between statements that are true by meaning and statements that are true by fact, but that distinction does not seem to capture the moving part here, which is the oscillation itself — the fact that the claim is allowed to be one sort of thing when challenged and the other sort when used. If there is no established term for the phenomenon, I would rather you coin one and tell me where its edges are — which cases fall inside it and which, though similar, fall outside — than hand me a name and stop.
