Ok so this is a weird request, bear with me. Six months ago my sister got hit by a car. The driver had an undiagnosed seizure at the wheel, so there was no negligence anywhere in the story, she just happened to be standing on that stretch of pavement at that moment. She's alive and mostly ok, but she isn't going back to her old job, probably not her old life either. And we've spent the last few months inside the compensation process, where the number they eventually came up with for her turns out to be a function of what she used to earn. Which, fine, whatever. But my roommate is a second-year philosophy PhD and he keeps saying the whole apparatus is just luck egalitarianism in practice, and I honestly can't tell if that's an insult, a compliment, or just wrong. It's gotten under my skin and I want to settle it properly.

So here's what I want. Act like someone who knows both the philosophy and the boring institutional end of these schemes, a philosopher who has actually read the actuarial memos. Before you write anything, spend some time thinking it through: brainstorm what luck egalitarianism actually commits you to, and only then go test it against the real thing. Don't skip to the verdict.

The real thing I want you to use is the 9/11 Victim Compensation Fund in the US, the one Congress set up right after the attacks with Kenneth Feinberg as special master. Two features of it I'm reasonably sure about, correct me if I've got them wrong: the awards were built off an estimate of what each victim would have earned across a lifetime, so a bond trader and a dishwasher killed in the same building on the same morning by the same event generated very different payouts; and taking an award meant giving up your right to sue the airlines, so it wasn't really a gift from the state, it was a settlement that bought away a lawsuit. I also have a vague memory that other money the family received, life insurance or charitable donations, was counted against the award somehow. If that's right, it seems like it matters a lot and I want you to say why. (Related, and I may be making this up: I think the same administrator ran the Boston Marathon fund a decade later and paid people by injury category instead of by salary. The actual task, in three parts. I expect the earnings-based part to be the interesting one: the death is the bad luck, sure, but is the lost salary part of the bad luck or just the pre-existing distribution of luck showing through? Be careful with the baseline, because I think that's exactly where my roommate's little slogan falls apart. Third, run it the other direction. What does this episode show about luck egalitarianism? Then give me an actual verdict. Was the 9/11 fund defensible as a luck-egalitarian scheme, or is the honest description of it something else wearing luck-egalitarian clothes? Commit to an answer.
