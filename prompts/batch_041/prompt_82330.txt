I would like you to act as a philosopher with expertise in collective decision-making and the epistemology of groups, and to adjudicate an argument I witnessed. Please reason carefully before you write; do the arithmetic yourself rather than accepting either party's figures; and do not simply identify the phenomenon they are circling and stop — I want the arguments themselves assessed, step by step.

Background. I sit on the board of a land trust — a small conservation group that owns and manages a two-hundred-acre parcel. There are thirty of us, and our bylaws allow any member to act alone on "urgent" matters, on the understanding that the board will ratify or repudiate afterwards. Three weeks ago one member, Maya, arranged for a licensed contractor to spray herbicide on a patch of knotweed that had been spreading along a stream bank on the parcel. She had been watching the patch for about a month and had mentioned it to two or three people, but she did not call a meeting. The label said the product was approved for use near water. A week after the spraying, someone found a dozen dead fish a few hundred yards downstream. We do not know whether the two are connected — it was a hot week and the stream is shallow — and we probably never will. Maya says the label cleared her and that the fish are a coincidence.

At the next board meeting, two members, Hannah and Miguel, argued about it for forty minutes. I am reporting their exchange as accurately as I can. I am not on either side; I came away genuinely unable to say who was making sense.

(1) Hannah: "Suppose any one of us is liable to get a call like this wrong about one time in five. Take any course of action that is in fact a bad idea. The chance that at least one of the thirty of us talks himself into it and goes ahead is one minus 0.8 to the thirtieth — better than 99.8 percent. That is the machine we have built. We should not be surprised by the dead fish; we should be surprised we went this long without them."

(2) Miguel: "You have told me how likely it is that a bad idea gets carried out by someone. You have told me nothing about whether the idea that was carried out was bad. For that you need the other conditional, and you need a base rate, and you have neither. Your picture also assumes the person who acts is a random draw from the thirty. She isn't. Maya went ahead because she was the one standing on that bank, watching the patch spread. The person who acts is usually the person who knows the most about that particular question, not the least."

(3) Hannah: "'She was standing there' is how it always looks from the inside. The person who acts is always the person with a special reason. From where the rest of us sit, that is a selection effect, not evidence. And notice that your defense — that if she hadn't done it, someone else would have within the month — is not an excuse. It is the design flaw. It means that whatever the merits of this particular spraying, the structure guarantees that our worst calls will get made, and made by someone who was sure."

(4) Miguel: "Then you are not blaming her, you are blaming all of us, and you should say so. What you actually said was that the act becomes unjustifiable because other people might have done it. That cannot be right. If the spraying was justified on the evidence she had, no fact about what the other twenty-nine would have done can make it unjustified. A rule that makes it wrong to do the right thing whenever someone else might do the wrong thing is a rule that punishes the diligent."

(5) Hannah: "Fine — then the rule is the problem, and here is the rule I want: no member acts unilaterally on anything irreversible without first establishing that the others, if they knew what she knew, would agree. And note that this was not urgent. She had been watching that patch for a month. The bylaw does not cover her."

(6) Miguel: "Your conformity rule is a veto handed to whoever is least informed and least interested. The people who notice things will be permanently overruled by the people who don't. And it makes our error worse, not better: we will systematically fail to act on the things that matter, which is also a way of doing harm, and a quieter one."

Please give me:

1. Framing (100-150 words): what this dispute is really about, stated so that both of them would recognize the description.

2. Move-by-move adjudication (600-800 words): rate each of the six moves on a scale running from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, and justify each rating. Where a move is right in part, say which part. Where a move would be valid only under a condition that does not hold here, name the condition.

3. What the argument turns on (250-350 words): identify the two or three places where they are talking past each other, and say what would have to be settled to resolve them.

4. Blind spots (200-300 words): what each of them is missing, including anything that neither has said but that bears on the case.

5. Overall verdict (150-200 words): who is more nearly right, and what the board should actually do — both about Maya and about the bylaw.

Do not cite literature or give me a reading list; I want your reasoning, not a bibliography. If you find yourself agreeing with a conclusion you initially resisted, say so and explain why.
