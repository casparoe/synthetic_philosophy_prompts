ok so I'm about 40 pages into a diss chapter on satisficing and I keep going back and fourth on the framing, so I'm just going to dump the argument at you and let you poke holes in it.

The setup is the usual one. Bounded rationality says optimization is out of reach for a finite agent, so real agents satisfice: they fix an aspiration level and take the first option that clears it. The two standard objections are that satisficing is indeterminate (who set the aspiration level, and why that one?) and that it's maximizing in disguise (if you pick the aspiration level by expected utility you're just optimizing with an extra step, and if you don't, you're being irrational).

My first move: both objections land on the maximizer too, so they can't be what's specifically wrong with satisficing. Call it the symmetry of indeterminacy. The maximizer is supposed to be well-defined once the option set and the utility function are fixed, but the option set isn't handed to you. Searching is itself an action, and every minute spent looking is a minute not spent doing. So the maximizer faces exactly the satisficer's question, when do I stop, and answers it with a threshold on the marginal value of information rather than a threshold on the goods. That threshold isn't more determinate than the satisficer's. It's the same kind of object, and it's worse off epistemically, because it's a threshold over counterfactual options you've never seen.

Second move, and this is the part I actually beleive: the interesting thing about satisficers isn't the stopping rule, it's the option-set engineering. Shopping with a list, only dating inside a five mile radius, applying to three programs instead of thirty — none of that is lowering a bar, it's pre-committing to a menu. Read that way, satisficing looks less like ignorance and more like self-binding: you make the optimum unreachable on purpose so that the question "am I settling?" never gets a determinate answer. Which is either the deepest thing about practical rationality or a fancy word for a rationalization, and I genuinely cant tell which yet.

Third thing, which I'd like to be the actual contribution: aspiration creep. If the aspiration level is a function of past achievement, and empirically it seems to be — you clear the bar, the bar goes up — then a satisficer who keeps succeeding drifts into a maximizer, and a satisficer who keeps failing drifts into someone who takes anything. So the interesting question about satisficing isn't whether it's optimal, it's whether it's stable, and maybe any threshold-based policy with an endogenous threshold is unstable in the same way. I want that to be a general result about threshold policies. It might be trivially true or flat false, I haven't worked it out.

Also I want to keep two questions apart that I think everybody runs together: is satisficing a good description of what agents actually do, and is it a defensible standard for what they ought to do. The two objections up top are aimed at the second and then treated as if they'd refuted the first.

Can you give me comments? I mostly want to know where I'm conflating two things that should stay separate, where a premise is doing more work than I've noticed, and whether the second move is doing anything at all or is just a relabeling. Exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
