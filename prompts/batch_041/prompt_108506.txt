After a career in experimental physics, I retired and, for lack of anything better to do with my mornings, began working through the Madhyamaka literature. I have arrived at a dispute that looks to me as though it has stalled, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The dispute concerns how to read Nāgārjuna and the tetralemma — the four-cornered form that runs through a proposition, its negation, both together, and neither. On one reading, he is putting forward a metaphysical thesis: that all phenomena lack intrinsic nature (svabhāva), that this lack is in some sense their ultimate nature, and that the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth is a claim about how things actually stand. On the other reading, he advances no thesis whatever: the tetralemma is a therapeutic or critical instrument that declines all four corners in order to dissolve the compulsion to hold metaphysical positions, emptiness is itself empty, and the two truths are an expedient of teaching and ordinary practice rather than a piece of metaphysics. Cutting across this is a further division — the one the Tibetan scholastics marked — between those who hold that a Madhyamika may argue from premises his opponent is bound to accept as independently established, and those who hold that the only legitimate procedure is to take the opponent's own commitments and show that they collapse.

What I cannot understand is why the two camps, agreeing as they seem to on the texts and on the logical moves, never converge. My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the impasse is not at bottom about what the texts assert. It may be that "conventional truth" is being used in two registers at once: descriptively, for what ordinary practice takes to be the case, and normatively, for what it is permissible to assert without falling into contradiction. If that is so, then part of the disagreement is a disguised disagreement about norms of assertion, conducted in the vocabulary of metaphysics, which would explain why neither side can be refuted on its own terms. I also wonder whether the whole quarrel repeats a pattern familiar to me from physics — the disagreement between those who treat an effective theory as a mere calculational device and those who treat it as describing the real structure at that scale. If conventional truth functions like an effective description, the two readings may be less rivals than answers to different questions.

Please give me a diagnosis, argued from what each side actually maintains, rather than a survey of the positions. Is this a substantive disagreement about what Nāgārjuna holds, a merely verbal one about the word "metaphysics," or two parties answering different questions — one about what the texts assert, one about how they are to be used — under a single heading? Is a hidden formal or interpretive question doing the work, or is the disagreement instead resting on starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formalization, a change of question. If you think no progress should be expected, say so and explain why. I have no interest in a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement. Please answer in American English.
