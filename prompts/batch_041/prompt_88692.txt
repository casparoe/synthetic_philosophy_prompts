I host a philosophy interview podcast, and next month's guest is the author of a recent book on the moral-saint problem. I want the episode to be an interrogation of the book's argument, not a tour of the usual positions, so assume I have read it closely and want questions that presuppose its contents.

The book's central move is a distinction between two ideals that, the guest argues, the existing debate has conflated: the ideal of a will that is maximally answerable to the moral reasons there are (the sainted will) and the ideal of a life whose moral output — good done, harm averted — is maximal (the sainted ledger). The guest's thesis is that the familiar objection that a maximally moral life crowds out the nonmoral excellences, the objection usually traced to Susan Wolf's 1982 essay "Moral Saints," lands only against the ledger ideal; the sainted will, by contrast, is both coherent and attractive, and is compatible with ordinary attachments and personal projects. The guest adds a second, more surprising claim: because a finite agent cannot represent the indefinitely many reasons bearing on it, full responsiveness is unattainable, so the sainted will is a regulative ideal while the sainted ledger is at least in principle measurable — and this asymmetry, the guest insists, tells in favor of the will ideal rather than against it. The book's key test cases are a "reluctant saint," who reliably does the right thing but always with resentment, and a "serene idler," whose will is perfectly oriented yet who, through no fault of her own, produces very little good; the guest claims these pry the two ideals apart.

Draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation, together with a follow-up for each, keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give. I need at least one question each on: (i) whether the sainted will collapses into the sainted ledger once we say what a maximally responsive agent would actually be responsive to, and if not, what does the separating work; (ii) whether the responsiveness ceiling makes the will ideal idle, or self-undermining under an ought-implies-can principle; (iii) what the reluctant saint and the serene idler are supposed to demonstrate, and whether the cases in fact demonstrate it or merely redescribe a familiar intuition; (iv) whether the two-ideal distinction answers the crowding-out objection or simply relocates it, since a fully responsive will may be obliged to hold its own projects permanently revisable; and (v) as a closer, what argument or case the guest would count as counting against the distinction itself. Press where the position is weakest, anticipate the standard reply and build the follow-up onto it, and avoid any question that only invites the guest to restate the book.
