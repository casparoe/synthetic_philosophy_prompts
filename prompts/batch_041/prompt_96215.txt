A friend in my department has produced the following argument, which he says shows that lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — the view that disvalue below some threshold is lexically prior to positive value, so that no quantity of pleasure can compensate for it — collapses into the pure negative utilitarianism it was invented to avoid. I think it proves too much, but I cannot find the error, and I would rather keep its first and fifth steps than surrender either.

(1) If disvalue below the threshold is lexically prior to positive value, then no amount of positive value outweighs a sub-threshold disvalue.
(2) So a world containing a sub-threshold disvalue is not made better by any amount of positive value added to it.
(3) Consider Ward A, containing one patient with a mild but genuine distress below the threshold, and Ward B, which is Ward A plus a billion causally isolated people living at the highest level of positive welfare we can coherently imagine.
(4) By (2), Ward B is no better than Ward A.
(5) But Ward B is better than Ward A: adding lives worth living to a world, while leaving everyone already in it no worse off, improves it.
(6) So (2) is false, and since (2) merely restates lexical priority, lexical priority is false.
(7) Nothing turns on where the threshold is set, so long as it is above zero; the reasoning goes through for any such threshold. The view therefore slides towards the position in which the faintest trace of disvalue trumps everything, and with it goes any claim that pleasure and pain are not commensurable. He adds that the view then cannot even object to a policy imposing mild hardship on a few for enormous aggregate gain, which he takes to be its chief selling point.

I do not see how (1) can be true without (2), since outweighing and trumping look to me like the same relation, yet I am unwilling to give up (5), and I doubt that (7) is licensed by anything before it.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. In particular: if (1) and (5) can both be kept, does that require reading lexical priority more narrowly than it is usually stated, and what does that narrower reading forfeit? Is the flaw confined to this argument, or does it reappear for any ordering that ranks some goods ahead of others? And if instead (5) is the step to give up, say what the lexicalist owes us in its place. Please answer in British English.
