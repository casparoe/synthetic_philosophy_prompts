I am assembling cases for a paper on the dispute between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of lawhood, and I need one built to order. The cases that get discussed most run in a single direction: the anti-Humean counts as a law a regularity that the Humean refuses to count, either because the regularity has a single instance or because the anti-Humean holds that two worlds agreeing on all occurrent fact can differ in their laws. I want the reverse direction, and I want the result generated by the theories rather than stipulated by me.

Construct a case in which the Humean — whose laws are the axioms and theorems of the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength over the whole history of occurrent fact — must count a given regularity as a law, while an anti-Humean must deny that it is one. The requirements:

1. The regularity must be exceptionless, and it must not be derivable from the other members of the best system; it has to earn its own place as an axiom.

2. The anti-Humean's denial must follow from what the theory says lawhood requires, such as a necessitation that obtains wherever the regularity is instantiated, or a grounding in the natures of the properties involved. It must not rest on my stipulating that nothing grounds the regularity. If you need a particular version of anti-Humeanism for the case to work, say which one and why, and say whether someone who treats lawhood as primitive would side with the Humean instead.

3. The regularity must be exhibited across the world's history rather than in a single instance, so that the case cannot be folded into the familiar single-instance objection.

4. No alien properties, no one-off universals, no pair of worlds that agree on all occurrent fact and differ only in their laws, no gods or demons. A toy physics is welcome, but it must be specified well enough that the contents of the best system are not simply announced.

5. The scenario itself should be one paragraph.

Then, in order: give each side's verdict and the reasoning behind it; identify the single feature of the case that does the work; change that feature alone and say whether the disagreement survives and, if it does not, which side shifts its verdict; say whether the case depends on the regularity being a decisive rather than a marginal gain for the best system, and if the gain is marginal, whether the disagreement then becomes one of degree; and give the strongest reply available to whichever side fares worse. If you conclude that no version of anti-Humeanism can deny lawhood here without stipulation, say which requirement blocks the construction and what that shows about the dispute. No citations needed — argue from the views themselves. A case that stipulates the verdicts instead of deriving them does not count.
