I run a reading group on moral responsibility — second- and third-year undergraduates who have done the standard free will module and can recite the compatibilist/incompatibilist taxonomy, but who have not read Strawson. Next week's session is seventy-five minutes on the reactive attitudes, and I want a teaching plan rather than an explanation: assume I know the material, and tell me how to order it.

The sequence first. What has to be in place before the participant attitude can be presented as anything more than a mood? I am tempted to bring in the objective attitude before the excuses, on the grounds that students need to see the two stances before they can see that an excuse is addressed to one of them rather than a verdict issued from outside; tell me whether that is right, and what breaks if I reverse it. Then say where the paper's closing turn belongs — the move that treats the demand for a justification of our practices from somewhere outside them as misplaced — so that it arrives as a conclusion the students have been walked into rather than as a shrug.

Then the carrying case. I had planned to run one case the whole way through: someone close to me whose behaviour I used to resent, and whom since her diagnosis I now handle instrumentally and without resentment, which feels less like dehumanisation than like clarity. Test it against the job it has to do — what it shows, what it cannot show (I suspect it blurs letting someone off for a particular act with ceasing to hold them to account at all, and that it risks making the objective attitude look like an epistemic achievement rather than a loss) — and if it will not carry the session, say what the example must have and must lack instead.

Then the misconceptions, which is where I expect the session to be won or lost. I predict at least these: that Strawson is a compatibilist entering that debate; that his point is that we cannot help having these attitudes and so determinism is beside the point; that the objective attitude is the scientific or enlightened stance; that excusing someone and exempting them from the practice altogether are the same move; and that his naturalism leaves the paper descriptive, with no normative work to do. Give me the map as a compact table — misconception, the point in the sequence where it surfaces, the move that heads it off — and add any misconception this audience in particular will bring that I have missed.

Close with one question I can put at the end, answerable in two minutes, that separates a student who has understood why Strawson thinks the sceptic's question is misplaced from one who has merely learned the vocabulary; and tell me what to leave out at this level, and to say openly that I am leaving it out.

Under 800 words. No readings, no citations.
