I am in the middle of a graduate seminar on formal systems and the philosophy of mind, and last week a classmate floated a thesis I have not been able to shake. She began from the MIU system, the string-rewriting puzzle that starts from the single string MI and permits four moves: append U to any string ending in I; take any string beginning with M and double everything after the M; replace any occurrence of III with U; and delete any occurrence of UU. The famous fact about this system is that the target string MU cannot be derived, and the usual proof of that fact looks like a textbook case of stepping outside a system: instead of manipulating strings, one observes an invariant, namely that the number of I's in any derivable string is never a multiple of three, which the target MU violates, since it contains no I's at all. My classmate's point was that this observation is itself a piece of arithmetic and can be formalized: in a system rich enough to talk about codes, one can derive as a theorem that no string satisfying those four rules has an I-count divisible by three. On her view, which she calls the relocation thesis, what we describe as jumping out of a system is nothing over and above changing which system you are currently working in; there is no distinct act of getting outside, only the ordinary act of relocating a derivation into a larger setting that happens to contain the old system as its subject matter. In the seminar we have taken to calling supposed escapes joots, so her claim is that every joot is a relocation.

I would like you to argue that the relocation thesis is false. Take that position and defend it: there is something in the MIU case that no enlargement of the system absorbs, and your task is to say precisely what it is and why formalizing the invariant argument fails to capture it. Two constraints on the argument. First, it must not turn on any claim about human minds versus machines, or on Gödelian limits on what a formal system can prove about itself; the issue is the structure of the object-level and meta-level relation, not who or what can perform the move. Second, use a variant of the system as a test case: keep the axiom MI and rules one, two and four as given, but replace the rule that turns III into U with a rule that turns II into U, and work out what happens in that variant both to the target MU and to the invariant argument; a successful argument should explain what the variant reveals about where the joot in the original case actually lived. Write the argument in continuous prose of roughly 700 to 900 words, engaging the strongest version of the relocation thesis rather than a strawman of it.

After the prose, give me a brief structured summary as a single JSON object with these fields: thesis (one sentence stating the position you defended); residual_act (a short string naming the element you claim no system enlargement can absorb, with a one-sentence gloss); premises (an array of objects, each with the fields claim, support and vulnerability, one object per load-bearing premise); test_case (an object with the fields original_system and variant_system, each of which contains a verdict field taking the value absorbed or not_absorbed, and a reason field); strongest_objection (a paragraph stating the best case for the relocation thesis as you understand it); reply (a paragraph answering that objection); confidence (a number between 0 and 1 for your credence that the relocation thesis is false); and most_mind_changing_consideration (one sentence on what would move that number most).
