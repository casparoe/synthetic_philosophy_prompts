My friend and I spent a six-hour drive arguing about when several things add up to one thing, and we gave up somewhere in the second state. I gather this is what philosophers call the special composition question, which at least means other people have been annoyed by it before. I'm writing my verdicts down so you can tell me whether a single principle lies behind them or whether I'm improvising case by case.

What I actually think, going by gut:

Two bricks, one stacked on the other: no third thing exists. There are the bricks and nothing else.
A rock in my hand: one thing, no hesitation.
A pile of sand on the beach: nothing there but grains. This is where the fight started.
My cat, asleep: one thing.
My car, parked and switched off: one thing.
My bicycle taken apart and laid out in the garage, every part present and labelled: not a thing. The same bicycle assembled: one thing. I'll grant that the parts are in a tidier arrangement when it's disassembled than the sand grains are on the beach.
A candle flame: one thing, though its matter is being replaced constantly.
A whirlpool: not a thing. A hurricane: one thing. I cannot say what separates them except that one is hot and one is cold, which is obviously not the point.
A cloud: I flip-flop. Ask me twice and you'll get two answers.
A swarm of bees in flight: one thing. An ant colony: one thing. My friend says this is the sand pile with better publicity.
A coral reef: one thing. Same objection from my friend.
The western half of my cat: not a thing.
A statue and the bronze it was cast from: I want to say there are two things there, and I can't square that with anything else on this list.
The arrangement of the furniture in my living room: not a thing, even though I talk about my living room as though it were one.
Twins joined at the chest and sharing a heart: two people, and I can't tell you what the single body is, which worries me.
My left shoe and the Eiffel Tower, considered together as one candidate object: nothing. I feel this more strongly than anything else here.
A company or a country: I'll say one thing in one mood and a useful fiction in another, so set that aside if you must.

The two objections my friend kept pressing were these: the sand pile and the bee swarm look like the same kind of case, so either both are things or neither is; and if I'll allow the statue and the bronze to be two things in one place, I ought to allow the sand pile and the grains to be two things in one place, which I refuse to do.

My own attempt at a rule, which keeps collapsing: several things compose one thing when they are caught up in a single ongoing process that maintains them in their arrangement, and not merely when they touch or causally interact. That is meant to keep the cat, the flame, the swarm, the reef and the assembled bicycle, and to drop the pile, the bricks, the disassembled bicycle and the shoe-plus-Eiffel-Tower. It probably mishandles the rock and the statue, and I have no idea what it says about the cloud, a candle that has just been lit, or a swarm that is half settled.

What I want from you: the principle, or a small set of them, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself; the case that forces each clause; what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, say a virus, a river, a traffic jam, a person in a coma, the cells of my body turning over, two people carrying a piano, a puddle drying in the sun, a fire spreading through a forest, a queue, a country's economy, or a computation running on machines in three different countries; and a straight answer about which of my verdicts the principle cannot have.

Please don't survey who said what. I want the rule and its costs, not the pedigree, and I can't go look anything up. Tell me plainly which parts of this are settled and which are live disputes among people who know the topic; I would much rather hear that a question is contested than be handed one side dressed up as the consensus.
