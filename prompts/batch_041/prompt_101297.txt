I write a long-form column on science and ideas, mostly where decision theory, artificial intelligence and the rationalist subculture overlap, and for the past two years I have also sat as a trustee of a small charitable fund — a modest giving circle, five-figure grants, a committee of six that meets quarterly and signs off collectively. The two roles have collided in a way I did not expect. A surprising share of the proposals and pressure letters that reach the fund are built on decision-theoretic arguments of a single recurring shape, and I keep meeting that shape in my reporting as well: a correspondent who claims a predictor has already acted on the basis of what I would have chosen; a donor who frames a demand so that paying him would be evidence that I had not been singled out for misfortune, and refusing would be evidence that I had; a source who insists that my refusing now will be punished in a branch of reality I shall never observe. I understand the canonical cases well enough in the abstract — counterfactual mugging, where a perfect predictor flips a fair coin, asks me for a hundred pounds if it lands tails, and would have paid me ten thousand had it landed heads and had it predicted that I would pay; and the so-called XOR blackmail, where Omega tells me it has sent me a message if and only if my house is infested with termites or I pay it, the infestation being ruinous and the payment trivial, so that paying looks like evidence against the infestation rather than a way of preventing it. What I lack is anything I can actually run in a committee meeting, under time pressure, with a real person and real money in the room and no Omega that can be verified to exist.

Please turn whatever the theory genuinely knows about these cases into a procedure a lay trustee like me can follow. I want the questions asked in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it must hand the matter to a person because what remains is a question of value or of nerve rather than of fact. I would rather it kept the distinctions that carry weight than flattened them: whether I am choosing a single act or a policy that will be applied recurrently; whether the benefit of the cooperative answer falls on me or only on a counterpart self in a branch I shall never see (the difference between the all-upside and the mixed-upside cases the literature distinguishes); whether the reasoning on offer is causal, evidential, or of the functional sort that treats the predictor's model of me as the thing actually being chosen; and, above all, what follows when the predictor's existence and honesty cannot be checked at all, which I suspect is the ordinary situation and where I expect the procedure to collapse most cases. Then run it on three worked cases: the canonical counterfactual mugging; the termite message; and one ordinary case of your choosing in which the procedure returns an answer I shall not want — a promise or commitment I would rather wriggle out of, say — so that I can see what it costs to follow it.

Please write in British English, and be candid about the limits: tell me which steps are genuine inference and which are judgement dressed up as procedure, where the procedure is most likely to misfire on a case that superficially fits, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about the central verdict — in particular, what would have to be true of the predictor, or of the recurrence of the choice, for the paying answer to be the right one rather than a rationalisation. If you think no honest procedure can be given here, say so plainly and explain what someone in my position should do instead.
