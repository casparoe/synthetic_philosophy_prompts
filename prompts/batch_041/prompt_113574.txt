Your a game theorist and I'm the senior economist at the consultancy you work at, so treat this as a spec for something I'm actually going to make the juniors use — we do two or three of these a month and a bad one ends up in a board paper.

The recurring job: a client wants to know which of the other side's options they can stop worrying about — "surely they'd never do X" — so they can narrow down to a couple of scenarios before picking their own move. Right now the juniors do it by eye: they look for a strategy that's worse than some single other strategy the other side has, cross it out, look again in the smaller table, keep going until one cell is left, and read that cell out as the recommendation. Turn that into a written procedure, grounded in what game theory actually knows here rather than in what sounds plausible.

Format: a checklist in order, for reducing a two-player payoff table (up to six options a side) by eliminating options the other side will never play — including options that are only beaten by a *mixture* of their other options rather than by any one of them — and iterating the elimination until nothing more comes out. Constraints: runnable in under an hour by someone with no game theory training; every elimination gets a one-line reason in a log; our payoffs come out of a market model so they're estimates, and I want to know what that does to an elimination that only just clears the bar. Be blunt about which steps are safe and which are a gamble, in particular whether the order you cross things out in can change where you end up, and what you have to assume about the other side's rationality — and about us having listed all of their options — before any of it is licensed. Say what to do when the procedure bottoms out in a table with no single cell left, and mark clearly the points where a human has to decide rather than the procedure.

Then run it, with the paperwork, on two cases. Payoffs are (us, them) in £m.

Case 1 — rows are our options A, B, D; columns are theirs L, C, R:
A: L (0,0), C (3,3), R (2,1)
B: L (3,3), C (0,0), R (2,1)
D: L (1,1), C (1,1), R (2,1)

Case 2 — rows are our options X, Y; columns are theirs P, Q:
X: P (1,1), Q (0,0)
Y: P (1,0), Q (1,1)

For each one give me the elimination log, the recommendation, and how you'd break it to a client whose pet option is the one that gets crossed out — in Case 1 that's option D, they're keenest on it.

Finish with a short section on where the procedure misfires, concrete enough that a junior would notice it happening mid-job. Dont pad it with hedging — I'd rather have three sharp failure modes than a tidy procedure that pretends the theory says more than it does. Take a minute to think through the awkward bits before you write.
