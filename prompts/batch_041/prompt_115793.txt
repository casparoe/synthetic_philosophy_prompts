I did my undergrad in physics, and my advisor — who'd drifted into philosophy of science late in her career — used to hold forth over terrible office coffee about which of the things physics says exist are actually real and which are just bookkeeping. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: phlogiston, not real; caloric, not real; the luminiferous ether, not real (she was adamant it was never anything but a placeholder, even granting that a lot of the equations born of ether theories survived); the planet Vulcan that Le Verrier put inside Mercury's orbit, not real; atoms and molecules, real; electrons, real; neutrinos, real — and she insisted she'd have said so even back when they were pure inference from energy bookkeeping. She also had two habits of mind she never reconciled for me: she trusted whatever survives a theory change, and she distrusted anything you can't push on. I could never get her to say which of those was doing the actual work.

Now a younger colleague of mine is writing about whether the quantum wave function — the state vector, whatever you want to call it — is a real physical thing or just a device for churning out probabilities. He thinks it's real. I want to guess what my old advisor would have said, and I definately want to know which rule she was really running. The candidates as I see them: (a) entity realism — a posit is real iff you can causally intervene on it; (b) structural realism — you're entitled to believe in the structure that survives theory change, and agnostic about the entities themselves; (c) straight no-miracles realism — believe whatever the best current theory needs to explain the phenomena; and (d) my own hobbyhorse, that a posit is real iff taking it out changes what we can *do* to the world, not just what we can *say* about it.

Tell me which of these fit her verdicts, what each predicts for the wave function, and which further case would actually force the issue between them. Lay it out as: a one-line verdict to open; then "The rules that fit her," one paragraph; then "What each rule says about the wave function," a few sentences per rule; then "My best guess for the advisor," a short paragraph; and finish with "The case that would settle it," two or three sentences.
