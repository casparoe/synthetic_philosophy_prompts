Explain how Parfit's hitchhiker scenario is supposed to bear on the question whether the Self-interest Theory is indirectly self-defeating, and then evaluate the reply that the theory escapes self-defeat because a rational agent can arrange to have the disposition to pay. In doing so, distinguish (a) the rationality of paying once one is safe, (b) the rationality of taking steps beforehand to become the sort of person who will pay, and (c) the rationality of the driver's confidence that one is such a person. Then consider whether the scenario supports the broader thesis that it can be rational to cause oneself to act irrationally, or whether that thesis trades on an equivocation between the rationality of particular acts and the rationality of long-term policies or character traits. Finally, say whether the hitchhiker case reveals a genuine defect in the Self-interest Theory or merely shows that the theory must be applied indirectly, and explain what turns on that distinction.
