Six months ago I started a small independent newsletter covering local politics, and twice since then I have watched the saying that the crowd is untruth do real rhetorical work. The first time, a friend offered it as consolation after a coordinated pile-on in my comments section; the second, a columnist invoked it to argue that a petition signed by several thousand residents should count for nothing, since a mass of signatories is not a set of individual judgments. I have also been reading Kierkegaard on the press and the public, where the phrase keeps surfacing next to his name, and I genuinely cannot tell whether it summarizes his position, states something he would have qualified into oblivion, or has simply absorbed his authority without earning it.

What I want is a menu of readings with verdicts attached, not a survey that leaves the choice to me. Set out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making. Does it assert that a crowd cannot have a belief at all, so the question of its truth or falsity does not arise? That crowds are unreliable truth-trackers even when they do believe things? That membership dissolves individual answerability, so no one in a crowd is accountable for what the crowd asserts? That the public is a phantom manufactured by the press, so appeals to public opinion appeal to nothing? Or something narrower about the particular press culture that provoked it? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and say which reading each of my two interlocutors needed in order to get the conclusion they wanted.

Then commit: tell me which reading is strongest, whether either use I described is illegitimate, and why.
