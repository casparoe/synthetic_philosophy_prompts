I run a small maintenance company and I do all my own books, and I have never studied philosophy. What I actually know is cash flow, working capital, opportunity cost, compounding, depreciation, maintenance, and the difference between an asset's cost and what it returns. Lately I have been reading Keynes out of curiosity — the 1930 essay about the economic possibilities for our grandchildren, and a later memoir in which he looks back on what he and his Cambridge friends believed as young men under the influence of G. E. Moore. I would like Keynes's picture of the good life restated in the only vocabulary I genuinely trust, the one I use for my books: budgets and binding constraints, income versus capital, returns that compound, spending that is really an arms race against my neighbours, diminishing marginal benefit and the point of satiation, upkeep and depreciation, risk, and the distinction between a flow I can spend and a stock I have to protect. Do the translation as faithfully as you can, and do not quietly assume that a good state of mind is just a means to some later payoff — that is precisely the assumption I suspect my vocabulary smuggles in without noticing.

Then give me the residue report. As I read him, Keynes holds something like the following: what finally matters are states of mind, chiefly one's own and only secondarily other people's; these are valued independently of action, achievement, and consequence, and are in some sense not worn down by time; wanting money as a possession rather than as a means is a disease rather than a miscalculation; once the absolute needs are met — the ones we feel regardless of how our neighbours are doing — the binding constraint on a good life stops being resources and becomes taste and character; and the conventions that hold a civilisation together are a fragile crust, easily broken, which cannot be justified from inside the logic of getting and spending. Tell me which of those survive translation into my ledger vocabulary cleanly, which survive only as a rough approximation that loses something, and which cannot be said in it at all. As a test, run one concrete case through both vocabularies: a man who has more than enough, keeps working anyway to enlarge a pile he will never spend, and says he does it because he likes watching the number go up. Say what my books say about him and what Keynes says about him, and where the two accounts stop being comparable at all.

For every failure of translation, I want your honest verdict on whether the gap is a defect in my framework — something an accountant's way of thinking simply cannot represent — or a sign that Keynes's claim was vaguer than it sounded and looked deep only because nothing had been pinned down. I would rather be told that the task is badly posed, or that my reading of Keynes is a common misreading, than be handed a tidy table that flatters the translation. Please also mark clearly which parts of the above are Keynes and which are my own paraphrase, since I am working from a quick first reading and my memory of it.
