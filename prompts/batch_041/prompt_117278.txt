I work at a mid-sized hospital, and I have recently been placed on the staff committee that reviews both our employee benefits and our patient-facing programs, so a puzzle I used to find merely irritating now determines how we spend money and how we treat people. Here is the pattern I keep running into. Last year the hospital enrolled every employee in the retirement plan by default, at a fixed contribution rate, unless they actively opted out; almost nobody opted out, and the committee now cites that as evidence that employees want exactly this plan. I was one of the people who did not opt out, and when I said I would have preferred a different arrangement, I was told that my behavior, not my stated opinion, is the trustworthy evidence of what I want. Something similar happened with a small fee we introduced for missed clinic appointments: the fall in missed appointments is cited at once as proof that the fee works and as proof that patients never really valued the appointments to begin with. What troubles me is not the familiar thought that circumstances shape what people want. It is the loop. The arrangement produces the choices, the choices justify the arrangement, and the side that favors the arrangement always has a fact on its side, while the side that opposes it has only a guess about what people would have chosen under some other arrangement. Asking people directly does not seem to break the loop either, since the asking is itself an arrangement.

Is there a name for this? I would want to know which existing ideas about wants, choices, and evidence come closest, where each of them falls short of what I have described, and whether the loop is a distinct problem or only a loose version of something familiar. If nothing fits, I would rather be told so and given a term with a definition and some boundary cases than handed a near-miss.
