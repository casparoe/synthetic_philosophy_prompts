I'm a compiler engineer by training — static analysis and build systems, mostly — and over the past year I've drifted into reading epistemology on my own, largely because a long-running argument at work about whether circular module dependencies are ever acceptable kept mutating into an argument about whether circular justifications are ever acceptable. I've now got a reasonable grip on the standard taxonomy of responses to the epistemic regress: foundationalism, coherentism, and the infinitist option that says an infinite chain of reasons is not merely tolerable but required, together with the surrounding disputes over whether the regress is vicious at all, whether it concerns propositional or doxastic justification, and whether the demand for a further reason is legitimate at every step or may be refused at some point. What I want from you is a systematic translation exercise. Take the role of someone who genuinely works at the intersection of epistemology and programming-languages theory — a philosopher who can read a termination proof and an Agda guardedness error — and restate each of the three positions, plus the sub-variants where they come apart (modest versus strong foundationalism; linear versus holistic coherentism; any infinitism worth its name), as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in: dependency graphs and acyclicity, inductive data types with base cases and well-founded recursion, mutually recursive definitions and strongly connected components, coinductive types and the productivity condition on infinite streams, termination and totality checking in proof assistants and the fact that the checker simply refuses a definition that fails it, memoization and lazy evaluation, and the difference between a cycle that is merely detected and one that is domesticated by a fixed-point semantics. Assume I'm fluent in all of that and will notice if you use it loosely. Be scrupulous about not letting the engineering vocabulary do the philosophical work for you: if you catch yourself writing that a circular justification "is fine because a fixed point exists", mark that as a translation artefact rather than a result.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the main deliverable rather than an appendix. Which claims survive translation intact? Which survive only after distortion, and what precisely gets distorted in the process? And which have no counterpart at all — for instance, whatever there is to the basing relation beyond a directed edge, or whatever distinguishes a reason that supports a belief from a premise that a program depends on, or the difference between a chain of reasons that exists and a chain an agent could actually traverse. I suspect my framework has specific blind spots, and I'd like you to test three of them: (i) justified false belief and defeasible support, since nothing in a dependency graph is defeasible — an edge either holds or it doesn't, and there is no such thing as an edge that holds until something else comes along; (ii) the normative character of the regress argument, since my vocabulary is descriptive and can say what a system does but not what an agent ought to accept; and (iii) the fact that a proof assistant's totality requirement makes it foundationalist by fiat, which I take to be a design decision rather than an argument. For each residue item, tell me whether it exposes a genuine gap in the engineering vocabulary — something a better formal framework would capture — or whether it is a sign that the philosophical claim was doing less work than it appeared to, or was trading on an ambiguity that the translation usefully exposes.
