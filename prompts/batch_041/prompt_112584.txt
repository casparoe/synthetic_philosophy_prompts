I'm an undergraduate research assistant in a consciousness lab and I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of mind. My PI wants us to adopt heterophenomenology as the lab's standard method for collecting and interpreting first-person reports, and she has asked me to draft a protocol we can actually run. We conduct about ten 30-minute sessions a week with untrained participants; the reports feed into a paper and a grant renewal, so mistakes are costly, but I cannot change the IRB-approved script or the session length. Please write a reusable procedure: the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to the PI because the question is interpretive or evaluative rather than factual, and what the procedure cannot settle at all. Ground it in what heterophenomenology actually says—bracketing the reality of the reported experiences, treating reports as building a heterophenomenological world, using the intentional stance, and not confusing the subject's reports with third-person evidence about mechanism—but keep it usable by a student who has read the theory once. Then run it on three cases: (1) a participant reports a red afterimage that is more real than the screen and insists it persists after the stimulus is gone; (2) a participant in a masked-priming task says she just guessed but her accuracy is far above chance; (3) a participant says the experimenter made him press the button and that he felt no agency, which I want to use as evidence of unconscious volition. For each case, say what the procedure licenses, what it forbids, and where I have to stop and ask the PI. Include one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like. Finally, give me a table with columns Step, Action, Sufficient answer, Stop condition, Judgment call, and a second table for the cases with columns Case, Heterophenomenological world, What the reports license, What they do not license, Decision, Residual judgment. Do not give me citations or a reading list.
