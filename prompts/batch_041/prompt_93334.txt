Next month I am presenting a paper to a statistical-mechanics reading group, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion that follows. The thesis I am defending is this: the standard exorcism of Maxwell's demon — the one that runs through Landauer's principle and concludes that the demon must pay an entropy cost to erase its memory — conflates two debts that ought to be kept apart. The first is the entropy the demon must export to its surroundings in order to acquire information about a molecule; the second is the cost of resetting its own record. Because the standard account runs these together, it proves more than it is entitled to. It purports to show that no intelligent being can ever lower the entropy of a gas, but what the argument actually establishes is only that no being can lower entropy while also forgetting. I then argue that a demon whose memory simply grows and is never reset still cannot win, but for a different reason than the usual one: what defeats it is not the erasure but the correlation that accumulates between its ever-longer record and the gas it has been watching, and that correlation — not the reset — is where the free lunch is lost. I want to be ready for the objections this specific room will raise.

Here is the room. Roughly a dozen statistical mechanicians who regard Landauer's principle as a settled theorem and who are weary of hearing that information is physical treated as a slogan to be questioned. Two members who work on quantum thermodynamics and can recite the Szilard engine, the Jarzynski equality, and the Crooks relation from memory, and who will want me to say where the demon sits in a fluctuation-theorem accounting. And one philosopher of physics who is professionally suspicious of information doing any physical work and will press me on whether entropy is a state function or an epistemic quantity. Do not give me the objections I would get from a philosophy-of-mind audience, from a quantum-foundations crowd, or from the popular-science internet; give me the ones this room will actually raise, in their vocabulary.

Deliver the prep sheet as a single table with exactly these columns, one row per objection, with about eight rows:

- Rank: integer, 1 for the objection that does the most expected damage to my case (likelihood times severity), ascending.
- Objector: which segment of the room raises it (e.g. "the thermodynamicists", "the quantum-thermo pair", "the philosopher of physics").
- Objection: the objection phrased the way that person would actually put it in the discussion, using their idiom, in no more than three sentences.
- Why this room raises it: the commitment, background, or prior result that generates the objection from their side.
- Likelihood: High, Medium, or Low.
- Damage to my case: High, Medium, or Low.
- Concede or Hold: whether I should grant the point, hold firm, or split it, and in one clause why.
- Two-minute reply: what I actually say, written as speech I could deliver, not as notes.
- Where the reply is soft: the specific spot where a sharp interlocutor will push next, and the honest weakness.

After the table, add one short paragraph under the heading "The question I am hoping nobody asks," phrased the way the sharpest person in the room would put it, followed by your blunt assessment of whether I have a real answer to it.
