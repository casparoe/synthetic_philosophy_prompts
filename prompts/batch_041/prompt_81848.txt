I run a small "civic experiments" unit inside the planning department of a mid-sized city — basically we're the people who get handed the proposals nobody else wants to touch: a supervised consumption site, a guaranteed-income pilot, closing a downtown street to cars, renaming a street, letting sixteen-year-olds vote in school-board elections. Right now the process is a mess. We either talk for eight months and produce a report nobody acts on, or we get pressured into a straight up-or-down vote that just ratifies whatever the loudest side wanted, and then everyone calls it 'democracy.' A collegue keeps telling me I should read Dewey — that for him democracy isn't mainly a matter of voting but a way of living together in which people jointly inquire into their shared problems, and that inquiry is itself experimental: you don't start from a settled question and pick the answer, you start from a situation that has gone indeterminate and you work to make it determinate again, treating your ideas as plans of action you then test against how the world pushes back, and treating what you end up 'knowing' as a claim you're warranted in asserting only for as long as it survives that testing. That sounds right to me but I cant hand my staff a book.

So: you're a philosopher of social inquiry who has also had to make this stuff work inside a bureaucracy, and I want you to write me a decision procedure my team can actually run on a proposal — a checklist, in maybe ten steps. Think it through carefully before you commit to the order of the questions, because the order is the whole game: a step that comes too early turns everything after it into a formality. For each step, say what a sufficient answer looks like, and say which steps are genuinely factual and which are value questions dressed up as procedure — so we know when to stop and hand it to a person with the authority to decide, and who that person should be. Where does the procedure say no, and where does it say not yet, and here's the experiment to run first? I also want it to do something our current process can't: whenever we decide to run a pilot, it should force us to write down, before we start, the conditions under which we'd call the pilot finished or abandoned, and who is carrying the risk if it goes wrong — because with social experiments the people who bear the cost are rarely the people who asked for the experiment.

Then actually run the procedure, in full, on two of those proposals, and make one of them the hard one — the renaming, or something like it, where there's no experiment to run and the whole thing is about who we are together rather than about what works. If the procedure tells me that case has to be handled differently from the street-closure case, say so plainly, even if it means my nice uniform flowchart falls apart, and tell me what the procedure still can't do at all. Don't give me a survey of what Dewey said with a procedure bolted on the end; I need the procedure first, and the theory only where it's actually load-bearing. And please take a definite position — tell me where you think the line really falls between "we need more inquiry" and "this is a matter of conviction that inquiry won't settle" — rather than telling me it depends.
