Bit of an odd request, I think. I retired from a biology department a few years back — entomologist by training, spent most of my career on social insects — and I've been trying to teach myself alot of philosophy in the evenings, working through Singer and Regan and some of the newer stuff on wild animal suffering. Last month my granddaughter got me reading Charlotte's Web out loud to her at bedtime, and it's been stuck in my head ever since. I want to write something for our local philosophy meetup about it, and I'd like your help thinking it through.

So: read the book through the lens of animal ethics and speciesism, and don't be polite about it. What bothers me is how Wilbur gets saved. Fern spares him as the runt because she can't stand the idea of the axe, fair enough, but that's a child's sympathy for one animal she happens to know. Then Charlotte saves him a second time, and her method is to turn him into an attraction — words in the web, crowds coming to the barn, a prize at the fair. He lives because he becomes worth more alive than as pork. Isn't that the speciesist logic in its purest form, an animal earning its life by being exceptional, useful, or famous? And isn't the book quietly telling kids that the ordinary pigs, the ones without a clever spider working for them, are on their own?

Second thing. Charlotte is a predator. She eats flies, and the book adores her and never once mourns the flies. Meanwhile Wilbur's terror of the smokehouse is the emotional center of the whole thing. Is there any consistent rule in the book about whose death counts, or does it just track cuteness and how close an animal sits to human beings? After the reading I've been doing on wild animal suffering I find that pretty glaring.

Third, the one I keep chewing on: Fern seems to grow out of it. She stops paying attention to the animals as she gets older, and by the fair she's mostly interested in other things, and the grown-ups around her treat the whole business as a phase she'll get over. Is that the novel's actual position — that caring about animals is something for children? Or does the ending push back against that?

Which scenes would you point me to for each of these? And at the end I'd like you to say what the book gets right that the arguments don't — I suspect there's something in it about what it's actually like to live among animals, day in and day out, that a philosopher at a desk misses. Take it seriously as a work of art, though. Don't just turn it into a fable with a moral I already believe.
