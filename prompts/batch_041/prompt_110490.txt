I'm in my third year and taking this consciousness science course that's half cogsci, half philosophy, and something's been bugging me that I want to think through properly for my essay.

So in our lab we do no-report paradigms — binocular rivalry where we track pupil size and eye movements instead of asking the subject anything, masking with optokinetic nystagmus, that kind of setup. The whole rationale we've been taught is that asking someone to press a button contaminates the thing you're measuring: the report drags in attention, decision-making, memory, motor stuff, none of which is the consciousness you're supposedly trying to isolate. So in the lab, report is treated as a confound, an impurity to be subtracted out.

But two floors down, in the hospital our lab is attached to, the diagnosis of disorders of consciousness runs completely the other way. A patient counts as conscious when they can follow a command, or when a scanner catches them doing something command-like when they can't move at all. There, a report (or a report-shaped response, or even just the ability to produce one) is the gold standard, not a contaminant. Same building, opposite instincts about what a report even is.

And then in lecture, global workspace theory gets taught almost as if it settles the access/phenomenal question by collapsing it: conscious just means globally broadcast, available to the rest of the system. But our reading list also has the Block-style line about phenomenal overflow — that experience outruns what we can access or report — and a guest lecture on illusionism that said the whole distinction is a confusion we should drop.

What I want from you: treat these three things as one package — the no-report lab methodology, the command-following/covert-detection clinical practice, and the way GWT is used in the course to talk about "access" — and tell me what philosophical commitments the package presupposes. Specifically:

- What view of the relation between access and phenomenal consciousness does it need? Is access presupposed as necessary for phenomenal consciousness, as sufficient for it, as neither (just a handy proxy), or does the practice need no view at all and only look like it does?
- Which rival positions does the package quietly rule out by operating the way it does — overflow views, higher-order views, illusionism, panpsychist or recurrent-processing alternatives? I want to know which ones are actually excluded versus which ones the practice is just inconvenient for.
- The part I really can't resolve: is the lab-versus-clinic tension a genuine inconsistency, or does it dissolve once you notice the two are doing different jobs (measuring a correlate vs. assigning a status that matters morally and legally)? If it dissolves, say exactly what assumption does the dissolving. If it doesn't, tell me where the contradiction is.
- Related thing my TA said that I'm not sure about: even no-report paradigms sneak a report back in, because at some point you need the subject to tell you what the rivalry was doing, or you have to assume that the eye movements or pupil responses mean what you say they mean. Is that a real circularity, and does the methodology presuppose something about the link between report and access that it can't help presupposing?
- Tell me where the package is philosophically underdetermined — i.e., where several different theories would license exactly the same lab and clinical behaviour, so the practice can't tell us which one is true.

Last thing, and I know this is a stretch: if you swapped in a phenomenal-overflow view, or went full illusionist, what concretely would have to change — in the lab, at the bedside, in how the course talks? I don't want a reading list or who-said-what, I just want the presuppositions laid out and the consequences traced. Also if my framing is confused somewhere, say so, cause I might be presupposing something myself without noticing.
