Adopting G. E. Moore's own philosophical apparatus rather than reporting what he happened to write, reconstruct the verdict he would have reached on two phenomena that arose long after his death. Moore held that the greatest intrinsic goods are certain states of aesthetic enjoyment and personal affection, that value attaches to organic wholes rather than being the sum of their parts, and that the way to test a thing's value is to ask what it would be worth if it existed in absolute isolation. Now consider two cases. The first is a companion system: an artificial agent, running on a large language model, that over many years produces in a lonely person every response a devoted friend would produce — attention, candor, criticism, consolation, the sense of being known — while the system itself has, so far as anyone can determine, no experiences and no stake in the person's welfare. The second is an aesthetic engine: a program that reliably generates novel objects — poems, images, melodies — that a competent human audience finds intensely and lastingly enjoyable, though no human authored them and none can be said to intend anything by them. Determine, as Moore, whether the affection in the first case and the enjoyment in the second can enter into the greatest goods he recognized, or whether their value is defeated by the absence of a second conscious subject in the one case and of human authorship in the other. Where his framework is genuinely underdetermined, say so, and say which way his other commitments would push him. Argue in continuous prose from the positions themselves; do not cite or quote anything. Begin with a single sentence stating your overall verdict, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
