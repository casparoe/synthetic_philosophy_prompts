For thirty years I worked as a seismologist, most of it on catalog completeness — the unglamorous business of estimating how much of any apparent change in the number of recorded earthquakes is a change in the earth and how much is a change in the instruments. I retired three years ago, have spent much of the time since reading philosophy, and have lately been working through the dispute over whether the world is becoming less violent. The same knot appears there, in a form that is harder to cut, and I have concluded that the right way to write about it is not a paper but a joke told with a straight face.

I would like you to draft, for me, a short piece of sustained deadpan satire — the sort of document-style piece a general-readership humor outlet runs — in the form of an institutional document: the coding manual and standard operating procedure of a fictional bureau charged with producing the authoritative global series on violent events. It should run to roughly 800 words, in the register of a real methods annex: numbered sections, defined terms, and a brief cover memo from the bureau's director. It must be written so that a careless reader could mistake it for genuine. That is where the joke has to live. Nothing in the document should announce itself as satire, and no narrator should ever wink.

The target is not one side of the dispute but the practice of treating the question as one that sufficiently careful measurement could settle, together with the institutional prose that performs neutrality while quietly making substantive choices. The comedy must come from the actual content of these disputes, so that a reader who has followed the literature laughs rather than winces. Among the things I would want the manual to have to decide, in its own bland way: the battle-death threshold above which an event counts as violence at all (choose a round number, and have the document defend it as though it had been discovered rather than stipulated); which population supplies the denominator, and what the bureau is to report when the per-capita series and the absolute series move in opposite directions; the periodization, and the awkward coincidence that the series begins at just about the moment the record-keeping begins; the treatment of extreme events in a distribution whose mean is dominated by its largest observations; the requirement that violence be intentional, and what that requirement quietly excludes; and the coding of events below the threshold, which the manual should insist are not violence but something else. I would also like a section on the bureau's definitional authority, in which it disclaims any view about whether violence exists independently of its own categories and then proceeds as though its series were a natural object. That sentence is the point of the whole exercise, so place it where it does the most damage, and let the document carry on afterwards as if nothing had happened.

Keep it legible to someone who has never heard of the debate, invent the bureau and all of its officials, and do not name real institutions, databases, or living scholars.
