Alright, I'm a backend engineer, never taken a philosophy class, and my buddy and I have been arguing about many-worlds for a week and I'm stuck. Need help.

Quick background so we're on the same page: many-worlds is the reading of quantum mechanics where the wavefunction never collapses -- when you measure something in a superposition, every outcome actually happens, just in different branches, and you end up as a copy of yourself in each. Cool, fine.

Here's the argument we built. I can't find the hole and it's driving me nuts.

(1) In many-worlds, when I measure a qubit, both outcomes happen: I split into two copies, one that sees up and one that sees down.
(2) So when I ask "what's the probability I'll see up?", the only thing that can possibly mean is: the fraction of my future copies that see up. There's no other fact of the matter -- a copy of me sees each outcome.
(3) A two-outcome measurement makes two copies, one per outcome. So that fraction is 1/2.
(4) So many-worlds says every two-outcome measurement is a fair coin.
(5) But the actual rule in QM (the Born rule) says the probability is the amplitude squared. That's only 1/2 for a balanced superposition. For a state like sqrt(1/4) up + sqrt(3/4) down, QM says 25/75.
(6) And 25/75 is exactly what the lab spits out.
(7) So many-worlds predicts the wrong thing and has to staple the Born rule on as a separate axiom. Which means it doesn't actually explain where probabilities come from.

My buddy's fix is that step (3) is wrong: not all branches are equal, some are "thicker." But that smells like cheating to me. If a branch is thicker, what is thickness? If it's probability, we've gone in a circle. If it isn't, then why should I, standing here about to measure, care about thickness? So I'm inclined to keep (2). I definately can't give up (6), and I'd rather not give up (1).

So: which step do I give up? Then tell me what that step was quietly doing for the argument, and what I pay by dropping it. Concretely, if I drop the copy-counting idea, whats left that's supposed to tell me what my probabilities even are -- or does my buddy basically win either way, just with extra steps?

One more thing that nags me and might be a red herring: in a real measurement I don't think there's even a clean number of branches.
