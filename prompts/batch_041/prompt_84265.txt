You are a philosopher of statistics who has spent a career on direct inference and the foundations of evidence, and who is also known among colleagues as the most useful person to ask before a hostile question period: you can hear a room, you know which objections are load-bearing and which are reflexive, and you can say what to concede without giving away the paper. I am giving a colloquium next month, and I need you to prepare me for the questions.

The room. A joint meeting of a statistics department's theory seminar and the epistemology reading group, about twenty people. Most are applied statisticians and biostatisticians who work on post-selection inference, preregistration, and reproducibility; their standing suspicion is that any philosophical worry about "the right reference class" is a dressed-up version of the garden of forking paths, and that anyone who worries about it has not heard of sample splitting. Three are Bayesians of the de Finetti stripe who are courteous but regard reference-class talk as a frequentist inheritance they thought they had left behind. One is a philosopher of science who knows the direct-inference literature cold and will want to know whether my constraint is synchronic or diachronic, and whether it governs credences or only assertions. One is a doctoral student in algorithmic fairness who audits risk-scoring instruments and who enters from the group-definition end, where the choice of reference class is openly political. Nobody in the room has read the material I am working from. I have forty-five minutes plus twenty minutes of questions; assume about two minutes per reply.

What I am defending. The reference class problem is standardly posed as a question about which class is objectively the right one for a given individual. Posed that way it is insoluble, which is why the usual conclusion is that direct inference is incoherent and that every reference-class choice is ultimately arbitrary. I claim this is the wrong framing. The problem is not about correctness but about entitlement, that is, about which classes an agent may use. I propose a single constraint, which I call ex ante availability: a reference class may be used to fix the probability of an outcome for an individual only if the agent could have specified the class and its associated statistics before learning the outcome in the case at hand. I then argue three things: that this constraint is what is really doing the work in the standard objections to frequentist direct inference; that it survives in Bayesian dress, because exchangeability does not by itself tell you which partition to be exchangeable over; and that the familiar law cases about naked statistical evidence and the fairness-auditing cases are the same problem in different clothes. I will insist throughout that the constraint is not merely a demand for preregistration.

What I want. A prep sheet, not a defense. Begin with a scratch list, to be discarded, of what each faction in this room treats as a decisive objection, and then give me the sheet itself:

- the eight to ten objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumbled them, each phrased the way the person who raises it would phrase it, with a line identifying who that is;
- for each, a two-minute reply in my voice, followed by an honest note on where the reply is soft or where I am bluffing;
- an opening concession or two that I should make in the first five minutes, and what I should hold back;
- the single objection that decides whether the talk succeeds, and what happens if I lose it;
- the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Two objections I expect to be in there, and want handled well: one built on the charge that I have simply rediscovered the old idea that the right class is the narrowest one the agent has frequency information about, so that my constraint only makes credences hostage to the agent's ignorance; and one from the fairness side, that a purely procedural constraint on entitlement will always ratify whichever classification the powerful already use, so that my view is politically inert exactly where it matters.

Write it for someone who has to answer out loud, in real time, to people who will not be persuaded by anything I cannot state in three sentences. Do not give me citations, a reading list, or instructions to consult the literature on something; nothing I say can depend on the audience having read anything. Where an objection has a standard name, use it, but assume no one is impressed by the name.
