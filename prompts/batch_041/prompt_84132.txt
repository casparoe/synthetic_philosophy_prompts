For a seminar on algorithmic fairness I've been asked to circulate my position so it can be stress-tested before I present. Here is where I've landed. (1) Fairness is a property of outcomes rather than of procedures: a model that never looks at a protected attribute can still produce systematically unfair decisions. (2) For a group that has suffered historical disadvantage, the right target is demographic parity — equal selection rates — not merely parity in error rates. (3) I accept the standard impossibility results (within-group calibration and equalized odds cannot both hold once base rates differ), so I take fairness to be a matter of choosing a criterion for the context rather than satisfying all of them at once. (4) A decision rule must nonetheless stay accurate and calibrated; a fairness fix that destroys predictive validity is no fix at all. (5) I think the deeper question is which counterfactual baseline a decision is being compared against — the distribution we would have seen absent the historical wrong — and that the impossibility results are downstream of our refusal to fix that baseline. (6) I hold that fairness constrains the system, not each individual decision: a single decision can be defensible even when the system that produced it is not.

I don't know whether these hang together and I would rather find out now. Tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each real tension is; then say what would have to give in each case and which single revision would resolve the most. Order the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and do not tell me that the set is simply an interesting mix of views. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" naming the one commitment that should be revised, or "Verdict: consistent" if none should.
