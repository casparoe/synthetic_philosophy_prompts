I have recently been seconded to chair a working group at our national AI evaluation institute, and the timing is awkward. The oversight standard our legislature passed this spring requires that any system deployed in critical-infrastructure planning carry a cognitive-grade label, and the label determines how much human-in-the-loop supervision is mandatory. The drafters, without much explanation, borrowed Daniel Dennett's ladder from Kinds of Minds, so we are now stuck with four rungs: Darwinian creatures, whose behavior is fixed by selection and can be updated only across generations; Skinnerian creatures, which adjust within a lifetime by trial and error and by the consequences those trials actually bring; Popperian creatures, which can model a situation internally and try out candidate actions there, so that the hypothesis is discarded rather than the organism; and Gregorian creatures, which are Popperian plus the thinking tools — notation, language, arithmetic, checklists — that they inherit from a culture and can install in themselves. I studied philosophy before moving into evaluation, and I have an uncomfortable suspicion that we are about to turn a genuinely interesting distinction into a compliance ritual. My working group has to convert it into something a junior evaluator can apply in a week with only black-box API access and a modest compute budget, and I would rather we design it deliberately than improvise it in Thursday's meeting.

Please write the protocol we should adopt. It should be a set of questions or tests in order, with a clear statement of what evidence counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates with a definite grade, and where it must hand the judgment to a person because the question is about acceptable risk, institutional values, or the interpretation of a borderline case. It must handle the cases we already know will break a naive version: a system whose fluent chain-of-thought narrative is generated after the answer is already determined and merely rationalizes it; a system that is a composite of a small internal model and external tools, or of a model and a human operator, so that we cannot tell whether the grade belongs to the model, the assembly, or the operator; and a system whose internal simulation is itself just a learned policy, so that the behavior looks Popperian while the mechanism is closer to Skinnerian. State explicitly what the protocol cannot do — I suspect it cannot settle whether a system merely behaves as though it were trying out hypotheses internally, and I want that limitation stated in the document rather than buried in a footnote. Constraints: each evaluation should take an evaluator two days or less; we can probe only through the public interface plus whatever logs the developer volunteers; and the final grade has to be signed off by me and countersigned by our legal officer, so the protocol should make clear which conclusions are the evaluator's to reach and which are mine.

Then run the protocol on three systems and show your work. First, a model-free reinforcement-learning agent trained with reward shaping to schedule maintenance on a power grid. Second, a large language model agent that writes out a plan before acting and has retrieval over maintenance manuals. Third, a hybrid that carries a learned world model and searches over it before committing to an action. For each, give the grade the protocol yields, the evidence that drove it, and the strongest reason someone would grade it one rung higher. I should warn you that I expect the procedure to give an answer I will not like: on the most defensible reading, the second system may come out Skinnerian, since whatever looks like internal deliberation may be a disposition shaped by training on human text rather than a search over its own model of this particular situation, and that verdict will be unpopular with the developers and possibly with my own director. Do not soften it to spare us. Finally, say which of the three grades would change if the system were allowed to write notes to a scratchpad and re-read them, and whether that change is a genuine move up the ladder or a bookkeeping artifact.
