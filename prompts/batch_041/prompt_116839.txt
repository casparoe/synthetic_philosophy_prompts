I teach a 90-minute session on Islamic occasionalism to a 14-person upper-level undergraduate seminar — philosophy and religious studies students who have read Hume on causation but know no Arabic and have not encountered Ashʿarite theology before. I want a runnable activity, not a lecture. The target is the pair of claims that al-Ghazālī's occasionalism runs together: (i) nothing in the world has a power to produce anything in anything else, so the connection between what we call cause and what we call effect is only a habitual regularity — ʿāda, God's custom — which reason cannot discover to be necessary and which God can interrupt at will; and (ii) God is nonetheless the sole agent, so that even when I act, the act is created by God and only acquired (kasb) by me, which is meant to preserve both the settled order of the world and my responsibility for what I do.

Design the activity so that students feel (i) from the inside. They should build up expectations, be rewarded for relying on them, and then discover that nothing in the game ever guaranteed the regularity they had come to treat as a law — and, if you can manage it, that what kept the regularity going was a choice rather than a mechanism. Let (ii) then land as the twist, so that they have to ask whether their own moves were ever really theirs. Give me roles or rules, materials, a scoring scheme that needs no money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the two claims and explains why al-Ghazālī needs (ii) once (i) is granted. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself — including any place where the design leans on a contested reading of al-Ghazālī. Commit to one design and one set of rules, with your reasons, rather than laying out alternatives for me to choose between.
