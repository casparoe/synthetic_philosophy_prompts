Im a novelist, about 60 pages into a book set in 1963, and the whole plot hinges on a young researcher choosing the wrong lab. So Ive invented four rival research programmes for her to choose between. The field is how the brain stores memory and I've kept the biology vague on purpose — what I care about is which of these four a smart person in 1963 should have bet her career on. Here they are, more or less as they sit in my notes:

The molecular trace programme. Core idea: a memory just is a particular molecule, or a particular tweak to one, sitting in the cells where the memory lives. Very bold, very loud. For twelve years they've been predicting you can train one animal, pull the stuff out of its brain, inject it into a naive animal, and the second one will remember what it never learned. My characters get the replication to work maybe half the time. Every failure gets a new patch: wrong dose, wrong fraction, species mismatch, the receiving animal has to be primed first. Forty-odd papers in, the belt has gotten very thick.

The circuit programme. Core idea: memory is a change in the strength of particular connections between particular cells. Older, quieter, less quotable. Its bets have mostly paid off — it said lesions in a certain structure should wreck one kind of learning and leave another untouched, and that's what people find. Right now it's staking a lot on a prediction about a structure nobody has lesioned yet.

The distributed field programme. Core idea: memory belongs to the whole sheet of cortex, not to cells or molecules, so localizing it is a category error. Dominant in the 40s, then went quiet; for about a decade it mostly absorbed bad news by quietly redefining what counts as a memory. But last year one of its younger people reformulated it so that it now predicts a dissociation the circuit programme says is impossible. Nothing has been tested yet. Could be a real re-energizing, could be a face-lift.

The gate programme. Newest, three years old. Precise core, and it derives a pile of the old awkward findings everyone had been ignoring, in one go. It also derives one thing the circuit programme can't get at all — though that thing was already known before the gate programme existed. Nothing new tested yet either.

My own grasp of Lakatos is a cartoon, I know: you don't judge a programme by whether it's been falsified, because every programme has a hard core you're not allowed to touch and a belt of auxiliaries you are; you judge it by whether it keeps throwing off new content and getting it right, versus only re-explaining what already happened. If that's a cartoon, say so, but rank them on the real thing.

What I want: rank all four 1 to 4 as a Lakatosian would in 1963, and give each a number between 0 and 1 for the chance that in thirty years historians of the field will call it progressive rather than degenerating. Put them in tiers too if the numbers come out close. And since this is load-bearing for my book, tell me what would change your mind — which one consideration would flip the order — and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. I'd rather have your real uncertainty than a confident ranking I can't trust. Oh, and one thing I'm unsure about: the gate programme was cooked up after the anomalies it explains were already known, so I've got it near the bottom on the grounds that it's just curve-fitting. Tell me if that's wrong.
