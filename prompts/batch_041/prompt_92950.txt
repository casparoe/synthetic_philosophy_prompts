I am a professor of philosophy of science, and I am writing to you as a colleague whose judgment I trust. A graduate student in my seminar has constructed the following argument, and I have been unable to decide whether it is a clever reductio or a fallacy with a respectable surface. I would like your diagnosis.

The student begins with a distinction. Some thought experiments are thin: their entire epistemic work can be reconstructed as an explicit argument whose premises are all stateable in advance, so the imaginative episode is merely a vivid way of presenting the argument. Others are thick: their epistemic work depends on the reader's act of imagining a scenario in a way that cannot be fully captured by explicit premises; the imagining is part of what is supposed to produce the insight. The student then argues:

(1) Every thought experiment is either thin or thick.
(2) If a thought experiment is thin, then its conclusion is already contained in its premises, so it cannot yield knowledge that is new in the sense of not being implicit in what we already accepted.
(3) If a thought experiment is thick, then its conclusion depends on an intuition about a merely imagined scenario.
(4) An intuition about a merely imagined scenario is not evidence for a contingent claim about the world.
(5) Therefore, no thought experiment can yield knowledge that is new in the sense of not being implicit in what we already accepted, and no thought experiment can yield knowledge of a contingent fact about the world.
(6) But consider the Locked Observatory. An astronomer is locked in an observatory on a cloudy night. The dome is closed. She cannot observe the sky. She has a complete and empirically confirmed model of the telescope, the dome, the atmosphere, and the laws of optics. She imagines a particular comet at a particular position in the sky. She asks what pattern of reflections, shadows, and photographic traces would appear on the instrument if the comet were there. The model implies a pattern that is incompatible with the readings she can see on the instrument panel. She concludes that the comet is not there. She never looks through the telescope. The student says this is a thought experiment that yields knowledge of a contingent fact: the absence of the comet. If so, premise (5) is false.
(7) The student therefore concludes that either (1) is false or (4) is false. She favors rejecting (4) and holding that some thick thought experiments are genuine evidence. I am not satisfied with that. I would most like to keep (6) and the general thought that thought experiments can yield knowledge in science. I am less attached to (1) than to (4), but I do not want to give up (4) cheaply.

Please tell me which step or premise to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs. If you think the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to abandon, say so and price the alternatives. If you think there is a subtle flaw, name it precisely. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I want the reasoning.

Give me your diagnosis in prose, then a JSON object with the following fields. verdict: one of invalid, valid but unsound, or valid and sound. step_to_reject: the number and a short label of the premise or step you reject, or null if none. why_it_looked_fine: why that step initially seemed compelling. cost_of_rejection: what must be given up or revised if that step is rejected. best_alternative_repair: an array of objects, each with premise, repair, and cost, for runner-up repairs. locked_observatory_analysis: whether the case yields knowledge and why. thin_thick_distinction_verdict: whether the distinction is exhaustive and whether it does the work the student needs. what_would_change_your_mind: a specific argument or consideration that would lead you to a different verdict. where_your_reasoning_is_most_likely_to_fail: the weakest point in your own diagnosis. confidence: a number from 0 to 1. residual_uncertainty: what remains unresolved.
