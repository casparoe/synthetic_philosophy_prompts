You are an expert in moral philosophy, specifically the ethics of blame, the conditions of moral responsibility, and the social practice of holding one another to account. I am running a single three-hour session for a mixed group of non-philosophers — five members of a hospital ethics committee, two practising lawyers, and three clinicians — and the topic is standing to blame: the thought that a person may be genuinely blameworthy for something and yet still not be in a position to blame someone else for the same thing. Do not explain the distinction to me; I already have it. What I need is a teaching plan. Assume the group is intelligent, argumentative, and completely innocent of the distinction, arriving with the intuition that calling someone a hypocrite is just a way of changing the subject. I also want the plan to make room for a specific twist I care about, and I want you to treat it as the real intellectual content of the session rather than a footnote: the difference between losing standing through one's own wrongdoing and losing it through one's unwillingness to acknowledge that wrongdoing — that is, whether the objection to a hypocritical blamer is that the blame is false, that it is unwarranted, or that it is expressive of nothing, and whether standing once forfeited can be recovered by later reform. Before you commit to anything, think carefully about the dependency structure — which idea has to be in place before the next, and which of the several candidate orderings collapses under its own weight — and then give me the plan and nothing else.

Write your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction, no preamble, and no closing summary.

1. The order of ideas: state the sequence in which the components must be introduced and, for each step, what it depends on and what would go wrong if it were moved earlier or later — in particular, say whether the group needs the picture of blame as an addressed demand before they can even make sense of standing, or whether blameworthiness must come first.

2. The carrying example: give the single case that should hold the whole session together, describe what it must have and what it must conspicuously lack, and tell me whether the case I am tempted to use can carry it — a clinician who publicly blames a colleague for a medication error, when the clinician had earlier made a near-identical error that harmed no one and was disclosed to no one, so that the two wrongs are only partly the same wrong — and if not that case, which one instead, and why the stock case of the adulterer reproaching his partner for adultery is the wrong choice here if it is.

3. The misconception map: list the misconceptions the group will form or bring, and for each one name the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off — I expect at minimum hypocrisy treated as a defence of the act, standing treated as all-or-nothing, the assumption that only the sinless may blame, and the confusion between losing standing and ceasing to be blameworthy.

4. The check for understanding: give me one question to ask at the end, and state what a group that has genuinely grasped the distinction will say and what a group that has merely learned a slogan will say.

5. What to leave out: identify what I should deliberately omit at this level — for instance the metaphysical grounding of standing, the distinction between standing and desert, and cases of collective or institutional blame — and tell me how to flag each omission honestly rather than letting the group leave thinking they have the whole picture.
