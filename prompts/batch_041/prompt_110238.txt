I teach in a philosophy department but I've been moonlighting in a consciousness science reading group for about three years now, and I'm finally writing up the thing I keep saying in there. The claim is this: the empirical markers that get trotted out as evidence for global workspace theory — the late, sustained, widely distributed frontoparietal activation, the P3b-type component, the gamma-band synchrony, the whole kit — are markers of reportability, not of access. I dont think that's a pedantic distinction either. Access, in the sense that made the access/phenomenal distinction worth drawing in the first place, is supposed to be about a state's availability to reasoning, to memory, to the guidance of action generally; report is one output channel among several. If the only thing our markers are actually sensitive to is the presence of a report, then alot of what gets said about consciousness in the empirical literature is really about the machinery of saying things, and the evidence has a much narrower reach than people assume.

What I want from you is the most important implications of that claim. Not the cheap ones — I already know the obvious move about the no-report paradigms, where you strip the report requirement out of the task and see what's left, and I already know the counter that nothing can be calibrated without report. I want the downstream stuff, ranked by how much it matters, with an honest note on which implications actually follow from the claim by itself and which only follow if you accept some extra premise I might not want to accept.

Three places I suspect it bites hardest, and I'd like you to work through each rather than gesture at it: first the clinical cases, where people lean on exactly these markers to argue that a patient with no behavioral responsiveness is nonetheless aware, and where I think we may be licensed to say far less than gets said; second the comparative and developmental cases, where you can't get a report out of the subject by definition, so the inference from marker to experience looks even worse; and third the taxonomy question, which is whether access survives as anything more than a restatement of reportability once you've pressed the distinction, and if it doesn't, what we ought to say instead. I'd also like the strongest objection to the whole line, phrased the way someone who does this work would phrase it, plus the one piece of evidence that would make me drop it. Skip the citations, I know the literature and I'm not asking for a reading list — just give me the reasoning, and write it in American English.
