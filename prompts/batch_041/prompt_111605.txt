I'm a third-year PhD student working on interpertability — mostly small transformers on toy tasks — and there's a thing I keep circling that I don't think is just a wording issue.

Here's the case I care about. Take a two-layer transformer trained on bracket matching. I've got two checkpoints that agree on every input in the training distribution. On a held-out family (strings where a distractor token sits between a bracket and its match) they come apart: checkpoint A looks like it's doing an attention-based copy of the matching bracket, checkpoint B looks like it's running some depth-counting heuristic that happens to agree everywhere else. Same mechanism? Now take a third checkpoint, fine-tuned from A, that agrees with B on every input I can actually generate. Same mechanism as A? As B?

This matters because mechanistic anomaly detection — the idea that you catch a model doing something off-distribution by asking whether its internals are the kind of thing your training data licenses, rather than by looking at outputs — seems to presuppose an answer. The whole point is to flag a divergence you can't necessarily see behaviorally.

So what I want from you: make the argument for the position that same-mechanism claims are only well-defined relative to an intervention class the auditor fixes in advance, and that this makes mechanism identity a joint property of (model, distribution, intervention class) rather than a fact about a model's insides. The candidate classes I can think of give wildly different verdicts — (i) nothing, just inputs; (ii) input perturbations within the training support; (iii) activation patching between checkpoints; (iv) retraining from different seeds; (v) up to a change of basis on the residual stream; (vi) up to permuting units — and (v)/(vi) make almost everything the same while (i) makes almost nothing the same. I want you to argue that internal-similarity accounts fail (multiple realizability, basis dependence, unit permutation) and that pure behavioral-agreement accounts fail too, precisely where anomaly detection is supposed to earn its keep, and that relativizing to an intervention class is the right way out rather than a cop-out. Then give me the strongest objection to that and say whether it does real damage or just relocates the problem.

One thing I don't want: a tidy story that hides where people actually disagree. Tell me which parts of this are basically settled among people who work on it and which parts are genuinely live disputes — don't hand me a contested view dressed up as the consensus. I'd rather have a smaller claim I can trust than a clean one I can't. And no reading list please, I can't look anything up right now anyway.
