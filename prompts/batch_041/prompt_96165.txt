Something about guardianship and medical decision-making has always bothered me, and I want to know what the rules are actually assuming. When an adult loses the ability to decide for themselves, the instruction to whoever decides for them is usually not do what's best for them, it's do what they would have wanted -- reconstruct their choice, don't make your own. Fine. But in a lot of places that same instruction is written to cover people who never had capacity at any point in their life: someone with a profound intellectual disability from birth, a child who will never be competent, a patient who's been unresponsive since an accident at nineteen. There's no earlier choice to recover. The surrogate is still supposed to ask what the person would have wanted, and is explicitly told not to substitute their own values -- and courts have authorized real, irreversible things on exactly that basis, which is where it starts to feel like a magic trick. Meanwhile the same hospital, sometimes the same statute, falls back on best interests for the very same kind of patient, and best interests is a seperate question: what's good for them, not what they want. And then the case that really gets me: a competent adult refuses a life-saving treatment while severely depressed, and we override the refusal on the grounds that it isn't really their decision, that the depression is doing the deciding. Take that seriously and you've said there's a gap between what someone actually chooses and what they really want.

So what does this cluster of practices presuppose about a person's will? Is it committed to there being a fact of the matter about what someone would want if they were thinking clearly -- a fact that could hold even for someone who has never wanted anything -- or could the whole thing run on something thinner, like a procedural rule (follow the last explicit statement, and where there isn't one, use best interests) that never needs an idealized will at all? Which parts of the practice strictly require the idealized-will picture and which parts merely happen to fit it? Is it internally consistent, or is the never-competent case doing something the depression case forbids, since there we discount the actual choice and here we elevate a counterfactual one? And if you gave up on the idea that there's a fact about what someone would want under ideal conditions -- if preferences only existed when they were actually had -- what would concretely change: who decides, what the surrogate is told to do, what counts as a good reason at the bedside? Last thing, if the idealization is legitimate for one person, whats the argument against extending it to a family, or a community, or a whole society's idealized will?
