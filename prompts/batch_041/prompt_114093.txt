I'm taking a metaphysics seminar on dispositions, and the final essay asks me to take a stand on a controversy: whether the simple conditional analysis of dispositions — roughly, the idea that an object is disposed to M when S just in case it would M if S were to occur — is defeated by finks and masks, or whether it can be salvaged. Please write that essay and defend a definite conclusion.

To keep it from becoming a stock survey, build the argument around a case of my own devising in addition to the usual finks and masks. Consider a class of ceramic vases, call them reactive vases, made from a clay with a single standing feature: if such a vase is struck, the strike triggers the clay to fuse into a rigid lattice that permanently prevents the vase from breaking, so it survives that strike and every later one. A reactive vase that is never struck stays in its ordinary, unhardened state. Note that the interfering response here is not an external machine but a built-in feature of the object itself.

Is a reactive vase fragile? Is it breakable? Do the standard repairs handle it and the ordinary fink and mask cases any differently — for instance, restricting the analysis to intrinsic dispositions, appealing to an object's causal basis for its disposition, or replacing the analysis with a dispositional-essentialist account? Weigh the main options and end by defending a verdict on whether the conditional analysis can be repaired or should be abandoned.

Please write in American English.
