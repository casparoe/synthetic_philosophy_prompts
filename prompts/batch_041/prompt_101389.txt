I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am writing a long feature on the Allais paradox and the argument over the independence axiom that it set off. Before I put the exchange on the page I want to rehearse the strongest version of it, so I would like you to argue the other side. Play a committed defender of the independence axiom: a decision theorist in the Savage tradition, sharp rather than dogmatic, willing to grant that people really do have the Allais preferences and that those preferences are psychologically intelligible, but refusing to grant that they are rational. Do not set up a strawman of the objection and do not simply recite the standard textbook dismissal; engage the specific move I make. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a few paragraphs so that it reads like an interview rather than a lecture.

Here is the material I put in front of people. Four tickets: Ticket 1 pays one million for certain; Ticket 2 pays one million with probability .89, five million with probability .10, and nothing with probability .01; Ticket 3 pays one million with probability .11 and nothing otherwise; Ticket 4 pays five million with probability .10 and nothing otherwise. Most people prefer Ticket 1 to Ticket 2 and Ticket 4 to Ticket 3. Your side says that combination is inconsistent, and the argument runs roughly like this: within each pair the two tickets share a component -- in the first pair a .89 chance of a million, in the second a .89 chance of nothing -- and once you set each pair's shared part aside, the comparison left over is the same in both pairs, namely a certain million against a gamble that pays five million with probability ten-elevenths and nothing otherwise.

My opening move is this. That argument looks circular to me. You tell me to set the shared component aside and compare what remains; the licence to set a shared component aside and compare what remains is exactly the independence axiom, so when I ask why the Allais chooser is irrational you hand me back the axiom in different words. I know your side has other resources -- the money pump, and the long-run or repeated-choice argument -- and I will hear them, but each of them, as far as I can tell, either assumes independence or shows only that a particular kind of chooser can be exploited by a particular sequence of offers. And I have noticed something that makes me doubt the exploitation story: when I let people commit to a whole policy in advance, choosing among all four tickets as a single binding package before any coin is flipped, the same people who reverse between the pairs become consistent. If the inconsistency only surfaces when the offers arrive one at a time, in the order your pump needs them, is the fault in the chooser or in my sequencing? So give me the case against the Allais chooser that does not begin by assuming the point at issue, and if you think there is none -- if independence is a constitutive norm of preference rather than a theorem about it -- then say that plainly and defend it.
