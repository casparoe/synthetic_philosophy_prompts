I have no formal training in philosophy; I read on my own in the evenings and argue about this sort of thing with friends, and I have now spent the better part of a year circling one family of problems. The case that started it is the one usually credited to Parfit: you are stranded in the desert and dying of thirst, a driver appears and offers to carry you to the city if you promise to pay him a thousand dollars on arrival, you promise, you are saved, and once you are safe you have no reason whatsoever to pay, so — being a self-interested rational sort — you don't. What keeps me at it is the second layer. The driver can see all this coming, so the promise only buys your rescue if you are the kind of person who would pay anyway, which means your best strategy is to have a character trait that, from the standpoint of your post-rescue self, is a plain defect. I understand this is where people begin talking about rational irrationality, precommitment, the toxin puzzle, and Newcomb-style cases, and I have read enough to know that the terrain exists without knowing what is genuinely still open on it.

Two things of my own that I would like you to treat as part of the material rather than as digressions. First, I keep suspecting that being the sort of person who pays and arranging the world so that one cannot avoid paying — an escrow, a deposit held by a third party, a note already signed — are two different solutions wearing the same clothes, and that the second might dissolve the puzzle rather than solve it, by moving the commitment out of my character and into my circumstances. Second, I invented a variant I have not seen anywhere and want to know whether it is a real contribution or a confusion: an insurer who, for a fee paid before the trip, guarantees the driver that I will pay on arrival. If I buy the policy, the driver is certain of his money and therefore certain to rescue me; but had I been rescued I would never have needed the policy, so the premium looks like pure waste. Is buying it rational? Does it change the lesson of the original case, or merely restate it?

Here is what I actually want from you. I have roughly a year of evenings before I want to write something substantial, and I would rather spend that time on the questions that matter than re-derive what is already settled. Given that, which open questions in this area are most worth working on, and in what order? I am not asking for a reading list or a survey of who has said what — I want an agenda. For each question I want to know why it sits where it does: whether other questions depend on its answer, whether it is the kind of thing an untrained but stubborn person could actually make progress on, and whether it is being neglected or is quietly already closed. Where those criteria pull against one another, tell me how you would trade them off. And tell me what would move a question up or down the ranking — what, if I came across it, would tell me I had the order wrong. I would also like a verdict on my two additions: which of them, if either, is a genuine open question, and which is a restatement of something I simply have not read yet.
