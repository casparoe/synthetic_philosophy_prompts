I'm a junior and the de facto secretary of my school's history club, which for the last two months has been in a state I can only describe as a cold war. One faction holds that the shift from foraging to farming was the worst thing that ever happened to our species: a bargain in which human beings traded a comparatively easy, healthy, mobile life for back-breaking labour, epidemic disease, a narrower diet, and the first durable hierarchies. The other faction thinks that is nostalgia dressed up as science — that the picture of the well-fed, leisured forager is drawn from a handful of unusually favoured groups, that farming fed vastly more people, and that a worse life which many more people got to live is not obviously inferior to a good life available to few. Both sides are articulate, both cite the same small stock of facts (the changes in stature and teeth read off skeletons, the difference between calories produced per acre and calories returned per hour of work, the inequality visible in grave goods), and neither will concede anything without feeling it has conceded the whole argument. Our adviser, a biology teacher who has flatly refused to adjudicate, has told us to produce the document a real adversarial collaboration would produce, on the theory that a text both sides can sign is worth more than another lunchtime shouting match, and I have been handed the drafting.

What I would like from you is that document. Open with a numbered list of claims that both camps can honestly sign: about what the question means (what "mistake" would have to mean here, and by whose lights and at what moment it is being judged), about the transition itself (that it was not one event, one place, or one century), about which kinds of evidence bear on it and what each kind can and cannot show, and about which parts of the standard story each side already regards as overstated. Then a table of the disagreements that survive, each stated in wording its own side would accept as fair, with the strongest version of the opposing position written alongside it. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and for each, the evidence or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I have my own suspicions about where the real cruxes lie (the ambiguity of "affluence," whether it means leisure or material goods and whether the two trade off; whether the honest comparison is between whole categories, foragers against farmers, or between specific populations in specific environments; whether farming produced inequality or merely permitted it, given that some sedentary societies with marked hierarchy never farmed; and whether the transition was chosen or was a trap that could not be exited once entered), but I would much rather be told which of my suspicions are genuine cruxes and which are red herrings than have my list taken as given. Where a crux turns out to be partly a dispute about words, or a straight clash of values that no evidence could settle, say so and say which part it is. Please do not send us a reading list or citations — we want a document we can put on the club's website and defend in our own words — and please be willing to tell us plainly that some piece of the disagreement cannot be resolved with any evidence we could actually obtain, if that is the truth of the matter.
