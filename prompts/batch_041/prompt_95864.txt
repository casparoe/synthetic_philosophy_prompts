I've been reading LessWrong and the broader rationalist blogosphere since the old days, back when everyone was arguing about whether the Sequences were a religion, and I've somehow ended up as the TA for a fourth-year seminar on population ethics. The prof is a proper old-school philosopher who doesn't do the internet, so guess who's writing the final. Every year the Benatar question comes back and every year it's the same: students recite the asymmetry — absence of pain is good even when nobody's there to enjoy it, absence of pleasure isn't bad unless someone exists to be deprived of it — tack on 'and therefore coming into existence is always a harm', and walk out with a B+ for saying nothing false. I want to kill that question and replace it with one that actually bites.

Here's the crux I want them to hit. I was arguing about this in the group chat with a friend who does metaphysics and despises Benatar, and her line is that the asymmetry is gerrymandered: she says we only get to call the absence of pain 'good' in a life that never happens because we're covertly picturing the somebody who would otherwise have had the pain, while we call the absence of pleasure 'not bad' precisely because we don't posit anybody there to miss it — so the two halves of the asymmetry are trading on different implicit subjects, and the whole thing is rigged to land on anti-natalism. I'm not sure that's fair, I have a nagging feeling Benatar has a move here about goods that aren't good *for* anyone, but I can't articulate the reply well enough to put it on an exam without embarrassing myself. What I want is a question where the student has to (a) state the asymmetry precisely enough that the disagreement becomes visible, (b) adjudicate between my friend's charge and Benatar's defence, and then (c) take the asymmetry somewhere it wasn't built to go — the distinction between a life worth continuing and a life worth starting, and what happens to a life that's clearly net positive but only mildly so. And the last move has to force them to say whether any of this gets you to a *duty* not to procreate or whether the asymmetry is purely axiological and the deontic step needs a separate premise, because that's the bit everyone in my section elides, including, honestly, me.

So: please write the exam question itself first, exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the suggested time and the marks. Then a model answer of roughly 800 words — I want it to show what it looks like when someone actually separates the axiological claim from the deontic one rather than sliding between them. Then a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks for each (total 25), and a one-line note on how a candidate typically blows each one, plus a short closing note on what separates an A answer from a B+ one. No citations or reading list, please — it's a closed exam, half the class has only read the Wikipedia page, and anything the question leans on has to be reconstructable from the question itself, so definately don't build it around some paper I'd have to name. I'll seperate the sections myself when I typeset it, but keep them in that order.
