I've been reading the Euthyphro on my own — no class, just trying to finally get what the "Euthyphro dilemma" actually is — and there's a bit near the start that's throwing me off. Here it is in the old Jowett translation: "I am amused, Socrates, at your making a distinction between one who is a relation and one who is not a relation; for surely the pollution is the same in either case, if you knowingly associate with the murderer when you ought to clear yourself and him by proceeding against him. The real question is whether the murdered man has been justly slain. If justly, then your duty is to let the matter alone; but if unjustly, then even if the murderer lives under the same roof with you and eats at the same table, proceed against him."

What I can't get past is this: everyone tells me the dilemma is about wether something is good because God (or the gods) love it, or whether the gods love it because it's already good. But in this speech Euthyphro doesn't appeal to the gods at all — he appeals to whether the man was justly slain, as if there's a fact about justice just sitting there regardless of what Zeus or anyone else thinks. And then a bit later he's saying piety is whatever all the gods love. So is he contradicting himself, or is that not a contradiction because "pious" and "just" are two different things for him? I think Socrates asks him something like whether all the pious is just and whether all the just is pious, and I never quite follow what work that distinction is doing.

So what I really want is this: given the passage above, what is Euthyphro actually committed to, and does that commitment do any work in the argument, or is it just him sounding confident? And if he really does think justice is one thing and divine approval another, is the famous dilemma even aimed at him — or is the whole thing a later way of attacking a divine-command view that Euthyphro himself doesn't hold? Please don't just recite the standard horn-by-horn version at me, I've read that a dozen times. And if I'm wrong that he's appealing to a god-independent standard here, or if this passage is just a bad place to look for that, say so plainly rather than humoring me — I'd much rather find out my framing is off than get a smooth answer that lets me keep it.
