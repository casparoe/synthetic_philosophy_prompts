I'm hosting a visiting speaker in our dept's work-in-progress series next month — she's defending the view that you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg (the necessity-of-origin thing). I'm the discussant, so I need to come in hot but fair. Can you draft me nine or ten questions, plus a one-line follow-up for each keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? I want to be ready to push a second step rather than just nod along. A few constraints: don't ask her to summarise the view, don't ask what drew her to it, and please don't lean on citations or 'as so-and-so argued' — she'll deflect with 'go read the paper' and I want to keep her in the room. Also I'd rather not burn the questions on the usual queen-and-wooden-table examples, she's answered those alot.

Things I definately want covered, roughly one question each:

- The twinning case: if a zygote can split into two, and each twin traces back to that one zygote, what does her view say about whether either twin is the original? And if the zygote was already a person, did the twinning kill them? I want to see if she bites the bullet or starts adding epicycles.
- What exactly is doing the essential work — the two cells, the haploid genomes inside them, or the whole causal episode of conception? Wedge here: the sperm's tail gets discarded, the mitochondria come from the egg, and in some IVF setups there's a third donor involved. If the essence is the genome, identical twins wreck it; if it's the cells, ask her what makes a particular egg that egg.
- The regress: an egg is individuated by the meiosis that produced it, which is individuated by the ovary, which is individuated by the body it grew in, and so on. Does the view secretly require essentialism about her grandmother?
- Newish tech: gametes grown from ordinary body cells, mitochondrial replacement, embryos frozen for decades then implanted. Does 'the same sperm and egg' even pick out a stable thing across those cases?
- Chimerism and vanishing twins: two embryos fuse into one later person, or one gets absorbed by the other. Whose origin is it, and does her view have anything to say or does it go quiet?
- The debunking angle: people's intuitions seem to flip depending on whether you describe the case as 'the same DNA' or as 'the very same act of conception'. Is that a reason to distrust the intuition her whole view rests on?
- Whether the dispute is verbal — if a counterpart-theorist can paraphrase 'you could have come from a different egg' without losing anything, what would show her that something real is at stake?
- Timing: same sperm, same egg, but fertilisation happens thirty seconds later. Is that still you? If she says no, press on how much of the causal chain around conception gets to count as essential and why, and whether that leaves any room for her to have been born at all if anything upstream had gone differently.

End with one question about a case where she thinks her own view gives an answer she finds unattractive, and she keeps the view anyway — I want to see what she's willing to pay for it.

Keep each question to two sentences max, and make each follow-up genuinely dependent on what she actually says, not just 'and why?'.
