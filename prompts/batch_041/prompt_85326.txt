I am in my second year and taking a course on environmental ethics this term, and something that happened over the winter break has left me unsure how to think in a way the course has not yet helped with. I spent a fortnight volunteering at a wildlife rehabilitation centre near my parents' home, which takes in injured birds, hedgehogs and the occasional fox, treats them and releases them where they were found. On my last shift a fellow volunteer, a biology student, told me he thought the centre's work rested on a mistake. The animals we release, he said, will almost all die slowly — of starvation, disease, exposure, or being eaten — and the same is true of the overwhelming majority of animals born in the wild every year; a life that ends that way, after weeks or months of it, is not obviously a benefit to the animal living it. If we take animal suffering seriously, he said, we should not be repairing the damage people have done and then returning animals to the conditions that injured them, but trying to reduce the suffering that goes on in nature as such: vaccinating wild animals against disease, putting out food when food runs short, protecting prey from predators, and in the long run perhaps even altering predators so that they no longer need to kill. He was entirely serious. The centre's coordinator, who has run the place for thirty years, said that our responsibility ends with undoing the harm people have caused, and that anything further is playing God with a system we do not understand.

I could not tell whether the coordinator's reply was a principle or a slogan, and I could not tell what my friend's argument, if it is right, actually requires of me. Next term I sit on the volunteers' committee, which will vote on a proposal to move a third of our small budget from rehabilitation to a pilot vaccination programme for wild animals in the area. I have to decide how to vote and what to say in the meeting. What I want is not encouragement to care about animals but an account of what is genuinely at stake between these two positions. Does the fact that the suffering is caused by natural processes rather than by people change what we owe? Is "we only undo the harm we have done" a defensible line, or a convenient one — and if it is defensible, what exactly marks the boundary, given that the centre already intervenes in natural processes when it treats a fox for mange or keeps a bird alive through a hard frost? How much weight should the likelihood that we will make things worse carry, and how much of the coordinator's objection is an argument rather than a reaction to something unfamiliar? Finally, I would like to know what my friend's argument, if it is sound, implies for the ordinary things people do — putting out food for garden birds in winter, letting a hedgehog with an infection fend for itself, keeping a cat. That, in the end, is what I am voting on.
