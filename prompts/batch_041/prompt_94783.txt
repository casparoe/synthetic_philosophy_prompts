I'm not a philosopher or anything, just someone who reads to much on the internet and now can't sleep. I came across this puzzle called counterfactual mugging and I can't stop thinking about it. The setup is something like: a superintelligent being flips a coin. If it comes up heads, he asks you for $100. If you say no, nothing happens. If you say yes, you're out $100. If it comes up tails, he doesn't ask you for anything, but he gives you $10,000 if and only if you would have said yes in the heads case. So the question is, should you say yes when he asks? The weird answer that some people give is yes, because if you say yes, you lose $100 in the heads case, but you gain $10,000 in the tails case, and since the coin is fair, the expected value is positive. But then I realized this seems to prove way too much. Here's my argument:

(1) In the counterfactual mugging case, you should pay the $100 when asked.
(2) The reason is that your decision in the heads case is correlated with a reward in the tails case, even though it doesn't cause it.
(3) But any blackmailer can set up the same structure. Suppose a blackmailer sends you a letter: I have planted a bomb. Pay me $100 or I'll detonate it. But he also tells you: I only send this letter if a coin comes up heads. If it comes up tails, I don't send the letter, and instead I give you $10,000 if you would have paid in the heads case. So if you pay, you lose $100 in the heads case, but gain $10,000 in the tails case. By the same reasoning as (1), you should pay.
(4) But this means you should pay any blackmailer who sets up this kind of counterfactual reward scheme.
(5) And worse, you can't tell which blackmailers are running this scheme and which are just ordinary blackmailers. So you should pay all of them, just in case.
(6) But that's absurd. You can't just pay everyone who threatens you. That would be a nightmare.
(7) So something is wrong with the reasoning in (1).

Where does it go wrong? Is it step (2)? Is it that correlation isn't enough, you need causation? But then what about the original counterfactual mugging? If I give up (1), I'm saying you shouldn't pay in counterfactual mugging. But I kind of feel like you should pay there? I don't know. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (6) and reject (1), but I want to know what that costs. What am I giving up if I say you shouldn't pay in counterfactual mugging? And is there a way to keep (1) but block the blackmailer argument? Like maybe the blackmailer's scheme is different because he's threatening you, not just asking? Or because the bomb is already planted? I can't tell if that matters. Please tell me which step to reject and what the price is.
