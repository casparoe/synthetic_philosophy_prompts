I am a doctoral candidate working on the epistemology of testimony, and I have come to suspect that the literature on the norm of assertion has been looking in the wrong place. The familiar candidates — that one must know what one asserts, or that one must merely believe it, or that it must be true, or that one must have adequate justification — are all framed as though assertions were addressed to a single terminal audience that consumes them and goes no further. But a great many of the assertions we care about epistemically are made precisely so that they can be passed on: the spokesperson's statement, the inspector's report, the physician's diagnosis, the expert's summary for a lay reader. In these cases the immediate hearer is not the consumer of the assertion but a relay, and whatever the hearer later asserts to others is licensed, at least in part, by the original speaker's act. The standard theories, it seems to me, treat this as a marginal complication rather than as the central case.

I would like you to argue for a position about the norm governing assertion in these relay cases. Specifically, argue that the fundamental norm of assertion is not knowledge but what I will call transmission-robustness: the speaker's epistemic position must be such that the assertion remains properly assertible not only by the speaker but by the audience to whom it is passed on, even after the ordinary losses of transmission — the audience's inability to reproduce the speaker's evidence, its weaker grasp of the caveats, its tendency to strip hedges and qualifications. On this view the knowledge norm is not false but derivative: it is what transmission-robustness collapses to in the special case where the audience is terminal, that is, where no further assertion will be made on the strength of the original. Argue for the claim, and be candid about its costs.

In making the case, engage at least with the following pressures. First, whether making robustness the norm renders assertion unreasonably demanding, since almost no speaker can control what a downstream hearer will do with what she is told. Second, whether the view can avoid collapsing into a mere norm of caution rather than a norm of assertion — that is, whether it still governs the act of asserting rather than merely the wisdom of asserting. Third, whether the distinction between terminal and relay audiences is stable, or whether in fact every audience is a relay for someone, in which case the knowledge norm may have no domain at all. And fourth, what the view implies for the standard cases used to motivate the knowledge norm, in which a speaker asserts something she knows on good evidence to a hearer who will act on it immediately and will pass it on to no one. I want a genuine defense of the position, not a survey of the existing options, and I want the argument to say plainly where it is weakest.
