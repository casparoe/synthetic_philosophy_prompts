I'm in my third year and taking a course on aesthetics and philosophy of film, and last week we did the paradox of fiction. You know the shape of it: (1) we genuinely feel things — grief, fear, relief — for characters; (2) we don't believe those characters exist; (3) you can only feel an emotion about something if you believe it exists. Our lecturer ran through the usual escape routes and then gave us Walton's line, which is that what we feel for a character isn't an emotion at all but a quasi-emotion, a make-believe version of one, on a par with the fear a child has during a game of monsters. I think that's a relabelling of the problem dressed up as a solution, and that's basically what I want to write my essay about, except I'd rather do it through a film than through the usual Anna Karenina examples everyone uses.

The film I want to use is The Princess Bride. The frame story looks to me like the paradox staged in miniature: a grandfather reads his grandson a book the boy knows perfectly well is made up, the boy keeps interrupting to say it's boring and soppy, and by the end he's asking his grandfather to come back and read it to him again tomorrow. On the standard picture every one of those interruptions should be breaking the spell, but the boy gets more invested rather than less, and so do we, and the film keeps the interruptions in and still lands its emotional payoff at the end — the reunion, the Miracle Max scene where Westley turns out not to be quite dead after all, the last kiss. So it looks like a counterexample to (3). But I don't want an essay that just says 'look, premise three is false, isn't fiction clever'. I want a reading that's actually faithful to the film and to the literature at once.

Here's the thing that's really bugging me though, and it's where I'd like you to push hardest: if I can be moved by someone who never existed, then what's so special about fiction? I can be moved by the dead, by people I'll never meet, by things that might have happened, and arguably by a merely possible person or by a mathematical object that couldn't be harmed even in principle. Either the paradox of fiction is just a special case of a much more general puzzle about emotion and non-existence — in which case premise (3) is false across the board, which is a big claim about what emotions are — or fiction is special for some other reason, and I want to know which reason you think it is. There's also a nesting thing I can't get my head round: the film never asks whether Buttercup exists, it just shows that we care, and the frame is itself fiction, so we're watching a made-up boy being moved by a made-up book. And my flatmate says being moved by invented people is a kind of self-deception or a waste of feeling, and that the ending is the film quietly admitting it's just a comfort blanket; I think she's wrong but I can't say why. So — you're a philosopher of art who knows this literature properly and also reads films closely. Brainstorm the possible lines of response before you commit, then commit. Tell me definately which of the three claims should go and why, walk me through the specific scenes in The Princess Bride that carry the weight (and any that resist the reading), and say what the film adds to the debate that the arguments on their own don't. I want a verdict on the quasi-emotion question, not a survey of options, and if you have to choose between fidelity to the film and fidelity to a philosopher, tell me which one you're sacrificing and why. No reading list please, I've got enough to read.
