Two weeks ago my city's environmental commission told me I'd be getting its annual conservation award, and I've agreed to give a five-minute acceptance speech at the dinner — local officials, my family, and a room of volunteers who have spent years on recycling drives and tree plantings. The difficulty is that over the past year I've quietly concluded that the very thing the award praises is close to illusory: my cycling, composting, and canvassing almost certainly changed nothing, since whatever difference I made is swamped by what everyone else does, and I can't bear to stand up and either accept a compliment I believe is false or lecture the people honoring me. I'd like the speech to take that objection seriously rather than dissolve it into the usual reassurance that every drop counts, and to be candid about what my individual effort was actually for. Build it around the reframing I've arrived at — that the right question was never whether my actions altered the outcome but whether my life was a coherent response to a problem I understood, an integrity question rather than a causal one — and be honest about what that reframing gives up. I also can't tell whether this is the same problem as voting or eating meat or a different one, so the speech should say something about that, without turning into a lecture on moral philosophy. Please write it. End with a final line in the form \summary{...} giving a one-sentence summary of its central claim.
