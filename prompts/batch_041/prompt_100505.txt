I am a retired physical chemist — thirty-five years of spectroscopy, and since I shut down the lab I have spent my mornings on philosophy, which I find I enjoy a good deal more than I expected and understand a good deal less than I would like. Last month, over a long dinner, a former colleague of mine — a statistician who left the bench years ago to advise a research foundation — entertained the table in his usual way, by putting numbers on things. He went round the room assigning probabilities: the probability that this or that recently reported finding would survive a well-powered, independent replication. I have been turning his numbers over ever since, because I have lately been reading about the reference class problem, and it now seems plain to me that his forecasts were never bare numbers. Each one arrived with a class quietly attached to it, and I cannot tell which class he was actually using.

Here are the verdicts as well as I can recall them. For a finding drawn from a study of several hundred participants, concerning a perceptual effect of a kind that in general replicates reliably, he said eighty-five percent. For a finding from a study of perhaps twenty participants, concerning a social-behavioural effect of a kind that in general replicates poorly, he said fifteen percent. For a second large-sample study of a robust perceptual effect, eighty percent. For a second small-sample study of a fragile social effect, twenty percent. And for a middling study — a hundred-odd participants — of a perceptual effect of middling robustness, he said fifty percent. At the time these seemed to me simply careful guesses; but I now notice that in every case where his number was high the study was both large and about a robust effect, and in every case where it was low the study was both small and about a fragile one. The two features travelled together throughout the whole set, so I cannot tell whether he was classing each finding by how it was studied or by what it was about.

The new case is this: a large, well-powered, preregistered study — several hundred participants — reporting a social-behavioural effect of precisely the fragile kind that has fared badly whenever it has been tested elsewhere. What would he say the probability is? I would like you to lay out the reference classes he might be running — the method by which the finding was produced, the family of phenomenon it concerns, or some third class that fits his earlier numbers just as well — and to say what each of those rules predicts about this new case, since I suspect they come apart here for the first time. Then give me your best guess at what he will actually say, with your reasons for preferring that reading of him over the others, and tell me which single further case, if I could get him to pronounce on it, would most cleanly separate the candidate rules from one another. If his earlier verdicts genuinely leave the matter open, say so rather than manufacturing a resolution.
