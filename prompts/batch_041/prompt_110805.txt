Until this year I would have said I understood heritability well enough to nod along to, but two things have changed that. The first is personal: my younger brother was recently assessed for a reading difficulty, and the summary we were given says that such difficulties run in families, which my uncle has taken to mean that nothing can be done and that the family should stop asking what the school is or is not doing. The second is that I have just started a master's in education policy, where a phrase I keep hearing in seminars — that heritability is "a population statistic" and therefore says nothing about any individual — gets used to end arguments in two opposite directions. It is produced to deflate the uncle-style inference that a trait running in a family means it is genetic in this particular child, and it is produced, by different people, to insist that because a trait is strongly heritable there is nothing a classroom can do about it. I have the uneasy sense that one sentence is being asked to do two incompatible jobs, and that neither set of users could tell me which precise claim they are leaning on.

What I would like from you is not a verdict on the slogan but a map of it. Please set out, as a numbered menu, the distinct propositions that sentence could be asserting, ordered from the ones that are true almost by construction, given how the statistic is defined, to the ones that would be genuinely surprising if true. For each, say whether it is (a) a trivial consequence of the definition, (b) a substantive and defensible claim that requires its own argument, or (c) an overreach — or a flat falsehood — that the slogan does not license. Then tell me which reading is doing the work when the sentence is used to block the inference from "it runs in families" to "it is genetic in this child", which reading is doing the work when the same sentence is used to block the inference from "highly heritable" to "nothing can be done", and whether either use survives contact with the reading that actually supports it.

Three things I would like the map to accommodate, since they are what convinced me that the slogan is concealing a menu rather than stating a single fact. First, my lab — I am an unpaid assistant on a twin study — estimates heritability one way, from the classical twin design, while a review I read last term reported much lower values for the same trait from genome-wide data; a postdoc told me this is not a contradiction but two different quantities sharing one name, and I would like to know precisely what distinguishes them. Second, a lecturer told me that heritability is not a causal decomposition of anything, that it partitions variance only under an assumed additive model, and that a different model of the same data can partition that variance differently — which sounds considerably stronger than the version of the slogan I am used to hearing. Third, height: heritable on every estimate I have ever seen, and yet the adults I know from my grandmother's generation are visibly shorter than the people I work with. If the slogan can cover all of this, tell me how; if it cannot, tell me how many slogans I actually need, and which of them I should stop repeating.
