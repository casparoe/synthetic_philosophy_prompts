I'm a novelist — third book, first one with any real statistics in it — and I've gotten stuck on something I noticed while researching a hospital for it. The hospital is fictional but the setup is stolen wholesale from real inspection reports: the same year, the same patients, two official numbers for how deadly the place is. In the glossy annual report, and in the local paper's coverage of it, you get the raw figure, deaths per thousand admissions, full stop. In the fat spreadsheet that goes to the regulator and decides whether the place is failing, you get the number after case-mix adjustment — after you've accounted for the fact that this hospital happens to take the older patients, the sicker ones, the ones from the poorer postcodes. Everyone involved talks as if theirs is the real number and the other one is a PR exercise. In my draft, the junior doctor who narrates notices that in the year in question the adjusted mortality fell in every single ward while the raw figure rose, because the hospital had quietly shifted most of its beds over to the elderly-care side — so every part got better and the whole got worse, and both official numbers are defensible depending on who's holding the page. My statistician friend says there's a name for the general shape of this (somebody-or-other's paradox, he said, then said it isn't really a paradox at all, it's just two seperate questions, and told me not to worry about it, which is definately the kind of thing that makes me worry about it). A friend in school inspection tells me they do the reverse over there — the published league table is raw and the funding formula is adjusted — which I'd have assumed was the same problem in a diferent hat, though he says it's completely diferent.

What I'm after isn't the arithmetic. I want to know what this whole way of doing things takes for granted: publish the adjusted number when you're making decisions about the place, publish the raw number when you're talking to the public, and treat the ward-by-ward result as the one that tells you what's really going on whenever the two disagree. Does it presuppose a particular view of what a rate is for — describing a population versus predicting the next patient through the door versus finding out what the hospital's own care did? Does it quietly assume that the thing you adjust away, the patient mix, is the kind of thing that sits upstream of the outcome rather than the kind of thing the hospital's own practice helped produce — and is that assumption ever tested, or does it just come bundled with the practice? And is the same office being incoherent when it uses one number in the annual report and the other in the regulator's return, or can a single consistent picture license both, so that the inconsistency I'm smelling is really just two questions wearing one coat?

Then the part I actually care about, since I'd like the book to have a spine and not just a clever appendix: is the practice pinned down to one philosophical picture, or would several quite different views about causation and evidence all tell you to do exactly this — in which case which bits are genuinely presupposed and which are decoration? If you had to swap the underlying picture for another one — say, that the only thing a rate is good for is predicting what happens to the next person admitted — what would have to change about the way the hospital reports, the way the regulator reads it, and the way my doctor explains it to her patients?
