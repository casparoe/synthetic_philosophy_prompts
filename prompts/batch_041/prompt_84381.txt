Everything I know about this topic comes from books written for general readers and from arguments with friends, so I would rather describe the case plainly than pretend to a vocabulary I do not have.

The argument I keep meeting goes like this. Money that has already been spent is gone in the way that spilled milk is gone: no decision I make now can bring it back, so it cannot be a reason for anything I do next. On this view, the person who keeps paying into a failing shop, or stays in a marriage that has gone bad, or finishes a book that nobody will read, because of what has already been put in, is making the same mistake as the gambler who doubles his bet to win back his losses. The picture behind it is a ticket stub: the price of admission has been paid and cannot be refunded, so the only live question is whether the show from here on is worth sitting through. I have heard this used to end arguments — you are just honoring sunk costs — and it usually does end them.

I mostly believe it, but three things I have noticed do not fit, and I cannot tell whether they are real exceptions or just ways of dressing up the same mistake.

First, people sometimes go out of their way to create costs they cannot recover: burning the boats, buying the ticket that cannot be refunded, telling everyone they have committed to something. If a past outlay were simply inert, deliberately manufacturing one would be pointless, and it plainly is not.

Second, what looks like a loss may have left something behind that only pays off if I stay. A friend ran a shop that failed. She says the money is gone but the years are not: she came out of it knowing the trade, the suppliers and the customers, and none of that would exist if she had quit in the first year. The ticket stub picture seems to assume that the money bought nothing that survives.

Third, whether continuing is worth anything often depends on whether other people believe I will continue. A partnership, a team, a marriage, a firm — these work partly because each party can count on the others not to walk away at the first bad quarter. If being known as someone who does not quit is what makes people willing to commit to me, then my past outlay is not merely a cost to be ignored; it is evidence about me that others price.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy rather than another restatement of it. Lay it out properly, with a table if that helps — what plays the part of the ticket, the seat, the second act, the box office — then say where it breaks, and separate the breaks that actually matter for the conclusion people draw from it, namely that honoring sunk costs is always irrational, from the ones that are merely decorative. I suspect the analogy quietly imports more than it admits: that the past outlay bought nothing durable, that there is a single decision-maker with fixed and known preferences and nobody watching, that the odds of success do not change as I go. Tell me which of those assumptions are doing the work and which are harmless. Then answer the question the whole thing turns on: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying it all along? If there is a better picture, say what it is — burning the boats looks like the same fact used to prove the opposite point — and what it would and would not license.
