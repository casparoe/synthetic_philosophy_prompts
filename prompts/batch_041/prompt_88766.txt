Play a committed defender of the all-subjected principle for me, and dont soften it: the demos for any given decision is whoever is actually subjected to the coercion that decision carries, and anyone else's complaint is a moral complaint, not a democratic one. Hold that line across turns, a paragraph or two each, and don't step out of character to explain both sides. My opening move: I've been working out a rival where membership goes to whoever's compliance the decision actually needs in order to work. Take a city that bans a pesticide. The farmers outside town have to stop spraying or the ban is nothing, so on my view they're in the demos. The people downstream who drink the runoff are not in it — they can't do anything about the ban either way, they just get sick. I know how that sounds. But your view has the mirror problem: to get the downstream people in, you have to count the state's failure to stop the poisoning as a coercion of them, and once omissions count, every decision coerces practically everyone and your demos becomes the whole world. Keep it narrow and you're stuck saying the poisoned have no democratic standing; widen it and theres no boundary left to draw. So which of us is really dressing up might makes right as legitimacy?
