Hey — I run a discussion section for a second-year metaphysics course and I'm sick of handing out the usual list of -isms for the problem of universals. Everyone just memorizes the labels and can't say what actually seperates them. I want to replace the handout with a decision tree the students work through by answering questions, where their answers do the sorting and the labels only show up at the leaves.

Start from a commitment nobody in the room would deny: there are true predications — "this patch is red," "this is a cat" — and they're not just about what we happen to say. Then each branch node should be one question that genuinely splits the space, phrased so a student can answer it without already knowing where it leads. I want the tree to force a choice about: whether the repeatable things (if there are any) are prior to resemblance or resemblance is prior to them; whether the items doing the work are particular or repeatable; whether there has to be one for every predicate we can formulate or only for the ones that carve nature; wether uninstantiated ones are allowed; whether they do any causal work or are just along for the ride; and whether a theory here even owes us truthmakers for predications, or whether it's allowed to be a story about what our predicates mean.

Please build these cases into the nodes, since they're what we argued about last term: a universe containing exactly one red thing; a predicate like "red-or-round" that nothing would ever need; a continuous color spectrum where every shade seems to get its own item but nothing marks the joints; two things that resemble each other in exactly one respect but are particular in different ways; and a predicate whose extension obviously depends on our conventions, like "being a game."

Leaves: transcendent realism, immanent realism, trope theory with a resemblance or natural-class story, resemblance nominalism with resemblance taken as primitive, class nominalism, predicate nominalism, the paraphrase/"ostrich" option, conceptualism, and whatever structuralist or deflationary-about-property-talk view you think belongs. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where the tree can't separate two leaves — I suspect trope theory and primitivist resemblance nominalism are hard to pull apart on some paths, and so are deflationism and the ostrich option — say so instead of pretending a node does the work. Also tell me which combinations of answers nobody occupies and whether that's because the view is incoherent, ugly, or just unexplored.

No reading lists or citations, we're not doing that. American English please. Aim for about 8-10 branch nodes.
