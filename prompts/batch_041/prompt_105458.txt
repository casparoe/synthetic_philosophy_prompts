I'm a senior and the co-president of my school's philosophy club, and our final meeting of the year is a short one at which each officer gets two minutes. I'd rather open my slot with a joke than with a summary, and I've spent a week failing to write one about the material we gave the most time to, the three stances and the notion of a real pattern. Everything I draft falls into one of two ditches. Either the humour comes entirely from the vocabulary — someone says "intentional stance" about a toaster, and we laugh because the phrase sounds odd in that context — which would earn the same polite laugh from a listener who has never read a word of the theory; or the thing turns into a lecture with a semicolon where the punchline should be. What I'm asking you for is a joke that would actually be funny to the eight or nine people in that room who know the material, in the sense that the punchline depends on the content of the view rather than on its vocabulary, and that I could read aloud in about thirty seconds.

Two things that may be useful. First, the point I most want the joke to turn on, because it took me longest to grasp and I suspect it is where the funny is: the three stances are not three competing answers to the question of what a thing really is, one of which is the honest answer and the other two convenient fictions, but three strategies for predicting the thing, and whether the belief-and-desire description is legitimate is settled by whether it predicts better than the alternatives, not by whether some inner item answers to it. Our club president made this vivid for me with a comparison to a center of gravity. A chair really has one, you can use it to say which way the chair will topple, and it is not a part of the chair — there is no pellet of center-of-gravity inside it. She says belief-talk is like that: a pattern can be real in precisely this sense, genuinely there to be found and more efficient than the raw data, while the entity it appears to name is not there. Second, I'd like the joke to have a target — some system that can be described all three ways without any of the three descriptions being false, whether that is a chess engine, a thermostat, a cat, a committee, or a market — since the funniest consequence of the theory to me is that it licenses you to be completely right three times about one object. If it helps, the wrinkle I keep returning to is that the intentional stance only earns its keep when the thing you point it at is roughly rational or well built, which is why it is magnificent on a chess engine and slightly embarrassing on a friend in a bad mood; a punchline could live there.

Please don't lean on any quotation or passage from the reading — I'll be speaking without notes and I don't want to get a phrasing or an attribution wrong — and please avoid the well-worn shape in which a list of thinkers each answer the same riddle, which everyone in the club has heard. My test for whether you've succeeded is this: if the joke would work just as well for a listener who has encountered only the phrase "intentional stance" and knows nothing else about the view, it isn't the joke I'm after.
