I have been going back and forth with a colleague about the centipede game, and I would like your own credence rather than a summary of the literature. Here is the version we are arguing about, so that there is no ambiguity. I move first. At node 1 I can take, in which case the game ends and I receive 2 while you receive 0, or I can pass, which hands the decision to you. At node 2 you can take, ending the game with you receiving 3 and me 0, or pass, handing it back to me. At node 3 I can take, receiving 4 and leaving you 2, or pass, in which case the game ends and I receive 3 while you receive 5. The payoffs are common knowledge, the game is one-shot, there is no pre-play communication, and there are no irrational or committed types. The standard backward-induction argument runs as follows: at node 3 I prefer 4 to 3, so I take; knowing this, at node 2 you prefer 3 to the 2 you would get after passing, so you take; knowing that, at node 1 I prefer 2 to the 0 I would get after passing, so I take. The argument is valid, yet it delivers an outcome that both of us would trade away if we could coordinate on passing to the end.

I want your credence that, under exactly those assumptions, taking at node 1 is the uniquely rational move for me. Before you answer, consider a reframing that I think the usual discussion hides. Suppose neither of us can commit in any binding way, but each of us is the sort of agent whose dispositions already determine what we would do at every node — not an agent who chooses afresh at each node, but a policy that maps the whole tree to actions, with each of us knowing that the other is such a policy. If rationality attaches to policies rather than to isolated choices, it is no longer obvious that the policy which takes at node 1 is the one a rational agent would adopt; the policy that passes at node 1 and takes at node 3 might be evaluated differently, and the backward-induction argument may be answering a question about choices that the dispositional framing dissolves. I am not certain the reframing is legitimate, but I would like your credence to be sensitive to it.

Please give me a number for the proposition as stated, decomposed into the sub-questions you think it rests on. I would suggest at least: (a) the probability that common knowledge of rationality is a coherent and jointly satisfiable condition for two agents rather than an idealization that cannot actually be met; (b) the probability that, conditional on its being coherent, backward induction identifies what rationality requires rather than merely what subgame-perfect equilibrium permits; (c) the probability that the correct reading of 'rational' here is expected-utility maximization given beliefs about the other player, as opposed to a dispositional or policy-level notion, and how much the answer turns on that choice; and (d) any further term you think belongs. Give a number and one line of justification for each, then combine them and show the arithmetic. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument, thought experiment, or formal result would move it most.
