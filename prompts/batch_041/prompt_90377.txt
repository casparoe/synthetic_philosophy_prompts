Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge — the view that you know p iff p is true, you believe it, you wouldn't have believed it if it were false (sensitivity), and you would've believed it if it were true (the flip side of that). The thing everyone pounces on is that it makes knowledge fail closure: you can know p, and be perfectly able to deduce q from p, and still not know q, because q can fail sensitivity even when p passes it. Defenders shrug and say closure was never a real intuition, just a habit. Critics say any theory that forces you to say you know you have hands but don't know you're not a handless brain in a vat has already refuted itself.

I want an essay that actually adjudicates this instead of surveying it, so do two things. First, invent a fresh version of that nasty conjunction yourself — some mundane thing you know, paired with a skeptical-ish thing you can obviously deduce from it that nonetheless fails sensitivity — and use it to squeeze the tracking theorist. Second, take seriously the best repair I can think of: bolt a closure condition onto tracking, so that you only count as knowing p if you also track the obvious consequences you actually bother to draw from p. Does that rescue the theory or wreck it? Work through whether it (a) makes ordinary knowledge impossible, since those skeptical consequences weren't trackable in the first place, or (b) collapses into an ad-hoc patch that does all the real work and leaves the tracking conditions sitting there doing nothing. You can also say something, if you want, about whether the method-relativity in the sensitivity condition is even precise enough to be well-defined — that's always seemed like a soft spot to me.

Then commit: is giving up closure a price worth paying for tracking, or does the failure of closure show tracking is just the wrong theory of knowledge? Don't end on a wishy-washy both-sides-have-a-point note. Say which way you come down and defend it. No citations needed, I know the literature.
