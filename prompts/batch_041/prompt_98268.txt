A friend of mine runs a small sci-fi reading night and we're doing Lem's Solaris next — the novel, though the Tarkovsky film is fair game if it helps the reading. I want to do it through the problem of other minds, because I think it's a much better fit for that problem than people usually bother to notice. The standard version of the problem is sceptical: how do I know the person in front of me has a mind at all? But Kelvin isn't doubting his visitor's mind — he's half-hoping she has one — and the thing he really can't reach is the ocean itself, a mind, if it is one, so unlike his own that no amount of evidence or experiment would ever confirm it. So two jobs. First, read the novel as a study in what we can and can't know about other minds, including the limit case of a mind that isn't remotely like ours, and tell me which scenes carry that best and which ones muddy it. Second, I keep half-remembering an idea that I think came from someone in the ordinary-language tradition, roughly this: that the problem of other minds isn't really about knowledge at all but about acknowledgement — not a fact I could verify, but a response I'm called on to give, a matter of treating someone as a someone rather than proving that they are. Take that idea seriously and test it against the book. Does Solaris back it up, or does the novel keep dragging us back to wanting proof? Where does the reading strain — is the ocean a mind at all, or is Kelvin just projecting onto a mirror, and does the book know the difference? And where does it resist the whole exercise? Be honest about what the reading can't settle, and end on what the novel adds to the debate that the arguments on their own don't. No headings, bullet points, bold or tables please — just continuous prose, like you'd write in an email.
