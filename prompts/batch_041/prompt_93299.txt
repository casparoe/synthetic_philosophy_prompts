ok, I'm writing something on van Fraassen's reflection principle and I want an actual number from you, not a tour of the debate. The principle as I've got it: if you're certain that at some later time your credence in A will be x, then your credence in A now should be x. Everyone knows the standard counterexamples -- you know you'll be drugged or hypnotised later, you know the evidence you're about to recieve is misleading, etc. And the standard rescue is to restrict it: reflection holds when you're certain your later credence will be *rational*. So here's the proposition I want your credence in:

There exists a restriction of reflection that is (a) true, (b) non-vacuous -- it doesn't just amount to 'trust your future self when your future self is right' -- and (c) still does work in the ordinary case where you expect to think harder and gather more evidence about A.

Decompose it, please. What it hinges on, as I see it:

1. does the diachronic dutch book argument establish a requirement of rationality, or only some weaker pragmatic coherence thing?
2. are all the counterexamples self-locating -- centered-content cases that a centered-worlds framework handles -- or do some survive that fix?
3. the one that nags me: can 'you'll be rational later' be spelled out non-circularly? If rational = gets it right, the restriction is empty. If rational = follows the right procedure, you owe me the procedure, and specifying it without already presupposing reflection is the whole game.
4. is there a case where you're certain your later self is both rational and better informed, and reflection still fails?
5. the misleading-evidence cases -- do they sink even the restricted version, or only the unrestricted one?

Here's a case I keep using and can't decide about: you're certain that tomorrow you'll look at exactly the same evidence you have now and come out more confident, not because of any new argument but because you know you 'wear in' whatever view you currently hold -- thinking about it just makes you more confident in it. You're not certain this is irrational; you think it might just be you seeing the arguments more clearly. Does reflection tell you to raise your credence now? Does the rational-later restriction even have a verdict here?

Two extras if you have room: (i) does reflection have any grip at all if credences are imprecise -- if you're certain your later credence will be the interval [0.3, 0.6], what does that constrain your interval to be now? (ii) is the failure for full belief the same failure (you can rationally believe you'll later not believe it, without that undermining the belief now, which looks reflection-violating) or a different one?

Tell me where you think our disagreement actually lives -- which of those five -- and what single argument or case would move your number the most.
