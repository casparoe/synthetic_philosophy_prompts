The question is out of 10, 6 for part (a) and 4 for part (b):

Question 3. A beetle species carries a groove on its hind leg. In the ancestral population the groove was selected for because it improved grip on the host plant's stems. On an island, the beetle's original host plant has been replaced by a smooth-stemmed species; in the island population the groove no longer has any measurable effect on grip, survival or reproduction, though it persists at the same size. On that same island there is also a small pit on the leg, which is a byproduct of the way the leg joint folds during development — it is not homologous to any structure that was ever selected for anything, and the island population is only a few generations old, so nothing has had time to be selected on it — and the pit happens to measurably reduce water loss. (a) For the etiological account and for the systemic account, say what the function of the groove is in the island population and what the function of the pit is, and say in each case whether the two accounts agree or disagree. (b) Do these cases support the view that the two accounts are rivals competing over a single property, or that they are answering different questions that happen to share the word function? Defend your answer.

And the student wrote:

Both accounts treat a trait's function as one of its effects, but they pick out that effect differently. On the etiological account a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected — the account is historical, and the function is fixed by the trait's selection history. On the systemic account a trait's function is the effect by which it contributes to a capacity of the system it belongs to — the account is ahistorical and looks only at how the parts fit together now.

(a) Island groove. The groove was selected for grip, and on the usual etiological line it keeps that function even though grip no longer matters on the island, because the history is what fixes it. (A stricter version that demanded recent selection would say the function has lapsed, but that's not the version we did.) The systemic account says it has no function, since it contributes nothing to any capacity of the beetle. So the accounts disagree here.

Island pit. The pit is a byproduct of development with no selection history at all, and it happens to cut water loss. On the etiological account the pit's function is to reduce water loss, because that is the effect it is having on the beetle's survival, and when we ask what the pit is for we're asking what it is doing. On the systemic account the function is also to reduce water loss, since relative to the beetle's capacity to retain water the pit makes a real contribution. So here the accounts agree.

(b) Taken together the cases suggest the accounts are answering different questions rather than competing over one thing. Where they do come apart — the groove — each account is still giving the right answer to its own question, one about origins and one about present organisation. So the appearance of a disagreement is mostly verbal: once you say whether you're asking the historical question or the systemic one, there's nothing left to argue about.
