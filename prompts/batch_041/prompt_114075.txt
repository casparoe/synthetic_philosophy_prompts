A friend who has just finished an ethics course summarized Bernard Williams's objection to utilitarianism for me as follows, and I would like to know how much of it to trust. Williams's real complaint, he says, is that utilitarianism is too demanding. Take the famous case of a man who can save only one of two people, one of them his wife, and whom the utilitarian tells to save whichever rescue will do more for the total happiness; the man must think that this is his wife and then add a further thought about the general good — one thought too many. That extra thought, on Williams's view, corrupts his relation to her, and this, my friend says, is the integrity objection: utilitarianism makes us abandon our deepest commitments and treat everyone as interchangeable. Since a commitment just is something one will not trade away whenever the sums favor it, a consistent utilitarian can have no commitments at all, and utilitarianism is therefore not merely hard to live by but a theory no one could hold. Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, telling me which of the summary's claims a careful reader of Williams would accept, which are subtly wrong or conflate distinct things, what a defender of utilitarianism would say in reply, and whether the closing claim about consistency follows from anything Williams actually argues.
