You are a historian of economic thought with a specialism in the Scottish Enlightenment and a particular expertise in Adam Smith's writings on empire, colonies and the mercantile system. You have been asked to write a speech for a specific occasion, and you should approach the task with care, intellectual honesty and rhetorical sensitivity.

Occasion: the annual dinner of the (fictional) Edinburgh Society of Merchants and Planters, held in 1776 to mark the publication of Smith's Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations. The speaker is a guest of the Society, a scholar known for independent views. The room is mixed: about half are merchants and planters who have grown rich through the colonial trade, together with their spouses and children; the other half are Edinburgh literati, clergymen and a few abolitionists. The Society has asked for a speech of six to eight minutes proposing a toast to "the commerce and prosperity of the British Empire". The speaker has accepted, but intends to use the occasion to present Smith's actual argument about colonies — not as a hostile attack, but as a piece of uncomfortable truth-telling that the audience can hear.

Your task: write the speech. It should do real philosophical and rhetorical work. In particular, it should build its structure around Smith's argument that the colonies were founded and maintained for the benefit of a narrow merchant interest; that the monopoly of the colonial trade is a monopoly against the home consumer; that the expense of defending and administering the empire falls on the general public while the profits are captured by a few; and that a project of empire for the sake of creating customers is, on Smith's own analysis, not fit for a nation of shopkeepers but rather for a government influenced by shopkeepers. The speech should not pretend that Smith was a simple anti-imperialist in a modern sense, nor should it reduce him to a caricature of laissez-faire. It should engage with the tension between Smith's sympathy for the American colonists' grievances and his belief that the colonial relationship was economically irrational for Britain. It should also acknowledge, without resolving too neatly, the moral horror of slavery and the slave trade, which Smith condemned but which the colonial economy depended upon.

The speech must land for both halves of the room. The merchants should feel that they are being addressed as intelligent adults, not scolded; the literati should recognise the accuracy of the Smithian argument. The toast itself — to the commerce and prosperity of the British Empire — must be delivered in a way that is sincere in its form but subversive in its content, so that the audience only fully realises what has happened as the speech concludes. Avoid anachronism: do not put modern anti-colonial vocabulary into Smith's mouth, and do not make the speaker a twenty-first-century moraliser. The speech should sound like something that could plausibly be delivered in 1776 Edinburgh, even if its arguments are uncomfortable.

Before you write, brainstorm briefly: (a) what are the three or four most important Smithian claims about colonies that the speech must convey; (b) what are the main rhetorical risks — where the audience might stop listening; (c) how the toast can be structured so that the subversion is earned rather than cheap. Then write the speech. Do not include the brainstorming in the final output; the final output should be the speech only. Do not use quotation marks around any passage from Smith unless you are certain of the exact wording; paraphrase instead. Do not invent specific page numbers, letters or citations. Write in British English.
