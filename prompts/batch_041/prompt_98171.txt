Write an essay on whether the separateness of persons is a freestanding objection to utilitarianism or merely a restatement of the complaint that utilitarianism is insensitive to distribution. Reach a verdict and defend it.

Take the objection in its strongest form: utilitarianism treats a life as a receptacle of value, so it licenses sacrificing some lives for a larger total, and in doing so it fails to take seriously that each person is a distinct locus of claims rather than a stage in a single life. The objection, as Rawls presses it, comes in at least two readings, and I want you to keep them apart. On one reading, the trouble is metaphysical: distinct persons are not one person, so their goods cannot be combined into a single good to be maximized. On the other, the trouble is about claims: persons compete over a shared world, so trade-offs among them stand in need of a justification that trade-offs within a single life do not. These come apart, and your essay should make them come apart rather than run them together.

Test both readings against cases you construct yourself. One should involve a sequence of psychologically isolated occupants of a single continuous body, none of whom remembers or cares about any other, where the total welfare across occupants exceeds what that body could enjoy if it housed one person throughout. Another should involve a person who endures decades of deprivation within one life for a payoff at the end. Ask of each reading what it predicts about these cases, and whether the reading that fits the cases best is the one that gives the objection its apparent force.

Then consider whether the objection survives replacing utilitarian aggregation with a rule that maximizes the number of lives that go well enough rather than the total. If it does not survive, say whether that shows the objection is really about aggregation, about consequentialism's impersonality, or about something else. Finally, state what would have to be true of persons for the objection to lose its force, and whether we should accept that picture. You may draw on Rawls's handling of the objection and on Nozick's contrast between a utilitarian attitude toward animals and a Kantian attitude toward persons, but the essay must stand on the arguments rather than on those texts.
