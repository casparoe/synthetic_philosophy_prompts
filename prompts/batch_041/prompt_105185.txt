I'm in a grad seminar on moral responsibility and we've been on the standing-to-blame stuff for three weeks now, and I've realized I can talk about hypocrisy cases all day but I've never actually built one from scratch — every time I try I end up smuggling in the verdict I wanted. So I'd like you to do it, and do it properly. Take the role of a philosopher who works on the second-personal ethics of blame (address, resentment, accountability — that whole neighborhood), and before you write anything, spend a few lines brainstorming: list three or four different features a case *could* have that would let a hypocrite's blame survive, then tell me which of them make the case too easy and bin them. Then build the case.

Here's the spec. The scenario has to be one ordinary paragraph, a real-life setting (shared flat, friend group, department meeting, family — nothing science-fiction, no brain scanners, no institutions doing the work for us). The blamer must be a genuine hypocrite on the plain tu quoque reading: she is doing, right now, unrepentantly, the very thing she's blaming, and she knows it. She also has to be sincere — not a cynic, not someone using the accusation as a weapon, she actually believes the norm. And yet the case has to make it intuitive that she still has standing to blame. No easy exits: she hasn't apologized or reformed, she isn't invoking a role that authorizes her to call people out, and she isn't blaming on someone else's behalf (third-party blame is its own mess and I don't want to be in it). If you think the only honest way to pull this off is to make her the person the wrong was done to, fine, go there — but then tell me that's what you did and whether it shows that "hypocrite" was never the right description of her situation in the first place. Once you've got the case, give me a near-miss variant that changes exactly one feature and flips the verdict to no-standing, and say why that one change is doing all the work.

Then the part I actually care about. Walk me through how three different takes on this come out: (1) the view that hypocrisy undercuts standing because blame is a form of moral address that presupposes the blamer and the blamed stand on equal footing with respect to the norm; (2) the view that hypocrisy is irrelevant to standing and only bears on whether the criticism is true or warranted; (3) a test one of my classmates proposed, which I'm not sure about — that the question is just whether the person being blamed could reasonably demand that she not be blamed, i.e. whether the hypocrite's blaming violates some right of the wrongdoer's, so that hypocrisy is really a defense the wrongdoer can raise or waive rather than a defect in the blamer. Say which of these your case embarrasses, and be honest if it embarrasses all three. Also keep separate, and keep straight throughout: standing to blame vs. standing to sanction or punish; the warrant of the criticism vs. the standing to voice it; and blaming as an act of address vs. blaming as an attitude or belief you hold. I want to know which of those distinctions the case actually tests, and whether pulling them apart changes anything. Finish by saying how someone who holds the strict view from (1) would attack your case — either by denying the verdict or by redescribing the situation so the feature you think is doing the work turns out to be a red herring. No citations, no reading list, don't guess at titles or dates; if you name anyone, only where the name is doing real argumentative work, and think hard before you commit to a verdict on which feature is load-bearing.
