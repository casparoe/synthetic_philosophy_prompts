I have been reading a popular book on freedom whose central chapter I will paraphrase below, because I want you to assess the summary rather than the book. Negative liberty, it says, is the absence of actual interference by other people; positive liberty is the absence of internal obstacles to acting on one's authentic or rational will; and republican liberty is a recent revival of the first ideal, adding only that a free person must also be a virtuous citizen. On this account, Isaiah Berlin's contribution was to show that positive liberty is inherently authoritarian, since anyone who says you must be free in the sense of being your true self licenses the state to coerce you into that self, so a pluralistic society must settle for negative liberty alone. The book then proposes a taxonomy it calls modal liberty: negative liberty concerns actual interference, positive liberty concerns possible internal obstacles, and republican liberty concerns possible external interference. It concludes that the three ideals are ultimately the same thing, namely the absence of constraints on the will, so the long debate is mostly terminological and the only real question is how much interference a state may use.

How much of this is fair? Please go claim by claim. For each, tell me what a careful theorist in the field would accept, what they would regard as a common simplification rather than an error, and what is simply wrong or a conflation. I am especially interested in whether the modal taxonomy is a genuine third position or a restatement of one of the others, whether the charge that positive liberty is inherently authoritarian is a fair reading of the tradition or a polemical exaggeration, and whether the final conclusion follows from the accurate parts of the summary. If the summary is unfair, say what a defender of each of the three ideals would object to first. Keep the whole answer under 900 words.
