I write literary fiction, and a recurring problem in revision is that a character learns something partial — she overhears a fragment of a conversation, gets a garbled phone message, reads a note that says only that if she is going to the party, it is at the lake house — and then has to act. I need a repeatable procedure for deciding how she should revise her beliefs in such scenes, grounded in what the philosophy of probability actually says about updating on conditional information, but usable by a novelist with no math beyond arithmetic, in about ten minutes per scene. The core puzzle I am trying to operationalize is the one I know as the Judy Benjamin problem: a person knows she is in one of four situations, formed by two distinctions — Red sector or Blue sector, and Headquarters or Second Company — starts with equal credence in all four, and then receives a garbled message whose only recoverable content is a conditional: if she is in the Red sector, then she is at Headquarters. There is no uncontroversial answer to how she should update, and I want a procedure that is honest about that while still telling me what to do on the page. The procedure should tell me, in order, what to fix, what to leave alone, and when the theory itself says there is no single right answer and I have to choose for dramatic reasons and then be consistent. It should handle at least: (a) a message that is a conditional, like the one above; (b) a message that is a disjunction, like that she is either at the lake house or the cabin; (c) a message whose source is unreliable, so I only partly trust it. Please write the procedure as a checklist with a short explanation of why each step is there, then run it on two scenes: one where a character hears that if the meeting is in the east wing, then the ledger is in the study, and one where a character hears, from a source she trusts about seventy percent, that the meeting is in the east wing. For each, give me the posterior in plain numbers, say what a reader would find psychologically plausible, and flag the step where I am making a values choice rather than a factual one. The procedure should also say what it cannot do — where the philosophy is unsettled and I should just pick a convention and stick to it. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question. Write in American English.
