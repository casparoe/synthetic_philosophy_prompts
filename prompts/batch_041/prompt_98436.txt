I'm on the board of our county heritage trust and we've got a decision Thursday that's turned into a referendum on what history is even for, and I have to circulate a memo before teh vote. Short version: we have money for one flagship project for the bicentennial, and two proposals are on the table.

Proposal A is the safe one — the gentry, the magistrates, the turnpike, the courtroom, built out of the estate papers and assize records we already hold, curated by a professional, tells the "story of the county" the way the tourist board likes it.

Proposal B is a microhistory. One of our curators found a person in the records who otherwise leaves no trace at all: a woman who shows up only in a poor-law settlement examination, a couple of pages where a parish clerk wrote down her life — where she was born, the places she was sent into service, who she married — because the overseers needed to know which parish had to pay for her if she ever fell destitute. That examination plus a few other fragments is everything we have. The curator wants to hang the whole bicentennial on her, on the argument that you only ever reach ordinary people through the exceptional traces the state happened to keep, and that going deep into one obscure life tells you more about how the county actually worked than any panorama of its great men.

The board is split down the middle. The skeptics say she's documented precisely because she was unusual — she got tangled up in the poor law, she talked, someone bothered to write her down — so she's a biased sample dressed up as "the people," and if we really want to know how most people lived we should be doing quantitative social history, wages and rents and mortality, not one woman's story. The curator's camp says that's backwards: the aggregate is the artifact, since somebody chose those categories in the first place, and the "typical" only ever becomes visible as a departure from a norm — you learn the rule from the exception. Our mission statement, helpfully, promises both to "preserve the record of the county" and to "tell the story of all its people," which is no help at all.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. Recommend a concrete decision — fund A, fund B, or a hybrid if you can actually defend one — say what conditions if any attach, and give reasons the board can act on and argue with. Take the losing side's strongest point seriously instead of flattening it, and be explicit about what each choice commits us to regarding how we can know the past at all: if we go with the single life, what are we claiming; if we go with the aggregate, what do we give up.
