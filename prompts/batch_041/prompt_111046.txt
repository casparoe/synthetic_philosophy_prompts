I am a fourth-year PhD student in behavioural economics, and I have been asked to run a single session of a departmental methods workshop for a mixed group of graduate students in economics and philosophy: twenty-four people, seventy-five minutes, one seminar room, no devices. My aim is not to explain escalation of commitment but to put the group inside a structure in which escalation is the locally reasonable thing to do, so that they run into the trap themselves and discover that it need not involve any failure of reasoning on anyone's part. What I want from you is a fully specified, runnable design: rules, materials, payoff scheme, timing, facilitator script in outline, and a debrief, together with a candid account of how the first run will go wrong.

Constraints I am treating as fixed. No real money changes hands, and no participant may finish with a negative balance. I plan to distribute tokens at the start, with each participant's remaining tokens at the end converting into raffle tickets for one modest prize; tell me whether you think that conversion is strong enough to make the incentives bite, and what to change if it is not. The stakes must be legible to someone who has never seen an auction: the prize, the increment, and who pays what should be stateable in two sentences. The session has to run three short variants within the hour, so that the group compares structures rather than merely playing once.

The provisional structure below is mine; redesign it if it is unsound, but tell me why.

Variant A, baseline. A prize worth 100 tokens goes to the highest bidder; both the highest and the second-highest bidder pay what they bid; increments of 10; a hard three-minute clock.

Variant B. Identical to A, except that each bidder privately draws a budget ceiling, roughly a third of players receiving a low ceiling and two thirds a high one. Ceilings are never revealed, and a bidder who cannot cover the next bid is out. I want the group to see that the escalation in A was sustained by what they did not know about one another, and that common knowledge of the ceilings would have collapsed the auction before it began.

Variant C. Identical to A, but once the price passes the value of the prize, either of the two current leaders may call an armistice: the auction stops, the prize is void, and each of the two pays half of their own bid, with the caller paying a small additional penalty. Note the asymmetry I expect this to generate, and check my arithmetic. At bids of 110 and 100 the leader nets minus 10 by continuing, while the trailing bidder nets minus 100; under armistice they pay 55 and 50. So the trailing bidder gains immediately by calling, whereas the leader prefers to continue until the price exceeds twice the prize, at which point the leader also wants out. I want to see whether an exit that is available, cheap, and jointly beneficial is taken, who is resented for taking it, and whether the leading bidder tries to buy the trailing bidder's forbearance. If you think the rule should require mutual consent, say so and show what that changes.

For each variant give me the exact rules, whatever board or sheet the facilitator needs, what the facilitator says and conspicuously does not say, the bidding cadence that will actually produce escalation inside three minutes, and what would count as a clean result. Tell me how to handle collusion between the two leaders, a player who refuses to bid at all, and a player who bids wildly to make a point rather than to win. I am also unsure whether the floor at zero is a kindness or a distortion, since limited liability may simply relocate the escalation rather than damp it; I would like your view.

Then the debrief. It should map particular moments in the three variants onto particular parts of the idea: the difference between a sunk cost that ought to be ignored and a position from which withdrawing is worse than continuing; the fact that the losing bidder pays for nothing; why the pair ends up badly off while neither individual's next bid is obviously a mistake; what the private ceilings in B do to backward induction; and why the armistice in C is a settlement problem rather than a generosity problem. Keep the language free of technical vocabulary until the group has named the phenomenon themselves; I do not want the name of the phenomenon supplied before the debrief.

Two things I especially want from you. First, an honest statement of which feature of escalation the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that a seventy-five-minute game can deliver the structural trap but not the self-justification and audience effects that are supposed to drive escalation over months, and I want the debrief to concede this rather than let the group over-read what they felt. Second, a list of the ways the first run will predictably fail, with a fix for each; I would guess the group treats the whole thing as a joke and stops responding to the incentives, the clock is too slow to reach the interesting region, the raffle conversion is too weak, and the facilitator leaks information by reacting to bids.
