Question: Rawls's later work allows religious arguments in the public political forum provided that public reasons for the same position are given in due course (the so-called proviso). Consider this case: a state legislator votes for and publicly defends a law restricting commercial activity on Sundays, explicitly grounding her vote in her church's teaching about the Sabbath. Two years later, in a committee report, she defends the same law on grounds of workers' rest and the health of small businesses. Is the proviso's requirement met here? And does the proviso, so understood, answer the objection that public reason unfairly burdens religious citizens? Answer both parts.

Student's answer:

The proviso is Rawls's response to the charge that public reason silences religious citizens. In the earlier version of his view, religious arguments were excluded from the public political forum altogether; in the later version he allows them, provided that public reasons for the same position are supplied in due course. So the legislator's case looks fine at first: she gave a religious reason up front, and the public reason came later. Order doesn't matter, only that the public reason eventually arrives.

But the proviso is weaker than it looks. Rawls still treats the duty of civility as a moral duty rather than a legal one, so the legislator can't be punished for failing to give public reasons; the worst that happens is that other citizens can criticize her. That's a genuine concession to religious citizens, since the constraint isn't coercive.

Where the proviso really fails is sincerity. If the public reason in the committee report is just a rationalization, the proviso is satisfied on paper but the position hasn't really been justified to citizens who don't share the legislator's faith. Rawls doesn't say much about this, which seems like a serious problem, since a legislator can always produce some public-sounding reason after the fact.

Also, Rawls applies the proviso not just to legislators but to the background culture as well — churches and universities have to eventually translate their internal doctrines into public reason once they enter political debate. That's a much bigger burden than it sounds, and it's why critics like Wolterstorff say public reason is asymmetric, restricting religious citizens while leaving secular comprehensive doctrines untouched. Habermas, by contrast, agrees with Rawls that it's the citizens themselves who must do the translating, and only adds that the state should encourage it.
