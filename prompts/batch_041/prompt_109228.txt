I teach an upper-level seminar on the definition of life and its borderline cases, and I am drafting the final examination. I have read rationalist blogs and forums for close to two decades, and the habit they drilled into me — asking what a concept is for before asking which cases fall under it — is the single thing I most want the exam to test. The problem is that the obvious essay question on this material can be answered by recitation: state the working definition a NASA committee settled on for exobiology (roughly, a self-sustaining chemical system able to undergo Darwinian evolution), observe that it excludes viruses, note that some would prefer an autopoietic or thermodynamic account, conclude that the concept is irreducibly fuzzy. That is a competent B answer, producible from memory by any student or model, and I want a question that separates it from an A.

To make that possible, I am stipulating a test of my own and asking students to work with it rather than report on it. Call it the lineage-and-maintenance test. On this test a system is alive only if both of the following hold: first, it sustains a bounded, far-from-equilibrium organization through its own machinery, drawing on gradients in its environment; second, it is a node in a lineage, descending from and able to contribute to a population whose variations are heritable and differentially perpetuated. I want the question to put this test under pressure with three cases: a bench-built chemical system that replicates with heritable variation under selection but whose lineage begins in the laboratory and shares no ancestor with Earth's biosphere; a virus; and a sterile hybrid organism, such as a mule. The question should force students to say which of these the test classifies in a way they find unacceptable, whether the test is a discovery about life or a stipulation about the word, and what the concept is being asked to do — sort a natural kind, license a generalization, or mark a boundary for some practical purpose such as planetary protection or moral standing.

Please write the essay question itself (forty-five minutes, a single question, no citation of literature required), a model answer of roughly 750 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth four marks each, twenty in total. For each point, add one line naming how candidates typically lose it. Close with a short note on which single move should separate an A from a B — including what to do with a candidate who argues that the stipulated test is the wrong tool altogether and defends that claim well.
