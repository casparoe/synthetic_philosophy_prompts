I'm putting together a two-page (call it 1,200 words) proposal for a small summer bursary in my department, on how norms like duelling and slavery actually die, and I keep flip-flopping on how to frame it, so I'd rather you just write the thing. The background, roughly: I got into an argument about this at a birthday dinner and it's been rattling round my head since. The standard trio of explanations for a moral revolution seems to be (a) argument — someone shows the practice is inconsistent with something the society already accepts; (b) interests — the payoffs to keeping the practice up shift, so the people who enforced it stop; (c) honour — upholding the norm stops being a source of esteem and starts being a source of contempt. Bernard Williams somewhere (I think one of his last books, though I might be garbling which) suggests duelling died out without anyone refuting it — more a matter of the social scaffolding going than of anybody being persuaded — and Appiahs honour book makes the case that the big ones go through honour rather than reason. What I want to do is refuse to treat the three as rival answers and instead ask about their *order*. My own machinery, and I'm fairly sure nobody is using it in quite this form: split the death of a norm into three separate events — avowal death (no one defends it publicly any more), conduct death (people stop doing it) and esteem death (the honour gradient flips, so upholding it now costs you standing instead of earning it). Then the question becomes: can esteem death happen without a prior shift in the interests of the people who were enforcing the norm? If it can't, Appiah's honour story is an interests story in disguise and argument is epiphenominal; if it can, honour is a genuinely independent engine and argument sits downstream of it. Second bit of machinery, also mine: the testimony test. My hunch is that arguments against a practice only become usable once the victims of it become admissible sources of knowledge about what the practice does — and that admissibility is itself an honour-or-interests phenomenon rather than an argumentative achievement, which would make the whole "it was reasoned out in the end" story backwards. The project would try to say which of these is doing the real work, using only cases I already know well enough to reason about without living in the library: duelling in Britain, the slave trade and its abolition, footbinding, and sati.

Please write it in British English, and give me committed answers rather than a menu — pick which engine you think is actually doing the work, commit to an ordering, and defend the choice even where it looks shaky. Don't give me a reading list or references; refer to positions by name or by description and I'll fill the citations in myself, what I need is the shape of the argument.

Use these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: "The question" (about 150 words — stated so that it has a determinate answer and so I'd know what would settle it either way); "Why the existing answers leave it open" (about 250 — what interests-explanations and honour-explanations each fail to rule out, and why the three-engine framing is not just a relabelling of what everyone already says); "What I would actually do" (about 350 — the method, month by month, including how I'd tell avowal death from conduct death from esteem death in a case where all three land within a few decades of each other, what I'd do where the historical record is contested, and how the testimony test is meant to be applied rather than just asserted); "What would count as a result and what would count as failure" (about 200 — including at least one way the project could fail that isn't just me running out of time, e.g. if the three deaths turn out to be inseparable even in principle); and "Scope and honest risks" (about 150 — what's too big for a summer and what I'd cut first).
