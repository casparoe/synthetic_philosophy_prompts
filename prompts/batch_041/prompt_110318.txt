I've been reading Aristotle on money and exchange, and I'm trying to figure out what my own gut verdicts add up to. Flipping a house after renovating it: fine. Buying a house from an elderly seller who doesn't know what it's worth and reselling at market: not fine, feels like theft. A bank charging 6% on a mortgage: fine. A payday lender charging 400% APR: not fine, should be illegal. Lending my brother $5,000 and charging him interest: off, though I can't say why — it's not the amount, it's the relationship. Buying a stock and selling it a week later at a profit: fine. Short-selling a stock because I think the company is badly run: fine, though it feels more predatory. Buying a painting at a garage sale for $20 when I know it's worth $20,000: if I'm a casual buyer, fine; if I'm a dealer who spotted it, feels like taking advantage. Selling my concert ticket at face value when I can't go: fine. Scalping it for 4x: not fine. Getting interest on a government bond: fine. Charging my tenant late fees: fine if it's in the lease, not fine if I'm just squeezing them. My tentative rule is: profit from exchange is fine unless you're taking advantage of someone's ignorance or desperation, or unless you're in a relationship where you owe them something other than a fair deal. But that rule doesn't explain why I'm fine with the bank making money off my mortgage and not fine with the payday lender — both are anonymous, both charge interest, and the only difference I can articulate is the rate, which seems like a weak basis for a moral distinction. Is there a better principle? Does it need to distinguish use-value from exchange-value, or natural from unnatural acquisition, or is it about vulnerability, or relationships, or something else? And what does it say about arbitrage — buying a good in one market and selling it in another at a higher price with no transformation — and about a landlord who buys a building, evicts the tenants, and converts it to condos?
