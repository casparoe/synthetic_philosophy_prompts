I write the lives of people who are mostly dead, and I keep meeting a pattern I can describe but cannot name. Two people perform what I can only call the same act — the same intentions, the same conduct, the same effects in the world — and one is remembered as a benefactor while the other is remembered as a cautionary tale. Nothing in the world differs; only the reading differs. A voyage that one generation praises as courage the next condemns as plunder, and the third, with no new facts in hand, praises again. A confidence offered as generosity is received as condescension, and the giver is blamed for a slight she did not intend and could not have foreseen. A disclosure that, had it been believed, would have made its author a hero, and that, being disbelieved, made him a crank.

I know there is a familiar idea in philosophy to the effect that we hold people answerable for matters outside their control — that the driver who strikes a child is judged more harshly than the one who arrives home, though both drove the same way. That idea I take myself to understand, and it is about what happens. What puzzles me is that in my cases nothing happens. Only the description changes, and it can change again: the same person can be acquitted and condemned and acquitted by the same community across a century, for the same deeds, on the same evidence. Sometimes the shift lies in the audience's moral standards, sometimes in its interests, and sometimes in nothing I can identify at all.

Does this second thing have a name of its own? Is it a distinct kind of bad luck — luck in what an act is taken to mean, rather than in what it causes — or is it the familiar phenomenon in disguise, with the interpretive setting counted as one more circumstance beyond the agent's control? If it is distinct, I want it defined and marked off from its neighbours: from the luck of outcomes, from the luck of circumstances, from the luck of the character one was born with, and from plain moral disagreement. I want its edges as well: whether being misread in one's own lifetime is the same phenomenon as being reinterpreted after death, and whether a society's changing its mind about an old act is bad luck for the actor at all, or something else entirely. A bare name would not help me; I need the thing drawn precisely enough that I can tell when I am looking at it and when I am looking at something else.
