I have just spent four hours arguing with a friend, and I would like to know whether the thing we kept circling has an established name, or, failing that, whether it can at least be given a careful definition.

The argument began with a case neither of us could leave alone. Two people who have never met and will never meet again are each asked, separately, to choose between two options. For each of them, taking the first option is better whatever the other does — this is not in dispute — and yet if both take the first option, both end up worse off than if both had taken the second. My friend insisted that the second option is the one to take, and his reason was not that his choice would affect the other's, but that the other is the same kind of reasoner as he is, so that his own choice tells him what the other will do. I said that this is not a claim about influence at all, and that he was treating two separate decisions as though they were a single event.

We then tried to find the edges of his move, and that is where I lost the thread.

First, the move seemed to depend on the two parties being alike. But alike in what respect? They are not alike in body, memory, or information. If being the same kind of reasoner means only that both reason competently, then the move applies to every competent reasoner, and would license treating the choice of any stranger — or of an adversary who also reasons well — as a sign of what I am about to do, which cannot be right. If it means something stronger, that the two are running the same procedure on the same inputs, then it almost never holds, and it fails immediately when the two know different things. So the premise is either so weak that it covers everyone or so strong that it covers no one, and the argument needs a middle condition that neither of us could state.

Second, I built a case to test the middle. Two firms of the same type, using the same procedure, must each decide whether to build a plant; if both build, both lose. One firm holds a private report showing that the market is larger than the other supposes. Now the shared procedure returns different answers for the two of them, and the reasoning that my choice predicts yours yields nothing: the firm without the report cannot conclude that the other will refrain, and cannot even work out what it would do in the other's position, since in that position it would have the report.

Third, and this is the part I most want a name for: when I pressed him on what two parties should do when they share a procedure but not their information, he said that each should do what he would do if he were the other. That sounds like an answer until you notice that it is circular. Which of them is 'the other'? There are two answers, they differ, and the prescription therefore depends on a choice of vantage point that the argument itself gives no way to make.

So: is there an established name for this family of arguments — arguments that reach a conclusion about what I should do from a premise about what a class of people like me will do — and, separately, a name for the failure I kept running into, in which the relevant class is either too broad to carry information or too narrow to have members? I know the 'what if everyone did that' style of reasoning in ethics, and I know the ordinary two-person puzzle it is grafted onto, but neither is what I am after: the ethical version is about the form of a rule rather than about what another party will actually do, and the puzzle itself says nothing about how to reason when the parties are alike. If nothing established covers it, please coin something — but I care far less about the label than about the carving. What exactly is the phenomenon, what is it not, where is the line between the case where the reasoning works and the case where it collapses, and is the variant in which the parties share a procedure but not their information a special case of something already named, or a problem in its own right?
