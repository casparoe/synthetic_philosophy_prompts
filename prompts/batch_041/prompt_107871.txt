I've built an argument in a seminar that proves something I can't accept, and now I can't find the leak. Background: I'm a mild verificationist, i.e. I think any truth is in principle verifiable -- for any true claim, there could in principal be an inquiry that establishes it. That's the claim I most want to keep; give it up and I'm back to being a realist about truth, which is the view I was trying to avoid. Here's the argument.

(1) The end-state of inquiry is factive: if someone knows (has verified) p, then p is true.
(2) Whoever knows a conjunction knows each conjunct.
(3) For every truth p, it's possible that someone knows p.
(4) There are truths nobody knows and never will -- the exact number of hairs on some long-dead stranger's head, say. So for some p: p, and no one ever knows p.
(5) By (3), it's possible that someone knows that whole conjunction.
(6) By (2), that someone knows p, and knows that no one ever knows p.
(7) By (1), their knowing that no one ever knows p entails that no one ever knows p -- so in particular they don't know p.
(8) But by (6) they do. Contradiction.
(9) So no truth of the form p-and-nobody-ever-knows-p exists. Every truth is known by someone.

I can't accept (9). A friend says (3) is the culprit and I should just give it up; I'd rather not. So which step goes, why did it look fine, and what does each repair cost me? I'm most interested in whether (3) can be saved by restricting its range on a principled basis rather than just amputating whatever the paradox happens to bite on -- and if I do restrict it, does what's left still do the anti-realist work I wanted? Also, does it matter whether the knower in (6) is the same person as the one (3) invokes, and whether I read the second conjunct as not-known-now or never-known? Tell me where the real cost lies.
