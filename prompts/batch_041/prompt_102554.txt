So here's my situation: I'm a science journalist — cognition, philosophy of mind, that beat — and I'm applying for a mid-career fellowship for science writers, the kind where a university basically pays you to sit around for a year and think. They want a two-page research proposal and it's supposed to be a real project, not just "I'll write some articles." Half reporting, half actual conceptual work. I have a half-formed idea and I need you to turn it into something with a spine.

The idea is sphexishness. You know the thing — Hofstadter's wasp, the digger wasp that hauls a cricket back to its burrow, goes inside to check the coast is clear, and if you nudge the cricket while it's in there it just comes back out, drags the cricket over, and checks again, on a loop, forever. The point of the story is that behavior that looks purposeful is actually a rigid script, and then comes the creepy implication that we might be exactly the same, just with fancier loops. Dennett leans on it, Hofstadter frets about whether he himself is sphexish, and it's basically the folk image of "we're all meat robots." I'm told the wasp anecdote itself may not even hold up as biology anymore, which is fine by me — the concept has a life of its own and I don't want the project hanging on whether one insect does the thing.

Here's what bugs me about all of it. Everyone treats sphexishness as a fact about the creature — this one is mechanical, that one is free. I think that's the wrong shelf to put it on. Rigidity only ever shows up when you disturb the setup. The wasp isn't sphexish in the abstract, it's sphexish relative to a certain kind of intervention, and my hunch is that every system is sphexish given the right intervention, which either makes the whole notion useless or makes it interesting. So the question I actually want to answer is roughly: is sphexishness a property of an agent, or a property of the coupling between an agent, a world, and an observer who's doing the poking? And if you relocate it like that, does the free-will worry the word was invented to raise get dissolved, or does it just get shoved somewhere worse — like, do we now need a notion of genuine flexibility that isn't itself just sphexishness one level up?

I've got the seed of a second idea too, and I don't know if it's good: maybe you have to break perturbation down by kind. A system might fold under object-substitution (swap the cricket, it keeps going) but handle interruption fine, or cope with novel goals but not with being watched, etc. If the verdict on the same creature flips depending on which kind of disturbance you use, then there may be no single fact of the matter about how sphexish anything is — which is either the punchline or a reductio, and I can't tell which yet.

What I need from you: a proper two-page proposal. State the question so it has a determinate answer instead of being a vibe. Say why the standard framing — the mechanical-versus-free, degrees-of-freedom, comparative-cognition version of this — leaves it genuinely open and hasn't already settled it. Lay out the method, and be honest about why the conceptual route rather than the obvious one (the obvious one is more wasp experiments or more animal-cognition data, and I want to say why that won't reach the question I care about). Tell me what a real result would even look like here, and what would count as the project failing — I'd rather know now. And keep the scope realistic for one year of a journalist's attention, since I'm not going to be in a lab.

Don't give me a reading list or citations, I'll fill those in myself and half of them would be guesses anyway. Just make the argument for why this is worth a year.
