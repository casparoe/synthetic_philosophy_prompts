Having retired last year from a career in experimental physics, I have spent my new leisure reading philosophy of science, and I have hit an argument I cannot properly assess. A popular introduction I recently picked up summarizes the underdetermination of theory by evidence along these lines: "Duhem and Quine proved that no hypothesis is ever tested in isolation, since any failed prediction can always be blamed on the auxiliary assumptions instead; it follows that the evidence can never decide between rival theories, and that the choice between them is settled by convention, by taste in simplicity, or by social consensus rather than by nature. Lorentz's ether theory and Einstein's special relativity make exactly the same predictions, yet we call one true and the other superseded -- which shows that observation has no power to adjudicate, and that the older hope of letting experiment choose between theories was always naive." My instincts tell me this is unfair to the position, but I no longer trust those instincts, since a lifetime of laboratory work left me convinced that better experiments settle disputes, and I am not certain how far that conviction survives contact with the philosophy. Please audit the summary sentence by sentence: tell me which claims a philosopher of science would accept as fair, which are oversimplifications or conflations of distinct theses, and which are simply wrong. Then tell me whether the sweeping conclusion the passage draws -- that theory choice is arbitrary or merely social -- actually follows from whatever is correct in it, and how a defender of the underdetermination thesis would answer the charge that working scientists plainly do have good, non-arbitrary reasons for the theories they adopt.
