I'm putting together a seminar that jumps from the history of social choice straight to the modern stuff, and I want an exercise that makes the students reason rather than recite. So: imagine the Marquis de Condorcet — jury theorem, voting paradox, and a real faith that decent institutions let the truth win out — got brought back and told about Gibbard–Satterthwaite, i.e. that any voting rule with three or more options that isn't dictatorial can be gamed by strategic voting. What would he actually make of it? Reason as him, not about him — no potted summary of the theorem, and no potted summary of what he really wrote either. Where does he dig in, what does the result force him to give up, and does he treat it as a footnote to his optimism or as the thing that sinks it? Press hard on the tension with his own jury theorem, thats the bit that's always bugged me. Write it as straight continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold or tables — like a long email to me.
