I hold a default-opacity view of the citizen's relation to the state: the state may collect data about a person only under a specific, publicly avowed purpose, and must re-justify each collection on its own terms rather than appeal to its possible future usefulness. I am tempted by a stronger, symmetry-based version — the mirror rule — on which the state may collect a category of information about a citizen only if the citizen can collect the same category of information about the state. Tell me what would count against the position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish the objections a committed defender would themselves concede from those they would brush aside. In particular, assess whether the view can absorb the charge that opacity is unevenly distributed — sheltering the powerful while burdening those who must prove their entitlements — and whether the mirror rule does independent work or merely restates a demand for government transparency. Say what the position predicts that purpose-limitation and contextual-integrity approaches do not, whether it can account for why the German, American, and Chinese arrangements around state access to personal data diverge as they do, and whether it has insulated itself against disconfirmation by qualification.
