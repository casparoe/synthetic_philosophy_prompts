I sit on the technical advisory group that a national psychiatric association convenes when it revises its diagnostic manual. We handle perhaps six proposals a year: a working group proposes a new category, or a change to an existing one, and we produce the assessment that goes forward. The full manual is rewritten only every decade or so, but between revisions there is a shorter field-review track for categories that clinicians are already using informally, and that is where most of my work lands. The decisions are consequential. A category in the manual determines what insurers reimburse, what support plans schools will write, what training programmes teach, and how people describe themselves to one another. Our reports are published, advocacy organisations read them closely, and although the assembly has never rejected one of our recommendations outright, it has twice sent the reasoning back to be redone.

I have come to think we handle one class of risk badly. When we assess a proposal we ask the familiar questions — is there a coherent clinical picture, is there evidence of impairment, can it be distinguished from neighbouring categories, does it predict course or treatment response — and we ask them as though we were discovering divisions that were already there. But a category of people is not a category of minerals. People learn the label. They come to understand themselves through it, they reorganise their lives around it, they form communities and institutions around it, and in doing so they change what the label picks out. A category that begins as a description can end as a project. Sometimes this is exactly what we want: recognition relieves suffering, and a name is often a precondition of getting help. Sometimes it is a harm we have authored, because the category creates a niche — clinics, forums, self-tests, a market — and the niche then sustains and widens the category, so that a decade later the population being diagnosed looks nothing like the population the original evidence described. This feedback is usually called a looping effect, and I have never seen us do anything systematic about it. At best it appears as a caveat in the discussion section. At worst it is not mentioned, because we have no method for mentioning it.

I would like you to give my group a procedure we can actually run. Assume the people applying it are clinicians and methodologists, not philosophers; assume we have about six months, five members, and no capacity to commission longitudinal studies; assume the output must be a written recommendation that will be published, defended in public, and read by people who want a particular answer. Do not supply citations or a reading list — the procedure should stand on the descriptions given here, and I do not want it padded with references I cannot check.

The deliverable is a decision procedure, in words, that we can apply to a proposal in order, together with the commentary needed to use it honestly. It should include:

- The questions to ask, in the order we should ask them, with a short statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what an insufficient answer looks like. Say plainly which questions can stop the process with a refusal and which cannot, and say what we should do when the evidence on a given question is simply absent rather than unfavourable.

- A dedicated section on the feedback risk: what we should treat as evidence that a proposed category is likely to loop, which of those signals are available to us before the category is released and which are not, and the concrete design choices that reduce the risk. These might include how the criteria are worded, where the threshold is set, who is permitted to make the diagnosis, whether the category is dimensional or categorical, whether it is defined by symptoms or by a person's self-understanding, and whether it carries a scheduled review. For each lever, say what it buys and what it costs.

- A statement of where the procedure must hand the decision to a human being because the question is one of values rather than evidence, and what exactly that person is being asked to decide.

- Worked applications to three proposals. First, a category for a grief-like reaction that persists far beyond the culturally expected period and causes marked impairment, where the clinical case is strong and the worry is precisely that we would be putting a name to something people already live through. Second, a category for a very common pattern of exhaustion and detachment tied to work, which many clinicians regard as a normal response to abnormal circumstances and which unions and advocacy groups support because a formal diagnosis would force employers to act. Third, a category for a compulsive relationship with a recent digital technology, where the technology is changing faster than the manual can be revised and where clinics, apps and self-tests are already forming around the proposed name.

- At least one of those applications should end in a recommendation I will not want to hear, and you should say why the procedure forces it. For what it is worth, my own sympathies lie with the clinicians who want to widen access and with the advocacy groups, so I expect to be made uncomfortable at least once.

- A closing account of what the procedure cannot do, and of the failure modes we should expect: the ways it will be gamed by people who want a category added or blocked, and the ways it will misfire when the clinical evidence is strong but the feedback risk cannot be measured within any horizon we can plan around.

Reach a verdict in each application rather than surveying the considerations. Where the procedure tells us to defer, say what would have to become true for us to revisit the decision, who should be responsible for noticing, and how we should word the deferral so that it does not become a permanent no by inattention.
