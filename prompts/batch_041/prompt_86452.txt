Okay so I need help with a dinner-party arguemnt I got stuck in the middle of. Two friends, Sam and Priya, went at each other about karma for about forty minutes and both of them kept turning to me like I was going to rule on it, and I couldn't, becuase honestly I don't know enough. So I'm outsourcing. I want a ruling on the actual moves, not a general essay about karma.

Context: Sam grew up in a Hindu family and still thinks karma is basically right as a moral idea, though he admits he's fuzzy on the cosmology. Priya did a philosophy degree and thinks karma is a bad theory of desert with nice packaging.

Sam's opening was that karma is the only theory that says why suffering is *deserved*. If someone is born sick or gets hit by a truck, the view says it's the ripening of something they did. That's what makes it just instead of just bad luck, and he likes that it doesn't have to fudge anything the way arguments about God and evil do — the ledger just balances eventually.

Priya's first shot: "eventually" is doing all the work. If it balances in a later life, the one who suffers isn't the one who acted. No memories, no personality in common, just a causal chain with a person at each end. She said we don't make a man pay his father's debts out of his own body, and a causal chain between two lives is no better than a causal chain between two people.

Sam said that test is too strong, and I could tell he thought he'd landed one. Nobody remembers being three, nobody at sixty is the same person they were at twenty-five, but we still lock up the sixty-year-old for what he did at twenty-five. If memory and sameness of character were the bar, you couldn't punish anyone for anything a few years later. So karma only needs the kind of continuity we already accept inside a single life.

Priya had two replies. First: inside a life you can *tell* the person what they're being punished for, and she thinks that's not a decoration, it's most of the difference between punishing someone and just hurting them. If the books balance but the sufferer can never connect the pain to the deed, then it isn't desert, it's a machine that makes pain and has a moral story stapled to it. Second: the theory can't lose. Any distribution of suffering at all can be read as somebody's past deed ripening. A view that can never be surprised isn't explaining the world, it's decorating it.

Sam's comeback: the second point is a scientific standard and karma isn't a scientific hypothesis, it's more like the idea that your character is your fate — you don't test it, you live it. And he turned the first point around: the fact that you can't see the ledger is exactly why karma should make you humble about other people's suffering instead of smug. If you could see it you'd be insufferable.

Priya said that's the tell — unfalsifiability is being sold as a virtue and uncommunicativeness is being sold as humility, and both of those are precisely what would make a theory of desert useless. She said fine, call it a practice of humility, just stop calling it a theory of what people deserve.

Two more things got thrown in. Priya said that if there's no self there's nobody left to deserve anything, and Sam said that's a Buddhist problem, he's not a Buddhist, and if you drop the persisting self then karma really is just a causal chain, so she'd been attacking a theory neither of them holds. And Sam brought up something I didn't follow about merit — apparently there's a practice where you can hand over the good karma from your own good deeds to somebody else, like a dead relative, to help them out. He said that if karma were pure desert that should be impossible, you can't pay someone else's debt with your merit, so either the practice is confused or karma was never tit-for-tat desert to begin with. Priya said he was arguing against himself. He said no, that was the strongest thing he had.

Can you go through it move by move — the opening, the continuity objection, the sixty-year-old reply, the two-part reply, the humility comeback, and then the no-self and merit bits — and say who's right at each step, where they're talking past each other, and what both of them are missing? Rate each move if you can. I really don't need a reading list, just the adjudication.
