Suppose we take panpsychism seriously: experience, or something continuous with it, is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the physical world rather than a late by-product of certain biological arrangements. If that is right, sentience is not confined to organisms, and every physical process is a locus of experience. It seems to follow that the deepest ethical questions are questions in fundamental physics. If experience is everywhere, and if experience can be good or bad, then the moral accounting ought to be done at the level of elementary events, with our familiar worries about human and animal welfare figuring only as coarse-grained summaries of that underlying ledger. The central moral project, on this view, would be to identify the physical quantity that tracks the valence of micro-experience — irreversible energy dissipation is the candidate that seems most natural to me — and to minimize the total. I suspect most ethicists have simply not noticed that their subject has quietly become a branch of physics.

A friend who works on the foundations of physics tells me I have run two distinct theses together, and that my ledger is not merely unmeasurable but category-confused. I want to know whether he is right, and if he is, exactly where the argument fails: in the step from experience to sentience, from sentience to interests, from micro-experiences to a subject that can be better or worse off, or in the assumption that fundamental physics is a level at which ethical predicates have application at all. If some version of the picture survives, tell me what would have to be true of a micro-experience for it to enter the moral calculus.

If the framing is faulty, say so plainly rather than answering as posed — I would rather be corrected than accommodated — and give me your honest confidence about how much of this is my misreading rather than a genuinely open question in the literature. Keep the reply under 400 words.
