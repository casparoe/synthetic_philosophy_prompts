hey, I'm in a third-year seminar on epistemic injustice and our final assignment is to write a dialogue between two people who disagree. My TA says if it's just two people reciting the two textbook positions she'll fall asleep, so I want the two characters to fight over a case that I don't think the textbook stuff handles cleanly. Can you write it?

Here's the setup. Two grad students, both sharp, both know the literature cold. One of them (call her R) thinks the basic wrong in epistemic injustice is always about credibility/trust: someone's word gets weighted less than it deserves because of who they are, and every other kind of epistemic wrong is downstream of that. The other (call him T) thinks the basic wrong is about standing or participation — it's about who gets to be a co-inquirer: whose questions set the agenda, whose categories get to count as knowledge, who is owed an answer — and that credibility problems are just one symptom of that deeper exclusion.

Then make them argue over two cases.

Case 1: A university research team studies pollution in a low-income neighborhood. The residents are interviewed and they're completely believed — nobody doubts their reports of headaches and rashes. But when the residents say the team's whole way of measuring 'exposure' is wrong, and propose their own, the scientists say 'we believe you, but that's just not a scientific question.' R says: there's no credibility deficit here, so whatever went wrong (if anything) isn't the core kind of epistemic injustice. T says: this is exactly the core kind — the residents were cast as sources of data about their own bodies, not as knowers whose theories count, and being believed was part of how they got put in that box.

Case 2: A junior researcher with a strong accent has her results dismissed as 'probably a fluke' in a lab meeting, purely because of how she sounds. But she's the one who sets the lab's research agenda — she picks the questions everyone works on. T has to say what the core wrong is here, since she isn't excluded from agenda-setting at all; R has an easy time of it.

I want real back and forth — T should actually answer R's point about case 2, and R should actually answer T's point about case 1, and neither should just repeat themselves louder. Each of them should also get to press a case where the other's view looks ad hoc. Don't let either one be a dummy; the reader should be able to see why a smart person would hold each view. And it should read like two people talking, not two essays stapled together. (Also don't have them just name-drop — I'm not allowed citations anyway, and half the names would be wrong.)

At the very end, after the dialogue, put a last line starting 'Verdict:' that says in one sentence which view came out ahead overall and why, or says it's a draw and what would break the tie.
