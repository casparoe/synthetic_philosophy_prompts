I'm a retired clinical pharmacologist — thirty years running trials, mostly oncology — and since I stopped working I've been reading alot of philosophy, mainly the ethics of action, which I find weirdly addictive. Here's what I keep turning over. There's this old distinction between doing harm and merely allowing it, and everyone I read seems to assume doing is worse, other things equal. I can see the pull: when you do something to someone you're the author of what happens to them, whereas when you stand back and let a threat take them you're at most a bystander who failed to help, and the harm came from elsewhere. But I can never remember whose doctrine that's supposed to be, and lately I've started to wonder whether the asymmetry can run the other way. My hunch, which you should feel free to tell me is confused, is that being the author of a harm and doing something aren't the same thing, and there ought to be cases where the one who merely allows is more the author of the harm than the one who acts, so the doer comes out looking better. What I'm really after is whether this asymmetry is a structural fact about agency or just a proxy for duty and intention, so please build me a case.

What I want: a matched pair of cases, set somewhere ordinary — a hospital, a lab, an office, nothing sci-fi — in which one person performs a positive action that results in someone's death or serious harm, and a second person, otherwise as alike as you can make them (same knowledge, same resources, both decent, neither of them a monster), merely allows an equivalent harm to occur, and the doer turns out to be the *less* culpable of the two. Constraints: no trolleys, no organ transplants, no drowning children, no villains who kill for money; and the reversal has to fall out of the structure of the two agents' relation to the harm — who authored it, whose agency it flows from — not out of one of them having nicer motives, a smaller number of victims, or a special dispensation. Then do the checks: say which reading of the doing/allowing distinction generates the reversal, what a straightforward defender of the usual asymmetry (doing is worse, other things equal) says about each half of the pair, and what a plain act-consequentialist says; tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if you delete it. I'd also like to hear how a defender of the usual asymmetry would attack my pair — I assume they'd say I smuggled in a duty or an intention somewhere — and whether they'd be right. If it turns out no such pair exists, that's a legitimate answer, but then show me the closest thing and tell me precisely which of my constraints is the one that can't be met and why.

Please think this through carefully, and before you commit, sketch two or three candidate pairs and say why you picked the one you did — I'd rather watch you reject a case than be handed one that only works because you stipulated the answer. Assign yourself the role of someone who has read the literature on doing and allowing closely and is willing to tell me my framing is bad. Where is your reasoning most likely to fail, and what would change your mind — which reading of the author-versus-rescuer business, or which feature of the cases, would flip you to the opposite verdict? And end with a final line in exactly this form: \answer{<short label for the single feature doing the work>}.
