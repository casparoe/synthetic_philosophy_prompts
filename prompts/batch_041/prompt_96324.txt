I cover science and ideas for a UK publication, and I am one of two independent, non-industry members of the technical advisory committee of an aquaculture certification scheme. Our finfish and crustacean welfare standard is up for its five-year revision, the committee votes at the end of the month, and I have to circulate a written opinion setting out how I think we should vote. I would like you to draft it.

Three clauses are in dispute.

First, crustacean slaughter. The draft would require decapod crustaceans to be rendered insensible by a validated method before processing or sale — electrical stunning in water, or a two-stage percussive method — and would end the practice of chilling crabs, lobsters and whole prawns on ice or in ice slurry as the default means of killing them.

Second, eyestalk ablation. The draft would prohibit eyestalk ablation at supplier hatcheries and maturation facilities — the routine removal of one or both eyestalks from female shrimp to induce spawning, standard practice in almost all shrimp maturation facilities worldwide, research and commercial — with a phase-out schedule but no alternative yet validated at commercial scale.

Third, fish at harvest. The draft would require farmed finfish to be stunned before slaughter, percussively or by in-water electrical stunning, with a derogation where no method is validated for a given species, and would require slaughter to be carried out by trained personnel only.

The scientific note we commissioned finds the evidence for nociception in decapods and finfish substantial and growing, but the inference from nociception to suffering genuinely contested. One adviser argues that if we treat that evidence as sufficient for these three clauses, consistency obliges us to extend the same commitments to farmed insects, about which our standard currently says nothing. A second, who advises on climate policy, proposes that we adopt a least-regret rule, choosing whichever requirement has the smallest worst-case regret across the competing hypotheses about which taxa are sentient, rather than waiting for a settled answer. A third insists that welfare obligations should track probability of sentience, so partial evidence should yield partial obligations, limited in both scope and intrusiveness.

Producer representatives counter that the clauses add cost, that no validated stunning protocol exists for several species, and that smallholder suppliers who account for a large share of our shrimp volume would simply lose certification and sell to buyers with no welfare requirements at all — leaving the animals worse off. Our terms of reference require that any recommendation respect religious rites, cultural traditions and regional heritage, which cuts both ways, since some customary handling methods would fall foul of the crustacean clause. For context, the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 treats cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans as animals, but our standard governs suppliers in a dozen countries, several of which recognise nothing of the kind.

Please write the opinion, of roughly nine hundred words, addressed to the committee. It should reach a clear recommendation on each of the three clauses — adopt, adopt with conditions, defer, or reject — with any conditions specified concretely enough to be drafted into the standard, and a time frame for each step. It should state the criteria we ought to use in deciding when evidence is good enough to warrant a welfare requirement at all; that is the part I care most about, and I do not want a general essay on animal sentience. And it should treat the strongest case against your own recommendation honestly rather than as a formality.

Two further requests. Please be explicit about what would change your mind — the evidence, argument or practical finding that would move you to a different recommendation on each clause — and about where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong. And please write in British English, without citations, references or a reading list: I do not want you guessing at sources, and our members will know the literature better than you do.
