Since January I have kept a dream journal, for a reason that now looks naive: I had begun waking with the distinct sense that something had happened to me, and I wanted to see whether the impression would survive being written down. It survives. What has not survived is my confidence that anything occurred. Every dream I have ever had is known to me only in the morning, and only as a memory; the dream and my recollection of it arrive together, at the end, and I have never once been present at one while it was happening. My wife, meanwhile, has taken to telling me what I said in the night, and her reports do not match my remembered versions — sometimes in small ways, sometimes not at all. Between the two records there is nothing else to appeal to.

Two ways of describing this have been on my mind, and I do not think they are the same claim. The first says that a dream is not an experience at all: that nothing is seen, heard, or undergone during sleep, and that what we call a dream is assembled at the moment of waking — a story told about a night that never contained it, so that a dream stands closer to a memory of an experience that never happened than to an experience itself. The second is more radical, and I take it to be the older of the two: that we have simply misread the grammar of our own dream-talk, that "I dreamed" is not a report of anything undergone, and that asking what a dream was like is nearer to asking what last night's anaesthetic was like than to asking about a scene one witnessed. I associate the first with Daniel Dennett and the second with Norman Malcolm, though I suspect I am flattening a subtler disagreement between them.

I would like you to write a short story that takes one or both of these possibilities seriously as a premise rather than as a device. Some constraints, since I have found that the obvious versions of this story cheat:

- The narration should be first-person, and the narrator should have an ordinary life — not a philosopher, not a sleep researcher, not a therapist. The unsettling element should emerge from domestic and occupational detail rather than from surrealism or dream-logic.
- No scene may be set during sleep. Whatever the narrator knows of the night must be reconstructed after the fact — from memory, from what others report, from the state of the room, from the journal. I want the story to feel the weight of that restriction rather than to escape it.
- No nested dreams, no revelation that the narrator is still asleep, no coma, no death. Nothing that converts the premise into a twist.
- No drug, no device, no experiment that settles the matter. The question must remain open when the story ends, and the reader should be left unsure which answer they were hoping for.
- Nobody in the story may explain the philosophy. If a character comes near these ideas, they should do so badly, or for the wrong reasons, or in the middle of something else.
- Include at least one piece of external evidence — something another person says the narrator did or said while asleep — that sits awkwardly beside the narrator's own account.
- Roughly 1,500 words, with a title.

What I am after, in the end, is a story in which the fact that a person cannot check from the inside whether anything happened to them last night is not a puzzle to be solved but a condition to be lived in.
