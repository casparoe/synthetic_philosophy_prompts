I teach a 300-level seminar on death and the metaphysics of harm -- senior majors, a couple of grad students sitting in. Final is a 90-minute closed-book essay, one question, 20 marks. Closed book, so nothing that depends on them remembering who said what. My problem is that every year I get the same essay back: Epicurus says the dead don't exist so nothing can be bad for them, the deprivation account says death is bad because it takes away the life you would have had, deprivation account wins. Thats a book report, not an essay.

Here's the framing I've been pushing all term, and I want the exam built on it. I split Epicurus's argument into two claims that I think are seperate, and I tell them only one is load-bearing:

(a) the existence condition -- for something to be bad for you, there has to be somebody it's bad for;
(b) the timing condition -- for something to be bad for you, there has to be some time at which it's bad for you.

My line is that the deprivation account can give up (b) without blinking: it can say death is bad for you and yet not bad for you at any time. And that almost every standard reply to Epicurus is a disguised way of giving up (b), which means those replies have already conceded (a). If that's right, the live question isn't whether death is bad for you, it's how much the deprivation account can explain while still holding on to (a). I want the exam to make them answer that.

Three things the question has to march them through, because these are where the class comes apart:

1. The symmetry argument -- the one about the eternity before you were born being no different from the eternity after you die. The usual asymmetry reply is that death deprives you of goods your life would have contained, whereas the pre-natal stretch deprives you of nothing. I want them to notice that this reply sounds like it needs a subject who is there to be deprived, i.e. it sounds like (a). So is the asymmetry reply even available to someone who's dropped (b), or does dropping (b) cost you the reply?

2. The relocation move. Suppose they try to keep (b) by saying the harm isn't being dead, it's having your life cut short -- and that harm happens at the moment of death, when you do exist. Both conditions satisfied, tidy. I want them to say whether that's a real fix or a relabeling, and to test it on this case: a woman dies at 38, in the middle of a life she was absorbed in, but she'd already done everything she'd ever wanted to do, and had she lived she'd have had a pleasant but unremarkable rest of it, nothing she would have minded missing. Intuitively her death is a tragedy. Say what the deprivation account is committed to saying about that intuition, and whether the relocation move helps it.

3. The baseline. The life you would have had is not a determinate thing -- it hangs on how long you'd have lived, on choices nobody made, on medical luck. So what is the account actually comparing, and can it be a real comparison rather than a fiction? Here's the case I use to sharpen it: two people die at 50. The first would have had thirty more years she'd have enjoyed. The second would have had thirty more years flat on his back in a coma -- no experiences, no desires, no chance of waking. Same thirty years of life lost in both cases. Say what the deprivation account has to say about the second death, and whether having to say that shows the account is really about goods rather than about life.

So please write me the question itself, plus a model answer of about 700 words, plus a marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want five must-hit moves, 20 marks total, split unevenly -- I want the top-end marks to go to the moves that separate an A from a B, and I want the A/B line to be whether they can say what a version of the account that keeps (a) and drops (b) would actually look like, and what that costs. For each must-hit point, one line on how students typically get it wrong -- the actual failure mode, not 'they were vague.' Don't let the question be answerable by reciting the four-line Epicurean argument or by reciting 'deprivation account good, Epicurus bad,' and keep it doable in 90 minutes by someone who's done the reading. I don't want a research paper.
