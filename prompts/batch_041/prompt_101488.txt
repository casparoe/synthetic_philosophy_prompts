I am the outgoing chair of a small interstate compact of state environmental regulators, and in three weeks I have to give the farewell remarks at the retirement dinner for my deputy, who is leaving after thirty-one years. About eighty people will be in the room: roughly half are career regulators who know the regulatory-competition literature and will wince if I get the game theory wrong, and the other half are her family, a few journalists, and colleagues from outside the field who have never heard the word Moloch used in earnest. I want the remarks built around — not merely decorated with — a taxonomy she spent her career insisting on, namely that almost everything we call a race to the bottom is really one of three different structures, and that the structure determines whether there is any way out at all. The first is a genuine prisoner's dilemma: each jurisdiction would privately prefer a common high standard, the standard is not enforceable from above, and lowering it is a dominant move, so no amount of goodwill or table-talk escapes it. The second is a coordination problem with several stable equilibria: no one is defecting, everyone is simply already parked in the bad equilibrium, and the exit is a focal point or a first mover rather than a treaty — a distinction that decides whether a summit can succeed. The third is the case she was most stubborn about: the race whose so-called bottom is actually efficient in aggregate, where the losses that make it feel like a race to the bottom are positional and relative rather than absolute, so that the participants are made worse off by the comparison and not by the level. She also thought the multipolar-trap framing had been muddled by lumping all of this together with the depletion of a common-pool resource, which is a different animal — a rivalrous resource being used up, not a standard being relocated — and that the conflation had cost us decades of misdirected policy. The remarks should honor her by doing what she did in meetings: sorting the case at hand into the right box and being honest about which boxes admit hope and which do not. Warm, funny once or twice, under twelve minutes spoken, and it must not read as a lecture to the regulators or as a mystery to everyone else.

I would also like the closing movement to say something true about the difference between a trap and a dilemma — I have come to think the two words are not synonyms and that the whole point lives in the gap between them, but take that wherever you find it defensible, including against me. Please do not cite anything, name any books, or lean on any particular author; if you want to gesture at an idea, put it in your own words. Finish with a single last line beginning "In one line:" that states what the speech is ultimately claiming about races to the bottom.
