I have no academic training in philosophy or economics, but I have spent years arguing with friends, family, and strangers about what may and may not be bought and sold, and I want to stop trading intuitions case by case. Before I take any theory in this area seriously, I want to know what one would have to do to be adequate.

So write me the requirements: the list of things any account of the moral limits of markets has to deliver. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say why it is a requirement rather than a preference. Tell me plainly which items are non-negotiable and which are the house rules of one camp rather than genuine constraints. Then flag any pair or subset of the requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the collision — that is the part I most want, because I suspect the list itself is the answer. Finally, say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the main families of answer, and briefly how those families fare: the consent-and-coercion family, the welfare-and-consequences family, the family that talks about the meaning of a good being corrupted, and the family that talks about equality and who ends up subordinate to whom. Keep that last part short; I care much more about the criteria than the scorecard.

Here are the cases the list has to survive, including one family I have not seen anyone discuss and cannot get out of my head:

- Selling a kidney, and the related cases of paid plasma, paid sperm, and paid surrogacy, which we permit and forbid in inconsistent patterns.
- Raising the price of water, generators, or hotel rooms after a flood or hurricane.
- Selling a vote, and the near neighbour I keep noticing: selling a place in a queue, which nobody objects to, even though both are positional.
- What I will call substitute participation: paying someone else to perform an act that carries a personal obligation, where the act looks identical on the outside. Paying someone to fast or pray on your behalf; to attend your friend's wedding in your place; to sit through your child's school play; to serve your community service; to sit with your mother while she dies; to be punished in your place. In some of these the substitute performs the act better than you would — a hired mourner weeps more convincingly than the bereaved — and I want to know whether a theory has to say what makes an act mine in the first place, such that outsourcing it destroys the point rather than merely changing the price.

If the way I have set this up rests on an assumption I should drop first — if a single theory of the limits of markets is a mistake and the honest answer is a messier inventory of separate questions — say so plainly instead of producing a tidy list to be agreeable. I would rather be told the question is confused than be handed a list that quietly assumes it is not.
