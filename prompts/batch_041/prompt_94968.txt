I have no academic training in philosophy, but a puzzle has occupied me for a long time, and I would like a careful conceptual explanation rather than a reassurance that the puzzle is old and has a standard name. The puzzle is whether anything — a person, a machine, an institution — can genuinely be a cause by not acting.

It began with an everyday observation. We blame people for what they fail to do: a nurse who does not check on a patient, a landlord who never repairs the boiler, a bystander who does not shout a warning. We also say such failures caused the harm. Yet when I try to say what the cause was, I find that nothing happened. There is no event, no change, no motion to point at. My first question is whether this is a real obstacle or a confusion, and if it is a confusion, exactly where it goes wrong.

Beyond that, I keep running into three specific difficulties, and I would like each treated on its own terms rather than folded together.

First, selection. For any failure there are indefinitely many things the person did not do, all equally true: the nurse did not check on the patient, and also did not sing, did not resign, did not open the window. Only one of these strikes us as the cause. What does the selecting, and is the selection a fact about the situation or a fact about our expectations? I suspect that what counts as the relevant omission depends on what was expected, normal, or required of the agent, and if that is right I want to know whether the causal claim is then partly evaluative rather than purely factual. Please be explicit about what follows for the claim that omissions cause things.

Second, agency and expectation. Consider a system with no intentions and no one watching it: a home automation setup that is configured to send an alert when a freezer fails, and does not, so the food spoils; or a bank's automated filter that lets a fraudulent charge through. Is there an omission here, or only an absence of an event that we would have wanted? Does the answer turn on whether anyone had a duty or a reasonable expectation, and if so, does that mean omission causation is impossible in a world with no expectations at all?

Third, description. Take a case where one and the same inaction is a failure to do two different things, and where the harm would have been averted by either. If our verdict on what caused the harm shifts when we redescribe the inaction, does that show the causal relation is relative to our descriptions, or only that our descriptions are doing work that the causal relation is not?

I would like the explanation to make clear which of these difficulties are genuine disagreements about how the world works and which are disputes about words, and to say what, if anything, would settle the genuine ones. Where you take a position, please give the reasoning rather than just the conclusion.
