So my vicar knows I'm doing a philosophy masters and has asked me to give the short talk before the blessing of the animals this year — it's the churchyard thing on the Sunday nearest St Francis' day, everyone brings their dog or their hamster or, one memorable year, an extremely unhappy iguana, the vicar goes round sprinkling water on them, then we all eat cake. I'm on for about three minutes before the sprinkling starts. Normally that's a nothing job, but on Friday I got into a two-hour argument about it with my friend Dev, who's writing his thesis on early modern philosophy and is a proper Cartesian about animals. His line is that Descartes gets a bad rap: Descartes only ever denied that animals *think*, and was apparently quite careful to leave room for them having sensations, and the cartoon version — dog yelps, that's just the machine creaking, no more to it than a clock — is something later people did to him, not him. He also pointed out that Descartes' two marks, the one about language and the one about not being able to improvise outside your programming, are about thought, not about feeling. So now I can't just stand up and say "we bless these dear creatures who love us and feel things" — well, I can, nobody's going to arrest me, but Dev will be in the third row with two of his seminar friends and I'll have spent the whole three minutes dodging.

Write me the address. It has to actually say something definite about what these animals are and what we're doing when we bless them — not a hedge, not "well, who can really say" — and it has to be something that survives Dev's objection, so it can't just be me asserting that the dog loves me because it wags. But it also has to be sayable out loud in a churchyard with about forty people in it, half of them kids under ten holding animals, plus my mum, plus Dev and his friends. Three minutes, written to be spoken, no headings or bullet points, and please don't let it turn into a sermon — the vicar does the God part, I just have to say the thing that makes the blessing make sense. And don't put any quotes into Descartes' mouth or mine; I don't want a fake line from a letter I can't go and check, so paraphrase if you need to bring his view in.

One more thing, since I'll probably actually use this: after the speech, in a short note that isn't part of it, tell me where this is most likely to be wrong or to fall apart. If I've built the address on some claim about animal minds that a Cartesian would simply deny, say which claim that is, and say what would have to be true for Dev to be right and the whole thing to be a nice sentiment wrapped around nothing. And tell me what would change your mind about how you've pitched it.
