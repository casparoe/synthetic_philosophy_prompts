I am teaching a seventy-five-minute session on dispositions for advanced undergraduates in a metaphysics seminar. They have already met the simple conditional analysis—roughly, that an object is disposed to break when struck just in case it would break if struck—and they have seen the usual glass and sugar examples. I do not want the session to be a parade of famous counterexamples; I want them to leave with a structural understanding of why finks, masks, and mimics threaten the simple analysis in different ways, and of what a dispositional ascription is doing once the analysis is repaired.

Do not explain the distinctions to me; plan how I should explain them. First, give the order in which the ideas must be introduced, making the dependencies explicit: what has to be in place before a fink can be felt as a problem, what has to be in place before a mask can be distinguished from a fink, and where the labels themselves should arrive—I would like students to draw the distinctions before they are given the names. Second, choose the one carrying example. I want a single invented mechanism, not the standard glass and not sugar dissolving, that can be toggled between four settings: a plain disposition that manifests; a fink, where the stimulus itself removes the disposition so that the counterfactual fails although the object was disposed beforehand; a mask, where an independent condition blocks the manifestation while the disposition remains; and a mimic, where the object lacks the disposition but an independent condition produces the manifestation. The mechanism should be physically intelligible to students without specialist science, should not rely on a magical intervener, and should make the four settings feel like variations of one thing rather than four anecdotes. Say what the mechanism is, what the toggles are, and what each setting is meant to show. If the example can also yield a fifth setting in which the stimulus would create the disposition—so that the counterfactual is true for the wrong reason—include it and say what it adds.

Then map the misconceptions. I expect them to think that a fink refutes dispositional realism outright, that masks and finks are the same kind of counterexample, that any failure of the counterfactual shows the disposition is absent, that a ceteris paribus clause is a trivial fix, and that the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction is doing more work than it can. For each, say exactly where in the sequence it should be headed off and what the students should be made to see before the correction lands. End with one question I can put to them that would reveal whether they can apply the fink/mask/mimic distinctions to a new case rather than recite the labels. Finally, tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how I should say so, so that the session remains about the structure of the problem rather than the surrounding literature.
