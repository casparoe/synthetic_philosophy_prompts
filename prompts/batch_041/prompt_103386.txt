A writer I have been reading puts forward the following argument. An ant colony does things we should unhesitatingly call intelligent if a single animal did them: it distributes foragers across food sources in rough proportion to yield, reroutes around obstructions, chooses between candidate nest sites, and gives every appearance of remembering where it has been. Yet no ant understands any of this. Each follows local signals deposited by others; no ant surveys the colony; the queen issues no orders. The colony's intelligence, he concludes, exists only at the level of the whole, and the ants stand to the colony as neurons stand to a brain. He draws two further lessons: that a mind can be realised in any substrate whose parts are numerous, individually mindless, and densely coupled; and that we should therefore not be surprised if something like a mind is even now emerging from the internet — whereupon asking which part of it is conscious is as confused as asking which neuron is.

This is a familiar trope — Hofstadter's ant colony is its best-known version — and I am persuaded by the first half while suspicious of the last step, though I cannot say precisely where the slide happens. Please audit the analogy.

Give me, first, an explicit table of correspondences: what in the colony plays the role of the neuron, the synapse, the neural assembly, the sensory surface, the motor output, and the thing that is supposed to be conscious; and say, for each, whether the match is close, loose, or merely verbal.

Then tell me where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion he draws from those that do not. I am interested especially in these: that individual ants are whole organisms with their own metabolic interests and their own rudimentary representations, whereas neurons are not agents at all; that much of what the colony appears to know is stored in the environment rather than read out from any internal state; that the colony reproduces by fission and so has no persisting individual to be the subject of experience; and that the colony's competence is largely fixed by its species' architecture rather than acquired, as a brain's is.

Then say which features of the source the analogy quietly imports into the target — a genome that specifies the architecture, a selection history acting on the whole, tight and reliable coupling between parts, a clean boundary between the system and its environment — and whether the internet, or any other collective, actually has them.

Finally, adjudicate the conclusion. Does the claim that a mind can be realised in any sufficiently coupled substrate of mindless parts survive the audit, and does it support the further claim that asking which part is conscious is confused? If the argument needs a better analogy, say what it should be and why.

Please answer in British English.
