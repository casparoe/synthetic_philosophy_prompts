I have no academic training in history or philosophy; I read popular military and political history and I moderate a small online discussion group for people who do the same, and our arguments keep returning to one puzzle. Sometimes an outcome had two or more conditions, each of which on its own would have been enough to produce it — a general's order delivered by two independent couriers when one would have done, a regime toppled by a harvest failure that would have sufficed alone and by unpaid soldiers who would have sufficed alone — and we cannot agree on what to say about the conditions that did not do the work. I would like you to turn this into a sorting instrument for the group: a decision tree, laid out like a flowchart, that takes a reader from a starting commitment to a named position on what an overdetermined outcome is and what can be said about a redundant cause. It has to sort us by what we say about concrete cases rather than by which label we like, so no node may ask which theory the reader holds.

Build it in two stages: first set out the logical space as a tree whose every node is a question with two or three plain-language answers, then place the positions inside it. The entry point should be the thought that the outcome would have happened anyway without this particular condition. The branch questions should cover at least: whether a condition can count as a cause at all unless the outcome depended on it; whether two conditions can each be enough, or whether "enough" only ever means enough given everything else; whether two conditions that each suffice are one cause described twice or two causes; whether a redundant condition adds anything to the outcome or only to our knowledge of it; whether naming one condition the cause reports a fact about the world or answers a question someone is asking (why did it fall rather than stand, why then rather than later); and whether the things we call causes are events, actions, facts, or standing conditions, and whether that choice changes the verdict. Each node must be answerable by someone who has no idea where it leads.

Use these cases at minimum: two independent flaws in a bridge, each alone sufficient under the day's load; two bullets fired at the same moment by different shooters, one arriving first; a patient with two unrelated diseases, either of which would have been fatal; the couriers and the revolution above; and the addition I care most about, which is the contrast between a backup built precisely so that the outcome could not happen (a generator installed to prevent a blackout) and a backup nobody planned (a second power source that happened to exist). I suspect some positions that look distinct about the first are really the same position about the second, and I want the tree to show whether that is so.

Please include leaves for at least: the view that only dependence makes a cause, so a genuinely redundant condition is no cause at all; the view that whichever condition operated first is the cause and the rest are inert; the view that both are full causes; the view that causes come in degrees and a redundant one contributes little or nothing; the view that causal talk is always relative to a contrast and there is no single fact of the matter; the view that we single out the abnormal condition; the view that there is no fact about causes at all and history is narrative; and a system-level view on which what explains the outcome is that it was robust to the loss of any one condition. Ten leaves at most, each carrying the name of the position or "unnamed," plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where two leaves would answer every case identically, say so rather than pretending the tree separates them, and where a combination of answers is unoccupied, note it and say briefly whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I do not want a reading list or citations; describe each position by what it says about the cases rather than by who held it. The whole instrument should fit on about two pages.
