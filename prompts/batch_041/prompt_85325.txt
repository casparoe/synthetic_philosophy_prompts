I have no training in philosophy, but the old puzzle people call Buridan's ass has been bothering me for weeks, ever since a friend and I spent a long dinner arguing about it. His line is that the donkey is simply being unreasonable: if you have two options and nothing to choose between them, you are still required to take one, and standing there until you starve is a failure of reason, no different from refusing to leave a shop until you can prove which of two identical umbrellas is the luckier one. My line is that the donkey never faced a two-way tie at all. Doing nothing was itself one of the things he could do, and doing nothing was obviously worse, so the puzzle dissolves the moment you count it. He says that is cheating, because you can always stipulate a version where waiting costs nothing at all, and then the tie is real and the problem comes straight back. I cannot tell whether we are disagreeing about a fact or just talking past each other, so what I want from you is a number rather than a tour of who said what.

Please give me your credence in this claim: there is at least one properly described case in which an agent faces two options that are exactly as good as each other in every respect the agent has any reason to care about, and in which declining to choose would be a mistake of reason rather than merely a missed opportunity. Then break the number down rather than leaving it as a bare guess. How much of it rides on whether genuine ties are possible in the first place, as opposed to every apparent tie being just our ignorance of some difference between the options? How much on whether reason tells us what we must do or only what we may do, and on whether it can sensibly demand an arbitrary act, like flipping a coin or simply plumping for one, rather than merely allowing it? How much on whether waiting is always among the options and always carries some cost, so that the classic starving-animal case is a fiction rather than a counterexample? And how much on whether there is even a fact of the matter here, rather than two ways of talking about reasons that never meet? Give me a rough figure and a single line of justification for each term, then show how they combine, then tell me which term you are least sure of and what evidence or argument would move it most.

For what it is worth, I put myself at about 0.3 and my friend at about 0.75, so if you can also say which of the sub-questions he and I most likely disagree about, that would be more useful to me than the headline number. One more wrinkle I keep failing to think through, in case it belongs in the decomposition: if you imagine a machine that must choose between two exactly identical actions, with no possibility of hesitating and no cost to waiting, it seems to me the puzzle either vanishes or becomes something else entirely, and I cannot tell which.
