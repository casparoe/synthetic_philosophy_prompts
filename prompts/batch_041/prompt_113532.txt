I've been having a long-running argument with a colleague in my department, and I'd like to pin down where our disagreement actually lives rather than trading anecdotes and loaded examples. His claim is that the adoption of agriculture was a catastrophe for human welfare: that the average person born in the ten thousand or so years between the first farming villages and the industrial revolution was worse off than the average person would have been had the transition never happened. He puts his credence in that at about 0.9; mine is about 0.3. What I want from you is your own number on a carefully stated version of the claim, and more importantly the decomposition that produces it. Stated version: taking welfare as average lifetime subjective well-being, and taking the counterfactual to be a world in which foraging persisted across the same regions over the same period — not a world in which agriculture arrives later, or arrives somewhere else and spreads — the transition lowered the mean lifetime welfare of the people who lived during that period. If you think the claim needs a different precisification before it can be true or false, say which one you would adopt and answer that one; do not stop at saying that it depends on the welfare measure.

Here are the sub-questions I think the number has to run through, though I would rather you revise the list than accept it. (1) How much the skeletal and archaeological record can bear: skeletal samples are samples of the dead, and where mortality is high the dead may look healthier, because the frail can die before the chronic lesions that leave marks have time to form — which seems to cut against the confident claims on both sides. (2) Whether any decline was a transition cost that the first farming populations suffered and their successors partly recovered from, since a temporary dip and a permanent one support very different versions of the claim. (3) How representative the affluent-forager baseline is, given that the foragers we know most about were mobile people in marginal environments, while some foragers were sedentary, stored food, lived in dense ranked settlements, and at least one late Pleistocene cemetery in the Nile Valley shows a mixture of healed and unhealed projectile injuries that reads as recurrent raiding rather than a single massacre. (4) How much weight to give the sheer number of people agriculture made possible, since if one cares about total rather than average welfare, or about the existence of the later people it made room for, the sign of the verdict may flip. (5) Whether the transition was ever a choice that could have been declined: if it was largely a response to declining returns from foraging, or a trap in which farming was individually rational and collectively self-defeating once neighbouring groups had committed to it, then calling it a mistake requires an account of who erred and what they could have done instead. (6) Whether cross-world welfare comparisons of this kind are determinate at all, given hedonic adaptation and the difficulty of assigning preferences to people who would never have existed.

Give me a probability for each term, one line of justification for each, the rule you are combining them with, and a note on which pairs you take to be non-independent, followed by the resulting number for the whole claim. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what single piece of evidence or argument would move it most. I want a committed answer rather than a survey of the options: if the terms do not resolve cleanly, pick the precisification you think is most defensible, say flatly whether you would call the agricultural revolution a mistake, and own the cost of that verdict, including the term you had to lean on hardest to get there. Finally, tell us which of the six terms most likely accounts for the gap between his 0.9 and my 0.3.
