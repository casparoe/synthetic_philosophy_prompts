Context in case it matters: lottery = fair 1000-ticket draw, so it's 0.999 that any given ticket loses, so it looks like you can rationally believe of each ticket that it loses, but you also know one of them wins, so you'd end up believing something false no matter what. Preface = an author has good evidence for every sentence in her book, then says in the preface that she's confident at least one of them is wrong somewhere, and she doesn't seem irrational either time.

1. Marek: both cases show the same thing, which is that rational belief isn't closed under conjunction. You can rationally believe P1, believe P2, ... believe Pn, and not believe the conjunction of them. Anyone who insists you have to believe the conjunction of your beliefs is just refusing to look at the cases.

2. Tessa: she says that can't be the fix, and this is the bit I didn't follow. She says dropping closure doesn't help, because the problem isn't that you'd be forced to believe the conjunction -- it's that your beliefs can't all be true together even before you conjoin them, so the cheap fix leaves you holding a bunch of beliefs that are jointly unsatisfiable, which is worse than merely being unable to combine them. She also says nobody's denying the one-premise version -- if you believe P, you believe what follows from P alone -- so what Marek is giving up is exactly the multi-premise case, which is the only version that does any work.

3. Marek: then the thing to give up is the idea that belief is a probability cutoff. 0.999 isn't belief, so the threshold picture is just wrong, and once it's gone the lottery stops looking paradoxical -- you have high credence that each ticket loses and you don't believe it.

4. Tessa: she says that's ad hoc, since the only reason to deny you believe your ticket loses is that it rescues the theory. And she says it doesn't touch the preface at all, because there's no lottery structure there -- no partition, no 1000 equiprobable mutually exclusive outcomes. The author has separate evidence for each claim and can make each one as strong as she likes, so you can't wave the preface away by saying she doesn't really believe claim 47.

5. Marek: he says that cuts the other way. In the lottery every 'it loses' belief rests on the same evidence, the setup, so it's no surprise the whole package is rotten. In the preface the support for each claim is independent, and that's precisely why you can't dismiss the author's beliefs as mere high credence -- she's as well placed on claim 47 as on anything she believes. So the threshold view has to go in general, not just in the lottery.

6. Tessa: she says the independence is fake. All the author's beliefs lean on her own competence and her own methods, and the preface's doubt is about exactly that, so the claims are correlated through the very thing she's doubting -- they aren't independent at all. She also says Marek keeps helping himself to the idea that the author can rationally assert the whole book, but the book is one giant conjunction, so if closure fails, what exactly is she asserting when she asserts it?
