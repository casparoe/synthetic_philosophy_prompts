I am assembling a small research programme — one philosopher, three years, no empirical budget — around a single question: when should a theory whose long-run prediction failed be judged 'right but early' rather than simply wrong? The paradigm case is Malthusian: a mechanism predicted to dominate over centuries that does not dominate on the predicted timescale. I want to move past the standard Popper-versus-Lakatos exchange, so take the following three rescue strategies as the working taxonomy: delayed confirmation (the predicted effect arrives later than promised), absorption (the theory survives by quietly recasting its prediction as conditional on background conditions that turned out not to hold), and horizon drift (the deadline is extended each time it passes). Which open questions here most deserve the three years, and in what order? Please defend the ordering rather than merely listing: say which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for a philosopher working without data or modelling, which would genuinely change how we classify historical cases, and what evidence would move a question up or down the list. I am also interested in whether 'right but early' is ever a legitimate verdict at all, or only a face-saving description — and where that question sits relative to the others.
