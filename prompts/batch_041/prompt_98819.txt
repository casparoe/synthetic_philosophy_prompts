For decades the argument over whether honoring sunk costs can ever be rational has run in place, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on particular cases. The familiar experimental tradition treats the effect as a bias: once money, time, or effort is irrecoverable, treating it as a reason to continue is a departure from expected-value reasoning, and the live questions concern only when and why people commit it and how reliably it can be induced. Against this, a revisionist line holds that honoring a sunk cost can be rational in at least three ways — because the past expenditure carries information about a hidden state of the world, because it can serve as a commitment device against one's own future defection, and because abandoning a publicly undertaken project can impose reputational costs that are themselves forward-looking. What puzzles me is that the two sides rarely seem to be arguing about the same proposition: the experimentalists about whether the behavior is predictable and exploitable, the revisionists about whether a fully informed agent could have a reason, and neither body of results appears to bear on the other.

I want to test a hypothesis of my own about the structure of this disagreement. I suspect the literature conflates two things under the single heading of "honoring a sunk cost": (a) treating the expenditure itself as a reason, which I will call sunk-cost honoring, and (b) treating the expenditure as evidence bearing on what you would have chosen under a policy you cannot now renegotiate, which I will call sunk-cost licensing. My hypothesis is that the experimentalists are right that (a) is a bias, the revisionists are right that (b) can be rational, and the debate has stalled because each side mistakes its own case for the whole of the phenomenon.
