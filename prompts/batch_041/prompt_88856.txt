You are a logician and philosopher of mathematics with a deep working knowledge of model theory, set theory, and the philosophical literature on Skolem's paradox, and you have spent years coaching philosophers through hostile question periods. I need you to prepare me for one.

In three weeks I am giving a twenty-five-minute talk to my department's logic and set theory seminar, followed by twenty minutes of questions. The audience is about fifteen people. Most are working set theorists who treat the cumulative hierarchy as a definite subject matter and who, I suspect, regard Skolem's paradox as a puzzle for undergraduates that was settled decades ago and is faintly embarrassing to raise. Also present: a model theorist who works on absoluteness and on the contrast between transitive and non-standard models; a philosopher of mathematics who is an unrepentant Skolemite and will want me to go further than I am willing to go; and a visiting logician who has heard the standard line so often that any whiff of it will make him stop listening.

My thesis, roughly stated. Skolem's paradox is the fact that any consistent first-order theory with an infinite model — ZFC included — has a countable model, even though ZFC proves that uncountable sets exist. I argue that the standard reply, that countability is not absolute and that the countable model simply fails to contain the bijection, does not dissolve the paradox but restates it. The reply helps itself to a metatheory whose own cardinality predicates are subject to the same theorem; so either the metatheory's notion of a bijection is privileged by fiat, or it is model-relative too. What I want to defend instead is that being uncountable is not a property of a set but a relation between a set and a model, or between a set and an ambient universe, and that this is not a defect of first-order formalization to be patched by moving to second-order logic or by appealing to an intended interpretation. On my view the relativity is the correct and unremarkable account of what cardinality talk amounts to, and it is invisible to mathematical practice, because practice never poses a cardinality question except inside a fixed ambient structure, and every ordinary mathematical use of the word is preserved under the relativization. I am braced for the charge that this is self-undermining, and I have a reply, but I am not confident it is the right one.

What I want from you:

First, before drafting anything, think on paper for a few paragraphs about this audience. Work out what each faction already believes about models, absoluteness, and the determinacy of set-theoretic notions; which parts of my thesis threaten those beliefs; and which parts they will consider trivially true and therefore a waste of my twenty-five minutes.

Then give me a question-and-answer prep sheet, organized as follows.

1. The eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do to the talk — not by how likely they are, though flag which ones are near-certain. Give each in its strongest form; I would rather prepare for the version that hurts.

2. Each objection phrased in the voice of the person who will make it. A set theorist's objection should sound like a set theorist's and the Skolemite's like the Skolemite's, not like neutral prose that any philosopher could have written.

3. For each objection, a two-minute spoken reply, followed by an honest note on where the reply is soft: which premise I am leaning on that I cannot defend in the time available, and which concession would be fatal.

4. A short list of what I should concede up front, before anyone asks, and why conceding it costs me nothing.

5. The single reply that has to land for the talk to count as a success, written as a sentence I can say out loud.

6. The question I am hoping nobody asks.

Two constraints. Do not give me reading lists, citations, or references to who said what; I know the literature and I will have no time to deploy it in the room. And do not tell me that an objection rests on a misunderstanding of my view unless you also tell me precisely which feature of the way I stated the view invites that misunderstanding, so that I can change how I state it.

Finally, address the tension I have not resolved. The set theorists want me to stay a realist about the hierarchy; the Skolemite wants me to abandon it. These cannot both be satisfied, and I would rather choose deliberately than be pushed. Tell me which faction I should let down, why that is the right trade rather than merely the safer one, and what the cost of the trade will be when the person I have disappointed gets the floor.
