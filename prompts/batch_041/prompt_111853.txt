I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am building a diagnostic decision tree that sorts people's views on whether a person survives the gradual replacement of their brain or body — the slow, piece-by-piece swap that leaves a functionally equivalent system built out of entirely different material, together with the related case of copying a mind onto new hardware. I keep thinking of it as refactoring a running service, though I suspect that analogy is doing work I have not examined. I want the tree to be usable by engineers and other lay readers: someone should be able to walk it from the top and arrive at a named position without ever being asked which label they prefer.

Act as a philosopher of mind with expertise in personal identity and the metaphysics of persistence, and also as a careful designer of diagnostic instruments. Think the problem through before writing anything: first work out the axes on which the candidate views genuinely come apart, check that each axis really does divide views rather than merely correlating with them, and only then lay the tree out over that space. Take your time over the logical space before committing to a structure.

Constraints on the instrument:

- Build the tree from the questions, not from a list of doctrines. Start from the combinations of answers your axes permit, then say which named positions occupy which combinations.
- Every internal node must be a question or claim that a layperson can answer without philosophical vocabulary: use concrete cases, or plain-language claims about what matters. No node may amount to asking which theory the reader likes.
- Each branch must be labelled with the question that separates it from its siblings. Each leaf must carry the name of the position (or 'unnamed' where I take it that nobody defends it in print), a one-line statement of its central claim, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question in your tree, say so explicitly rather than inventing a distinction to tidy the diagram.
- Note combinations of answers that no position occupies, and diagnose each one: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

The tree must branch on at least the following distinctions, since these are the ones I care about and they are the reason I am not simply reproducing a textbook diagram:

1. Whether what matters is causal continuity of the replacing process — an unbroken chain in which each step is caused by the state immediately before it — or the structural and informational match of the end product, so that a causally discontinuous duplicate would count just as well.
2. Whether the view treats survival as all-or-nothing or as a matter of degree, and whether it admits vague cases in which there is no determinate fact about whether the original person is still present.
3. Whether the continuing being is the same subject of experience — the same stream that was there before the first part was swapped — or a successor that merely inherits the psychology, so that gradual replacement amounts to a slow death rather than survival.
4. Where the relevant continuity is located: the whole living organism, the brain specifically, the information pattern, or nowhere at all, on the view that the question is empty.
5. Whether the position makes a claim about first-person anticipation: whether the person about to undergo the procedure should expect to experience what the successor experiences.

Please also put these four cases to work, since I have constructed them so that the tree cannot be answered from memory:

(a) The interleaved swap: a person's parts are replaced one at a time by synthetic equivalents, but each removed organic part is immediately used to build up a second, initially empty system, so that at the end the first is entirely synthetic and the second entirely organic, each with an unbroken causal history running back to the original.
(b) The frozen duplicate: the original is destroyed and, from a complete record taken beforehand, an exactly equivalent system is assembled, with no causal step from the original's final state to the new system's first.
(c) The staggered swap: only one part in a thousand is replaced each year, so that the process outlasts any plausible memory or narrative thread while never involving a single discontinuous jump.
(d) The halted and reversed swap: the procedure is stopped halfway and then run backwards, so that the system returns to its original composition along the path it came.

Do not include citations, references, reading lists, or the names of published books or papers; I have no way to check them, and I would rather have your reasoning than a bibliography.

Return your answer as a single JSON object and nothing else, with the following schema (use British spelling throughout):

- "axes": an array of objects, each with "id" (a short identifier such as A1), "question" (the axis stated as a question a layperson can answer), "answer_options" (array of strings), and "what_it_separates" (one sentence naming which views fall on which side).
- "cases_used": an array of objects, each with "id", "name" (a short label), "description" (the case in your own words), and "which_axis_it_probes" (an axis id).
- "nodes": an array of objects, each with "id", "parent_id" (null for the root), "node_type" (either "question" or "leaf"), "text" (for a question node, the question exactly as it will be put to the reader; for a leaf, the position's name), "branches" (an object mapping each answer text to the id of the child node; an empty object for leaves), and "leaf_detail" (null for question nodes, otherwise an object with "central_claim", "most_awkward_commitment", and "axis_signature", the last mapping each axis id to the answer this position gives on it).
- "root_id": the id of the root node.
- "unoccupied_combinations": an array of objects, each with "combination" (an object mapping axis ids to answers), "diagnosis" (one of "incoherent", "unattractive", "unexplored"), and "reason".
- "inseparable_pairs": an array of objects, each with "position_a", "position_b", and "why" (why no question in this tree separates them).
