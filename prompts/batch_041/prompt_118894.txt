For a handout in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of economics, I need a decision tree that takes a student from one starting commitment to a named position on what a person's choices reveal about her good.

The starting commitment, which every leaf must accept: when a person consistently chooses X over Y, her choice is the best evidence available that X is better for her than Y. Everything past that is open. The tree should sort positions by what they add to, restrict, or deny beyond this commitment.

Build the tree from the space of answers first and attach names afterward, so that the branch structure is visible before the positions are located in it. Each branch node must be a question the student can answer by consulting her own view of a concrete case, phrased so that answering it does not disclose which position lies at the end of the branch. I need leaves for at least the following positions, whether or not you use these names for them:

(a) welfare is an index of felt experience, measurable independently of what the agent chooses, so that choice is at best a fallible indicator of it;
(b) the welfare-relevant ranking is the one the agent would have if fully informed and free of computational error, with actual choices serving only as evidence;
(c) actual choice remains the standard, but some choices are made under conditions the agent herself would repudiate — addiction, panic, a manipulated frame — so the welfare-relevant object is the ranking that survives removing those conditions;
(d) menu-dependence is taken at face value: an agent who pays to have an option removed is not erring but revealing a preference that pairwise choice cannot represent;
(e) choice and welfare come apart whenever the agent acts on a principle rather than on her interests, so choice reveals preference but not welfare;
(f) the whole apparatus is an as-if predictive device with no commitments about what is good for anyone;
(g) preferences are constituted rather than discovered — the act of choosing is what makes an option preferred, so there is nothing antecedently present to be revealed;
(h) satisfaction-based measures are systematically distorted by the way deprivation shapes what people come to want, which is a defect of the measure rather than of the deprived;
(i) welfare claims are indeterminate whenever an agent's choices fail to cohere, so there is no fact of the matter to be revealed;
(j) the preference-based framework should be replaced by a list of goods that does not run through anyone's attitudes at all.

Several of your branch nodes should be cases, at least these: a person who chooses A over B in one elicitation and B over A in another with no change in her circumstances; a person who buys a device to keep a temptation out of reach; a person whose choices cycle, so that a sequence of trades can strip her of everything she has; a person who reports contentment with a life she was pushed into by deprivation; a person who bears a cost to keep a promise that benefits no one she cares about; a person whose choices are so erratic that no ranking fits them, though she still has views about the options.

Requirements. Use no more than twelve leaves. Every leaf must carry the name of a position, or be marked unnamed if no one defends it, plus one sentence on the commitment its adherents find hardest to defend. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate. Finally, list the combinations of branch answers your tree permits that no one occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If you conclude that some of the positions above cannot be sorted by the same questions as the others — because they disagree about what the theory is for rather than about what it says — split the tree and say why, rather than forcing them onto one trunk.
