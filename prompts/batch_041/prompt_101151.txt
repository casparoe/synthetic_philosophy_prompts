Alright, so I've ended up pretty convinced by the Mises–Hayek line in the socialist calculation debate. Not really Mises's strong claim that it's impossible in some a priori sense — more Hayek's version, that the relevant knowledge is dispersed and often tacit, and that prices are the only thing that surfaces it, so a central board can't in principle assemble what it would need. I want you to tell me what should count against this. Skip the lazy "but we have computers now" objection on its own, because I think that only bites if you say which specific step it's supposed to replace. What I want is the real defeaters, ranked, from the ones that should actually make me drop the view to the ones that are just awkward.

Some things I'm already confused about and want folded in. Mises's argument and Hayek's argument seem to be doing differant jobs and could fail independently, so tell me which one the view actually rests on. Lange's trial-and-error market socialism — where a central board just nudges prices until supply and demand clear and firms take those prices as given — is that a genuine counterexample or does it just relocate the problem? And does the whole debate quietly assume static allocation, while the real force of the Austrian side is about discovery and innovation, which would mean the two sides are barely even arguing about the same thing?

Also, someone pointed out to me that big firms do exactly the thing the calculation argument says can't be done — they plan internally without internal prices — and mostly it works, at least for a while. Does that count against the strong version, or is it just the Coasean point about where the boundary of planning happens to sit?

And tell me what Hayekians themselves would concede counts against them, not just the stuff critics throw at them. The discovery-procedure part is the bit I find most persuasive, so weight that one.
