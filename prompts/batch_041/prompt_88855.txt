I am a junior in high school and the student keeper of my current-affairs club's prediction ledger: a public record of the forecasts members make during our debates, when each was made, the date it was supposed to come true, and how it turned out. The ledger goes up in the hallway every month, and at the end of the year it settles our club's forecaster award, so whatever I write down has to be defensible to the person whose prediction I am judging. My difficulty is the misses. Our club has exactly one rule for them: a prediction that misses its date may be carried forward once, and only once, provided the person who made it names something specific that has to happen before their forecast could come true. In practice that rule has been pulled in every direction. A member who said our city would have driverless buses running by the start of this school year now says the technology is real and only the permits were late. A member who said school would go remote again before last winter says the forecast was always conditional on a new variant. A member who said our town's population would fall by ten percent by last year, and watched it fall by two, says he was right about the direction. A member who said our district would push its start time later this year points to a board vote scheduled for May on exactly that proposal. And I predicted the club would reach fifty members by spring; we are at thirty-eight, and I have been telling everyone that I always meant by the end of the year. We studied Malthus in my geography class, and the verdict our class reached, that he was less wrong than early, is precisely the move I have to police every month, and I cannot tell when it is legitimate. I can feel that some of these are honest corrections and some are just moving the goalposts, and that the difference has something to do with whether the correction predicts anything we could have checked before the original date, but I cannot turn that feeling into a rule I can apply in ten minutes with the person watching me.

Please write that rule for me as a decision procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, and the point at which the procedure stops and scores the prediction as a miss. I have no statistics background and no spare time, so it has to fit on a single page, and I need to be told plainly which steps are my judgment dressed up as procedure. It should mark the places where I have to hand the question to the club as a whole, because what remains is policy rather than fact — for instance what happens to credit if a carried-forward prediction eventually comes true, and whether we are scoring a person's single prediction or the larger view behind it. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of science actually knows about when a theory that has missed its prediction may be kept alive and when it must be given up, but explain it in plain words: no reading list, no citations, and do not assume I know the technical names for things. Then run the procedure on all five cases above, tell me honestly where it misfires or lands on an answer the club will hate, and for each case it scores as a miss, say what the person would have had to say at the time the prediction was made to make the carry-forward legitimate.
