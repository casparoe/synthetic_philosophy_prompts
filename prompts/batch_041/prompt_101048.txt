I teach a third-year undergraduate module on concepts, categorisation, and inductive inference, and I am drafting the final examination. For several years I have used Bongard problems as a running illustration, but I have grown tired of questions that can be answered by reciting that Bongard problems show concept learning is difficult or that the solver must find the rule. I want one essay question that forces students to confront the underdetermination of the intended rule by the examples, and the role of inductive biases—simplicity, naturalness, compositional or causal structure—in choosing between hypotheses that fit the data equally well. I spend more time than I should on rationalist blogs and forums, and the issue I care about is the one that keeps surfacing there: if a concept is a compressed hypothesis, what makes one compression better than another when the data do not decide? The question should be answerable in about forty-five minutes and should not depend on any particular reading.

To keep the question away from stock examples, I have devised a textual Bongard-style problem for the paper. The left-hand cards are the strings AB, AABB, and AAABBB. The right-hand cards are the strings ABA, ABBA, and AABAA. I want the examination question to be built around this set, not around any canonical Bongard problem. It should ask students to do at least the following: state two rules that classify all six cards correctly, and say which, if either, is simpler and why; explain why the examples alone do not determine which rule is the intended concept; propose one new card that would force a choice between the two rules and say what it would show; and discuss what a Bayesian or minimum-description-length model of concept learning would have to assume to prefer the rule that a human solver would naturally take. The question should also make them say something about the difference between a rule that is merely extensionally adequate and a rule that captures the concept, and about why the choice of a new test card is not itself theory-neutral.

Please write the examination question itself, a model answer of about seven hundred words, and a marking scheme out of twenty marks. The marking scheme should name the specific moves that separate an A-grade answer from a B-grade answer—for example, whether the student can distinguish underdetermination from mere ambiguity, whether they see that the new card is not theory-neutral, whether they can connect the example to the problem of induction without simply reciting Hume, and whether they can give a substantive account of simplicity rather than treating it as an unanalysed slogan. It should also list the common mistakes that should cost marks: thinking that the examples logically entail one rule, confusing the solver’s psychology with the normative question of which hypothesis is warranted, and proposing a new card that does not actually discriminate between the candidate rules. The model answer should be in continuous prose, not bullet points, and the marking scheme should be usable by a graduate teaching assistant who knows the topic but has not seen the question before. Please do not include reading lists, citations, or references to particular publications; the students will not have web access, and I want the question to stand on the problem itself.
