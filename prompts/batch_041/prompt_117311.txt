I have been asked to give this year's commencement address at the small liberal arts college where I teach, and I would like you to write it for me.

The occasion: a few hundred graduating seniors, their families and grandparents, and the faculty in regalia, on a Saturday morning in late May. The president's only instruction was to send them out with resolve and to avoid the standard fare about following their dreams. The difficulty is that I teach decision theory, and the final meeting of my spring seminar was on Kavka's toxin puzzle: an eccentric billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make you painfully ill for a day but leave you otherwise unharmed; the money is in your account before the drinking is due, and nothing stops you from changing your mind once you have it. About a dozen of the graduates in the front rows were in that seminar and know the puzzle as well as I do.

What the puzzle does to a commencement speech is the point. The genre runs on a single move — decide now, commit, make a promise to your future self — and the puzzle appears to show that the move is unavailable: I cannot, by deciding to, install an intention whose only recommendation is that having it pays, since intentions answer to the reasons for the act they are intentions to perform. So the address has to give the graduates something to do with their futures that does not depend on that move, and it has to be honest about the gap rather than papering it over with the word resolve. I do not want the puzzle explained in one paragraph and then abandoned; I want its shape to govern what the speech can and cannot advise, including at least one moment where the speech has to admit that the advice it would most like to give cannot be given. I would also rather it not pretend to solve the puzzle — the seminar ended without agreement, and I want the difficulty left visible rather than resolved.

Two further constraints. First, the college has a tradition — which I appear to have invented last month and now have to honor — of handing each graduate a sealed envelope at the ceremony containing a letter they have written to themselves, to be opened on the tenth anniversary of their graduation. The speech should say something about what that ritual is actually for, given what the puzzle suggests about the reach of the will. Second, the speech has to reach the parents and grandparents in the audience, who will not know the puzzle and should not feel they are being lectured at by a philosopher; one passage in particular should be written for them.

Finally, give me a separate speaker's note, clearly not for delivery, that marks where I am on solid ground and where I am not. Distinguish the claims about the puzzle that are common ground among people who work on intention and decision theory from those that are genuinely contested: whether the case is coherent as stipulated, whether it shows something about the nature of intention or only about an artificial payoff structure, whether the agent really has no reason to drink once the money is in hand, and whether Kavka's own use of the case — it grew out of his work on deterrence and mutually assured destruction — survives the objections, including the commitment reading associated with Gauthier, on which someone who genuinely intends to drink cannot afterwards treat not drinking as an option. If the speech leans on one side of a live dispute, say which side and why; do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus.
