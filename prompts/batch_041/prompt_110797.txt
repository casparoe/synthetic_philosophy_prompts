I am drafting a chapter on the Principal Principle and would like comments before I commit to the line I have sketched. The argument is meant to show that the principle cannot be both a stipulation that fixes what chance means and a substantive epistemic norm, and that it is needed in both roles. If the principle is constitutive — chance is, by definition, the quantity such that knowing it licenses you to adopt it as your credence — then a theory of chance that generates undermining futures violates nothing; it describes a different quantity, and the charge that it cannot satisfy the principle becomes a complaint about changing the subject rather than about error. If instead the principle is a substantive norm, it stands in need of an argument, and the arguments on offer either presuppose it or establish something weaker, namely that a known chance is admissible evidence one may use rather than a rule one must follow. My tentative way out is to restrict the principle to propositions that are chance-independent, in the sense that their truth does not bear on the chance distribution itself, and then to observe that in a world permitting undermining futures, or in a deterministic one, almost nothing is chance-independent, so the principle survives as true but nearly empty.

I would like comments on three points: whether the constitutive/substantive fork is a real dilemma or a false dichotomy, given that a constraint on what counts as a theory of chance can be both definitional and answerable to our epistemic practice; whether the chance-independence restriction does any work beyond the admissibility clause or merely relabels it; and whether the near-emptiness result is as damaging to a Humean as it looks, or whether a Lewisian can accept the restricted scope and keep everything she cares about. I am least willing to give up the thought that the principle is a genuine norm rather than a definition, so I would also like to know what that commitment costs me.
