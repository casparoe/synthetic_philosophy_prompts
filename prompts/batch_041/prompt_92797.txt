Ok so I'm a retired geophysicist — 35 yrs, mostly instrument calibration and error analysis, the boring end of the field — and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy, mostly epistemology. I've gotten stuck on the peer disagreement thing, conciliationism vs steadfastness, split the difference or hold your ground. And I keep running into the same analogy in the stuff I'm reading and in a reading group I go to, and I want you to take it apart for me.

The analogy goes like this. You and I are both competent at some task — say we both do the same mental arithmetic, or we're two lab scales weighing the same rock — and we come out with different answers. Since we're equally good, neither of us has any reason to think the other is the one who's off, so the sensible thing is to move toward each other, meet in the middle, split the difference. Thats then used to push the conciliatory line: if two epistemic peers disagree and neither is in a position to claim they're better, you should reduce your confidence and shift toward the other persons view, maybe all the way to the midpoint.

Here's my problem with it. I spent my whole working life getting two measurements that disagreed, and I can count on one hand the times when "average them" was the right move. Usually it meant one instrument was miscalibrated, or they shared a common-mode error so averaging did nothing except make me feel better, or the two were actually measuring slightly different things. So my gut says the analogy is doing way more work than it can carry — but I don't trust my gut here, I know the philosophy has its own machinery and I might be importing a pile of assumptions from metrology that don't belong.

So: act as an expert on the epistemology of disagreement who also genuinely understands measurement and error theory — someone comfortable both with the conciliationism/steadfastness debate and with what it actually means to combine two noisy estimates. Before you answer, brainstorm a bit first (on paper, or whatever the equivalent is for you), then give me:

1. A mapping — what corresponds to what. On the instrument side: the two instruments, the quantity, the readings, the true value, the error distributions, the act of averaging, what "equally good" amounts to. On the philosophy side: the two peers, the proposition, the credences, the truth, reliability, the conciliatory move. A table is fine.
2. Where the mapping breaks. I care most about the parts that bear on the conclusion: are the peers' errors independent the way two separate instruments' errors are? Does "equally reliable" mean the same thing on both sides? Is there a well-defined quantity with a fact of the matter in the disagreement case? And what happens once the disagreement is itself evidence about the instruments, not just about the rock?
3. Which features of the instrument picture get quietly imported into the disagreement picture that the disagreement picture has no right to — and vice versa.
4. The verdict: does the split-the-difference conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If some weakened version survives, which one?
5. And if the instrument analogy is a bad fit, whats a better one? I'd rather have a better analogy than a bare verdict.

I don't need a reading list or citations — I can't check them anyway and I'd just be taking your word for it. I want the actual reasoning. Take your time, and please don't smooth over the places where the two sides really do come apart; the interesting bit for me is exactly where the analogy stops working.
