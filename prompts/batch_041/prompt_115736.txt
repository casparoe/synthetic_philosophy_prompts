I'm a reporter who covers science and ideas, and I've got an argument stuck in my head that I can't get out. It's not from a book — a friend of mine who left econ for philosophy laid it out for me after a panel on climate policy, and he delivered it like it was obvious. Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) Climate policy now will make a real difference to how well people live in 2100, 2200, and beyond.

(2) A person's interests don't matter less just because the person lives later than me. Discounting someone's welfare for lateness is like discounting it for distance — morally arbitrary.

(3) He was careful here: discounting money is fine. If the future is richer, a dollar is worth less to them. But that's a fact about dollars, not about how much a person counts. The pure rate of time preference on welfare should be zero.

(4) So a unit of welfare in 2200 counts the same as a unit of welfare today.

(5) There will be alot more people in the future than there are now. Over a few centuries, ten times as many people living as are alive today is not a crazy number.

(6) If their welfare counts the same and there are ten times as many of them, their side of the ledger is ten times heavier than ours.

(7) So when we weigh a policy, gains to future people get added up the same way our gains do, and by that arithmetic almost anything we spend on ourselves loses to mitigation and adaptation.

(8) It doesn't matter that we can't point to which particular future person gets the benefit. The benefit is real even if it's nobody's in particular; the cost to us is real too. Add them up.

(9) Therefore we're required to go on cutting our own consumption until another dollar for us costs the future more than a dollar — with a ten-to-one ratio, that's somewhere near subsistence.

(10) And once you're adding welfare across everyone who will ever live, you get a second result: it's better if there are more future people, even if each of them has a somewhat worse life, because the total goes up. A small rich future is worse than a huge poor one.

(11) But I dont think I'm required to live at subsistence so that strangers two centuries out can have more, and I don't think we should maximize the number of future humans at whatever quality of life. So something in there is broken.

I'd hate to give up (2). I really do think a kid born in 2200 counts as much as I do. But I can't see which of the other steps is the bad one — they all sound fine when I say them out loud, and each one seems to follow from the one before it. Which step do you reject, why does it look so good until you poke it, and what does rejecting it cost me? Don't tell me it's just a matter of taste in moral theories; I want to know which step is doing the work and what I have to give up to kill it. Also please don't send me to a reading list — I want the argument, not the citations. American English, please.
