I have been reading decision-theory blogs and rationality forums for about fifteen years, and I would like to turn a long-standing irritation of mine into an actual research project. I am applying for a small grant — six months at roughly half time — and I need the proposal document itself: two pages, written in the register of a serious research proposal rather than a blog post, and persuasive to a reviewer who is not already inside the argument.

The irritation is Good's theorem. The result, as it circulates, is that the expected value of free information is non-negative, since an agent free to ignore what it learns can always do what it would have done anyway. In practice this gets deployed as a conversation-ender: more evidence cannot hurt; if you would rather not know, simply do not act on it. The theorem is true under its assumptions, but the theorem and the slogan are not the same claim, and I have never seen anyone lay out precisely where they come apart. The assumptions doing the work — that the information is free, that it can be ignored, that the space of possibilities is fixed in advance, and that the agent's preferences and capacities survive the learning unchanged — are exactly the ones that fail in the cases people actually care about.

Here is the half-formed idea I want you to build the proposal around. I suspect non-negativity is not a theorem about information at all but a theorem about option sets: free information helps because it weakly enlarges the set of contingent plans available to the agent, so the result inherits the near-triviality that a superset of options is at least as good under a fixed preference ordering. If that reading is right, the interesting structure is not whether the theorem holds but how it degrades when the identification between learning something and having more options breaks. I want the project to distinguish at least three kinds of informational change: information bearing on which act is best given fixed options and fixed preferences; information that changes what the options are; and information that changes the agent — its preferences, its awareness of possibilities, or the trustworthiness of its own deliberation. The conjecture is that the first kind is the theorem's exact domain, and that the second and third are not counterexamples to it but a different subject currently smuggled in under the same label.

Please have the proposal use two probe cases to test this. First, an oracle that tells you, before you decide, which act you are in fact going to choose: the information is free and formally ignorable, so the theorem applies, yet it is the kind of thing that plausibly has non-positive value for reasons the theorem's framework cannot represent, because the information concerns the deliberation rather than the world. Second, a case where learning does not merely reweight a fixed partition but brings a possibility into view that the agent could not previously have entertained; here I suspect the non-negativity claim is not false but not well formed either, since there was no act defined for the newly visible case. Say what follows for the theorem if either probe lands.

The proposal should give a precise question with a determinate answer; argue in terms of positions and arguments why the existing treatments leave it open (no bibliography — I will supply references myself); set out the method month by month, and defend formal reconstruction of the option-set reading plus regimentation of the probe cases as the right instrument rather than the obvious alternative of cataloguing how the result gets used in practice; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give an honest scope assessment for six months at half time. It should also make the case for why anyone outside this argument should care — my own reason is that the slogan is what people carry into real decisions about whether to gather more evidence, and it deserves better than to be a conversation-ender. If the honest answer is that this is one good paper rather than a research programme, say that too; I would rather scope it correctly than pad it.
