I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy; I read epistemology in my spare time and have been stuck for a couple of years on a dispute I can only describe in my own words. Please correct my vocabulary rather than adopt it.

The dispute concerns what I have seen called epistemic akrasia: whether a person can be rational in holding a belief while also holding that their evidence does not support it — or, in the sharper versions, that their evidence supports its denial. Three cases bother me most. First, you work out the answer to a hard problem and then learn that people in your position get problems of that kind wrong most of the time; are you still entitled to your answer? Second, the same situation, except that you have good reason to think the information about your own unreliability is itself unreliable — you were told you are bad at this kind of reasoning by a source you know to be careless, or you have taken something that induces self-doubt without impairing the reasoning itself. Third, a pilot believes the engine will fail and also believes that this belief is a symptom of oxygen deprivation; the two beliefs are about different things, the aircraft and himself, yet they seem to bear on one and the same question.

As I understand it, two positions have hardened. One holds that believing against your own evidence is irrational, or incoherent, whatever the pedigree of the higher-order evidence, and that the alternative licenses thinking that has come apart from truth. The other holds that in the second kind of case the belief can be perfectly rational, because reasons about your own unreliability are themselves reasons and can be defeated by further reasons, so that what matters is how a thinker responds to the reasons actually available to them. Some of what I have read suggests instead that the question is badly posed.

My working hypothesis, offered as no more than that, is that this is not one disagreement but at least two tangled together. One side is asking what a person is permitted to believe given the information available to them; the other is asking what a well-functioning thinker would end up believing; and the word 'rational' is doing duty for both. A related suspicion is that the sides mean different things by 'evidence' — for some, a body of information the person possesses; for others, a fact about the world standing in a certain relation to the belief.

There is an engineering distinction I cannot stop applying here: between a result and the reliability of the process that produced it. A green test run on a suite that everyone knows to be flaky strikes me as exactly the situation in dispute. I would like to know whether that distinction marks a real joint in the debate or whether it is the picture that creates the trouble in the first place.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two questions travelling under one heading? If it is substantive, do the sides differ about facts, about what counts as a reason, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or should no progress be expected, and why? If I have set the problem up badly — if 'epistemic akrasia' names several distinct puzzles, or if the diagnosis I am after cannot be given without first settling which side is right — please say so plainly instead of answering the question as I posed it. Do not cite any literature; I will look things up myself.
