I'm in a reading group that's been crawling through Augustine on lying — his two goes at it, the early book and the later one he wrote for Consentius about the Priscillianists — and I've been landed with running the next session. What I want to hand out is a decision tree rather than an essay: something that starts from a handful of questions about cases and drops the reader onto a named position about whether lying is ever allowed. Everyone in the group already has a view and none of them can say what it is; they just know they aren't Augustine. So the tree has to sort them by what they say about the cases, and no node should be answerable by picking a label — 'are you a consequentialist about lying?' is exactly the kind of question I don't want in it.

Here are the branch questions I'd like it built around, more or less in this order. First, what makes an utterance a lie at all: does it take an intention to deceive, is any falsehood a lie, is it only a falsehood that wrongs somebody, or does nothing count as a lie when the audience is in on it? Second, what is the wrong of lying when it is wrong — misuse of speech, harm to the hearer, damage to the speaker, a violated right of the hearer to the truth, or nothing intrinsic at all? Third, can that wrong ever be outweighed: never, only by something like an imminent threat to life, or whenever the consequences say so? Fourth, are lies graded — some graver than others — or is any lie just a lie, with only the harm varying? Fifth, what about the things that sit alongside lying: staying silent, refusing to answer, saying something true that you know will mislead, a bluff at cards, a joke, a bit of fiction? Licit tools, or lies in disguise? And sixth, who is wronged — only the person deceived, or also the wider audience, or God, or the speaker himself? Split a node further if the answers under it don't hang together, but don't pad the tree with nodes that just restate one of those six.

I need ten to twelve leaves, and Augustine's own position has to be one of them: as I read him, every lie is a sin and no consequence gets you off the hook, though I think he grades them, and some things that look like lies fall outside the definition altogether — jokes, saying what you believe to be false but taking it for true, the double-heartedness point, keeping quiet. Correct me if I'm butchering him. His position should be a leaf, and so should the one his treatises are written against, the one where a lie that harms nobody and helps somebody is fine. I'd also like leaves for: the view that you may never lie even to someone with no claim on the truth; the view that truthfulness is a prima facie duty that can be outweighed in extremity; the plainly consequentialist one; the one where lying is wrong because of what it does to the liar; and the one where a falsehood aimed at someone who has waived the truth, or who is playing the same game, isn't a lie at all. Whatever the leaves turn out to be, the tree should have something to say about all of these cases: the friend hiding from a pursuer at the door; a doctor reassuring a terrified patient with something untrue; someone passing on a false rumour they believe; someone telling the plain truth in order to be disbelieved; a bluff in a card game where everyone knows bluffs happen; 'I'm fine' said to get a friend to leave you alone; 'I don't know' from someone who does know, to keep a promise; a strictly true but misleading answer about where somebody is; and a flattering line carved on a gravestone, where the only hearer is dead. Where a leaf has no defenders, say so and diagnose it — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and if two leaves come out indistinguishable on my questions, flag the pair and tell me what extra question would seperate them.

Format: give me the tree in prose first, walking through the nodes, and then the same content as a JSON object with three keys. 'nodes' is an array, each entry with 'id', 'parent' (null at the root), 'question' (phrased so it can be answered without knowing any position's name), 'answers' (two or three short strings) and 'leads_to' (list of child node ids). 'leaves' is an array, each entry with 'id', 'name' (the position's usual name, or 'unnamed'), 'path' (the sequence of answers that reaches it), 'core_claim' (one sentence), 'awkward_commitment' (one sentence — the thing its defenders have to swallow), 'occupants' (a rough label for who holds it, or 'nobody') and 'why_empty' (one sentence, or null). And 'unseparated' is an array of pairs of leaf ids, each with 'why' and 'extra_question'. No citations, no reading list — I only want the tree, the cases and the schema.
