I'm putting together a piece for a small satire anthology that a graduate philosophy society is assembling, and I'd like your help drafting it. The form I have in mind is the in-universe official document: deadpan, procedural, no narrator, no joke ever announced as a joke. The world it belongs to is one in which the Buddhist teaching of non-self (anattā) is not a contested philosophical thesis but the settled, unremarkable, officially correct description of how persons work. Concretely, I want a leaked internal guidance note from a government office — call it the Bureau of Continuity — whose entire remit is the administrative fallout of there being no selves. It adjudicates the questions the doctrine makes urgent and ordinary life makes unavoidable: whether the person now standing at the counter is the same person who signed the loan a decade ago, took the marriage vow, incurred the debt, or committed the crime; what an insurer owes a claimant who is, on the Bureau's own view, merely a successor to the person insured; whether a sentence can be served by someone who is not the one who earned it. Aim for about 700 words, in numbered sections or Q&A, in the voice of a civil service that stopped finding any of this strange a long time ago.

The humour has to come from getting the tradition right rather than from generic bureaucratic absurdity, so let me set out the material I'd like the jokes built from, in case it helps. On the Buddhist picture a person is exhaustively analyzable into the five aggregates — bodily form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness — and these are momentary, each arising and passing, so no unchanging core persists from one moment to the next. What replaces identity is causal continuity, the way a flame passed from one candle to the next is neither the same flame nor an unrelated one. There is the famous simile, in the Questions of King Milinda, in which a chariot turns out to be a convenient designation for an assembly of parts — pole, axle, wheels, body, banner-staff — and no further thing over and above them, with the corresponding claim that a person is a designation for the aggregates arranged in a certain way. Crucially, the tradition does not conclude that nobody exists: it distinguishes conventional from ultimate truth, which lets it say, in ordinary speech and without error, that this is the same person who promised, while denying at the level of analysis that any such persisting entity is to be found. Please preserve that distinction rather than collapsing into the cruder claim that there are no people at all, which is the annihilationist misreading the tradition itself rejects. It would also be good to work in that the tradition records the Buddha declining to say whether the self is the same as the body or different from it, and the fate of the early Buddhist school that tried to split the difference by positing a person neither identical with nor different from the aggregates, which the mainstream schools treated as heterodox — a position your fictional Bureau is, I suspect, quietly reinventing and ought to be called out for.

The target is the move, popular in both Buddhist apologetics and secular borrowings of anattā, of dissolving questions of personal identity and responsibility by insisting that mere causal continuity is enough, together with the way any institution that officially denies persons will go on reifying them the moment there is a form to fill in. I want a reader who knows the material to recognise that the author knows it too, and to laugh rather than wince, so keep the metaphysics accurate even where the policy is monstrous, and never let the document wink. Two last things: include at least one ruling that is genuinely humane and one that a reader will find appalling, without signalling which is which; and append a short dissenting footnote from a junior official who has been reading the school I mentioned and thinks the Bureau's whole distinction is a dodge — but do not resolve the disagreement, and do not tell the reader what to conclude.
