I am taking a political philosophy module on historical injustice this term and have an essay to write on reparations. The seminar discussion has become circular in a way that I now suspect is not a disagreement about reparations at all, but a disagreement about what a theory of reparations is for. So before I commit to any particular account, I want the specification rather than a verdict: set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of reparations for historical injustice would have to satisfy, grouped as the cases it has to be able to handle, the distinctions it cannot afford to blur, and the constraints it has to be consistent with, and give for each requirement the case or consideration that motivates it rather than a general statement of principle.

Cases I want covered at a minimum: a wrong done to victims who are still living, where the state that did it has already paid and apologised (the American redress for the internment of Japanese Americans, which gave $20,000 to each surviving internee along with a formal apology and an education fund, is the clean instance); a wrong whose victims and perpetrators are all long dead (Atlantic chattel slavery, or the dispossession of indigenous peoples); a wrong committed by a state that no longer exists, or by an empire whose successor states disclaim its liabilities (Germany's 2021 declaration with Namibia, which acknowledged the genocide of the Herero and Nama but offered €1.1 billion in ex gratia development aid whilst expressly rejecting legal responsibility, is a useful test case); a wrong where the present holders of the disputed good acquired it in good faith and have nowhere else to go; a wrong without which the present-day claimants would not have existed; a claim old enough that most people would say it has gone stale (the Norman conquest is my preferred example); and a wrong that is not really historical at all, because its effects are ongoing. Add any case you think my list misses, especially ones that pull in opposite directions.

Distinctions I want the theory either to respect or to argue it may collapse: compensation for a loss, restitution of a thing, apology and recognition, and forward-looking reform, since a theory that treats these as a single currency will say something absurd about at least one of the cases above; guilt or fault as against liability, since the defendants will mostly be people who did nothing; the wrongfulness of the original act as against the wrongfulness of the present distribution; and the ground of the duty itself, whether it is that we did it, that we benefit from it, or that we are the continuing political community that owns the state and its assets.

Constraints: it must be consistent with a general account of distributive justice, since the resources in question are the same ones other claims are made on; it must not presuppose a controversial metaphysics of group identity, or treat descent as by itself sufficient for either owing or being owed; it must be determinate enough to say who owes what to whom, in what form and how much, or else explain why that cannot be demanded of it; and it must explain the difference between the claims it recognises and the ones it rejects, if there is one, between slavery and the Norman conquest.

Two things I want on top of the list. First, separate the requirements that any theory must meet from those that merely reflect the commitments of a particular camp, and say which pairs of requirements you believe cannot both be met; if the list is internally inconsistent at some point, that is the most interesting result it could have. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the main families of view, and then score corrective-justice, beneficiary or unjust-enrichment, structural-injustice, supersession (the thought that a historic claim can lapse once entitlements have arisen that cannot be undone without fresh injustice), and purely forward-looking distributive accounts against the list in a few lines each. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are what I am after.
