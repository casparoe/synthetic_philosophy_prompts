I'm in a bit of an odd spot and could use a philosopher-critic's help. My book club picked The Turn of the Screw for next month (my suggestion, long story), and seperately my mum has gotten really into a spiritualist church since my dad passed last year — she goes every Sunday now and keeps telling me she's been getting messages from him. I'm not mocking it, I honestly don't know what to think, and it's pushed me back to something I half-remember from a seminar: Henry Sidgwick, the Victorian utilitarian, poured an enormous amount of his life into the Society for Psychical Research — he co-founded it and was its first president — not as a side hobby but because he thought evidence about survival after death might be the one thing that could settle whether the universe is morally serious, whether the moral point of view is finally reasonable or just a noble preference sitting alongside self-interest. And here's the bit that stuck with me: he ended up convinced that telepathy was real, but stayed cautious — sceptical — about whether any of it actually proved survival.

So put on your hat as someone who knows both the Victorian psychical-research literature and literary criticism, and read The Turn of the Screw through that lens. Please think it through carefully and maybe brainstorm a bit before you commit to a line — I don't want a tidy thesis, I want the honest reading.

Some things I'd like you to hit:

- The governess as a witness. Sidgwick and his circle were obsessed with what makes testimony count — corroboration, the quality of the observer, whether one good witness beats a crowd of poor ones, the difference between an apparition and a hallucination, whether the percipient was already expecting to see something. How does she fare against those standards? Where is she a better witness than the critics who just call her hysterical allow, and where does the story make her testimony unusable?

- The telepathy problem, which I think is the juicy part. Suppose you grant she really perceives something. The standard objection — I think it's now called living-agent psi, or super-psi — is that anything that looks like a message from the dead could equally be telepathy or clairvoyance among the living: the governess picking up what Mrs. Grose knows or dreads, the children reading each other, and so on. If that's a live option, then even a genuine hit is worthless as evidence of survival. Does the novella dramatize that collapse? Is there a scene where the only way to keep the ghosts as ghosts is to deny the living any channel at all?

- The children. Miles and Flora aren't just victims; they're ambiguous evidence — maybe colluding, maybe perfectly innocent — and the governess's certainty about them is exactly the kind of certainty Sidgwick distrusted in himself. What does the book know about how wanting a conclusion corrupts the reading of evidence? Sidgwick wanted it so badly to be true; does she?

And then the honest part: where does the book resist this reading? If I'm forcing a psychical-research frame onto a ghost story that's really about sex, repression, class, or the Gothic, say so. If Sidgwick would have written the governess off as a bad case, or if the story shows something his methodology can't capture — what testimony does to the person giving it, say, or the way the need to believe is itself a datum — tell me. End by saying what the novella adds to the debate that Sidgwick's own careful prose doesn't.

Don't quote the text at me (I don't have it in front of me, and you can't check it), and skip the reading lists — just the reading. Ta.
