I've been following the handicap principle argument for years now and I still cant tell whether it's a real disagreement or just people using the word cost to mean different things. Signaling is basically the official religion of the blogs I read, so I've absorbed alot of this secondhand. Here's the shape of it as I understand it, correct me if I've got the history wrong.

Zahavi comes along in the 70s and says signals stay honest because they're handicaps: the peacock's tail is expensive, and it's more expensive for a low-quality peacock than a high-quality one, so only the good ones can afford to send it. Maynard Smith and others hate this for a while. Then Grafen writes down a formal signaling model in 1990 that gets honest signaling at equilibrium, and the field basically treats that as vindication and moves on. Textbook story: handicap principle, established, next question.

But then (a) people show honest signals need not be costly at all -- you get honest equilibria in cheap talk models with no handicap anywhere; (b) Getty argues Grafen's result leans on an additivity assumption about how costs and benefits trade off, which may not hold for fitness; (c) some philosophers of biology argue the whole unfakeable-index versus expensive-to-fake-handicap split is an artefact of how the models were set up, not a fact about animals; (d) a couple of biologists wrote a review basically saying there's still no decent empirical evidence that wasteful biology is maintained by selection, and the principle should be abandoned. Meanwhile the defenders say all of (a)-(c) miss the point, because the principle was never a claim about this or that model, it's a claim about why waste is informative.

My hypothesis, and I want you to tell me if it holds up: this isn't really a fight about animals. It's three or four seperate fights stacked on top of each other. There's a definitional fight over what cost means (any fitness hit? or specifically a hit that falls harder on low-quality signalers?), a modeling fight over which assumptions are load-bearing, a philosophy-of-science fight over what it even means for a signal to be honest and whether the index/handicap distinction is a real dichotomy or a modeling choice, and a boring empirical fight about the evidence. And because one name -- handicap principle -- got pasted over all of them, everyone thinks they're arguing with each other when they're often arguing past each other.

Heres a toy case I keep chewing on. Signal A costs 5 units to everyone, high and low quality alike. Signal B costs 5 to a high-quality signaler and 10 to a low-quality one. Under the usual logic B can be honest and A can't. But does anyone actually claim A is a handicap? If not, then cost in the slogan is doing none of the work and the real content is the differential-cost clause. If yes, the theory seems to swallow every signal with any cost at all, honest or not, and then I dont see what it rules out. Is that a genuine ambiguity in the literature or am I misreading it?
