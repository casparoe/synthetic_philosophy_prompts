I've been retired from the lab for about six years now — forty years of analytical chemistry, mostly mass spec work on contaminated groundwater, if that tells you anything about the kind of person I am — and with the free time I've finally started reading philosophy properly, which has been equal parts thrilling and irritating. A former postdoc of mine gave me Marcus Aurelius as a joke retirement gift and I ended up going down the rabbit hole: the Handbook, some of the letters, and then a couple of the modern Stoicism books, which are what I actually want to ask you about. The pitch there is simple and I basically buy it: draw a line between what's up to you and what isn't, spend your effort on the first pile, stop tormenting yourself about the second. But when I try to sort my own life into the two piles I keep getting snagged, and I want to know what principle my gut is actually using. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like you to take them seriously rather than correct them into orthodoxy. Getting out of bed at 6 to swim before the pool gets crowded: up to me. Whether my knee lets me swim that morning: not up to me. Flinching when a flask cracks in the fume hood: not up to me, can't help it, never could. Still getting a lurch in the chest walking past the hospital where my wife died eight years ago: not up to me. Spending the whole drive home afterward replaying the argument I lost at the department meeting, rehearsing what I should have said: up to me, and I'd say it's a failing when I do it. Whether my last paper gets accepted: not up to me. Whether the data I collected in 1994 was any good: not up to me anymore, though it was at the time. Whether I tell my doctor the truth about how much I drink: up to me. Whether my hands shake during a conference talk: not up to me (I can hold the laser pointer in both hands, but the shaking itself I can't stop). And the one that really bothers me — whether I keep believing something after I've read the counterevidence, say about a supplement my brother swears by: I want to say it's not up to me, I can't just decide to stop believing it, but I also think I'm responsible for it in a way I'm not responsible for the shaking. Also, in the middle of all this, the three-way version you see in the modern Stoicism books — total control, no control, partial control — strikes me as an improvement for cases like my knee or the paper, but it also seems to give up the thing that made the original idea bite, since almost nothing interesting is total control.

The tentative principle I came up with, which I'm not wedded to, is this: a thing is up to me just in case it's settled by my assent, and my assent to something is up to me just in case, had I been paying attention, I could have withheld it. That handles the flinch versus the grudge, I think — same emotion, but the grudge runs on assent I could have withheld. What it does badly is the belief case, where the assent is right there and doesn't feel withholdable, and it also makes the first pang of grief and the eighth year of grief differ only by whether I was paying attention, which feels arbitrary when I say it out loud. The Stoics apparently had a notion of the involuntary first twitch of a passion, before you've signed off on it, which sounds like exactly the distinction I want, but I can't tell whether it does any work or just relabels my problem. And a separate thing that nags at me: the old image of the dog tied behind the cart, following along if it's willing and dragged if it isn't, suggests the Stoics thought everything was fated anyway, so I don't see how assent can be up to me and fated at the same time — maybe that's the same puzzle as mine, maybe it's a different one, you tell me. So: what principle, or small set of competing principles, actually captures the verdicts above? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up and why I should give them up, and test it on at least one case I haven't listed — I'd suggest whether I forgive my brother, since that one I genuinely don't know about. Please give me the answer as a JSON object with these fields: "principle" (the principle in one sentence, no hedging), "restated_plainly" (two or three sentences in ordinary language), "verdict_check" (an array with one entry per case above, each entry an object with "case" as a short label, "my_verdict" as one of up_to_me / not_up_to_me / partly, "your_verdict" using the same three values, and "reason" as one line), "cases_i_got_wrong" (array of the short labels where your principle overrides me, each with a one-line justification), "new_case" (an object with "case", "verdict", and "why" for the case I haven't listed), "objection" (the single strongest objection to your own principle, stated in a couple of sentences, and your reply), and "leftover" (anything my verdicts reveal that no version of the dichotomy of control can accommodate, even if it means telling me the whole framing is confused).
