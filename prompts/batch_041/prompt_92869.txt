I've got a question I keep chewing on and I cant tell if I'm just confused, so bear with me. I spent about 35 years doing experimental physics -- condensed matter, mostly -- and since I retired I've been reading a lot of philosophy, self-taught, so apologies in advance if I mangle the vocabulary.

Here's the position I've basically come around to. I think the self is a center of narrative gravity. Not a thing in the head, but the kind of entity a center of mass is: a real, useful, trackable abstraction that falls out of the whole system, with no material part you can point to and no causal powers of its own. I like it because it's exactly how I always thought about phonons and holes in semiconductors -- genuinely useful fictions that earn their keep. And I want to push it further than I've seen it pushed: the self is whatever shortest description compresses a body's behavioral history into something that the body can also, at least partly, report on.

What I want from you is the case against. Not the greatest hits I've already run into a dozen times (the Buddhist no-self stuff, the split-brain cases) -- I want to know what actually bears on *this* version. Which empirical results or thought experiments would count against it, which would a defender just wave away as beside the point, and which of them should I really take seriously given my background? Sort by how decisive they'd be, fatal down to merely awkward. I'd especially like to know: if the self is like a center of mass, what should we expect to find that we don't? Does anything about the way a self-model seems to reach back down and change the body -- placebo and nocebo effects, say -- threaten the "no causal powers of its own" bit, or is that just me being sloppy about levels of description? And be honest with me: has a view like this been qualified so hard that nothing could count against it? If so, is that fatal, or just the price of the metaphor?

If it helps aim your answer: what would move me most is a concrete case where the compression-and-report story gives the wrong answer about who's there -- or an argument that the analogy to centers of mass breaks in a way I haven't noticed.
