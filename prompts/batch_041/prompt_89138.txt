You are a philosopher of economics with expertise in formal epistemology, social epistemology, and the theory of financial markets. I am preparing a position statement for a workshop on the epistemology of markets, and before I circulate it I want you to audit my commitments for internal consistency. Work carefully: begin by restating each commitment in your own words and identifying the motivation that makes it attractive — that is often where two of them turn out to be drawing on incompatible sources — and it may help to work through the commitments pairwise before writing anything up. Then give me the audit.

Here is where I have landed.

1. Market prices are epistemically authoritative. When I see that a share trades at forty dollars, I take myself to know, at least to a good approximation, what the share is worth, even though I know nothing about the company, its management, or its prospects.
2. Prices are informationally efficient: available information is reflected in them, and systematic mispricing is self-correcting, because correcting it is profitable.
3. I accept the no-trade result. Roughly: if agents share a common prior, are commonly known to be rational, and the only motive for trade is to profit from a difference in belief, then no trade occurs at any price, because the offer itself carries information and what it reveals cancels the gain the other side hoped for. The corollary I draw is that observed trade signals asymmetric information, differing priors, or some non-informational motive.
4. I am an internalist about justification. To know something you must possess the reasons that make your belief likely to be true; a belief whose grounds are entirely hidden from you is at best lucky.
5. I am an individualist about epistemic subjects. Only individual agents believe and know things; talk of the market's view, or of what the market knows, is shorthand at best for what individual participants can infer.
6. Three verdicts I would defend in the workshop. (a) An index investor who has read nothing about the firms she owns nonetheless knows, approximately, what they are worth. (b) An insurance market, in which nobody has private information about whether their own house will burn, is a paradigm of rational, mutually advantageous trade. (c) My own purchase of an index fund should not be treated by my counterparty as evidence about the value of anything.

Tell me which of these hang together. Be specific about which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be — I would much rather be told that two of them sit fine together than be handed a manufactured conflict. Order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say which commitment has to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me: a qualification, a weakened claim, a shift to a different notion of warrant, or an outright abandonment. Then tell me which single revision would clear up the largest number of them, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere. I am not attached to any one of these in particular; I want to know which one is doing the damage.
