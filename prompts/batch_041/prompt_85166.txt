OK so I've ended up with a view about how consciousness is spread across the animal kingdom, and I want you to try and break it.

The short version: I don't think the interesting line is brain size, or neuron count, or vertebrates vs invertebrates. I think it's whether the animal can represent the world in a way that's decoupled from what it's actually sensing right now -- whether it can hold something as being the case when it isn't in front of it, and so in a sense be wrong about the world rather than just malfunction. If that's the marker, you get a distribution that cuts across the usual groups: mammals and birds, yes; cephalopods probably yes but arrived at in a completely different way; social insects right on the borderline and maybe yes; and then a long tail of things that look clever from the outside -- plants, slime moulds, the simpler worms, all the stuff that gets breathlessly written up as smart -- where the behaviour is definately real but nothing ever gets decoupled, so no consciousness in the sense I care about.

Now tell me what would count against it. I don't mean "consciousness is mysterious" or "we can't know from the outside." I mean the actual defeaters: which animals, which results, which arguments, and how hard each one bites. Rank them from fatal to mildly awkward, and for each one say whether someone holding my view would just concede it or dig in -- I want to know which parts are load-bearing and which are just decoration.

Two things I already suspect are problems, and I want you to be straight with me about whether they're as bad as I think. First: if you define consciousness by a functional property like this, have you just changed the subject? It now looks like the property could in principle come apart from experience -- something could decouple its representations and feel nothing, or feel something without ever decoupling -- and there's no way to tell from the outside. Is that a real objection or just the usual hard-problem hand-waving? Second: I keep running into the argument that you can take any system with feedback loops and unfold it through time into a purely feedforward one with exactly the same inputs and outputs. If that's right, then tying consciousness to the wiring rather than the function makes a theory either unfalsifiable or committed to saying that two systems which behave identically differ in consciousness. Does that argument actually work, and if it does, does it hit my view too or only the ones that make consciousness depend on some specific causal structure?

Last thing: does my view predict anything its rivals don't? If it can't be seperated from "all the clever animals are conscious" or from a plain functionalism, I'd rather find that out now. And tell me what would actually move me -- a result about bees, about octopuses, about something with no brain at all -- versus what's just noise that people will keep citing at me for the next ten years.
