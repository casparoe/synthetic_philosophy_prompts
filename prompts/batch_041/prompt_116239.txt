I'm a second-year PhD student, and for the past term I've been sitting in on a reading group run by a colleague who is finishing a book on regress arguments — specifically on how to distinguish a vicious regress from a benign one — and I've been keeping a log of the rulings she gives on the standard cases, because I want to reconstruct her criterion for a seminar paper and she refuses to state it in general terms. Her verdicts so far are these. The regress Bradley runs against relations — if a and b are related by R, then R must itself be related to a and to b, and those relating relations in turn to R and to a and to b, and so on — she calls benign, a curiosity rather than an objection. Plato's Third Man, by contrast, she calls vicious, and she says it shows that the theory of Forms, as she understands it, cannot be stated without incoherence. The regress that is supposed to embarrass the resemblance nominalist — two things resemble each other in virtue of a resemblance relation, which itself resembles them, which requires a further resemblance relation, and so on — she treats as benign, on the ground that the nominalist can simply accept the sequence. Frege's observation that the concept horse, once we name it, turns out not to be a concept she says yields a hierarchy but no regress at all, and nothing to worry about in any case. And when another member of the group objected that if truths need truthmakers then the truthmaking relation itself needs a truthmaker, and that one needs one too, she waved it away as benign, saying the further truthmakers come along for free.

The case I cannot place comes from a draft chapter by a visiting speaker, who wants to explain the unity of the proposition — what makes the constituents a and F-ness hang together in the proposition that a is F, rather than sitting there as a mere list — by appeal to a relation of instantiation. His stated principle is that every proposition has a unifier and that the unifier is always a further relation, distinct from the constituents it unifies, so that the account yields an infinite hierarchy of instantiation relations, each one relating the previous one to the constituents. He is entirely explicit about this. But he insists the hierarchy is harmless, because each successive relation is supplied automatically by the proposition's being the proposition it is: the proposition could not exist without its unifier, so the regress adds nothing to our ontology and nothing to what we must explain. I cannot tell whether my colleague will call this benign, like Bradley's regress, or vicious, like the Third Man, and I have a suspicion that the difference turns on some feature of the case I haven't managed to isolate.

Please predict her verdict. Tell me which rule or rules she might be running that fit all five of her earlier rulings, what each of those rules predicts about the visiting speaker's regress, which prediction you would bet on and why, and which further case — described concretely enough that I could put it to her — would force her to choose between the rules you identify. I am much less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule, and in whether the five earlier cases really do narrow the field as much as they appear to.
