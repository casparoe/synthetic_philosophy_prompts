I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and I have run into an argument in al-Ghazālī's critique of the philosophers that I cannot sort out. I am working from a translation and my own notes, so what follows is my paraphrase in my own words, not the text's wording, and I would rather you correct my reconstruction than treat it as authoritative.

The argument concerns fire and cotton. When the two are brought together, the cotton burns. The philosophers Ghazālī is attacking hold that the fire is the real agent of the burning and that the connection is necessary: given the nature of fire and the absence of impediments, the burning must occur. Ghazālī denies the necessity. He argues roughly as follows. If the fire produced the burning by the necessity of its own nature, it could not fail to produce it when in contact with the cotton; yet we see that a barrier placed between them prevents the burning, and that a substance can be treated so as not to burn. If instead the fire produced the burning by will and choice, it would have to know the effect it produces, and fire has no knowledge. So on neither alternative is the fire a genuine cause. What we observe is a regular conjunction, which Ghazālī attributes to the habitual action of God, who creates the burning directly on the occasion of the contact and could as easily create it without the contact, or withhold it when the contact occurs. He also says that the philosophers cannot demonstrate that the connection is necessary rather than merely habitual, and that he needs only to show the opposite to be possible in order to refute their claim that it is impossible.

Here is what I cannot get straight. The argument from prevention looks to me as though it proves too much, and I suspect the trouble lies in how the nature of fire is being understood. As a physicist I would say that the disposition of fire to burn cotton is not unconditional; it holds only under a range of conditions, and a barrier removes one of those conditions. So the fact that a barrier prevents the burning does not show that the fire was not acting by its nature; it shows that the conditions under which its nature operates were not met. Does Ghazālī's dilemma require him to treat the nature of fire as unconditional and exceptionless, and if so, is that a fair reading of the philosophers he is attacking, or a simplification he imposes on them? And even granting the dilemma, how does the conclusion that God is the immediate cause follow? It seems to me that the argument at most establishes that we cannot perceive or demonstrate a necessary connection, which is a claim about our knowledge, whereas occasionalism is a claim about what actually does the causing. Does Ghazālī treat those as the same claim, or is there a further step I am missing? Finally, if the regularity is due to God's habit, what distinguishes that from a natural necessity in the philosophers' sense, apart from the fact that it could be interrupted? I would like the difference explained in terms that do not simply presuppose the theological conclusion.

Please lay out the structure of the argument as you understand it, say where you think my reconstruction goes wrong, and answer the three questions above. Tell me how confident you are in each of your claims, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
