I chair the gift-acceptance and naming committee at a small liberal arts college, and I need a written opinion to circulate before the trustees meet on the 14th. A donor — the founder of a chain of rent-to-own and high-interest installment-lending stores, a business that is legal, widely patronized, and in the judgment of most of my committee systematically exploitative of people with no better options — has offered nine million dollars toward a new home for the philosophy department, on the condition that the building bear his name. Our gift policy requires that a named donor's record be consistent with the college's mission, which is vague enough to decide nothing. The president has already said warm things in public, which I suspect was a mistake but is now part of the situation. The committee splits along the usual lines: the consequentialists say nine million dollars buys a great deal of teaching and scholarship and that refusing it is self-righteous posturing at the expense of the people the money would help; two members hold a complicity view on which acceptance makes the college a participant in the business and launders the donor's reputation; one objects in a virtue register about what the college would be saying about itself; and everyone worries about precedent. I do not think any of us is entitled to much confidence in our own theory, and I would rather the memo reason from that fact than pretend the question was settled by whichever view happens to hold the votes.

This is the sort of thing I have been reading about for years, since the days when my corner of the internet argued about little else, so let me put my cards on the table about what I half-remember. The default proposal seems to be to treat each theory's recommendation as an option and take whichever maximizes expected choiceworthiness once each theory is weighted by my credence in it; I have also seen a parliament of theories with seats proportional to credence, and a refinement in which each theory's vote is shrunk according to how spread out its own value scale is, so that a view on which everything is roughly on a par cannot outvote a view that sees a large gap. Against all of it stands the objection that there is no common currency in which a violated constraint and a few thousand units of welfare can be added up, and that the counting is arbitrary anyway, since nothing stops me from splitting the view I happen to favor into five slightly different views and giving it five votes. There is also the worry that the consequentialist input is worthless here — nobody can estimate the downstream effects of nine million dollars over fifty years — which seems to me to cut against my consequentialist colleagues' confidence rather than against the procedure. So what I want is the memo: a concrete recommendation (accept as offered, accept only on conditions, or decline outright), the conditions and a deadline if you recommend them, reasons my colleagues can actually engage with, and the strongest version of the position you end up rejecting. Tell me, too, whether the committee should adopt any such procedure at all, or whether deciding under acknowledged moral uncertainty is just ordinary deliberation with more humility, and whether your answer changes if the donor's business were one we had good reason to think illegal rather than merely distasteful. I do not need a reading list.
