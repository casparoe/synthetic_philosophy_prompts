I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on Buddhist philosophy and personal identity for fourteen students. Most have read some Parfit and some early Buddhist texts, but none has thought carefully about the difference between denying that there is a self and refusing to answer the question whether there is one. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of that difference from the inside.

The target is the position suggested by the Buddha's silence when the wanderer Vacchagotta asks first whether there is a self and then whether there is no self, and by his later explanation to Ananda that answering yes would side with the eternalists, while answering no would side with the annihilationists and would leave a bewildered questioner worse off. A minority Buddhist school, the Pudgalavadins, tried to hold a middle path: the person is neither identical with nor different from the five aggregates, and is in an important sense inexpressible — not a soul, but not a mere label either. I want the activity to put students in a position where they must make decisions that force them to take a stand, and to find that every available stand either reifies something they cannot find or dissolves something they cannot do without.

Design the activity. It should be runnable in one 90-minute session with no special materials beyond paper and pens. Give me the rules, the roles or prompts, a timeline, and a debrief. The idea should show up in what happens, not in what I say: the structure should make them run into the problem themselves before it is named. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical distinction, say what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply. You may use the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth if it helps, but do not let the activity become a lecture on it, and do not have them hear the name Pudgalavada until the debrief. The activity should not depend on citations, and it should not require students to have memorized any texts.
