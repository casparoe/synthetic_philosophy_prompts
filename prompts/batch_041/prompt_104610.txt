I am working through Kant's Toward Perpetual Peace and have hit a stretch in the Second Section — the part where he sorts states by two different principles — that I cannot get to sit still. Here is the passage in an old English translation:

"The Republican Constitution is not to be confounded with the Democratic Constitution. But as this is commonly done, the following remarks must be made in order to guard against this confusion.—The various forms of the State (Civitas) may be divided either according to the difference of the Persons who hold the highest authority in the State, or according to the mode of the governing of the people through its supreme Head. The first is properly called the form of the Sovereignty in the State (forma imperii). There are only three forms of this kind possible, according as one only, or as some in connection with each other, or as all those constituting the Civil Society combined together may happen to possess the governing power; and thus we have either an Autocracy constituted by the power of a Monarch, or an Aristocracy constituted by the power of the Nobles, or a Democracy constituted by the power of the People. The second principle of division is taken from the form of the Government (forma regiminis); and viewing the Constitution as the act of the common or universal will by which a number of men become a People, it regards the mode in which the State, founding on the Constitution, makes use of its supreme power. In this connection the form of government is either republican or despotic. Republicanism regarded as the constitutive principle of a State is the political severance of the Executive Power of the Government from the Legislative Power. Despotism is in principle the irresponsible executive administration of the State by laws laid down and enacted by the same power that administers them; and consequently the Ruler so far exercises his own private will as if it were the public Will. Of the three forms of the State, a Democracy, in the proper sense of the word, is necessarily a despotism; because it establishes an Executive power in which All resolve about, and, it may be, also against, any One who is not in accord with it; and consequently the All who thus resolve are really not all; which is a contradiction of the Universal Will with itself and with liberty."

My difficulty is not with the vocabulary. It is that I cannot see how the two claims fit together. Kant tells me that republicanism and despotism belong to the form of government, and that this is a separate question from the form of sovereignty, which is what sorts a state into autocracy, aristocracy, or democracy. He then says that democracy in the proper sense is necessarily a despotism. If democracy is a form of sovereignty and despotism is a form of government, that sentence reads to me like a category mistake — as though someone had said that triangles are necessarily green. Either he means by democracy something narrower than the term he has just defined, or the two distinctions are not as independent as he says they are, or the word necessarily is doing something other than what it appears to do. And the reason he gives does not obviously rescue the sentence: he says that a democracy sets up an executive power in which all resolve about any one who disagrees, so that the all who resolve are not really all, which he calls a contradiction of the universal will with itself and with liberty. I can follow the words and still cannot reconstruct the argument.

What I want from you is an interpretation, not a summary. Specifically: first, give me the strongest reading on which the claim that democracy is necessarily despotic is not a category mistake, stating exactly what work each of the three terms is doing on that reading. Second, reconstruct in numbered premises the argument behind the sentence about the all who are not really all, and tell me whether it is valid as you have reconstructed it or needs a suppressed premise to go through. Third, say whether, on your reading, the demand elsewhere in the essay that the civil constitution in every state be republican is compatible with his having just classified one of the three forms of sovereignty as necessarily despotic, and if it is compatible, what a state with that form of sovereignty would have to do to satisfy the demand.

I would rather have your reading defended than hedged, so close by telling me which step in your reconstruction you are least confident about, and what would make you abandon it for a different reading — a further line of the text, a distinction I could draw, or a consequence of your reading that Kant would plainly have rejected.
