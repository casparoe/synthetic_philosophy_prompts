Ok so I'm a novelist — three books, the third one is stitched together out of documents, a deposition transcript, a marriage vow, a will, a bunch of letters — and because of that a philosophy department invited me to give a talk next month at a small symposium they're running on fiction and language. The title they handed me is "the novel as speech act" and I've written a paper for it that I'm actually pretty happy with, but I've never presented to philosophers and I'm getting nervous. My thesis, in one breath: when I write a sentence like "the house on Appleton Street had been empty for eleven years," I'm not pretending to assert something the way Searle says novelists do — I'm doing a different thing entirely, I'm instating a world — and whether that sentence succeeds or fails isn't a matter of truth but of whether the reader will let themselves be bound by it. Uptake is the felicity condition, basically. Which means the interesting failure mode in fiction isn't Austin's misfire, a botched baptism, it's what I've been calling a graft that won't take: a sentence that's grammatical, perfectly consistent with everything else in the book, and still does nothing, binds nobody. I think Austin's taxonomy has no slot for that, and that's the part of the paper I most want to survive the question period.

Here's the room, as best I can tell from the emails. Mostly philosophy of language people in the Austin/Searle line, and a couple of them will die on the hill that fiction is just pretended speech; one of them works on possible-worlds accounts of fictional truth and will tell me that what I'm calling instating is just truth-in-a-world talk dressed up; two narratologists who I'm pretty sure will say I'm confusing my narrator with me; one person who does Derrida on Austin and will say my whole uptake move is just citationality under a new name; and a handful of MFA students who will ask why I'm ruining novels with theory. There's also a Kant person on the panel for some reason, no idea what she'll want.
