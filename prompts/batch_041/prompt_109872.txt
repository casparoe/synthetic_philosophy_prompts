I am assembling a small sequence of poems, one for each of a handful of problems in moral philosophy, on the principle that the verse form should carry the argument rather than paraphrase it. The poem on genuine moral dilemmas has to be a pantoum, and I would like you to write it.

The form's rule, so that we agree on it: in each quatrain the second and fourth lines become the first and third lines of the next, and the closing quatrain brings back the first and third lines of the opening one, so that the poem is a closed circuit — begin it again at the end and it continues without a seam. Five quatrains, so that the loop closes exactly.

The speaker is someone who has made a promise to two people in the same words, and who has just kept it for one of them. Nothing else distinguishes the two claims — no prior loyalty, no greater need — so the comfortable move of ranking them is unavailable, and the poem must be spoken from after the choice rather than before it. Because the promise was made in identical words, one line of the poem should be able to stand for both promises at once, and the reader should not be able to tell from the line alone which of them is being kept.

Two things must be held in view at the same time, and their tension is the point. The first is the argument that there are no genuine dilemmas: if I ought to keep my promise to the one and ought to keep my promise to the other, then I ought to keep both; if I ought to keep both, I can keep both; I cannot keep both; so at least one of those obligations was never what it seemed. The second is the remainder: whichever promise I keep, the other is not discharged, and my inability to have done otherwise does not cancel what is still owed to the person I let down. A poem that only presses the first is a proof; one that only presses the second is a complaint. The argument should be audible in the poem's movement rather than recited in it, and the recurrences are what should do the work neither half can do alone: the line that first appears as what I am obliged to do should return as what I have done, and the reader should recognize it as the same line while being unable to say which reading is right.

Do not resolve it. Leave the reader where the argument actually leaves one — seeing that a genuine dilemma would require giving something up, whether agglomeration, or the assumption that 'ought' has a single sense, or the step from 'ought' to 'can', or non-contradiction itself — and unable to say which. Let the last quatrain close the loop so that the reader wants to begin again and dreads beginning again.

Someone who has taught this argument should be able to point to the line where it is being made; someone who has never heard of it should still find the poem true.
