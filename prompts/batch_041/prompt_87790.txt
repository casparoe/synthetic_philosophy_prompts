Someone in a philosophy discord I'm in — a phil PhD student, so not a random — posted this in a metaethics thread and everyone nodded along:

Expressivism is the view that when you say "lying is wrong" you aren't describing anything, you're just expressing your disapproval: the sophisticated descendant of the old boo/hurrah theory of Ayer and Stevenson. The Frege-Geach problem is what killed it. Geach noticed that moral words turn up in places where nobody is expressing any attitude — inside conditionals, questions, negations. Take the argument: if breaking a promise is wrong, then getting your friend to break a promise is wrong; breaking a promise is wrong; so getting your friend to break a promise is wrong. That's plain modus ponens, so it's valid. But for the expressivist "breaking a promise is wrong" can't mean the same thing in the conditional as it does in the second premise, because in the conditional you're not disapproving of anything, you're only supposing. So "wrong" has to be ambiguous between the two occurrences, and the argument's validity is an illusion. Expressivists spent decades patching this — Blackburn's quasi-realism says we can talk about moral truth as if it were real, Gibbard bolts a theory of norms onto it — and it's generally agreed the patches don't hold. Which is why the field more or less moved on to non-naturalist realism. And note it only really bites on crude emotivism: any view that says moral sentences have truth conditions, like Hare's prescriptivism, is immune.
