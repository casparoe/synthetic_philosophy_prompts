Exam question: Reconstruct Mill's late position on socialism. In your answer, (a) explain the distinction he draws among forms of socialism, and (b) assess the role that individuality or liberty plays in his evaluation of those forms.

Student's answer: Mill's late writings on socialism, left unfinished at his death and published afterwards, mark a significant shift from his earlier classical-liberal commitments. Where he had once defended free markets and limited government, he came to believe that the existing distribution of property was indefensible and that some form of collective ownership of the means of production was probably inevitable. His remark that "The restraints of Communism would be freedom in comparison with the present condition of the majority of the human race" expresses his considered verdict that communism, rightly understood, is preferable to capitalism. Mill also engaged seriously with Marx's critique of political economy, though he did not live to see the full development of Marx's system.

The most important distinction in these chapters is between two forms of socialism. The first is the decentralized, voluntary kind associated with Owen and Fourier, in which small communities own and manage production jointly. The second is the centralized, revolutionary kind, in which the state takes possession of all productive resources and administers them for the general good. Mill thought the second form was more promising, since it could be implemented all at once rather than piecemeal, and he regarded centralized planning as the logical culmination of socialist thought.

Mill's reservations about socialism were chiefly about efficiency rather than liberty. He worried that collective ownership would weaken incentives and that central planners would lack the information needed to allocate resources. He also objected, on grounds of individual liberty, that any socialist scheme would subordinate the individual to the collective will, and he thought this objection applied with equal force to the decentralized and the centralized forms alike. In the end, then, Mill remained a liberal individualist who could not bring himself to endorse socialism, despite his sympathy for its critique of existing society.
