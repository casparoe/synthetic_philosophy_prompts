I'm a graduate student TAing a second-year course on social choice, and for the week on judgment aggregation I want to run a seminar on Twelve Angry Men, because the jury's deliberation seems to me to stage the discursive dilemma far better than any of the toy examples in the textbook. The stock illustration runs like this: a three-judge panel must decide whether a defendant is liable, which requires both that he breached a duty and that the breach caused the harm; one judge finds a breach but no causation, one finds causation but no breach, and one finds both — so a majority endorses each premise, while a majority rejects liability, since only the judge who found both votes for it. The film's jury has the same logical shape, its verdict being effectively a conjunction over separately arguable claims (the switchblade, the old man's testimony, the boy's alibi, the woman's eyesight across the elevated tracks), with the twist that the court asks only for a general verdict and never for issue-by-issue findings, so the jurors argue about premises while voting on a conclusion. And the drama isn't aggregation in the formal sense at all: the inputs move. No one's verdict is fixed and then counted; the jurors talk one another into and out of judgments, which is precisely what the standard model holds constant.

What I'd like from you is a reading of the film in these terms. Does it show the dilemma being solved, avoided, or merely concealed? The film's plot depends on a unanimity rule — nothing is settled until the last man agrees — and unanimity on the verdict is perfectly compatible with wholesale disagreement about the premises, so which of the two familiar procedures does the film actually depict: the premise-based one, where each issue is settled and the verdict derived, or the conclusion-based one, where verdicts are simply counted? Does its rhetorical force depend on our not noticing that these can come apart? I'm also interested in the agenda. There's a known result that when propositions are logically connected and voting proceeds sequentially, the order in which the issues are taken up can determine the final outcome even if every voter is sincere and nobody's mind changes; the film's arc is exactly a fixed sequence of defections, and it is largely Juror 8 who determines which issues get taken up and when. Is his success a matter of argument or of agenda control, and does the film want us to distinguish the two, or does it quietly conflate them? Relatedly, a known sufficient condition for consistency in majority voting over interconnected propositions is unidimensional alignment — the voters can be lined up in a single order such that on every issue the supporters of a proposition occupy one of the two ends of the line. The plot literally manufactures such a line, one juror peeling off at a time. Is that the condition being dramatized as satisfied, or is the film showing something the condition cannot capture, namely that agreement is manufactured by a sequence and a room rather than guaranteed by a structure?

Finally, I suspect the film's real subject is not aggregation but the sincerity of the inputs. A vote counts as a judgment only if it is held for the reasons that bear on the issue, and the film is ruthless on this point: the jurors who hold out longest are the ones whose votes have least to do with the evidence — the man who wants to get to the ball game, the man whose prejudice does the work for him, the man relitigating his own son. Condorcet's jury theorem, by contrast, requires only independence and better-than-chance competence, and would happily count those votes so long as they were honest guesses. So does the film reject the epistemic defence of majority rule in favour of a reason-based one, or does it merely relocate the difficulty, making the group's competence depend on a screening procedure no real jury has? I want the reading to be honest in both directions: tell me where the judgment-aggregation frame illuminates scenes a purely legal reading misses, and where the film resists it — I have a nagging suspicion that the film's true object is reasonable doubt, which is not a proposition about the world to be aggregated in the first place. No references needed; I want the argument.
