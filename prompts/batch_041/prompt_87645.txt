Me and my friend had another three-hour argument last night about whether history can be objective, and it hit me that we've had this exact fight like five times now and neither of us has moved an inch. She's basically a Ranke person — the historian's job is just to show how things actually were and let the sources speak — and I'm on the Carr/White side, where the past only reaches us through somebody's selection and narrative, so the story is made, not found. Here's the thing that gets me: we agree on all the factual stuff. We agree the sources are all we've got, we agree every narrative leaves stuff out, we even agree that White is right that the archive doesn't contain a story just sitting there waiting to be told. And then we still talk past each other every single time. So I don't want you to pick a winner, I want a diagnosis of the stall. Is "objective" quietly doing two different jobs for us — a working norm about not cooking the evidence versus a metaphysical claim about a mind-independent past — so that we're half-agreeing and half-disagreeing at once? Are we answering different questions under one word, one of us about the past and the other about historical writing? Is some hidden claim doing the real work, like whether the evidence genuinely underdetermines which narrative is right, or whether selection is unavoidably value-laden — which I don't think is the same thing as underdetermination, but maybe I'm wrong about that? Take a concrete case if it helps (a revolution told as tragedy vs. as farce, same facts, different story). Or is it bedrock — we don't share a picture of what getting it right would even look like? Engage what each side actually says rather than surveying the literature, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a reframing, or an honest nothing-will-move-this.
