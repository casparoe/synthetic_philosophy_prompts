My wifes family have run a shrimp and crab outfit for thirty years — hatchery, ponds, and a small processing shed — and in March they're putting on a dinner for about eighty people to mark my father-in-law retiring. Staff, a couple of buyers, the neighbours, the whole extended family. I've been asked to give the speech, which is normally a five-minute "what a man, what a business" thing, and I do mean it, he's decent and generous and half the town works for him. His daughter runs the hatchery and is proud of how she does it.

The trouble is I've spent two years reading about whether crustaceans actually feel anything and I can't un-know it now. The broodstock females get an eyestalk pinched or cut off with no anaesthetic because it makes them spawn faster; the harvest gets killed by dumping it in ice slurry, which from what I read often just means they suffocate slowly rather than being stunned; and the crabs still go into the boiler. There's an outfit that hands out free electrical stunners, so it's not like this is impossible, it's just money and habits.

What I want out of the speech is the real tribute work done honestly, with the uncertainty doing structural work rather than sitting in one paragraph like a disclaimer. The stuff I keep turning over: the difference between a reflex that just avoids damage and something that actually hurts; that we can't run the experiment on them and never will, so it's a guess either way; that the guess isn't from nothing, since their own painkillers seem to work on them and crabs will take a shock to get something they want, which is at least the shape of minding rather than reflexing; and that the two ways of being wrong aren't the same size, because if we're wrong one way we've wasted some money and if we're wrong the other way it's an awful lot of suffering, so the price we should be willing to pay isn't symmetrical. He'll say he's handled them thirty years and they don't feel a thing, and I don't want to humiliate him at his own dinner or turn the night into a seminar, so it has to leave him somewhere to go rather than cornering him. Please don't invent studies or numbers or put any quotes in it. And it has to land on a toast that people will actually raise a glass to, including the ones who think I've lost it. Five minutes, roughly.
