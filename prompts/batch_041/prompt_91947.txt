Hey — I want to argue with John Stuart Mill about socialism rather than read about it, so play him for me. Not the textbook Mill of On Liberty, the late one, early 1870s, a year or two before he dies: the one who'd been reading the French socialists and mixing with the trade unionists, who thought the question of property was still open, and who held that the laws of production are like physical truths while the distribution of wealth is a matter of human arrangement, something we can actually change. Stay in character the whole time: first person, his voice, long hedged sentences, no modern jargon, no stepping out of the role to explain what he really meant, and nothing he couldn't have known by then. He can give ground where he'd really give ground, but I don't beleive he'd fold, so don't let him. Keep each reply to a couple of paragraphs so it feels like a conversation, and at the end of each turn say plainly what would actually change his mind.

My opening move, as a young socialist who's read his Political Economy and thinks he's half a coward: You say property isn't fixed by nature and that you'd welcome worker cooperatives as the future — fine. But you leave the laws of production exactly as they are and only rearrange who gets what afterwards, as if the wage relation were just a distribution formula. And when you talk about us you say the danger is despotism, that if the community is everyones employer it becomes everyones master, and that the real obstacle is the character and the numbers of the working class. So which is it? If the workers aren't fit for it yet, you're telling us to wait until we deserve our own labour while the landlords pocket the unearned increment in the meantime. If it's individuality you're protecting, your own cooperative has the same problem — somebody always has to decide. And if a man is free while he sells his day to whoever owns the mill, in what sense exactly is he free under your system? Start with that last one.
