There is a standoff in the philosophy of mind that I keep circling back to, and I would like a diagnosis of it rather than another round of the argument. The epiphenomenalist holds that conscious states are caused by physical goings-on but do not themselves cause anything — not our behaviour, not our reports, not our beliefs about our own experiences. The objection is that this renders our phenomenal judgments unintelligible: when I judge that I am in pain, or that the wall looks red to me, the very state the judgment is about is stipulated to play no role in producing the judgment, so it becomes mysterious why such judgments should be reliable at all, and the epiphenomenalist appears to have undercut the evidential basis for the view he is defending. The standard reply is that the judgments are caused by the physical states that accompany the experiences, and that this secures everything the objector could reasonably demand — the judgments track the experiences because the experiences track their physical correlates, or because the content of the judgment is fixed by something other than its causal history. Both sides have had all their moves on the table for a long time, and neither has conceded.

What I want is a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, turning on two senses of causation — production versus difference-making — that let each side be right in its own idiom? Is it two questions under one heading, one ontological (does the mental have causal powers?) and one epistemic (are our judgments about experience justified?), with each side answering the question it finds interesting? Is a hidden empirical matter doing the work, namely whether the physical correlate alone suffices for the reliability the objector wants? Or do the sides differ about what a theory of content must deliver — specifically, whether a state must be caused by what it represents in order to represent it — so that the whole exchange is downstream of a disagreement about representation that is never stated? And is the persistence of the impasse evidence that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard?

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a verdict, is that the dispute is not really about causation at all: that both parties share a picture of the mind as a causal system and differ only over where content is allowed to enter, and that once this is made explicit the standoff either resolves into a tractable question about theories of content or is exposed as resting on competing intuitions about what reliability requires — intuitions that neither side can argue for from anything more basic. I would like you to test that hypothesis against what each side actually commits itself to, and to tell me plainly whether it survives.

Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the appearance of conflict, a formal or empirical result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. Engage the positions concretely enough that the diagnosis bears on them; do not give me a survey of positions on consciousness, and do not treat the existence of the debate as itself proof that it is substantive.
