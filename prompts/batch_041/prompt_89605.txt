I'm preparing a paper for a graduate seminar on decision theory and science fiction, and I want to read Denis Villeneuve's Arrival through the lens of evidential cooperation in large worlds. The idea, as I understand it, is that agents who cannot causally affect one another can still cooperate if each one's decision is evidence about the other's—because they share a decision procedure, or because they are both reasoning from a common prior about what agents like them would do. In a large enough universe, this can make cooperation with distant, unreachable agents rational even without any causal channel. Arrival seems to dramatize exactly this: the heptapods give humanity their language, knowing that in three thousand years they will need humanity's help; Louise chooses to have her daughter knowing the child will die young. But I'm not sure whether the film is a genuine illustration or a cheat. The heptapods' nonlinear experience of time means they already know the future, so their cooperation may be causal in a way that evidential cooperation is supposed to avoid; Louise's choice may be less a decision under uncertainty than an acceptance of a whole life she has already seen.

I want a reading that takes both the film and the idea seriously. Where does the film illuminate the structure of evidential cooperation—perhaps in the way Louise's choice is not about causing a particular outcome but about affirming a pattern that includes both joy and grief—and where does it distort it by removing the uncertainty that makes the problem interesting? Does the film's time loop make the trade acausal in the relevant sense, or does it collapse the distinction between evidential and causal reasoning? I'm especially interested in the difference between a one-off acausal trade, where two agents predict each other's cooperation, and the broader evidential-cooperation picture, where your choice is evidence about what a whole class of similar agents would do across a large universe. Does Arrival model the former, the latter, or neither? And what, if anything, does the film add to the philosophical debate that the decision-theory literature misses—say, about the role of love, commitment, or narrative in making cooperation with distant others intelligible? Please write the answer in American English, without citations or reading lists, and avoid quoting the film; I'm interested in the argument and the reading, not a bibliography.
