Every few years I come back to the moral-luck debate and leave convinced that the two sides are not actually contradicting each other, but I can never make the intuition precise. The no-luck side holds that a person can be assessed morally only for what was within their control, so the negligent driver who happens to hit a child is no more blameworthy than the equally negligent driver who happens not to. The luck side keeps producing cases in which our judgments plainly do track what was outside control — the outcome, the character one never chose, the situation one was born into — and concludes that the control principle is either false or idle. The exchange has run for decades and no one has moved. My hypothesis is that the impasse comes from a single word doing two jobs: fixing what a person is liable to (a backward-looking question about desert) and judging whether a response — blame, resentment, punishment, avoidance — is fitting (a forward-looking, partly evidentiary question about what the person is like and what they will do). On that reading, the outcome-luck cases are not counterexamples to the control principle at all; they show only that liability and fitting response can come apart.

Tell me whether that is the right diagnosis or whether I am papering over a genuine substantive dispute. I want three things. First, a verdict on what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, two camps answering different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical claim (for instance, about how reliably uncontrolled outcomes predict character), or bedrock disagreement about whether desert must be control-based at all — argued from what each side actually says rather than from the labels. Second, a stress test: take at least one case where the liability/response distinction seems to collapse, such as an agent whose uncontrolled outcome is also the best available evidence of a genuinely bad trait, or a case where the outcome is unlucky but reveals nothing about the agent at all, and say whether the distinction survives it. Third, an honest account of what would count as progress here — a sharper distinction, a change in the question being asked, or an argument that the stalemate is exactly what we should expect and that nothing short of abandoning one side's starting point would end it.
