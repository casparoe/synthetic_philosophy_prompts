Last week a committee at my university debated whether to recommend a statute criminalizing incitement to hatred against religious and ethnic groups — roughly the kind of law several European jurisdictions maintain. A colleague ended the discussion with a single sentence: "The remedy for bad speech is more speech." He offered it as though it settled the matter, and no one pressed him. I think he was owed an argument and did not give one, but I cannot say precisely what is missing, because I am not certain what the sentence is even claiming.

Please separate the distinct claims this one-liner could be making and assess each. I would like it decomposed along at least three axes. First, its terms: what kind of thing a "remedy" is here (a cure, a compensation, a permissible response, an obligation?); what work "bad" does (does the slogan presuppose that we can identify the speech as bad, or is it indifferent to that?); and what "more" is a comparative of — quantity of speech, or a claim that the remedy must be of the same kind as the harm rather than force? Second, its modal and normative status: is the sentence an empirical prediction that counter-speech reliably neutralizes hateful speech; a consequentialist claim that it does so better than suppression, and measured how; a deontological claim that suppression is impermissible whatever its results; a claim about the purpose of free expression — that the liberty exists for a contest of ideas, so any remedy must lie within that contest; a claim about what the First Amendment permits; or a fallibilist claim about how truth is discovered? Third, its addressee: is the remedy something the state, the audience, or the speaker is to supply?

For each reading, give a verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or question-begging — and say what that reading would require us to know or accept. Then tell me which readings could actually support my colleague's use of the sentence, that is, which of them would show that adopting the statute is a mistake; I suspect the reading doing the most rhetorical work there is the one with the least argument behind it, and I would like to know whether that is right. Finally, indicate which of these readings a European-style legal order is committed to rejecting, and whether the two camps are disagreeing about the same proposition or about which considerations count in the first place.

Lay the readings out as a numbered menu with a verdict on each, rather than choosing a single interpretation and defending it.
