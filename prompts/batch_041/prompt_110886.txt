So I watched one of those undercover videos from a factory farm last month and went vegetarian basically overnight — not because I think I'll personally save some specific cow, but because it started to feel gross to keep paying for it. My friend Dev, who's two years into a philosophy PhD and is definately the smartest person I know, told me I was wasting my energy and that the actual state of the debate is against me. Here's his summary, roughly verbatim: there's a well-known argument called the inefficacy objection, or the problem of collective harm, and it's pretty much settled. An act is only wrong if it makes a difference — if the world would've been exactly as bad without it, there's nothing to blame. Your burger makes no difference: the store orders the same cases whether or not you buy one, the slaughterhouse kills the same herd, the animal's already dead by the time you pay. Same with driving instead of taking the bus, same with voting — your one ballot never decides an election. So individual consumption choices are morally innocent, and the harm is really caused by "the system" or "society," which is why the only rational thing is to push for policy change and stop agonizing over personal lifestyle stuff. He says philosophers do have a comeback for people who want to guilt-trip you, namely that you're part of a collective that does the harm, and collectives can be responsible, so you're implicated only if you're a member of that collective — which means if you really wanted out you'd have to leave society entirely, and since that's impossible, personal morality just has nothing to say here. And the people who lecture you about your burger are mostly just moral grandstanding.

I want to stay vegetarian, but I don't want to stay for bad reasons, and something about his summary smells like it's sliding between points that aren't actually the same point. Could you go claim by claim and tell me what someone who really works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong — and then, separately, whether his conclusion (personal morality has nothing to say about my diet, so only policy matters) actually follows from the parts that survive? I'd much rather hear where you're least sure and what would flip your verdict than get a tidy answer that pretends none of this is contested.
