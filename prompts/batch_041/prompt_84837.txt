I have no academic training — I read ethics on my own and run a monthly discussion circle at my local library — but a question has been nagging at me for about a year, and it keeps splitting the group in ways I cannot predict. When two people with deeply different moral outlooks come to an agreement, when is that agreement a genuine gain for both of them, and when is it just one side buying off the other, or both sides quietly selling out what they believe? The comparison I keep reaching for is trade. Two traders do not need to agree about what is valuable; they need only differ in what they will give up at the margin, so that each has something the other wants more than they do, and then both can walk away better off. I want to know whether anything like this is true of values, and if not, exactly where the analogy breaks.

Please build me a decision tree — a flowchart I can hand round the circle and talk through out loud — that starts from commitments a person can state about their own life and sorts them into a position on what moral compromise between different value systems can and cannot be. I am not asking for a reading list, for names of authors, or for book titles; I could not check them and nobody in the room would recognise them. Give the positions short plain-language names, and where you think no such position has a standard name, say so and mark it as unnamed. What I want is the shape of the space, not a survey of who has occupied it.

Build the tree in this order: first the questions, then the positions located in the space those questions carve out.

Every node should be answerable without knowing where it leads. That rules out nodes like asking whether I believe values are commensurable. I want questions about concrete cases, or about claims a person would accept or reject, phrased so the labels stay hidden. For instance, a node might be: two housemates, one who will not have meat in the kitchen for ethical reasons and one who thinks there is nothing wrong with eating it — is their disagreement about which of the arrangements actually on the table is best, or about whether any arrangement they have thought of so far counts as a settlement at all? Another might be: a person who takes a well-paid job in order to give most of the money away — has she compromised her values, or has she simply found a better way of serving them? Each node should offer two or three answers, and for at least a few of them I want to see that the answers are genuinely open, that people I know would give different ones.

The distinctions I care about most, because they seem to decide everything downstream:

1. Whether the two sides can be ranked against each other at all — whether there is any answer to the question of which arrangement is better or worse all things considered — or whether each side's good is the sort of thing that only makes sense from inside a life, so that the question of ranking is a mistake rather than a hard question.

2. Whether the disagreement is about the ranking of options or about which options exist. Trade, as I understand it, lives entirely in the second case: the traders are not arguing about who is right, they are creating a new possibility, a package neither had before. If moral disagreement is only ever about ranking fixed options, there may be no room for trade at all, and compromise would mean something else — a split, a rotation, an alternation, or a capitulation.

3. Whether what each side is offering up is the kind of thing that can be given away. In trade, what you hand over is yours to hand over. But some commitments look like limits a person will not cross even to prevent something worse, and some look like commitments that belong to them in particular, things they would not demand of a stranger, which might be tradeable in a way the limits are not. I want the tree to separate these, because I suspect most of the disagreement in my circle comes from people sliding between them.

4. Who is at the table. Two individuals, one person negotiating with her own future self, two traditions, or a whole society. And whether the people affected by the agreement are the people making it: a deal between two of us that lands on a third party who never consented seems to me a different animal from a trade, and I would like that written into the branches rather than mentioned as a footnote.

5. What has to be justified, and to whom. Does the agreement need to be defensible to each party in its own terms, or to an outside observer with no stake in it, or is it enough that neither party can improve on it?

6. Whether the settlement has to be a single package, or can be spread across time — I do this your way now and you do it my way later — and whether spreading it changes its character.

7. Whether a party can be paid off in its own currency. If one side's concern is for animals and the other's is for compatriots, can a gain on one ledger count as compensation for a loss on the other, or is that a category mistake dressed up as a deal?

Then, once the questions are laid out, place the positions: seven to nine leaves, each with a plain-language name and one sentence on the commitment that sits most awkwardly with it, the thing a defender would have to swallow. I expect some of these will be: an agreement as mutual gain by each side's own lights; splitting the difference; taking turns; the view that there is nothing to compromise because the two sides are not measuring the same thing; the view that one side's concern simply outweighs the other's, so that talk of compromise is a distraction; the view that some commitments cannot be traded at all and the honest response is refusal; and the view that what looks like compromise is usually a change of heart in disguise, which is not a compromise but a conversion.

Finally, two things I would like done honestly rather than tidied away. First, list the combinations of answers that no position occupies, and for each say whether you think it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to explore it; I would rather have the empty cells diagnosed than filled. Second, name any two positions your tree cannot tell apart, where someone answering the same way could land in either, and say what further question would be needed to separate them.

Keep the whole thing to eight or ten nodes and seven to nine leaves. The audience is a room of adults with no philosophical training who are perfectly willing to argue, so plain words throughout, and no jargon that would need a glossary — if you must use a technical term, unpack it in the same sentence.
