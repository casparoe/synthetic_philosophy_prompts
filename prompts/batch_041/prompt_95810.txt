I'm a third-year history student, and I'm taking a seminar on comparative economic history this term, which is how I ended up as the grad rep on the steering committee for our department's Global Economic History workshop series. We have money for exactly one event this term, the two profs who normally decide are both on leave, and so it's fallen to me to circulate a short written recommendation before Thursday's meeting. I've been putting of writing it for a week because I genuinely don't know which way to come down.

Two proposals came in and they're weirdly opposed. The first is a standard Great Divergence affair: a one-day workshop on why sustained intensive growth took off in northwestern Europe rather than in the Yangzi delta, with the usual suspects on the program — coal, colonies, property rights, high wages, family structure, the whole California-school-versus-everyone fight. The second is from a young economic historian who thinks the way we ask the question is broken. Her line is that "why Britain and not China" is a badly formed question, because the contrast class is doing all the work and the outcome only looks like an outcome once you've already picked the endpoint — swap China for Japan, or for the Netherlands, or for the whole of Eurasia, and you get a different explanation out. She wants the workshop to be about how to pose contrastive questions properly, using the Great Divergence as the test case, and she's cribbing from the philosophy-of-science literature on contrastive explanation (the why-this-rather-than-that stuff). She has a concrete proposal: before you look at the outcome you have to name the contrast class, you have to show the two cases were comparable on the relevant background conditions up to the point of divergence, and you have to say what work the counterfactual is doing instead of just gesturing at it. I find her version more interesting, honestly, but I'm worried we'd spend our whole budget on a workshop that tells a room full of historians their field is ill-posed, and my supervisor does Needham-adjacent work and would probably take it personally.

Please draft the memo I can send round. I need an actual recommendation — one proposal, or a hybrid if a hybrid is genuinely defensible and not just cowardice — plus the reasoning, and the strongest version of the case for whichever option I don't pick, because two people on the committee will make exactly that argument and I need to have an answer ready. Be concrete about what the day would actually look like (sessions, who does what, what the output is), keep it under about 900 words, and please don't let it turn into a referendum on Eurocentrism — that isn't really the issue and it will blow up the meeting.
