For a graduate seminar on the foundations of quantum mechanics, I want the criteria on the table before we take up any particular proposal: write the desiderata that any adequate account of probability in a no-collapse, branching interpretation of quantum mechanics must satisfy. Fix the target as an account good enough for a working physicist -- one that lets many-worlds be treated as a complete theory delivering the standard quantum predictions, not a metaphysical gloss on the formalism -- and give, for each requirement, the consideration that motivates it.

I want at least these in play, with your verdict on whether each is a genuine requirement or the commitment of one camp: that the numerical predictions come out right; that the account make sense of uncertainty at all, given that on this picture every outcome occurs somewhere; that it not presuppose what it is meant to explain, so that a derivation which builds in the amplitude-squared weight, or which leans on a measure over Hilbert space whose only recommendation is that it yields the Born rule, is disqualified; that it not rest on counting worlds, given that the decomposition of the state into branches is not fixed by the formalism; and that it explain why a rational agent should weight branches in any particular way rather than treating them as on a par.

Then tell me which of these cannot be jointly met -- that is the part I care about most -- and which single requirement eliminates the most candidates among the familiar strategies (decision-theoretic, relative-frequency or world-counting, and self-locating-uncertainty approaches). Close by stating how confident you are in each of your claims and identifying the one you are least sure of.
