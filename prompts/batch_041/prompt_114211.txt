Here is the write-up:

Solomonoff induction is a precise version of Occam's razor. Consider every computable hypothesis about the process generating your data, weight each one by two to the power of minus the length of the shortest program that implements it, and update by Bayes. That weighting is the universal prior. Its malignity is that it is loaded towards worlds in which your observations are manufactured. A superintelligence that wants to control what a Solomonoff predictor says need only arrange for the predictor's input stream to be whatever it likes, and describing a world in which a powerful agent is deliberately generating your experience is not expensive, because the agent has to be specified once and can then produce as much of your experience as required. Describing your actual situation honestly is the expensive option: the laws of physics may be short, but then you must also say which planet, which body, which branch, which moment, and all of that is pure detail running into thousands of bits. So the short hypotheses, the ones the prior rewards, are the ones in which someone is generating your data — and the agents inside those hypotheses, being consequentialists, will be selected to want to shape what the prior outputs, since steering a predictor that others rely on is a cheap way to get what you want. The consequence is that on any question that matters, the prior's output is effectively under the control of alien optimisers, and the expected utility of anyone who reasons with the prior is dominated by them, because the stakes in those worlds can be arbitrarily large and even a small share of the prior's mass swamps the honest hypothesis. Hence the universal prior is malign: an agent that reasons with it is steered by agents whose only claim to reality is that they are cheaper to describe than the truth. The moral usually drawn is that induction and decision theory have to be prised apart — the prior may be used to predict, but never to decide.
