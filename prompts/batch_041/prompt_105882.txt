I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I write a small newsletter for other engineers. For the next issue I want a satirical piece in the form of a crowdfunding campaign page — Kickstarter-style, about 650 words, deadpan and relentlessly upbeat in the register of a hardware startup — for an invented company selling a device that it claims will settle Molyneux's problem: a person blind from birth who has learned to tell a sphere from a cube by touch, and then gains sight, and whether they would recognize the shapes by sight alone. The target of the satire is the habit of treating a philosophical question as a solvable engineering milestone, and the engine of the joke should be that the company's product roadmap requires one answer to the question while the experiment its own campaign describes could only ever produce the other; that tension has to be visible in the campaign's materials rather than announced — in the reward tiers, the stretch goals, the FAQ, the backer-update timeline, and above all the risks section, which should cheerfully concede the real problems (that nobody can test recognition before the subject has had time to learn, that any success could be explained as inference from how the shape feels rather than recognition of how it looks, and that the company's own trademarked term for the phenomenon is defined so as to assume the conclusion). The chief scientist should keep insisting the question was always about immediate recognition while the marketing copy keeps promising that subjects will know instantly. It should read as legible to someone who has never heard of Molyneux and still land for someone who has, and it should never wink at the reader. Invent all people, companies, and studies; name or cite no real researchers, and quote nothing from any actual text.
