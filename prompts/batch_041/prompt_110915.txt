I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so I would be grateful if someone could tell me whether a pattern I keep running into has an established name and a literature behind it, or whether I have invented a puzzle for myself.

The pattern concerns what happens when a concept is conveyed to me by a handful of examples rather than by a definition. In such cases there seem to be two quite different kinds of information in what I am handed. The first is the content of the examples: each one, taken on its own, rules something out. The second is the fact that these examples, rather than any others, were the ones placed in front of me. This second kind of information cannot be read off any individual example, and it is frequently the stronger of the two. Three instances, in increasing order of how much they have puzzled me:

First, there is a puzzle format I have seen called a Bongard problem: a set of small line drawings is divided into two groups, and the task is to state what distinguishes the groups. Suppose the intended distinction is something like the number of straight segments. Nothing in the drawings excludes the rule "has an odd number of straight segments, or is one of these particular twelve drawings", which fits the given material perfectly. What rules it out is not the material at all but my assumption that whoever drew the twelve drawings chose them so that a person could discern one particular distinction — an assumption about the setter's choices that is nowhere among the examples. I notice that I make that assumption without thinking, and that I am irritated by the disjunctive rule even though it fits.

Second, in my own work I sometimes prompt a language model with a handful of examples of a category I have in mind. If all my examples happen to concern invoices, the model will often take invoices to be part of the category, which they are not, because I chose those examples for reasons of my own. The converse also happens: when I deliberately include one example that sits at the edge of the category in order to indicate where the boundary lies, the model will often treat it as one more ordinary instance, and the boundary is not registered. So a signal seems to be present in the first case and is over-read, and present in the second and is missed.

Third, when a colleague explains a class of bug by showing me three reports, I infer the scope of the class from which three were chosen. That one report is from an old release and another from a current one tells me the class spans releases, although no report says so. Were those three reports merely the first three results of a search, the same inference would be worthless, and I would be wrong to draw it.

What I want to know is whether there is a name for this: for the practice of treating the selection of examples, as distinct from their content, as evidence about the extension of the concept — about which further cases would count and which would not — and for the corresponding error of reading selection as evidence when the selection was indifferent, or of ignoring it when it was not. I am reasonably sure the answer is not "overfitting" or "spurious correlation", since those concern a fault in the learner, whereas here the difficulty lies on the side of the evidence, and the error is sometimes the failure to use a real signal. Nor do I think it is simply the everyday reasoning of the form "why are you telling me this", because what I am inferring is not what the speaker intended to convey but the boundaries of a concept. A bare label will not help me; what I am after is the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, how many phenomena are involved, and where its edges lie.
