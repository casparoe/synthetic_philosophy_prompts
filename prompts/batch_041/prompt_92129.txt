I have spent several years defending a version of negative utilitarianism that I want to state as precisely as I can, because I have come to think the precise statement is where its problems live, and what I want now is a map of those problems rather than another round of general sympathy or general alarm.

The view — call it threshold-lexical negative utilitarianism — has five commitments.

First, welfare admits of a complete ranking: any two lives or episodes can be compared as better or worse. But the ranking is not underwritten by a single commensurable quantity of value running through the whole scale. Suffering and enjoyment are comparable in the region where they trade off, and not comparable above it.

Second, there is a threshold θ on the suffering scale. Suffering at or above θ is lexically prior to enjoyment: no finite quantity of enjoyment, however large, and no number of beneficiaries, however great, outweighs or compensates a single instance of supra-threshold suffering.

Third, below θ suffering and enjoyment trade off at a finite exchange rate, so mild harms can be outweighed by sufficiently large benefits. The view is therefore not purely negative; it is negative only above the threshold.

Fourth, aggregation is total and additive within each region: sub-threshold harms sum, and supra-threshold harms are compared by count and severity. What counts as supra-threshold is assessed at the level of the individual episode rather than the aggregate, though I have deliberately left open whether θ should be indexed to the episode or to the life, and I suspect that choice does most of the work in the theory.

Fifth, this is meant to be a decision-guiding theory and not merely a ranking of outcomes: it should tell me what to do under uncertainty, where the probabilities attach to outcomes that may fall on either side of θ.

What I want from you is an honest account of what would count against this.

1. Which cases the view gets wrong, and which of those verdicts are forced by its structure as opposed to repairable by resetting θ or the exchange rate. I am especially interested in the cases where it licenses imposing very large numbers of sub-threshold harms to prevent one supra-threshold harm, and in the mirror-image cases where a single supra-threshold harm is licensed to secure enormous sub-threshold benefits.

2. The formal results that bear on it. Can a structure with a lexical break be represented by any real-valued value function? What becomes of expected-utility representation when the preference order is lexicographic over lotteries — does the failure of continuity matter, and does it yield a theory that cannot rank gambles at all, or only one that ranks them badly? And what does partial incommensurability do to transitivity and completeness, given that the incommensurability relation is standardly taken to be nontransitive?

3. The collapse worry. Is the lexical threshold doing genuine work, or is it a way of smuggling in a deontological side-constraint, or instead a way of stating ordinary hedonistic utilitarianism with a priority weighting and a rhetorical flourish? If it collapses, into what does it collapse?

4. Whether any of this is empirical. Is there a defensible reading of the pleasure/pain scale on which intensity is the right parameter for θ, or is the choice of intensity over duration, frequency, and the subject's own evaluation itself a substantive and questionable commitment?

5. Whether the view is insulated. If every objection can be met by moving θ, what would it take to show that the view is unfalsifiable, and is unfalsifiability a defect for a normative theory of this kind or simply what any theory with a parameter looks like from outside?

Rank the defeaters from those that would be fatal to those that are merely awkward, and say of each whether a committed defender should concede it or dismiss it. Where a disagreement turns out to be verbal — for instance, whether the arbitrariness of the threshold is a defect at all — say so, and say why. Give me the divergence cases as well: the situations in which this view and its obvious rivals (hedonistic utilitarianism, a purely negative utilitarianism without a threshold, and prioritarianism) return different verdicts, since those are where the theory's actual exposure lies.

Tell me how confident you are in each of the claims you make, and single out the one point where you are least sure.
