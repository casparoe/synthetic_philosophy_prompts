I'm assembling the take-home portion of the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on scientific inference. Students have read the standard material on inference to the best explanation and on Bayesian confirmation, may use their notes, and are not expected to cite anything.

I need one essay question — 90 minutes, 20 marks — that cannot be answered by summarizing the familiar debate over whether inference to the best explanation is just Bayesian inference in other words, or by reciting the bad-lot objection. Build it around a claim a student made in our last meeting, which the class never resolved:

"IBE is two moves glued together: a generative move, in which a hypothesis is invented to explain the evidence, and a selective move, in which that hypothesis is then accepted because it is the loveliest one available. Only the first is distinctive of IBE. The second is just an informal way of reporting a comparison. So IBE is not a rule of inference at all; it is a name for the fact that we sometimes have to invent our hypotheses before we can test them."

Deliver (1) the question, framed so that a strong answer must decide whether this decomposition is correct and what follows for IBE either way; (2) a model answer of roughly 650 words; and (3) a marking scheme of five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, with at least two points on what is lost or gained if the decomposition is accepted and at least one on what would have to be true for the generative/selective distinction to do real work. After each point, add a single line on how candidates typically lose those marks. The question should not turn on any one author's formulation, and it should not reward students for merely naming positions.
