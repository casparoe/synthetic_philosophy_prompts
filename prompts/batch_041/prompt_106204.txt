Here's the question I set and, below it, the answer the student turned in, transcribed as-is (I've fixed nothing).

Question: "State the asymmetry Benatar relies on and explain how he gets from it to the conclusion that procreation is always wrong. Then state the strongest objection you can to the argument and say whether it succeeds."

Answer: "Benatar's asymmetry is a set of four evaluative claims about pain and pleasure. The presence of pain is bad; the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of pain is good, even if there is no one around to enjoy that absence; and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless somebody is deprived by it. Taken together, the last two say that the absence of a bad counts in favour of not creating someone, while the absence of a good does not count against not creating them. So when we compare creating a child with not creating one, the not-creating column never has a mark against it, whereas the creating column always has at least the possibility of pain in it. That is the sense in which the never-existing child is better off than any child who does exist, even a very happy one. Benatar uses the asymmetry to explain three other things we commonly believe: that we do not regret failing to conceive a happy child, that we do regret conceiving an unhappy one, and that we are obliged to prevent suffering but not obliged to create happiness. Since all three fall out of the asymmetry, the asymmetry is not just an ad hoc manoeuvre. So procreation is always wrong -- wrong to create a happy child, wrong to create an unhappy one -- because the child who never exists is spared something while losing nothing. Benatar also draws the further conclusion that since existence is a harm and non-existence is a benefit, people who already exist would be better off dead, though he concedes this is a hard conclusion to live with. The obvious objection is that the asymmetry is arbitrary: why should the absence of pain count but the absence of pleasure not count? Benatar's reply is that the asymmetry is intuitive and that it is confirmed by the three other asymmetries above. I think this reply mostly works, though it is doing a lot of work with 'intuition'."
