Institutions that allocate scarce goods and institutions that choose officeholders both depend on participants reporting what they want, yet they treat those reports in opposite ways. A spectrum auction or a second-price auction is engineered so that a participant's best move is to state her true valuation, and the deviation of a mechanism from that property is treated as a design failure worth years of refinement; whole subfields exist to certify that bidding honestly is a dominant strategy. General elections are run on plurality or on a two-round runoff, and procedures of that kind are known, from a result long established in social choice theory and usually credited to Gibbard and Satterthwaite, to be manipulable in a precise sense: once there are at least three alternatives, no restriction is placed on how voters may rank them, and the procedure is responsive to those rankings and can produce any of the alternatives, then some voter can always gain by misreporting her ranking -- unless the procedure simply lets one fixed voter dictate the outcome. The same society that will not tolerate a manipulable auction tolerates a manipulable election as a matter of course, forbids the buying and selling of votes, and runs campaigns urging citizens to vote their honest preference, as though the two domains had nothing to do with each other.

What philosophical commitments does this split presuppose? Begin with the preferences themselves. Markets of the auction kind are analyzed on the assumption that utility is transferable and that each participant's valuation is a private quantity comparable to money; the ban on vote-buying and the refusal to let intensity of preference be traded suggest that political preferences are being construed as something else -- non-transferable, perhaps incommensurable, perhaps not even the right kind of thing to be aggregated by a mechanism that could be made strategy-proof. Is that a substantive commitment about the nature of political value, or only a convention? Then take the purpose of the institution. If an election's point were efficiency, manipulability would be a defect of the same sort it is in an auction; if its point is legitimacy, or the truthful aggregation of judgments about a common good rather than of private desires, then manipulability might be tolerable or even beside the point. Which of these does the practice actually require, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Notice too what the choice of a manipulable rule implies about the domain: a strategy-proof rule does exist for single-peaked preferences, so running elections on plurality or runoff presupposes that we are not entitled to assume any such structure -- that political preferences are genuinely unrestricted. Is that presupposition load-bearing, or an artifact?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Distinguish what the split strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify the rival views it quietly rules out, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license the same practice. Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced -- if elections were redesigned on the assumption that they are pure preference aggregation and must be strategy-proof, or if auctions were redesigned on the assumption that they must confer legitimacy rather than allocate efficiently.
