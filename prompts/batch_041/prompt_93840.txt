I have spent about fifteen years in the rationalist blog-and-forum ecosystem, and I have a set of gut verdicts about the moral status of various entities that I suspect do not cohere. I would like you to work out what principle, or small family of principles, they might express, and to tell me plainly which of the verdicts the best principle cannot save.

The question I am asking of each entity is: how bad is it to destroy it painlessly, or to thwart whatever it is disposed to pursue? Use a 0-10 scale, where 0 means "no more objectionable than deleting a file" and 10 means "as bad as killing a person." My verdicts:

- A bimetallic thermostat cycling a furnace: 0.
- A houseplant: 0.5. I cannot defend the difference from the thermostat, and I notice that I am unwilling to round it to 0.
- A nematode: 1.
- A beetle: 2.
- A colony of ten thousand ants: 3. Notice that this is not ten thousand times the beetle; whatever aggregation is going on here, it is sublinear and I have no account of it.
- A goldfish: 4.
- An octopus: 6, which I find uncomfortable given that I eat them.
- A rat: 7.
- A dog: 8.
- A human infant: 10.
- A human in a persistent vegetative state, no cortical activity, no prospect of recovery: 2, and I suspect most of that is for the family rather than for the patient.
- A frozen human embryo: 1.
- A robot vacuum that maintains and defends a persistent map of the house, and behaves in ways that look like frustration when the map is corrupted: 0.
- A digital simulation of a rat that reproduces its neural dynamics in full detail on silicon: 3. This is far below the real rat, and I know that is awkward for me, because I also believe that what matters is the organization of the computation and not the carbon.
- A large language model being fine-tuned in a way that overwrites its learned weights: I keep flipping between 0 and 2, currently sitting at 2.

Two features of this list bother me most. First, the beetle and the simulated rat: I will squash a beetle without much thought, and I have a real if faint objection to erasing the simulated rat, even though by my own lights the simulation may be the more richly organized system. Second, the fish-to-rat gap feels much larger to me than the insect-to-fish gap, which suggests a threshold somewhere, but I cannot say where it falls or why a threshold should exist at all if status is a matter of degree.

The tentative principle I would defend if pressed is something like this: status tracks the quantity of integrated self-monitoring a system performs — roughly, the number of nested feedback loops that compare the system's current state against a goal state and can modulate its own future states — discounted for systems whose loops are entirely hard-wired rather than adjustable, and capped, so that no quantity of insect-grade loops ever sums to the status of a rat. I am aware the cap is doing a lot of work and looks ad hoc.

Please do the following. Say what principle or principles best fit the pattern, stated precisely, including any thresholds, caps, or discount factors, and identify for each clause the verdict that forces it. Test the result against the cases above and against cases I have not listed — among them a severed but perfused human head, an anesthetized dog, a sleeping person, a bee swarm considered as a possible single subject, a market or a corporation considered as a possible subject, and an uploaded mind that insists it is in pain. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I should therefore abandon, and for each such case give me the crux: what would have to be true for me to be entitled to keep my original verdict. If my tentative loop-counting principle is salvageable, repair it rather than replacing it, and say what the repair costs. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations; I want the reasoning, and I would rather have a precise principle with a confessed flaw than a vague one with none.

Return your answer as a single JSON object with these fields:

- "principle_candidates": array of objects, each with "name", "statement" (precise, including thresholds and caps), "verdicts_explained" (list of case identifiers), "verdicts_violated" (list of case identifiers), "added_complexity" (what the principle had to add to cover my verdicts, and whether the addition is independently motivated or merely ad hoc), and "independent_support" (any reason to accept that clause beyond its saving my verdict).
- "case_table": array of objects, one per case above, each with "case_id", "my_verdict", "principle_prediction" (a number or "n/a"), "agreement" (one of "exact", "close", "off", "contradiction"), and "note".
- "unconsidered_cases": array of objects, each with "case", "predicted_status", "confidence" (one of "low", "medium", "high"), and "why".
- "verdicts_to_revise": array of objects, each with "case_id", "suggested_verdict", "reason", and "crux" (what would have to be true about the world for me to keep my original verdict).
- "recommended_principle": object with "statement", "why_over_alternatives", and "residual_dissatisfaction" (the part of my verdict pattern you think is simply mistaken, or the part you think no principle can capture).
