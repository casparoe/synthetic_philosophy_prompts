For most of my working life I was an entomologist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with rather more enthusiasm than system; I now find myself asked to lead next month's session of the discussion circle at our lifelong-learning institute. The audience is a dozen or so educated non-specialists — several retired scientists, none of them philosophers — and the topic I proposed is one that has been nagging at me: the digger wasp that will not carry its paralyzed cricket into the burrow until it has inspected the entrance, and that, when the experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, finds the cricket, drags it back to the threshold, and inspects the entrance all over again, the loop resetting every time. Hofstadter called this kind of rigidity sphexishness, and the question I want the session to reach is whether the label is as comfortable as we assume it is: if our own cleverness consists in stepping out of the loop, what do we say when the stepping-out turns out to be a loop one level up, and where in any of this does free will actually live? Please do not explain any of that to me — what I need is a plan for teaching it.

Lay out the order in which the ideas must come, so that nothing is asked of the audience before it has been earned, and tell me which single example should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack. Left to myself I would have reached for a thermostat or a chess engine; say whether the wasp is the better spine, and why, and tell me how to keep that example from collapsing if someone objects — as I half suspect from the ethology literature — that the wasp is more adaptable than the parable allows. Then give me the misconceptions they will bring or form, and mark the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off: I predict at least "rigidity is for insects, flexibility is what we have," "flexible just means unpredictable or random," and a vague notion that free will requires being able to have done otherwise in that very moment. Close with the one question I should ask at the end to show whether they have grasped the shape of the problem rather than merely been unsettled by it, and tell me plainly what to leave out at this level and to say outright that I am leaving it out. Keep the whole thing to a single page — no more than 650 words — as a numbered sequence of steps, with the misconception-handoffs flagged inline.
