I teach a second-year lettering studio -- 16 students, one 90-minute session, big tables and a wall we can tape things to. Every year they show up thinking a letter is a shape you either draw right or wrong, and I want one exercise that makes them feel the actual problem: an 'a' isn't a shape, it's something about how a mark sits in a system of other marks and other readers. I want them to walk into the wall where making everything match your style starts eating the letters themselves, and then have to argue about who gets to say where a letter stops being that letter. No intro lecture. Don't name any theory, don't say prototype or essence or family resemblance, and save all labels and named ideas for the last ten minutes -- I want it to land as thier own discovery, not as me telling them a thing that has a name.

Materials: index cards or sticky notes, lots of them, thick markers, tape, the wall. No computers.

The plan, roughly (change details if you need to, but keep the order):

Round 1 -- invent a style, then get read. Teams of 3-4, so four or five teams. Each team invents a style rule they can state in one sentence -- something like "every stroke is an arc of the same circle," or "all strokes sit at exactly 45 degrees," or "no stroke is wider than a pencil line." Then each team draws six letters in that style. Make it a, e, o, n, t, plus one of their own choosing. They write the style rule on the back of a card and keep it secret.

Then the panels. All the glyphs go up on the wall, shuffled, anonymous. Every other team writes down (a) what letter each glyph is and (b) what they think the style rule is. Score the drawing team on both numbers: how many glyphs were read correctly, and whether anybody recovered the rule. They'll see the tradeoff immediately -- the more legible you are, the more you've just drawn a normal font, and the more your rule shows, the more readers guess wrong.

Round 2 -- the transplant, both directions. (i) Take your own style rule and now apply it to the letters you were avoiding, the awkward ones: g, s, k, r, and a second 'a' if you want one. (ii) Take one of your six glyphs and hand it to a different team, who put it into their alphabet. Ask two questions: does your 'a' still read as an 'a' when it's standing next to their letters, and does their alphabet still look like one family with your letter sitting in it? This is the part where it clicks that "is this an a" and "does this fit the style" are not two independent questions.

Round 3 -- the boundary strip. Everybody does the same letter, and it has to be 'a', it's the most loaded one. Each team makes a strip of five or six variants running from barely stylized and dead normal out to so stylized it's ambiguous with something else -- they'll find the neighbours on their own, 'a' drifting toward 'd', toward 'o', toward 'e', toward a squiggle. Tape all the strips side by side along the wall. Now the whole class walks the wall and, for each strip, puts a marker at the point where they'd say it stopped being an 'a'. Individual and silent, no negotiating first, then look at the spread. The spread is the lesson.

Then the reveal, which is the part I most want to get right. Put a normal text 'a' on the board next to a handwriting 'a' -- the two-storey one with the bowl and the hook over the top, and the single-storey round one. Same with 'g'. Ask whether those are the same letter, and what exactly is shared. Then write 'rn' next to 'm' in a couple of fonts, and put 'l', 'I' and '1' side by side. The thing to draw out, in their words and not mine: nothing in the shape survives across everything we call 'a'. What survives is a job. 'a' is whatever occupies that slot and stays distinct from its neighbours in the set. Which means the boundary is being drawn by readers, readers are a moving population, and the "essence" is looking more like a stable role than a fixed form. Let them fight over whether that means there's no essence at all or only a relational one -- that's a live disagreement and I don't want it resolved for them.

Debrief, last twenty minutes or so, mapping what happened onto what:
- the guessing panel = category membership is graded and reader-relative, not a switch
- the rule-recovery score = style is what makes a set a set, and it's relational too, you only see the rule by contrast
- the transplant = a glyph has no identity on its own, identity is a position in a system
- the spread on the boundary strips = the intuition that there's a fact of the matter about 'a'-ness is doing real work, and it's not obvious where it comes from

What will predictably go wrong, and what to do:
- At least one team will do a knockoff Helvetica-ish geometric sans and win the legibility half trivially. Let it happen and use it, that's the legible-but-anonymous pole.
- The guessing round will produce ties and shouting over specific glyphs. Don't referee. Make them flag the disputed ones, those are the gold.
- Someone will draw a non-letter, or a Cyrillic or Greek shape, and the class will get distracted. Give it a minute, it's on topic.
- Teams converge hard, everyone goes geometric. Keep two or three weird rule seeds in your pocket to hand out if the styles bunch up.
- The boundary-marking gets social fast, the first markers anchor everybody. Do it individually and silently, collect, then reveal.

And be straight with me about what the exercise can't do. It can't reproduce the thing that actually makes letterforms stable -- centuries of readers, printing, habit -- sixteen classmates arguing for an hour is a far more negotiable crowd than that, so the activity probaly makes the boundary look more up for grabs than it really is, and I'll have to say so out loud. It can't settle whether there's a real essence or only conventions, it can only make the question sting a little. And it can't touch why some contrasts are perceptually easy and others aren't -- that's a seperate session.
