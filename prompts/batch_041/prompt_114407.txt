Take the sixteenth-century quarrel between the Dominicans and the Jesuits over efficacious grace: the controversy that began in the 1580s, ran through Domingo Báñez's attack on Luis de Molina's Concordia of 1588, and ended up before the papal commission Clement VIII convened to settle it, which Paul V eventually dissolved without condemning either side, leaving both the Dominican theory of physical premotion and the Jesuit theory of middle knowledge teachable. I know the standard summary of the two positions. What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions, and that is honest about where the theory has nothing to say.

First, the theory illuminating the case. Map middle knowledge onto the dispute with some precision. What does Molina's threefold division of divine knowledge — natural, middle, free — commit him to, and what exactly are the Dominicans denying when they deny the middle term? Which features of the episode does the theory genuinely explain: why Molina needed a third kind of knowledge rather than stretching foreknowledge to cover hypotheticals, why the Dominicans read the middle term as turning God into a spectator of an independently choosing creature, why a quarrel could persist for decades even though both sides affirmed that efficacious grace infallibly secures its effect? And which features does it miss or misdescribe: the institutional rivalry between two religious orders, the Spanish crown's stake in the outcome, the pope's evident reluctance to condemn a doctrine held by a powerful order. Do not make the theory speak to the politics; mark that as its silence.

Second, the case testing the theory. My hypothesis, which you should treat as a hypothesis and not as given, is that the commission could not adjudicate because the premise the whole theory rests on was never secured: that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have determinate truth values, true independently of God's will yet grounded in something. If that is the real sticking point, then the impasse is not a failure of papal nerve but an anomaly internal to the theory, and the Dominican alternative is best read as an attempt to run providence without ungrounded counterfactuals at all. Tell me whether that reading survives contact with the episode.

Third, bring it forward. The same structure seems to recur in the decision theory I spend too much time on: Newcomb's problem, and the disagreement between evidential and causal decision theorists over what the predictor's reliability tells you about the evidential status of your own choice. Is that the same disagreement in modern dress — a dispute about whether would-counterfactuals concerning free agents are the kind of thing that can be true before the fact — or is the resemblance superficial? And with all of that in view, does the episode count as a confirmation of Molinism, an anomaly it never anticipated, or evidence that the question it was invented to answer is ill-posed?
