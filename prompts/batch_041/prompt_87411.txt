Write a dialogue in British English between two physicists, Mira and Hugo, who are arguing about a demon.

They agree about every individual step of the physics. A box containing a single molecule is in contact with a heat reservoir at temperature T. A thin partition can be slid into the box, and if it is placed so that the molecule is confined to one half, the molecule can then push a piston and perform kT ln 2 of work as it expands isothermally back to the full volume. A device — the demon — can determine which half the molecule occupies and place the partition accordingly. Both accept that the measurement need not be dissipative: a reversible interaction can leave the demon's one-bit working register correlated with the molecule's position, with the register's new ln 2 of marginal entropy accounted for by the correlation rather than by any entropy generated in the gas. Both also accept that clearing a register to a standard blank state costs at least kT ln 2 of dissipation per bit when the clearing is done in ignorance of what the register held, whereas a register whose contents are already known can in principle be cleared for nothing.

What they are arguing about is a demon with a very large stock of blank registers. At the end of each cycle it does not clear its working register. Instead it swaps the bit into a fresh blank register — a logically reversible operation, in principle free — so that its working register is blank again and ready for the next cycle, while the used register is placed in a cupboard and never read. Run repeatedly, the arrangement appears to deliver kT ln 2 of work per cycle indefinitely, with the cupboard slowly filling with registers each of which holds a definite bit that nobody has recorded. Mira holds that the second law is in no danger and that the demon has merely moved the bill to a place where it will eventually have to be paid. Hugo holds that she is defending an accounting convention rather than a law of nature.

Write their exchange. Mira must say exactly where she thinks the bill falls — in the preparation of the blank registers, in the finitude of the stock, in the moment the cupboard is opened or its contents put to use, or in the eventual need for some agent to bring its description of the world up to date — and Hugo must press her on each of these in turn rather than accepting the first one she offers. Hugo must argue that a reversible operation can shift a correlation about but cannot destroy one, and must then face the question whether the correlations in the cupboard are thermodynamic entropy at all or merely a record of what nobody has bothered to look at; Mira must press him on what, on his account, the second law still forbids, given that on his view the demon fails for reasons that have nothing to do with heat flowing uphill. Hugo must also say whether his version of the demon could actually be built, and if it could not, what rules it out, without simply restating the principle in dispute. If either of them reaches for the thought that information is physical, or that the second law is a law, the other should demand an argument rather than let the assertion stand.

Have each of them state the other's position in its strongest form at least once, so that a reader can see that neither is a strawman, and have them answer each other's actual points rather than talking past one another. Neither should appeal to what any particular physicist has written, and there should be no citations. End with a single sentence, from either of them, stating as sharply as possible what they still disagree about — whether they differ over the physics, or over what the second law is a statement about: the behaviour of reversible dynamics and the impossibility of destroying a distinction, the epistemic position of agents with finite memories, or the cost of preparing low-entropy resources. Do not declare a winner.

Aim for roughly 1,500 words.
