Here's the question I actually put on the exam:

"Consider a long series of cases c1 ... c1000 in which a subject's skin temperature is lowered in steps so small that she cannot tell any two adjacent cases apart from the inside: in c1 she plainly feels warm, in c1000 she plainly feels cold, and no single step produces any noticeable change. Call a condition luminous just in case, whenever a person is in that condition, she is in a position to know that she is in it. Assume the Margin Condition (MC): if a person knows that P in the case she is in, then P also holds in every case she cannot distinguish from hers. (a) Using MC and the series, argue that the condition 'feels cold' is not luminous. (b) Does this show that the KK principle is false, where KK says that if you know that P, then you know that you know that P? Explain."

And here's what the student wrote:

"(a) The key move is to sit in the middle of the series, say c500. She can't know that she feels cold there, because MC says knowledge has to survive every case she can't tell apart from her own, and c499 is indistinguishable from c500 yet one step warmer, so she doesn't feel cold in it — that kills the knowledge claim by MC. Symmetrically she can't know that she does not feel cold, because c501 is indistinguishable from c500 and she does feel cold in c501. But at c500 one of these has to be the case, so here's a case where she feels cold (or doesn't) and isn't in a position to know it, which is just what it takes for 'feels cold' not to be luminous. Really this is a point about vagueness: down near the boundary there's no determinate fact about whether she feels cold, which is exactly why she can't know it. And the reasoning doesn't depend on temperature at all, so it'll run for any condition, so nothing is luminous. (b) Yes, this kills KK. KK needs that whenever she knows something, she knows that she knows it; but we just showed that at c500 she can't know that she feels cold, so she certainly can't know that she knows she feels cold — the second-order knowledge is missing right where the case is. And since no condition is luminous, KK fails for the same reason across the board: you can never be sure from the inside that you know. So KK is false."
