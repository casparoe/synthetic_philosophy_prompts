During a reading group on Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," one of the other participants has started closing down arguments with a single move: nothing is immune to revision, not the laws of logic, not arithmetic, not even our confidence in our own reasoning. It has now come up twice. In the first case I had objected that a proposed weakening of classical logic, offered to handle a self-referential paradox, looked less like a discovery about reasoning and more like a change of subject; the reply was that since even logical laws are revisable, my objection presupposed a distinction between changing our minds and changing what we mean that the essay had already discredited. In the second case, someone inferred from the same line that arithmetic is really just an empirical hypothesis with an unusually good track record, and that a sufficiently recalcitrant observation could in principle overturn it. Both times the slogan functioned as a conversation-stopper, and I could not articulate exactly what work it was doing.

What I want is a disambiguation rather than a summary of the essay. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that the one-liner could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false. I expect the interesting axes to include at least the following: whether the claim is descriptive, about what people in fact do, or normative, about what there is reason to do; whether it concerns the psychology of belief, the justification of belief, or the meanings of our words; whether it is a claim about individual statements or about whole theories and bodies of belief; and whether the revision in question includes changing what a word means, in which case the thesis threatens to become either trivially true or a claim about language change rather than about truth. I am also unsure whether the slogan is best read as denying that any truths are analytic, as denying that any are a priori, or as denying that any are necessary, and whether these three come apart.

Finally, tell me which of these readings would actually support the two uses I described — the reply to my change-of-subject objection, and the inference that arithmetic is empirical — and which readings the ordinary person who repeats the line probably has in mind. If some readings are defensible but do not license either use, say so plainly, and if a reading licenses one use but not the other, keep them separate. I want a menu I can bring to the next meeting, not a verdict on the essay.
