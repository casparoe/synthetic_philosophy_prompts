I'm in a grad seminar on the ethics of memory and I have to write a paper pairing the Margalit/Rieff fight with a piece of fiction -- Margalit being the one who thinks we owe memory to the people we're thickly connected too, our own, and Rieff being the one who thinks that a lot of commemorating historical wrongs does the living more harm than good and that forgetting can be the humane option. I want to do it on Borges's "Funes the Memorious," the story where the narrator meets Ireneo Funes in Fray Bentos, a kid who can tell you the exact time without a watch, then comes back a few years later and finds that after being thrown from a horse Funes is paralyzed and now perceives and remembers absolutely everything -- every leaf, every crack in every wall of the houses around him, a whole day of the past in a whole day of recall, so that he barely sleeps. Funes also invents a numbering system where every number gets its own arbitrary name, and the narrator's closing thought is basically that thinking requires forgetting differences and generalizing, which is exactly what Funes can't do. Funes dies young of lung congestion, and that's the whole story. So what I want is a real reading of the story against both thinkers, not a plot summary with a moral stapled to it. Does Funes make the case for forgetting better than Rieff does -- does the story actually demonstrate that memory without forgetting destroys thought, and maybe destroys the self along with it? And here's my worry: even if it does, does the argument transfer to the collective case, which is where the whole duty-to-remember fight actually lives? Funes is one guy in a dark patio in Uruguay; he has no nation, no grievance, no enemy, no descendants. I suspect the analogy breaks, but I want you to pin down where exactly, and to say so if it does rather than forcing the parallel. One more thing a classmate said that's been bugging me -- she claimed the story shows that generalizing requires losing information. That seems wrong to me, since you can keep information and simply not attend to it, so is Funes's problem that he remembers too much or that he can't ignore anything? If it's the second, that changes what the story is evidence for, and I'd like you to take a position on it. And please treat the story as literature too -- the frame narrator, the way Borges makes an impossible memory sound like a burden from the inside, the Latin books, the note -- not just as a fable about forgetting. My seminar is mostly historians who don't read much fiction, so I need the reading to be defensible to people who'll be skeptical of leaning on a short story for a philosophical point.
