I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I've fallen down a game theory / decision theory rabbit hole because of AI alignment writing, and there's a fight I keep seeing that I can't work out the shape of. I've read alot of these back-and-forths and nobody ever moves.

The setup, as I understand it. Two cars drive at each other, whoever swerves loses. The classic trick is to make swerving impossible — weld the wheel, or better, make it public that you've welded it. Now let both players do that. Each one wants to lock in whatever beats what the other locked in. And it never bottoms out: whatever you commit to, the other guy can commit to something that beats it, if he gets to go second and see yours. People call this a commitment race. Two camps:

Camp 1 says the race is an artifact of the model. If you don't model players as picking actions at the same time but as picking policies — each writes a program, the two programs get swapped, and each can read the other's source before it returns an action — you can find a stable point. The programs condition on each other, and cooperation pops out of even a Prisoner's Dilemma. On this view the race is just what you get from insisting on the wrong solution concept, and the fix is to model it properly.

Camp 2 says that's cheating. The stable point only exists because you stipulated who sees whose code and when, which is the answer smuggled into the rules. Make the commitment itself a move — when you commit, how hard, whether you can revise — and the race restarts one level up. On this view there's genuine indeterminacy: rationality doesn't pick out a unique action, and whoever commits first or hardest wins, which is a fact about timing and power, not about reason.

My hypothesis, and I'd rather you test it than agree with it: the camps are answering different questions. Camp 1 asks what a well-defined solution concept looks like, which is a modeling question. Camp 2 asks what an agent has reason to do when another agent is modeling it back, which is a normative one. Or maybe its more boring than that and it's a verbal fight about the word 'commitment' — whether a commitment is a fact about your action set or just information the other guy has about you. If that's the real split, then the whole race is about who knows what, and who knows what is a modeling choice.

So diagnose it for me. Substantive, verbal, or two different questions wearing one name? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work — some theorem about whether these stable points are unique, or a proof that no solution concept survives one more level of precommitment — and if there is, would it settle anything? Is the question just ill-posed? And what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a theorem, a different question entirely? Or is this one of those things where nothing will move it and I should stop refreshing the forum. Don't send me a reading list, I won't read it — I want the diagnosis and the reasons, engaging what each side actually says.
