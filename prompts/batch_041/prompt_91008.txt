I write science and ideas features for a mid-size magazine, and I've been stuck for two weeks on a piece about a fight over the last working farm inside the town limits — 60 acres, a family that's been on it since the forties, and a developer who wants 90 townhouses there. After four months of reporting I can honestly say neaither side is simply lying. The farm really does lose money most years. The developer's drainage plan really would wet the lower field where the family has buried three generations of its own. My editor wants a he said/she said piece with a headline that splits the difference, and my gut says thats the wrong shape for this story, though I couldn't tell you what the right shape is. (One thing I do know: the family's loudest claim, that the developer forged the survey, is just false. The survey is fine, they misread the setback line.)

Here's what I actually want to ask about. My uncle by marriage is Jain, a retired radiologist, and when I call him to vent he says things like, well, in one respect the farm is dying and in another respect it's thriving, and when I push back he says his tradition has a whole machine for this — that a statement about a thing is only true from a standpoint, and that there are seven ways to state any single one of them. I always treated it as him dodging the question. Lately it sounds less like a dodge and more like a discipline I don't have.

So, is there a real doctrine there, and what does it actually commit you to? Does it mean I have to write the forged-survey claim as though there's a respect in which it's true? That seems like a betrayal of the reporting, but I can't articulate why. And if it doesn't mean that, what exactly is the difference between a standpoint and a mistake? Also tell me what the doctrine can't do for me, because I still have to file 1,800 words by Friday and I definately can't file something that says everything is true in some sense.
