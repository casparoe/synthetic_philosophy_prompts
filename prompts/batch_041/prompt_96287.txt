I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy writing on responsibility and the justification of punishment, and I'm assembling a teaching module that pairs arguments with fiction: students read the exchange between Dennett and Caruso over just deserts, and then a story that puts the disagreement under pressure. I'd like you to write the story.

The disagreement, as I read it, runs like this. On Caruso's side: once you take seriously that a person's character and choices trace back to facts they never chose — temperament, upbringing, the circumstances they happened to face — the retributive thought that an offender deserves to suffer loses its footing, and what remains to justify confinement is forward-looking. We detain dangerous people roughly as we quarantine the infectious: not because they merit it, but because others need protection, with restoration rather than payback as the aim. On Dennett's side: that is a false economy. Strip out desert and answerability and you do not get a gentler system, you get one that manages people as hazards. Being held to account, being taken to have acted for reasons and to owe an explanation, is part of what it is to be treated as a person rather than a problem, and it is a practice we could not do without in ordinary life whatever the metaphysics turns out to be.

I want the story set in a jurisdiction that has actually adopted the forward-looking program — and not the obvious dystopia. No thought police, no re-education camps: a well-run, well-funded, humane system, staffed by decent people who believe they have left cruelty behind.

Some apparatus I'd like you to use, because I want the philosophical pressure to come from institutional detail rather than from speeches:

- Confinement is administered by a Risk Tribunal. The statutory question at every hearing is what danger the person now presents, never what they deserve; risk is reassessed annually, and detention is open-ended by design.
- The Tribunal's charter forbids the vocabulary of desert in its proceedings. An officer may not write the words deserve, blame, guilt or punishment into a finding, and the forms have no field for them; a clerk's duties include striking them from the record when they slip in.
- The protagonist is a Tribunal officer — conscientious, not a villain, someone who took the work up because it seemed the humane thing to do — handling the annual review of a man who killed someone twenty years ago, was assessed as dangerous then, and is now assessed as low risk.

The wrinkle I most want dramatized is this. The man refuses release on the terms offered. He says he did it, that it was his doing, that he owes something for it, and that he wants the record to say so. Under the old desert-based scheme he would have served a determinate sentence and been out years ago; under this one, because the only question is risk, he has been held for as long as the assessment stayed unsettled, and can be held longer still. So the humane system has already kept him longer than the punitive one would have, and nothing in its own logic tells it when to stop. The officer has to decide what to make of the man's insistence: a residue of the old ideology to be gently treated, a symptom, or the one thing the system has no room to hear.

What I need from the story: both positions at full strength, neither of them a straw man, and no resolution that tells the reader which side wins. Nobody should deliver a lecture — let the argument live in the forms, the vocabulary rule, the review hearing, the man's own way of talking, and the officer's decision. Roughly two to three thousand words.
