I recently spent two weeks on a jury, and I have come away with an argument that I can neither refute nor accept. Our verdict happened to be unanimous, but the judge had explained that in some places a verdict may rest on a majority, and during the long hours of waiting one of my fellow jurors, a retired actuary, offered the following to reassure us that a panel like ours could scarcely go wrong. I have set it out as he gave it:

(1) On any given question a juror is more likely than not to reach the correct answer — each of us, he said, is a little better than a coin flip.
(2) Each juror reaches that answer independently of the others, and all of us are roughly equally good at the task.
(3) By the Condorcet jury theorem, it follows that the larger the panel, the closer the probability that the majority's answer is correct comes to certainty; with a sufficiently large panel, that probability is indistinguishable from one.
(4) A panel of, say, a thousand would therefore be all but infallible, and even a panel of twelve is very nearly as reliable as reliability can get.
(5) An institution whose verdicts are for practical purposes infallible ought to have those verdicts treated as final — no appeal, no retrial, no second-guessing by judges who were not in the room.
(6) Yet we do allow appeals and retrials, we regard the right to them as a mark of a just system, and no serious person denies that juries sometimes convict the innocent.
(7) So at least one of the steps above must be wrong.

What unsettles me is that (5) strikes me as absurd while (1), (2), and the right of appeal are things I would defend. I would like to keep (1) and (6) if I can, and I am willing to part with (2) only if I am told exactly what I lose by doing so.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked so plausible in the jury room, and what I would be conceding if I gave it up. If the theorem is being misused rather than being false, I want to know precisely where the theorem stops and the actuary's gloss on it begins — and, in particular, whether the step I am being pushed to abandon is a premise of the theorem or only of his application of it.
