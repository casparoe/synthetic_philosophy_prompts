I'm refereeing a long-running arguement between two friends and I want to actually build something instead of just talking in circles. One of them insists there's a clean line between real history and pseudohistory, the other says the "line" is nothing but who happens to hold institutional power, and I keep flip-flopping between them. Can you take the role of a philosopher of science who works on the demarcation problem and build me a decision tree I can run actual cases through? Everyone should start at the same commitment — "yes, there's a genuine difference between doing history and doing pseudohistory" — and every branch node should be exactly one question about what that difference consists in. Please pull the nodes out of the space of possible answers rather than off a list of famous names: is it about falsifiability, about whether the central claims are even testable, about the evidence-and-argument norms a community actually enforces, about whether the story hangs together with independent lines of inquiry, about the sociology of who funds and repeats it, about the motives and honesty of the people pushing it, or about whether there's a protected core that never gets revised no matter what the evidence says? Each node has to be phrased so an ordinary person can answer it without already knowing which named view it drops them into — the whole point is to sort people by what they think, not by which philosopher they like the sound of.

The leaves should be named positions, and I'd like at least these to show up somewhere: the falsificationist one, a Lakatos-style view where there's a hard core shielded by a belt of auxiliary claims, the "no epistemic difference at all, it's purely social" view, and the pluralist "there's no sharp line, it's a matter of degree" view — plus one or two more you think earn a place, each leaf getting a single sentence on its most awkward commitment. Stress-test the whole thing on the three cases my friends keep fighting about: Atlantis, the ancient-aliens material, and Fomenko's new chronology (the one where large chunks of ancient history get relocated). Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and which combinations of answers are actually coherent — and only then drop the named positions into it, and tell me which combinations nobody occupies and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just never explored. If two positions can't be separated by any of your questions, say so plainly instead of papering over it. Start by brainstorming the candidate criteria in a list before you draw anything, keep it to about eight to ten nodes so it stays usable, and think carefully about the branches — I'd much rather have eight sharp ones than twelve mushy ones.
