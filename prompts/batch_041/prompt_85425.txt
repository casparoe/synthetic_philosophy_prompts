My insurance policy has a clause excluding liability for an "act of God" -- floods, hurricanes, the once-in-a-century storm -- and the courts treat those as freak interventions nobody could be expected to plan around. Yet the same company, and the same regulators, spend millions mapping floodplains from decades of river-gauge data and setting premiums on the assumption that the background of normal weather is stable and knowable. Those two practices live in the same building. What view of causation does the act-of-God idea presuppose -- that geography and climate are just the fixed stage, and the event is what does the real causal work? Does the practice actually need that view, or could you get the same doctrinal results from a picture where the slow structures are the real drivers and the storm is only the occasion on which they show up? And what happens to the "freak" category once everyone agrees the background itself is drifting -- is the old doctrine incoherent, or merely underdetermined about which of the two pictures its committed to? Somebody told me this is a Braudel thing and I didnt follow, so keep it concrete and tell me which rival views the practice quietly rules out.
