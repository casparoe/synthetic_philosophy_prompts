I teach a graduate seminar on Madhyamaka, and every year the same thing happens: by the third week my students have sorted every reading of Nāgārjuna's four-cornered negation into two bins — he is a nihilist who thinks nothing exists, or it is all mystical paradox and anything goes — and once they are in a bin they stop thinking. What I want is a handout that makes the actual options visible. Please write me a decision tree that takes a reader from commitments they can hold before they know any labels to the positions people really do occupy. Use the four-cornered scheme as the spine — a thesis, its negation, both, neither, each refused in turn — as it is run against whether things arise from themselves, from another, from both, or from nothing at all; against whether the Tathāgata exists after death, does not exist, both, or neither; and finally against emptiness itself, which the tradition insists must be relinquished like every other view. The branching nodes should be the questions on which the readings genuinely diverge, and I want at least these distinctions somewhere in the tree: what kind of act the refusal of a corner is (denying it, declining to assert it, or holding that it never had a truth value because what it presupposes fails); whether the four corners are meant to exhaust the options or only to exhaust the options available to someone who thinks things have essences; whether the fourth corner is the joint denial of the first two or the assertion of something over and above them, and whether calling the question ill-posed counts as a fifth corner or as a denial of the first two; whether emptiness describes how things are or dissolves the question that asked after it; and whether there is an ultimate truth that could be stated at all, and if not, whether that ineffability is itself a claim. Every node has to be answerable by a smart reader with no Sanskrit, and phrased so that answering it does not give away which reading it leads to.

Aim for eight to twelve leaves, each labeled with the name of the position (or "unnamed" where nobody clearly holds it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Three cases must appear as nodes or leaves. First, the Buddha's refusal to answer the questions about the Tathāgata after death, since a reader may well treat that silence as a distinct move rather than as a second corner. Second, a thesis that is not obviously about anything's essence — for instance, the claim that a particle has a definite position before it is measured — because a reader who takes the four corners to be a general logical instrument will sort differently there than a reader who takes them to be a targeted therapy for essence-talk. Third, the tree turned on itself, so that a reader who says the four-cornered method is a rhetorical performance for inducing silence rather than an argument ends up somewhere definite rather than falling off the chart. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can phrase, say so instead of inventing a distinction; where an answer combination is left empty, diagnose whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. No citations or reading list — where you use a school label, just say in one sentence what accepting it commits you to.
