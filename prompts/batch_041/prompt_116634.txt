Right, I need help with a speech and it's a slightly awkward one, so bear with me.

Two weeks ago our eldest, Erin, got her place at the grammar school. She sat the 11-plus in September, we paid for a tutor for most of last year, and she got in. Everyone's delighted. My mum's doing a lunch on Saturday, whole family, and I've been asked to say a few words before pudding. (We looked at the private school down the road as well — they offered her a bursary and we turned it down, which I'm now not sure was principle or just cheaper pride.)

The problem is that for about six years I've been the person in this family who goes on about selection. I signed the petition against the 11-plus, I wrote a letter to the local paper, I've said at more than one Christmas that paying for tutoring is basically the whole racket in miniature. And now we've done it. So the speech has to be honest, in front of: my mum, who thinks it's all lovely and doesn't want a lecture; my sister, who home-schools her two and thinks schools are the problem full stop; my sister's husband, who teaches at the comp Erin would otherwise have gone to and who I think is quite hurt about it; my partner, who's as torn as I am; and Erin, who's eleven and must not come out of it feeling like she's the problem.

I don't want it to be a confession and I don't want it to be a defence. I want a proper speech for the occasion that happens to be honest, and I want the honesty to come out of the structure of the thing rather than out of me being brave. Here's what I keep circling round, and I'd like the speech to actually run on this stuff rather than name-drop it:

- Whatever edge she's got is positional — it only counts as an edge because not everyone has it. If every family did what we did, the edge would mostly evaporate and we'd all just be poorer and more knackered. So 'it's what's best for her' isn't quite available to me, because 'best' here is partly relative to other people's kids.
- Half of what makes a school good is who's in it. So it isn't a thing sitting on a shelf that you pick between — it's partly made out of the choices of people like us, and the comp gets worse as people like us leave. Which means I can't say it's simply the better school.
- Each family doing the sensible thing adds up to a system nobody chose. There's a name for that I've half-remembered, something like the tyranny of small decisions, and the annoying thing is that it's true of us and it still doesn't tell me to do otherwise, because the only way not to be part of it is to make my own kid worse off, and I'm not going to do that.
- And nobody seriously thinks parents have to be neutral between their own kid and other people's. Which is maybe the one thing that makes the lunch bearable: the family is a machine for passing advantages down, everyone's always known it, and no one's ever squared it with fairness. So this isn't me being a hypocrite, it's a genuine collision between two things I actually believe, and I'd rather say that out loud than pretend one of them isn't there.

What I need from you: the speech, four minutes or so, so about 550–650 words. The constraints matter more than the length:

- No quotes, no names, no books, no studies, no 'the evidence shows'. Partly because it's a family lunch, and partly because I've only half-remembered all of the above and I dont want to be caught out by my brother-in-law.
- It can't rest on 'she earned it', or 'it's simply a better school', or 'anyone would do the same' — I want those three visibly shut off, and shut off for the reasons above, not just quietly avoided.
- It has to work for the room: warm enough for my mum, honest enough for my brother-in-law, not smug enough to wind up my sister, and it has to leave Erin feeling the day is about her and not about a policy dilemma.
- Don't resolve it and don't end on a verdict about selection. End somewhere warm, with her and with us, and let the unresolved bit stay seperate and unresolved.

Then a short note at the end, two or three lines: which of those four points you couldn't actually put into a speech without it turning into a seminar, and what you did with it instead.
