Ok, so I'm building a little exercise for my philosophy reading group and I need you to actually write the document, not commentary on it.

Here's the setup. Two people in the group have been going at it for about a year about self-deception. One of them — call her the intentionalist — thinks self-deception is the real deal and paradoxical as hell: a person ends up believing something they also, somewhere in them, know isn't true, or at minimum they set out to talk themselves into a belief they had good reason to think was false. The Sartre-ish move, the "I am deliberately not facing this" move. The other one — the deflationist — thinks that whole picture is incoherent and that everything real about self-deception dissolves once you look closely. On his view there's no contradictory belief and no moment of deceiving yourself, just motivated not-looking: someone keeps a comfortable belief alive by never letting the disconfirming stuff get close enough to touch, and we slap the loaded word "deception" on it.

They've been talking past each other for a year. I want the joint statement they should've produced instead. Write it for them.

A few things I want in it:

- A numbered list of agreed claims. Things both of them can sign without wincing. Not truisms — actual substantive stuff, about what the phenomenon is supposed to explain, what would count as an instance, what the words are doing.
- Then the disagreements, each one worded so that the side it belongs to would accept the wording of its own view. Neutral framing, no strawmen.
- Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where, if you settled one, at least one of them would have to move. For each crux, give the case or argument that both of them agree actually bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. Like a bet. If a crux has no such shared test, say so and say why.
- Also: for each of them, what they'd count as evidence or argument against their own position. And is there anything both agree would actually move the debate, or is it stuck?
- If some chunk of the fight is verbal — if they're really disagreeing about whether the word "deception" has to involve an intender, or about what counts as "a belief" — say which chunk and why.

Format: agreed claims as a numbered list, then a disagreements table with a column for the crux and a column for each side's prediction.

Now, I made up three cases and I want them worked through explicitly, because I dont want you leaning on anything you half-remember from the literature. No citations, no reading lists, no names — just the argument.

Case 1. Priya is a senior researcher who has decent evidence that a close collaborator fabricated data in a paper they co-authored years ago. She never reopens the paper. She quietly stopped going to the seminar where it would come up. If it's raised she says the whole thing was looked into and she seems to mean it. And meanwhile, without ever deciding to, she's redesigned her own experiments so that she'd never generate a result that would make the old paper look like an outlier. Is she self-deceived? If so, where exactly is the deception located — and if not, what's the difference between her and someone who is?

Case 2. Marco says out loud, calmly, to a friend: I know I'm lying to myself about my marriage, and honestly it's the only thing holding it together. He's aware, he says it's on purpose, he keeps doing it. On the face of it there's no deception here at all — he knows. So what is he doing, and does it belong in the same category as Priya?

Case 3. Sam genuinely believes his business is fine for about an hour in the morning, and spends the rest of the day quietly certain it's collapsing. He doesn't notice the flip. Which hour is the "real" belief, and does the answer settle whether he's self-deceived or just ambivalent?

One more thing to fold in, and I think this is where it gets interesting. Take what I'll call the lucidity test: you catch the person in a calm, low-stakes, no-pressure moment, you walk them through the evidence slowly, and they immediately concede the uncomfortable truth. Does that show they never really believed the comforting thing in the first place? Or only that the belief was weak or compartmentalized? Make both of them say what they think this test shows, and whether it's even a test of the right thing.

Write it in a voice that sounds like two careful people who respect each other and are tired of the fight, not like a textbook. If you conclude that part of their dispute is just about words, fine — but then be specific about which words and what turns on them.
