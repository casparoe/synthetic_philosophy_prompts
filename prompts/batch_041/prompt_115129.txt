A colleague has been defending an argument that I suspect proves far more than he intends, and I can't find the exit. He defines a subject of experience as a system whose states are mutually accessible: for any two of its states, information carried by one is available to the processes that produce the other. He then argues:

(1) A subject of experience just is a system whose states are mutually accessible in this sense.
(2) In an idealized split-brain case, where the hemispheres of a commissurotomy patient exchange no information at all, no system has access both to what the left hemisphere registers and to what the right hemisphere registers.
(3) So the patient contains two subjects of experience, each with its own perceptual and motor life.
(4) But the same test fails for an ordinary person as well: the states that guide a blindsight patient's above-chance guesses, or that drive an implicit bias, are not available to the processes that produce deliberate report, and those reporting processes are not available to them in turn.
(5) So an ordinary person is not a single system whose states are mutually accessible either.
(6) The test therefore partitions an ordinary person into as many subjects as there are maximal sets of mutually accessible states, a number that shifts with how finely we individuate subsystems and that grows every time psychology discovers a new dissociation.
(7) A criterion for individuating subjects that implies each of us is an indeterminate crowd of subjects is worthless.
(8) So (1) is false. But if (1) is false, what entitles him to (3), that the split-brain patient has two subjects rather than one divided subject, or none at all?

I would like to keep (3). The split-brain findings seem to show something real, and I do not want to end up saying the patient is simply one person who is confused about himself. Tell me which step fails, whether the trouble is an invalid inference or a premise I should abandon, and what abandoning it costs me for the split-brain case specifically.
