I have recently begun advising a small conservation nonprofit on a habitat intervention, and I have had to make my uncertainty explicit in a way I never did as a student. Instead of assigning a single probability to outcomes, I have been carrying a low and a high estimate, and treating any value in between as admissible. That part is manageable. What I did not expect is a particular kind of change in those ranges. I will have a range for whether the intervention reduces erosion. Then I learn something about an unrelated variable—say, the results of a survey on local rainfall patterns—something that, under every one of my candidate models, is supposed to be independent of the erosion outcome. My range for erosion does not narrow. It widens. A decision that was robust across the old range—one that was worth taking no matter where in the interval the true value fell—can become fragile, even though the new fact was, by my own assumptions, irrelevant to erosion. It feels like learning about the rainfall survey has made my ignorance about erosion more visible rather than less.

Is there an established name for this? I have heard of ambiguity aversion, and of second-order probabilities, but the first seems to be about attitudes toward imprecision and the second still seems to collapse everything into a single higher-order distribution. What I am describing is a structural effect: the independence I assumed seems to be a property of each single model, not of the range as a whole. If there is a standard term, I want it and, more importantly, an account of how it differs from the nearest concepts. If there is no standard term, please coin one and define it, including what it excludes. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want the phenomenon carved at the joints.

Please structure your answer as follows: (1) The pattern, restated (2–3 sentences), so I can see whether you have identified the right thing. (2) Nearest established concepts (about 200 words), taking each candidate in turn and saying what it captures and where it falls short. (3) Verdict on the name (one paragraph), giving the best established term or a new one, with a definition. (4) Boundary cases and a diagnostic test (one paragraph), including one case that looks similar but is not an instance.
