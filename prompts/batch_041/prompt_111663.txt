I'm a retired organic chemist (35 years on the bench, mostly catalysts) and since I hung up the lab coat I've been teaching myself philosphy of science, badly, mostly out of a dog-eared copy of Lakatos. Mentioning this to my old colleagues was a mistake, because now I'm the designated referee.

Here's the fight. At our old group's reunion dinner, two former colleagues went at each other about whether the department should keep funding the group working on what we always called the two-site model — a thirty-year-old idea about why one family of catalysts is so picky about which product it makes. I'm on the committee that votes in two weeks, and they've both asked me to adjudicate.

Margit: the programme is degenerating. Every time an experiment comes out wrong, somebody bolts a new twist onto the model — a third site, a slow step, a "gatekeeper" — and none of those twists ever predicted anything that wasn't already sitting in the drawer. That's patching, not predicting. Defund it and move the students somewhere useful.

Ray: you can't call a programme degenerating from the inside. Lakatos says the verdict is retrospective; people wrote off whole lines for decades and then they came back. And the twists aren't ad hoc, they add content — the third-site patch made us go look for a new peak and we found it. The core of the model hasn't been touched in thirty years, and that's exactly what you'd expect: you protect the core and let the belt take the hits.

Margit: then your standard never tells us to stop. If every failure is just "the belt adjusting," if the verdict only arrives later, and if the only thing that actually kills a programme is a better rival turning up — and there isn't one, the competing models are worse — then the answer is always "carry on." That's not a criterion, it's a permission slip. On that logic we'd have funded cold fusion for another twenty years.

Ray: it's not a permission slip, because protecting the core and patching the belt with something that predicts nothing are different things. If you want a number: of the last twenty additions to the model, count how many were made before the experiment that tested them and how many were made after we already knew the answer. If it's mostly after, I'll vote to kill it too.

Margit: fine, but "before" and "after" is doing all the work there. The student who found the anomaly went looking for it because of the model, so nothing about it was ever really a surprise.

Neither would budge and it got a bit personal around the cheese course. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right where, what each of them is missing, and then give me the call I should actually vote for — I don't need a reading list, I need a verdict.
