I keep going back and forth on how much heterophenomenology can actually carry. Give me your credence that a mature science of consciousness could be built on heterophenomenological methods alone -- where "alone" means: take subjects' verbal reports as the primary data, treat those reports as reports about what the subject thinks or experiences, bracket the question of whether the subject is actually conscious in the phenomenal sense, and never need to add any other kind of data to explain everything we'd want a science of consciousness to explain.

Decompose it. My guess is the terms look something like: (a) whether the reports really are neutral between a subject having experiences and merely being disposed to emit the reports, and whether that neutrality is a feature or a bug; (b) whether the method has to presuppose the intentional stance in a way that stops it from ever testing whether that stance is the right one; (c) whether the hard problem is a further fact over and above what the reports plus a theory of report-production would give you, or just the feeling that there must be one; (d) whether the residual first-person stuff ("what it's like") can be recoved as a theoretical posit from the reports rather than assumed. A number and one line of justification for each, then the combination, and tell me which term you're least sure of and what would move it.

I'm at about 0.6 on this and a friend who works on it is at 0.15 -- where do you think we actually disagree? And if the question is malformed (say "alone" isn't well-defined, or the hard problem isn't a single thing you can assign a probability to), just tell me that instead of humoring me.
