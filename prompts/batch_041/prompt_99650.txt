I am leading a single session of a graduate reading group in philosophy. My readers have no biology beyond school, but all of them have absorbed the popular gene-centred framing of evolution and assume that the units-of-selection debate is a contest in which one must decide whether selection really acts on genes, on individuals, or on groups. I want a plan for teaching them otherwise — not an explanation of the debate itself. Set out the order in which the ideas have to come, and say what depends on what: I take it that some account of heritable variation in fitness-relevant traits, and of selection being partitioned into a within-group and a between-group component, must precede any talk of units, and that the notion of a unit of selection has to be prised apart from that of a replicator. Nominate the one concrete case that should carry the session; say what it must contain (opposing selection at two levels, so that the gene-centred and multilevel framings can be seen as reformulations of one another rather than rivals) and what it must avoid. Set out the misconceptions I should expect — that group selection was refuted and is therefore unscientific, that it amounts to selection for the good of the species, that the gene-centred view denies the reality of organisms, that kin selection and group selection are competing theories — and say at exactly which point in the sequence each should be headed off. Finally, give me one question to put at the end that would reveal whether they have understood the debate rather than merely learned its slogans, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level. Please write your answer in British English.
