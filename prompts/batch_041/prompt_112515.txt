I need help with a referee report. The submission is Thucydides' account of the Peloponnesian War -- the opening book, the archaeology and the bit where he explains his method -- reviewed as if it were an article. I'm the referee and I've been sitting on it for definately too long, so I want a report I can send to the editor, around 600 words, continuous prose only, no headings, no numbered points, no bold, just the way you'd actually write an email to an editor. Deadpan throughout: no winking at the reader about the author being dead for two and a half thousand years, no jokes about togas or triremes.

The humour should come from the actual gap rather than from costume. He explains how he worked once, in a couple of sentences, and then never again; he almost never says who told him what; he admits he couldn't remember the speeches and rebuilt them on the principle that each speaker should have said what the occasion called for; and his main engine of argument in the early chapters is what is likely to have happened given human nature, which is also the prosecutor's move. All of that gives the referee real grounds to complain. But I want the referee to be a bit of a fool in his own way too -- someone who keeps demanding things that are really just his journal's house style dressed up as eternal standards. So have him ask for a source table with a confidence rating attached to each claim, ask whether the speeches were verbatim and, if not, why they weren't flagged as reconstruction, ask for a clear statement of the criteria used to choose between competing accounts, and complain that the author's refusal to identify informants makes the whole thing unreproducible. Somewhere he should compare it unfavourably with a rival submission on the Persian wars that names its sources constantly and is much more enjoyable to read.

Two things to keep straight. Don't quote the manuscript -- the referee is summarising for the editor, so everything gets paraphrased, and nothing goes in quotation marks as if it were the author's words. And let him concede, grudgingly, one or two things the author gets right, so it reads like a referee genuinely torn between reject and major revisions rather than a hit job. What I want the reader left with is the uncomfortable question of whether we're judging the man or just judging ourselves.
