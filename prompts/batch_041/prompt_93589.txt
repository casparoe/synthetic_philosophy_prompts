I'm running a 90-minute session for a mixed group -- half econ grad students, half philosophy grad students -- on weakness of will vs. hyperbolic discounting, and I need you to plan how I teach it, not teach it yourself. What I care about most is that they leave able to tell the two apart, because right now they (and honestly, alot of the literature) use "akrasia" for anything where someone doesn't do what they said they'd do.

So: what has to be in place before the distinction can even be stated -- the judgment/action gap first, or time-indexed preference first, or what -- and in what order, and why that order and not the reverse.

For the carrying example I want to use a pair of cases I've been kicking around: (1) someone who's ranked finishing their thesis above everything else for years, sincerely and without wavering, and yet in the moment keeps not doing it; (2) someone whose ranking itself flips as the deadline gets close, so that at every moment they're acting on what they currently prefer -- no gap at the time, just a different person tomorrow. Then the alarm-clock case as the trap, since it can be read either way and I want them to feel that.

Misconceptions I'm expecting: that akrasia just is procrastination; that hyperbolic discounting is a fancy word for impatience; that time-inconsistent preferences mean the agent is irrational; and something vague about willpower being a muscle that runs out. Which of those get formed earlier in the sequence, and exactly where do I head each one off?

I also want the naive/sophisticated split in there -- the person who sees their own future reversal coming and plans around it versus the one who doesnt -- as the payoff. Where does it go, and why not earlier? And tell me what to leave out at this level and to actually say so out loud. Last thing: one question to ask at the end that shows whether they got the distinction, not whether they can repeat it.
