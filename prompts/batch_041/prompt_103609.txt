I do risk and security for a mid-sized company (around 800 staff, we hold a lot of customer data) and I've somehow become the person who writes our internal guidance for extortion demands — ransomware, the "we have your files and we'll leak them" emails, that sort of thing. We get maybe eight or ten a year that are serious enough to reach the exec committee, and I usually have about 48 hours to hand them a recommendation before somebody panics and wires the money. The line everyone repeats is never pay, because paying funds the next attack — fine, I get it, that's a deterrence argument. But some of these demands don't feel like that. Last month a group had already copied everything, published a sample to prove it, and set up an automated timer that dumps the rest if we don't pay; refusing wouldn't cost them anything and paying wouldn't stop them doing it to someone else next week. It put me in mind of the decision theory stuff about XOR blackmail and counterfactual mugging, where the usual reason to refuse (that it rewards and encourages the threatener) just doesn't apply, because the threat isn't contingent on your choice and the other side isn't a repeat player you can influence.

So what I want is a proceedure my team can actually run, built honestly on that litrature rather than on vibes: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a decisive answer to each, where the deterrence argument still bites and where it goes empty, and where it hands the call to a human because it's values and not facts (how much is the reputation hit worth, do we owe anything special to the customers whose data it actually is). Then run it on three cases: a plain ransomware demand with a working decryptor; the automated-leak one above; and a nasty one where the attacker says they'll only leak if we don't pay, but they've clearly spent months on us specifically and we're fairly sure they'll be back either way. I have a hunch the procedure tells me to pay on case two and I'm not going to enjoy defending that to the board, so say so plainly if it does. And please be straight with me about where the analogy to counterfactual mugging breaks — real attackers aren't Omega, I can't condition on a coin flip in a branch that didn't happen, and I don't want my analysts doing "acausal" reasoning in a meeting with the CFO. Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do.
