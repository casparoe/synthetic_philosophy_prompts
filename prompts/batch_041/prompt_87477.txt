You are a philosopher of biology and of mind who has taught the Darwin and Turing material for many years. I am writing the essay section of the final examination for an upper-level seminar on competence without comprehension, and I need one question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. Before drafting, think through where the real difficulty in this material lies, and consider a few candidate cruxes before settling on one.

The question must be answerable from my lecture material and the students' own reasoning rather than from anything they have read, and it must not be answerable by reciting the familiar pairing of the two inversions. So I am building it around a framework I will present in lecture, which is mine and appears in no text: any claim that a competence exists without comprehension has three separable components — a product claim (the competence is really present, as measured by performance), a provenance claim (the process that produced it contains no representation of the standard the competence meets), and a warrant claim (the performance is rightly assessed as correct or incorrect, and not merely as fitting or failing to fit). I also tell the students that the two standard inversions do not line up on all three components, and that the interesting cases are the ones where the first two hold and the third is contested.

The question should turn on a case we never discussed: a laboratory that improves an enzyme by iterating random mutation and screening, with a machine scoring every variant against an explicit objective function and propagating only the winners. The trap I want to catch is the student who calls this a Darwinian inversion because nobody in the laboratory understands the chemistry; a good answer has to say what, if anything, is doing the comprehending, and where. The question should have a second part asking what changes if the objective function is only a proxy for what the laboratory actually wants, and where the missing comprehension then sits.

Deliver the exam question, phrased so that a strong student sees what is being asked but not what to answer; a model answer of about 600 words; and a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, built from four or five must-hit points, each with its value and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates that point. Make the A/B boundary turn on whether the student sees what is doing the work in the laboratory case. Assume a 45-minute answer; no citations or reading are expected.
