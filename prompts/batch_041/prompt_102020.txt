I teach a philosophy of biology seminar and next month I'm doing the species problem — the whole tangle about whether "species" picks out something real in nature or is just a handy sorting device we project onto a messy continuum. Every year I front-load it with the menu of species concepts (biological, morphological, phylogenetic, ecological...) and every year the students dutifully memorize the menu, pick a favourite, and never once feel why anyone would loose sleep over it. So I want to open the unit differently: with a short story they can read in twenty minutes and then argue about for the rest of the seminar. Can you write me one?

Here's the kind of thing I'm after. A young field biologist is a season into cataloguing a population of some unglamorous organism — pick whatever, a lizard, a salamander, a little brown moth — at a remote station, and she's just discovered that her population interbreeds freely with the next population along the coast, which interbreeds with the one after that, all the way round; but where the two ends of the chain meet, on the far side, they overlap and don't interbreed at all. So depending on which definition you grab, the whole loop is one species, or it's a dozen, or it's an arbitrary slice of a continuum that only looks like discrete kinds because we're not standing close enough. Now add pressure: the museum needs a name for the collection, a conservation body is waiting to hear whether this is a distinct taxon worth protecting, and her supervisor and a rival researcher want opposite answers for reasons that are not purely scientific. I want the story to keep all the answers live at once, and I'd love one small scene where somebody points out that the interbreeding criterion everyone keeps invoking is completely empty for the bacteria in the lab down the hall, since they don't interbreed at all. And if you can, let the story make the students feel the strange idea that a species might be less like a class defined by shared traits and more like a particular historical individual — a lineage with a birth, a life, and maybe an end — rather than announcing that idea.

Make the biology load-bearing without lecturing: no narrator stopping to explain what a species concept is. I want them to finish unable to say which answer is right, but able to say exactly why each answer has a grip. And stick two or three sentences at the very end, outside the story, on which way the story is quietly leaning, if it is, so I can flag my own thumb on the scale in class.
