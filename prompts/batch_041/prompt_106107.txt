I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last four years auditing philosophy seminars, and I have rashly agreed to lead a three-session reading group at my old institute on logical uncertainty and the framework often called logical induction. The audience is scientifically literate but not logician: two engineers, an economist, a classicist, and a philosopher of science who works on confirmation. I do not want an exposition of the subject; I want a teaching plan for how to introduce it. Please take the role of an expert in mathematical logic, computability, and formal epistemology who has taught this material to non-specialists. Before you write, think carefully and brainstorm the dependency structure and the likely misconception map; then give me the plan. Begin your reply with a single sentence stating the spine of the plan—the one dependency that dictates the order of everything else—and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.

Here is the conceptual territory I need the plan to cover. The group will arrive knowing ordinary probability and having heard that Bayesian agents are supposed to be logically omniscient, which is absurd: a real agent cannot assign probability 1 to every logical consequence of what it already believes, because it cannot compute all the consequences. Logical induction is supposed to be a computable procedure that assigns probabilities to sentences—say, sentences of arithmetic or claims about what a program will output—without pretending to logical omniscience. It does so by imagining a market in which traders buy and sell shares in sentences; a trader who can make a guaranteed profit with a feasible computational budget is an exploit, and a good probability assignment is one that no such trader can exploit. The framework is also supposed to handle sentences that refer to the agent's own probabilities, and to learn empirical and mathematical patterns over time. The group will not know this machinery, so the plan must build it from what they do know. I am especially interested in the order of ideas: what has to be in place before the market metaphor can do any work, what has to come before the self-reference puzzle—ideally culminating in a diagonal sentence that refers to the agent's own probability assignment—and what has to come before the idea that a logical inductor can be uncertain about a theorem it will later prove. The carrying example should be something the group can work through by hand—ideally a small program, a finite set of sentences, or a toy market—and it should make the computational budget and the possibility of self-reference visible. Tell me what that example must have and what it must lack, and why the obvious alternatives—an agent betting on the digits of pi, a theorem prover with probabilities bolted on, the surprise exam paradox—would or would not carry the sequence. I expect misconceptions to cluster around the idea that logical uncertainty is just ordinary uncertainty about what follows, that coherence requires assigning probability 1 to every tautology one can name, that the market metaphor is a mere heuristic rather than the definition, and that self-reference is a sideshow rather than the central test. Tell me where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off, and give me one question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether they have understood the difference between logical uncertainty and empirical uncertainty. Finally, tell me what I should explicitly refuse to explain at this level—and how to refuse it without making the group feel cheated. Do not give me a bibliography, citations, or a reading list; the group has no web access and I want the plan itself.
