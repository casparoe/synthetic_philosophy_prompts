I'm applying to a small interdisciplinary workshop — philosophy of science plus foundations of ML, maybe fifteen people, one weekend — and I need the proposal, around a page and a half to two pages, for the talk I'd give there. I've got a seed of an idea but I want you to build it into something that actually stands up under scrutiny.

The seed: people in the ML-adjacent philosophy of science world keep citing the no-free-lunch theorems as if they'd settled something about the problem of induction. The move goes something like: no learning algorithm beats any other once you average over all possible problems, therefore your inductive method has no a priori justification, case closed. I think thats sliding between two claims with different quantifier orders. Hume's worry is about whether I'm justified in expecting the future to resemble the past in *this* world; the no-free-lunch result is a claim about an average over a whole class of worlds under some measure — uniform, or whatever the setup specifies. Different, seperate objects, different senses of "justified." So does no-free-lunch actually touch Hume, or is the appeal to it a category error wearing a theorem as a costume?

Here's the wrinkle I want the proposal built around. There's a measure-free cousin of the result: for every learner there is some world on which it does no better than chance. I think that version is trivially true and philosophically inert, because the hostile world can always be tailor-made to the learner's outputs — you just construct the world that makes it wrong. If that's right, then the real question isn't whether no-free-lunch is true, it's what you have to assume for some version of it to be non-trivial, and whether those assumptions are exactly the ones Hume says you can't have. If the non-trivial version needs a prior over hypotheses, then the theorem presupposes an answer to Hume rather than delivering one, and I want the talk to say that out loud. But I'm not certain that's the only option — there might be a middle version, measure-free but still substantive, that I haven't come across, and whether that middle version can exist is kind of the whole point.
