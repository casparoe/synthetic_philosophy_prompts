Before I read any more defences or criticisms of significance testing, I want the specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of statistical evidence in the empirical sciences must meet: what it has to deliver, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, and which results it cannot contradict. Treat it as a theory of evidence for a scientific claim rather than a recipe for decision, and make it answerable to the following: optional stopping and stopping rules; analyses chosen after the data have been seen; the large-sample case in which a fixed-threshold test rejects a point null that a Bayesian analysis with a diffuse prior leaves at high posterior probability; the difference between absence of evidence and evidence of absence; a single small study that contradicts a large, well-powered one; the difference that pre-registration does or does not make to the evidential import of the same data; and the fact that replication rates differ across fields. It should also be usable by someone who sees only the published summary and will never re-run the analysis. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and say whether it is non-negotiable or the prejudice of a particular camp; flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied — above all, whether the demand for error control under optional stopping can be reconciled with the demand that the evidence depend only on the observed data and the model; and say which single requirement excludes the most of the standard positions. Ultimately I want a list that would tell me whether the replication crisis is a crisis in our statistics or in our institutions.
