I run a small monthly thing for people who like ideas — its basically a thinking club, about 14 of us, mix of philosophers, a couple of graphic designers, one very patient linguist. Sessions are 90 minutes and I need one for next month. I've got hold of this lovely old idea I want them to run into with their hands rather than hear about, and I'd like you to design the activity for me. Write it all up in British English please (organise, colour, that sort of thing).

The idea is the old Hofstadter and Gary McGraw project called Letter Spirit — I think it's a chapter in Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies. As I remember it the aim was: you give the program a few letters that have been drawn in one particular style (they were drawn on a grid of dots, so everything's angular), and it has to design the remaining letters of the alphabet in that same style. What fascinates me is that it makes you ask two questions at once — what is it that makes a shape an 'a' at all, when a double-storey 'a' and a single-storey 'a' barely share a feature — and what is a style, when no two letters in a typeface are ever the same shape but they somehow all "match". I want my group to feel both pressures, especially the way they pull against each other: the more you stylise a letter the harder it is to still read it, and the safer you play the more the style evaporates.

So, an activity, not a lecture. Rules, materials, timings, roles, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the concepts. Things to build in:

- A first phase where they sort a sheet of about twenty printed 'a's (I can paste these together in a word processor — single-storey, double-storey, italic, blackletter, whatever) into definitely-an-'a', borderline, and not-an-'a', and then have to write a one-line rule for what an 'a' must have. Then give them two or three awkward shapes that break their rule — one their rule wrongly excludes, one it wrongly admits. The rule should fail in both directions and they should see it fail.
- A style-extension game. Each group secretly gets three letters drawn in a made-up grid style (you'll need to specify how I make these seeds, and what makes a good seed set), and has to design a few more letters of the alphabet in that same style on grids. Then groups swap and judge each other's letters blind: first they have to say which letter each shape is meant to be, then whether it belongs to the same style. Score readability and style-coherence separately and give them equal weight, so the two objectives actually compete.
- A short round where the whole room takes a letter everyone agreed was unambiguously an 'a', degrades it in five small steps passed along the room, and then has to say where it stopped being an 'a'. Nobody will be able to point at the step, and the disagreement between them is the point.
- A debrief that covers family resemblance versus prototypes versus the idea that letter identity is really about roles (something playing the part of a bowl, a stem, sitting on the baseline) rather than about geometry, plus the two independent dimensions of letter-identity and style, plus what Letter Spirit was actually claiming — that the hard part is perceiving the letter fluidly, not drawing it.

Also: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and how I should handle it, and an honest paragraph on which parts of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. No reading lists or citations needed, they'd just make people feel they should have done homework.
