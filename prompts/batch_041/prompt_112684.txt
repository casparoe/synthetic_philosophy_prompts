I'm the instructor of an upper-level undergraduate course on consciousness and ethics, and I'm grading a take-home midterm. Below is the exam question and one student's answer. Make the comments specific enough to hand back to the student — quote or paraphrase the sentences you are commenting on, rather than offering a general impression. For each criticism, say how confident you are that it is a genuine error rather than a defensible reading, and close by singling out the one judgment you are least sure of.

Exam question: In the second half of the course we considered the suggestion that if panpsychism is true, then the fundamental entities described by physics are themselves loci of experience, and physics therefore has direct ethical stakes — in particular, that there may be morally considerable suffering at the level of fundamental physics. (a) State the panpsychist thesis, distinguish it from panprotopsychism, and locate both with respect to Russellian monism. (b) Set out the "ethical physics" argument in premise-conclusion form, making explicit every premise the conclusion requires. (c) Assess the strongest objection to the argument, and say what would remain of the ethical conclusion if that objection succeeded.

Student's answer: (a) Panpsychism holds that experience, or mentality, is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the world rather than something that emerges from non-mental matter. It is thus a rival to physicalism, which treats experience as derivative, and to emergentism, which treats it as a late-arriving product of complex organization. What distinguishes it from both is that it is constitutive rather than emergent: the experiences of complex subjects like us are built out of the experiences of the entities that compose us, rather than produced by them as a novel kind of property. Panprotopsychism, also called Russellian monism, agrees that the intrinsic nature of physical properties is not exhausted by their structural and relational character, but holds that the intrinsic properties in question are proto-mental: not experiences at all, but the ingredients out of which experiences can be constituted. In practice, though, this difference is close to verbal, since a proto-experience sufficient for constituting experience is just an experience too faint to notice introspectively.

(b) The argument runs:
P1. Fundamental physical entities have experiences. [panpsychism]
P2. Every experience has a hedonic character — pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral. [follows from panpsychism, since if experience is ubiquitous then valence, which is inseparable from experience, is ubiquitous too]
P3. Unpleasant experiences are intrinsically bad for the subject who has them.
P4. Whatever has experiences that are bad for it is a proper object of moral concern.
C. So fundamental physical entities are proper objects of moral concern, and physics has direct ethical stakes.
The argument is valid, and P3 and P4 say about fundamental entities exactly what we already say about animals, so I don't think the trouble lies in the ethical premises.

(c) The strongest objection is the combination problem. Physicalism has to explain how experience arises out of non-experiential matter, and panpsychism's selling point is supposed to be that it avoids exactly this task. But the combination problem is precisely the problem of how experience arises out of non-experiential components: how do billions of tiny micro-experiences add up to a single unified subject of experience rather than a mere aggregate? If the objection succeeds, panpsychism has not avoided the explanatory gap at all; micro-experiences do not exist as genuine experiences, and there is no suffering at the level of physics, so the ethical conclusion collapses along with the metaphysics. The only remaining option for the panpsychist would be to deny that physics is complete and locate experience at some more fundamental level, which looks more like cosmopsychism than panpsychism.
