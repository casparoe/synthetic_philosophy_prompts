Last weekend I lost most of a Saturday to an argument with a close friend, a psychiatric nurse practitioner, that began with the recent addition of prolonged grief to the diagnostic manual and ended with her insisting that psychiatric classification does not discover kinds of people so much as manufacture them, and that this is why nobody can tell her what depression really is. I pushed back, and we both kept reaching for the same handful of examples: the walking fugues that French physicians catalogued in the 1890s and that no longer occur; the epidemic of dissociative identity disorder diagnoses in the 1980s and their steep decline since; the quiet removal of hysteria from the manual and its apparent replacement by labels that carry less stigma; the adult who reads a list of autism traits online and comes away diagnosed. What struck me afterwards was that we were not really disagreeing about the examples, we were disagreeing about what makes a diagnostic category hold together at all, and about whether a category's effect on the people it classifies is a defect in the category or part of its nature. She also floated a proposal I cannot get out of my head: if the classification itself causes distress in some of the people it captures, why not add a category for that harm and let the loop feed back into the manual? I could not say whether that was a serious extension of her view or a reductio of it.

I am due to run a session for a mixed group of philosophy graduate students and psychiatric trainees, and I want to hand them a decision tree rather than a reading list. Please build one. The entry point should be a claim I expect everyone in the room to accept, namely that these categories are made by committees, revised, and sometimes abandoned, and that none of them was simply read off the nature of the mind. From there, every branching question should be concrete and answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the names for the positions: each node should turn on a case or a claim on which the positions genuinely come apart, and no node should be the tired biology-versus-culture fork, since that fork is precisely what my friend thinks stands in need of explanation. The cases above must appear at nodes, along with at least one case involving a category that people now adopt for themselves and at least one involving a category that has quietly gone out of existence. Leaves should carry the name of the position wherever a position has a name, and be marked as unnamed where nobody defends it, and each leaf should carry one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. I want the positions located in the space rather than merely listed: work out first which combinations of answers the questions allow, then say who occupies them, and where a combination is empty, diagnose whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. The tree must separate at least these commitments: that the category picks out a causal structure independent of our classifying it; that it picks out no such structure but is nonetheless a genuine causal structure in its own right, sustained by feedback among the people classified; that it is nothing more than a useful instrument for coordinating treatment; that it is a vehicle of social control whose persistence demands a political rather than an epistemic explanation; and that the whole question of its reality is ill-formed, because what a diagnosis really is, is a promise about how to treat someone. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say why.

Two things should be built into the instrument itself. First, since my friend's view implies that the act of classifying people changes them, include a question asking which leaves predict that circulating the tree would shift how respondents answer on a second pass, and what that would count as evidence for. Second, her proposed category for diagnosis-induced harm should be one of the nodes, so that the group has to decide whether accepting the looping story makes such a category a natural extension or an embarrassment. Keep it to no more than seven branching questions and nine leaves, and write it so that a respondent is sorted by what she says about the cases rather than by which label she is willing to apply to herself.
