I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have reconstructed the following argument from something I read in the philosophy of physics. I have written it out as a numbered chain because I cannot locate the step I should reject, and because it appears to establish far more than the context suggested it was meant to. I would like to know which step to give up, why that step looked compelling in the first place, and what giving it up costs.

The argument relies on two background claims, which I state in my own words:

(A) A theory is deterministic when its equations of motion, together with a complete description of the world on an initial surface, fix the description everywhere else.

(B) General relativity is generally covariant: its field equations take the same form in every coordinate system. I have also read that general covariance is not peculiar to general relativity, since the equations of almost any theory can be rewritten so as to take the same form in every coordinate system.

The argument:

(1) If a configuration of fields — the metric together with whatever matter is present — solves the equations, then so does the configuration obtained by smoothly relabeling the spacetime points and carrying the fields along with them. Any smooth one-to-one relabeling of the points is thus a symmetry of the theory.

(2) Choose a region of spacetime, and consider a relabeling that leaves every point outside that region exactly where it was while moving the points inside it. Picture stirring the points within the region while pinning down the rest of spacetime.

(3) By (1), the stirred configuration is also a solution.

(4) The two configurations agree everywhere outside the region, including on any initial surface that lies outside it — for instance, one lying entirely to its past. The two configurations therefore have exactly the same initial data.

(5) They disagree inside the region: at a given point inside it, the metric takes one value in the first configuration and a different value in the second.

(6) If spacetime points are real individuals — if there is a fact of the matter about which point is which, independently of the fields sitting on them — then (4) and (5) describe two genuinely different physical situations that nevertheless share all their initial data.

(7) Hence general relativity is not deterministic, in the sense of (A).

(8) Nothing in steps (1) through (7) used any feature of general relativity beyond general covariance, and by (B) general covariance can be had by nearly any theory. The same reasoning therefore shows that every theory is non-deterministic.

(9) That conclusion is false; classical mechanics, for instance, is the paradigm of a deterministic theory.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am most attached to (9) and to the thought that the world has a definite structure rather than being an artifact of how we choose to describe it; I am least confident in my own formulation of the background claims, though I cannot tell whether that is a real doubt or only inarticulacy. One thing nags at me: (6) reminds me of two databases that hold identical rows but assign those rows different internal identifiers. If the identifiers are part of the data, permuting them changes the database; if they are merely handles, it does not. I cannot tell whether spacetime points are data or handles, or what turns on the answer.
