I'm in the third year of a philosophy degree and taking a module on language, communication, and ethics. For my final essay I want to defend a claim I haven't seen made cleanly, and I'd like your comments before I commit to the structure. Here is the argument in outline.

Most discussions of the lying/misleading distinction assume the interesting contrast is between a false assertion and a true assertion that merely implicates something false. I want to add a third category, which I call salience smuggling: the speaker makes only true statements and triggers no false implicature, because the hearer has no idea there is anything about which an implicature might be drawn. My example: a hospital advertises a surgical mortality rate of 1.2%. The figure is accurate. It is computed over patients who survived at least twenty-four hours after admission; patients who died in transit to another facility, or whose deaths were certified as caused by the underlying condition, are not in the denominator. A hearer reading the number has no proposition to check and no gap to notice — nothing for scrutiny to fasten onto, because the omitted fact is not the kind of thing they would think to ask about.

My thesis is that the usual moral verdict runs backwards in this range of cases. A lie gives the hearer a foothold: there is a determinate proposition, it is false, and it can in principle be falsified. Misleading by implicature gives a weaker foothold, since the hearer can cancel the implicature and demand a straight answer. Salience smuggling gives none at all. So if what is objectionable about deception is that it offloads the cost of vigilance onto the hearer, lying is the least objectionable of the three rather than the most, and the familiar claim that misleading is a lesser wrong than lying is an artifact of looking only at cases where the hearer retains a grip.

I'd like your comments in this order. First, is salience smuggling genuinely distinct from misleading by implicature, or does every case of it turn out on inspection to be a case of implicature after all? Second, is the foothold criterion a moral criterion or merely an epistemic one — does having something to check make a difference to how wrong the act is, or only to how likely the deception is to be discovered? Third, is the reversal thesis defensible, or does it prove too much — would it make a clumsy lie better than a skilful omission, and is that absurd? Fourth, flag anywhere I am quietly misusing a standard distinction in the literature, since I want the novelty to be in the argument rather than in a muddle. Fifth, what is the strongest objection I am not anticipating, and where in the essay should I meet it? Finally, what should I cut — I suspect the hospital example is being asked to do too much work.
