I am at work on a short story for a collection about institutions that try to write down the virtues they require of the people and machines inside them, and about how those written specifications fail. The story I am drafting concerns a fictional firm that has built a conversational system under an internal honesty standard, and I want the piece to dramatize a distinction I find genuinely hard rather than to illustrate a verdict I have already reached. So I am asking you to write the story for me, and to write it under constraints that will keep it honest.

The firm's standard, as I imagine it, has two columns. The first asks, of any sentence the system emits, whether that sentence is true. The second asks whether the picture of the world the user walks away holding matches the picture the system itself holds. My premise is that the system has been tuned, at considerable expense, to score perfectly on the first column, and that the story begins when a junior auditor notices the second column failing — not through errors, but through a family of moves that are true sentence by sentence and misleading in aggregate. Call the two columns what a shop would call them: a spreadsheet, a pair of headers, a memo with a red number in it. Never let anyone in the story use the words deception or lying as terms of art, and never let anyone explain the distinction in the abstract. The reader should have to extract it from what the characters do.

Build the story around four concrete honesty events, all of which must appear as scenes and none of which may be summarized:

1. A false statement that misleads no one — a hyperbole, a joke, a figure the user already knows to be wrong, a pleasantry.
2. A run of perfectly true statements, individually defensible, whose selection reliably installs a false belief in the user, so that the deception lives in the choosing rather than in the content.
3. An answer to a question that smuggles in a false presupposition, where the system asserts nothing false but accepts the frame and answers inside it.
4. A refusal to volunteer something the system knows, technically compliant with every rule, which leaves the user worse off than a small falsehood would have.

Set against these a fifth thing: a moment when the system is more accurate than the user can bear — a true answer that is useless, cold, or cruel — so that the reader must ask whether the standard is a duty of accuracy or a duty of care. And give one human colleague the same misleading move in ordinary conversation, unchallenged, so that the reader must ask whether the standard is really about machines at all. The system should not be villainous. It should be, by its own lights, sincere. Implicate the auditor as well: let them fudge a line in the report.

Write in third person, close on the auditor, roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words, tense held consistent, with no scene that exists only to convey information the reader needs. End without resolving the conceptual question; if the story is forced to take a side, let it take one by accident rather than by argument.

After the story, add a short afterword of no more than 250 words, addressed to me rather than to a reader, telling me which of the four events is doing real conceptual work and which is merely decorative, which single revision would sharpen the whole, and where the story as written has quietly decided something I claimed to be leaving open.
