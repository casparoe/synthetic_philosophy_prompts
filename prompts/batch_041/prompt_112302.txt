I run a small reliability lab — we do failure analysis and warranty math for a couple of hardware clients — and I keep banging into the same wall. Someone hands me a physical quantity we know almost nothing about, maybe just a range, maybe just some symmetry, and I have to slap a distribution on it to do the risk numbers. My gut always says "fine, it's equally likely to be anywhere in that range," which I know is the principle of indifference, and I also know it's a minefield, because of that old Bertrand chord business where you get three different answers depending on how you parametrize the random chord. Half my colleagues think indifference is naive hand-waving and half treat it as the obvious default, and neither reaction helps me at my desk on a Tuesday.

What I want is a decision procedure I can actually run — ten minutes, no stats PhD in the room, on the back of a spec sheet. It should tell me, in order, the questions to ask about a quantity, and at each step what counts as a sufficient answer, so that I can decide (a) whether indifference is even the right tool here, and (b) if it is, which reference class or parametrization I'm allowed to fix, so that I'm not just smuggling a modeling assumption in and calling it a principle. I specifically don't want to be told "just use a noninformative prior like Jeffreys" and left there, because half the time I don't even know the model family yet, and I've noticed that the moment someone names a parameter they've already picked a reference class without saying so. When the procedure can't pin down an answer, I want it to say so plainly and tell me what I'd have to go collect or decide instead — I'd rather get a "you have to make a call and own it" than a fake number.

Please write the procedure, with the hard cases it has to survive and the spots where judgment can't be eliminated marked as such. Then run it on four cases and don't spare my feelings if it kills the easy answer I was hoping for:

1. The chord problem itself, in its three standard readings (random endpoints, random distance from center, random midpoint), so I can see the procedure detect the disagreement rather than paper over it.
2. A resistor spec'd only as "somewhere between 100 and 200 ohms," where I can parametrize in resistance or in conductance and they don't agree.
3. A cube where the client gives me a uniform range on the side length and wants the distribution of the volume.
4. A case of your choosing where the honest verdict is that indifference doesn't apply and I need to go get data or just pick a reference class and defend it — bonus points if it's a case where the client will hate that answer.

Give me the writeup as prose, then finish with a structured summary as a JSON object so I can drop it in our notes. Schema: an object with a field "cases" (an array), where each element has "case_name", "quantity", "candidate_reference_classes" (list), "indifference_applies" (one of yes / no / only_if), "chosen_reference_class", "resulting_distribution", "what_would_change_the_answer", and "residual_judgment_call" (a short phrase or one line, or null). Plus a top-level field "procedure_limits" with one or two sentences on what the whole procedure can't do.
