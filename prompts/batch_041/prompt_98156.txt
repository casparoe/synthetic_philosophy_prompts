For the past year I have been sitting in on appointments at a specialist clinic for patients with disorders of consciousness — my younger brother is one of those patients — and the experience has driven me back to a problem I had set aside after graduate school. Watching clinicians disagree over whether a patient who tracks a mirror or follows a command is aware at all has convinced me that the most interesting version of the conceivability argument against physicalism is not the one everybody teaches. I am giving a departmental colloquium in three weeks defending a replacement for it, and I would like you to build my Q&A prep sheet.

Here is the position I will defend. Call a fuzzy zombie a being that is a complete physical and functional duplicate of an ordinary human being, and for which there is no fact of the matter whether it has any phenomenal experience at all — not a being that lacks experience, but one whose experiential status is genuinely indeterminate. My claims are these: (1) fuzzy zombies are conceivable in a stronger and more defensible sense than the familiar crisp zombie, because the vagueness of our concept of experience makes the indeterminate case easier to conceive than a determinate absence; (2) if conceivability is a reliable guide to possibility, then there is a possible world whose physical and functional facts are exactly like ours and in which it is indeterminate whether anyone is conscious; and (3) this is a worse result for physicalism than the crisp zombie, because physicalism promises that the physical facts fix all the facts, and the determinacy of the experiential facts is itself one of the facts that would have to be fixed. I also want to claim that the standard reply — that indeterminacy is a feature of our concepts rather than of the world — proves too much, since it would equally defuse the crisp zombie. I will sketch all of this in about forty minutes and then take questions.

The audience is my department's philosophy of mind workshop. Four of them are functionalists or representationalists about consciousness and will treat any zombie argument as a confusion about concepts; two are illusionists who think the phenomenal-realist framework is the mistake; one is a phenomenologist who works on embodiment and is allergic to disembodied thought experiments; and one is a visiting cognitive neuroscientist who studies disorders of consciousness, regards a priori arguments about experience as unserious, and will speak from clinical experience. There are no dualists in the room, and no one who already accepts a conceivability-to-possibility principle.

I want a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, phrased the way each of them would phrase it, ordered by how likely each is to be raised multiplied by how much damage it does — not by how philosophically famous it is. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede and what I should refuse to give up, and what I should not try to bluff. Tell me what to concede up front, before the first question, so that I look like someone who has already considered the obvious lines. Finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, if it comes, I will have no good answer to — and my least-bad move if it does.
