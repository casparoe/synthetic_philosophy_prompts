I am a retired field ecologist — thirty years of salt-marsh and estuary work — who has, since retiring, been sitting in on and now co-facilitating an adult-education philosophy seminar at our local university's continuing-studies programme. The group is about sixteen people, mixed backgrounds and no formal training, one ninety-minute session a week, and I have been handed the session on what I have learned to call the special composition question: under what conditions, if any, do some things together make up a further thing. I have read enough to know the usual landmarks — that one may say composition always occurs, never occurs, or occurs only under some restriction — but I have no interest in staging a lecture in which those three options are set out and compared. I want the participants to arrive at the problem on their own feet, and to feel the particular discomfort of finding that their own verdicts, given honestly, commit them to something they did not consciously choose. Please design a runnable ninety-minute activity for this group that does that, and give me the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no real money.

My rough idea, which you should feel free to discard or rebuild, is a kind of census or tribunal. Stage a scene — a table of objects, a projected photograph, or the room itself will do — and present the group with a sequence of assemblies, one at a time, each of which they must rule on with a token or a raised card: is there a single further thing here, or only the parts arranged this way? Let the sequence run from two pebbles merely resting against each other, through a heap, a glued pair, a welded pair, a pair joined by a living tendril, a swarm that moves as one, a vortex, and a colony, and have them mark on a shared board where the yes begins. The point of the staging is that they will not be able to place a non-arbitrary boundary, and that their earlier answers will have silently bound them to some criterion they never named. Then introduce the cases that pull candidate criteria apart: a pair of distinct organisms surgically grafted so that they share one circulation, alive and integrated but not one organism; a swarm that behaves as a unit yet has no life of its own; a boulder split and cemented back together. And seed one criterion I have not seen anyone use and would like the group to test, namely that some things compose a further thing when the parts are jointly subject to a single selective pressure — when they share one evolutionary fate rather than merely sitting in one place — and ask them whether that criterion, if adopted, would make composition a matter of the parts' history rather than of how they are arranged right now. I would keep the name of the problem and the names of the standard positions out of the room until the very end.

In the debrief, map the moments of the activity onto the parts of the idea: the failed boundary onto the thought that nothing in physics or observation tells us where composition begins; the grafted pair and the swarm onto the pull of a restrictivist criterion and the cost of choosing one; the split boulder onto the pressure that any restriction short of always or never forces us to draw a sharp line we cannot locate, which is what drives some people to the extremes. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — someone will insist the whole question is merely verbal and about our counting conventions, a couple of participants will anchor hard on the living cases and refuse to move off them, and the group will try to settle matters by majority vote or by fiat — together with how I should handle each without short-circuiting the exercise. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I, as facilitator, will have to supply in words: I suspect no amount of staging can reproduce the felt seriousness of asking whether a thing exists, as opposed to asking how we find it convenient to count, and I would like to be told plainly where the exercise stops and I must take over.
