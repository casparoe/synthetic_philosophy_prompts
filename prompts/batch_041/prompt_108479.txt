I'm in a little reading group that's been crawling through Grice for months — the papers on speaker meaning and implicature — and I've also been trying, mostly failing, to get back into writing fiction, so my friend Jonah suggested I try to mash the two together and see what happens. So: could you write me a short story where the whole thing turns on the gap between what a person's words mean and what the person meant by them, and specifically on the idea that meaning something is a matter of intending your audience to work out your intention, and to work it out at least partly *because* you intended them to? That reflexive bit is the part I keep losing my grip on, and I'd rather see it happen in a scene then in a paragraph of explanation. Please don't have anyone in the story state the theory or use the jargon — I want the reader to feel it working under the floorboards.

Setting, if it's useful (change anything that doesn't work): a lighthouse on a foggy stretch of coast, kept by two people who've shared the job for eleven winters and who by now barely say anything straight — everything is a hint, an understatement, a thing conspicuously not mentioned — plus a young relief keeper who's just arrived and can't read the idiom at all. Somewhere in there I'd like (a) one utterance that's literally true and totally innocuous but that, in the keepers' private idiom, is how you say something much heavier, so the relief keeper has to reconstruct what was meant; (b) a moment where the speaker is asked point-blank whether he meant that, and denies it, and it's genuinely unclear whether the denial is a real taking-it-back or a retreat — I'm curious whether an implicature can just be cancelled like that, and what that would show; (c) the same sentence turning up again later out of an old radio set or a tape, with nobody behind it, so someone has to decide whether anything was meant at all that time; and (d) a final beat where the question "was a warning given?" actually matters for something official — an inquiry, a report, a statement to the owners, whatever fits — and the answer hangs on whether the speaker meant anything, not on what the words meant. I'd love it if the ending doesn't tidy up: I want the last page to leave me feeling that you could know every fact about what was said and still not be able to say whether anything was meant. And it should be a story first — plot, people, weather, a bad night on the rocks — not a fable with a moral stapled to it.
