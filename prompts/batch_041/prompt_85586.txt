I ran the dig, and on Saturday I have to give the opening speech at our little museum's new gallery — first time any of it goes on display. Small settlement on the edge of the fen, occupied for a few centuries and then just abandoned. About four thousand sherds, a couple hundred fragments of clay figurines that look like they were deliberately smashed and tucked under the doorways of the houses, a big burnt granary, and one burial that's much richer than everything else on the site put together.

Here's my problem. The room is maybe sixty people: the volunteers who spent two summers trowelling in the rain, the couple who paid for the display cases, the landowner's family, a reporter from the local paper, and three actual archaeologists from the university who will visibly wince if I say one thing the evidence doesn't support. Everyone wants me to tell them what these people believed. Thats the whole reason they came. And I can't.

The figurines are the crux. Same pattern all over the site, under thresholds, smashed in a consistent way. Read one: it's an offering, a belief, a ritual. Read two: it's just how you got rid of a broken object in that culture, a disposal habit, no belief content at all. Read three: it's a one-generation fad, done because the neighbours did it, nothing underneath. Read four: something about ancestors, or about the house having a life of its own, or about keeping whatever was outside the door out at night. The dirt is identical in all four stories. And 'ritual' as a word does nothing except name the fact that I don't know — it's the label we reach for when we can't say what a thing was for.

Then the rich burial. My own instinct — and I'll admit it's an instinct — is that one person buried with ten times more stuff than anyone else means rank, a chief, an institution. But it could be one lucky individual, or one mourning family going overboard, and a rule that only ever got followed once is not a rule. Same with the granary: burnt grain tells me about storage, and probably about surplus, and probably about somebody being able to control that surplus, but 'probably' is doing an enormous amount of lifting in that sentence.

So here's what I want the speech to do. Three tiers, kept visibly separate the whole way through. What we know — the stone came from two hundred kilometres away, that's a fact about goods or people moving. What we think, and why, and what would have to be true for the other stories to be wrong. And what we have no idea about, said out loud as no idea, without apologising for it. I want people leaving feeling that the honesty is the interesting part rather than the disappointing part — that a room full of objects that refuses to tell you what they meant is more interesting than one that does.

Hard constraints. No 'the objects tell us', no 'if only these pots could speak', I'll gag. Don't resolve the figurines either way — the whole point is that I'm standing next to them not knowing, and I want the speech to make that a position rather than a failure. But don't go relativist on me either. I do know things. A lot of things. Pretending we can't know anything is its own kind of dishonesty and the three archaeologists at the back will eat me alive. No jargon. And the director wants me to end on 'these objects tell us who these people were' and I've told her I wont say it, so I need an ending that does the same job emotionally without that sentence.

Write it. Six, seven minutes.
