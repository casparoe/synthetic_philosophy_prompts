So two of my friends got into it at a bar last night about a history case, and I honestly couldnt follow who had the better of it. The setup: a historian works out that a famous general and an anonymous diarist whose memoirs everyone loves were the same man. My friend D says: "Thats a real discovery about the world. She didn't just learn something about her own filing system -- she checked handwriting, dates, sifted the evidence. And 'the general is the diarist' tells you things 'the general is the general' doesn't, like that the general wrote those memoirs. So the two sentences don't say the same thing, and what they don't share is real content." My friend R says: "No -- what she found out is that two names we had were hooked to one man. The worldly fact is just that he's identical to himself, which was never in doubt and couldn't have been otherwise. What changed was your information, your two files merging. Same proposition, different packaging, like '2+2' and '4.' You can know one without the other, but there's only one fact." Then D: "But then 'packaging' is doing all the work, and if the packaging is part of what you understand, you've just reinvented the sense you were trying to get rid of. And if it isn't part of the content, you can't explain why the announcement is informative rather than merely startling -- a loud noise startles me too." R: "Being informative isn't only learning a new truth; she handed us a new way to latch onto him, a new cognitive fix. That's a real epistemic gain even when the proposition is old. Your puzzle is about knowledge and reference, not about the world having extra facts in it." They went round like this a few times and neither budged. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what -- not who wins overall -- and what they're both missing? Keep it under 400 words, American English.
