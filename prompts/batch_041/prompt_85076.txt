I teach one ninety-minute session in a graduate decision theory course, and next week's topic is the common prior assumption. My students have had a term of measure-theoretic probability and are fluent with conditioning, but none of them has seen epistemic game theory; the group is mixed, roughly half economics and half machine learning, and I can rely on one of the latter asking, early, whether the assumption implies that two models trained on the same corpus must converge once they compare predictions.

I do not want the assumption explained to me; I want a plan for teaching it. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced and why the dependencies run as they do: what has to be in place before the assumption can even be stated, what has to be in place before the agreement result is more than a curiosity, and where the pivot occurs from "a modelling assumption one may adopt or decline" to "a constraint on rational disagreement." Then tell me what should carry the session: the single example or case I build it on (it has to fit on a whiteboard in two minutes), what that example must have and what it must lack for the hypothesis of the result to feel like a genuine restriction rather than a technicality, and whether the LLM case my machine-learning students will raise can serve as that example or whether it is a trap that will teach the wrong lesson — and if it is a trap, what I should say about it and at what point.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they will surface, with the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I am expecting "the theorem says rational agents cannot disagree," "a common prior means the agents share the same information," and "common knowledge is just everyone knowing." What else will they form? And I especially want to know which of these is load-bearing — the one that, if left standing, makes everything after it sound like words.

Close with a question I can put to them in the final ten minutes that would separate students who have understood the assumption from students who have merely memorized a statement of the theorem, and with the material I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level, saying so out loud.
