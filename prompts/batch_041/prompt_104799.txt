I have been arguing for weeks with a friend in my seminar on responsibility, and I have finally been reduced to asking for help rather than conceding. He has built his case on a principle of his own coinage, which he calls the Parity Principle: if two agents are alike in everything that counts as an exercise of their agency — the intentions they form, the deliberation they perform, the effort they expend — then no difference in what subsequently befalls them can alter what they deserve. From this he reasons as follows. (1) The drunk driver who mounts the curb and kills a child, and the equally drunk driver who swerves a foot short, are alike in agency and differ only in outcome; by the Parity Principle they are equally blameworthy. (2) There is no principled stopping point, because any two agents who differ in what they do differ, tracing back far enough, only in constitution and circumstance — upbringing, temperament, which considerations happened to occur to them — none of which is itself an exercise of agency. (3) So by repeated application the Parity Principle yields that any two agents whatsoever are equally blameworthy and equally praiseworthy, whatever they do. (4) And then the move I cannot answer: the Parity Principle, if true, governs our assessments as well as our conduct, since whether I arrive at the correct verdict is itself a matter of the luck of which arguments occur to me; hence no verdict is more creditable than any other, including his own. He insists this is not a reductio but the sober truth, and that our sense of absurdity is merely the residue of a vindictive and luck-ridden practice of blame.

I would like to keep something like the Parity Principle, because the bare thought that outcome alone should not decide desert seems to me the heart of any humane account of responsibility; what I cannot accept is (4). Tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs. If the answer is that the Parity Principle needs a restriction, I want to know precisely what restriction preserves (1) without licensing the slide to (3), and whether that restriction can be stated without covertly assuming the conclusion it is meant to protect. And if (4) is the culprit, I want to understand what kind of mistake it is — whether the friend has simply changed the subject from blame to belief, or whether there is a deeper reason the very same luck that corrodes desert leaves the assessment of arguments untouched.
