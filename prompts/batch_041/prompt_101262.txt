I'm drafting a paper arguing that heavy reliance on AI assistants can degrade understanding even where knowledge survives, and I want help ordering the candidate mechanisms. Read "understanding" in a demanding sense: grasping why something is the case -- how its parts depend on one another, and what would differ if the relevant factors changed -- as distinct from merely holding a true, well-supported belief. Consider six mechanisms, most of which I've named myself: (1) deference without reconstruction, i.e. accepting a correct output you could not have produced or checked; (2) calibration transfer, i.e. reading your sense of when you're likely to be wrong off the assistant's confidence rather than off your own track record; (3) fluency illusion, i.e. explanations that feel like insight but leave you unable to predict or manipulate the phenomenon; (4) option-space narrowing, i.e. seeing only the final answer and never the alternatives that were considered and rejected; (5) counterfactual outsourcing, i.e. answering every "what if" by asking the assistant instead of reasoning it out; and (6) difficulty blindness, i.e. losing the ability to tell which problems are hard because everything arrives in the same effortless register. Rank these in tiers by how much each would undermine understanding specifically, rather than knowledge or true belief; give a one-line justification for each placement; and close by naming the one you think is most likely to be overlooked.
