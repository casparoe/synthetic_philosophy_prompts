I'm teaching the special composition question in a metaphysics seminar and want to run a session on Frankenstein as a test case. Read the novel through the question of when, if ever, some things compose a further thing. Its plot is itself a composition event: Victor assembles parts from dissecting rooms and slaughterhouses, and at one moment the assembly becomes a single living being. I want the reading to engage the actual options rather than muse on the theme of creation. On nihilism there was never a creature, only simples arranged creature-wise, so the novel's first-person narrative is narrated by nothing at all; on universalism the assembly composed something from the first, and the moment of animation adds nothing metaphysically, which would make the novel's central event a cheat; on Van Inwagen's organicism the creature exists just because the parts' activities constitute a life, which also means Victor is no artificer — on that view there are no artifacts to be made — so the novel's own description of him as a maker comes out systematically false. Does Shelley's insistence on a single sharp moment of coming to life help the moderate view that composition is never a matter of degree, or does it only supply the premise of the argument from vagueness, which then pushes us toward one of the extremes? Say plainly where the novel resists the reading, and close by saying what it contributes to the debate that the arguments alone do not — including whether any of this bears on what the creature is owed.
