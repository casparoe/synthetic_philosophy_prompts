ok, so I need help writing a speech and it's turned into a whole thing. Context: I'm the training officer for a volunteer mountain rescue team, and in three weeks is our fortieth anniversary dinner. I've been asked to give the "why we do this, and where we go next" speech — five, six minutes. Room is about eighty people: four of the original founders, all in their seventies, who built the team around an ethos I'll get to in a second; maybe thirty current members; a lot of spouses and kids who've never been on a call and don't want to hear about load-outs; and the county commisioner plus two donors who paid for the new truck. So it has to work for people who've been on four hundred callouts and people who've never been on one.

Here's my problem. Our founding ethos, and I do believe in it, is basically this: if you're the one who sees it, you're the one who goes. No committee, no asking permission, no waking anyone up. Somebody's kid is missing in the dark, and the reason we're any good is that one person can just go.

But I've spent a year chewing on something and I can't get it out of my head. On any given call, every member who gets the page decides independently whether to launch. Say each of us is right about whether a call is real 90% of the time — and that's generous, honestly. About twenty of us get the page. So the chance that at least one of us launches on a call that didn't warrant it is one minus 0.9 to the twentieth, which is about 88%. Almost every bad call gets a crew, not because any of us is dumb, but because twenty people each rolled a die. Which means, arithmetically, a team of five careful people is better behaved than a team of forty careful people — and I'm supposed to stand up at a dinner celebrating forty years and say that out loud.

The standard fix is to require three people to agree before anyone launches, and I hate that too, for exactly the reason the founders were right about: at 2am with a kid in the woods, the whole point is that one person can go. But here's the part I can't explain properly to anyone. I don't think the three-person fix even works. Our mistakes aren't independent. We read the same avalance bulletin, the same forecast, the same trip reports, we all trained under the same two people. So it isn't twenty separate 90% judgments, it's closer to one 90% judgment wearing twenty hats. Which is good news in a way — we probably don't really get 88% — but it means when we're wrong we're wrong all together, and three people agreeing doesn't help, because those three people are the same person three times. And it cuts the other way too: the calls we should have gone on and didn't, we probably all stayed home together on those as well.

So, the speech. Requirements: warm, because I love these people and the founders should cry a little, they've earned it. Honest about the above, or at least not a lie. Not a lecture — no words that only show up in textbooks, the commissioner should follow every sentence. It cannot tell the founders that forty years of this was a mistake, because it wasn't. And it should land on something the team could actually do differently, concrete enough that we could start Monday, not just let's be more careful. Don't fudge the numbers to make it come out nicer, and don't invent a study or a quote to back me up; I'm not standing at our own dinner and citing a paper.
