The course has covered Millikan's biosemantics, Dretske's indicator semantics, Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory, and the standard objections to each, including the disjunction problem and the status of misrepresentation.

The exam question was as follows. Teleosemantic theories of content are often advertised as having an advantage over purely informational (indicator or covariance) theories because they can accommodate misrepresentation. (a) Using Millikan's biosemantics as your representative teleosemantic theory, explain how misrepresentation is possible, being explicit about the roles of proper function, Normal conditions, and the producer/consumer distinction. (b) Consider the following hypothetical case. The glimmer-bird has a neural state, B, that is tokened when it encounters a glimmer-berry, its primary food. Tokening B drives the bird to approach and eat. There exists a mimic-berry that is visually indistinguishable from the glimmer-berry but toxic; when the bird encounters a mimic, B is also tokened, and the bird eats and becomes ill. B was selected for in the bird's lineage because it drove the consumption of glimmer-berries. What content does Millikan's theory assign to B? Does B misrepresent when it is tokened in the presence of a mimic? (c) Explain why a pure covariance theory cannot give the same answer, and how this is supposed to show the superiority of the teleosemantic approach.

The student wrote: "Teleosemantics grounds content in biological function. On Millikan's view, a representation is a state that has the proper function of corresponding to some condition in the world, where proper functions are the effects for which the state was selected. Content is not fixed by what the state actually covaries with, but by what it was selected to covary with. Millikan distinguishes the producer of a representation from its consumer: the producer is the mechanism that tokens the representation, and the consumer is the mechanism that uses it. The representation's content is determined by the correspondence that the consumer was selected to exploit — the state is supposed to be tokened when the relevant condition obtains, and the consumer is supposed to act on it. Misrepresentation becomes possible because the proper function of a representation can fail to be fulfilled on a particular occasion. When the glimmer-bird's B-state is tokened, it is supposed to be tokened in the presence of a glimmer-berry and to drive eating. In the mimic case, B is tokened in the presence of a mimic, so the state fails to correspond to the glimmer-berry; it therefore misrepresents. The crucial notion is Normal conditions: these are the conditions in which the state was originally learned to be tokened — the conditions under which the bird acquired the association between B and glimmer-berries. Because the mimic is not a glimmer-berry, tokening B in its presence is a departure from Normal conditions, and this is what makes it a misrepresentation. And because the bird becomes ill, B has failed to perform its proper function, and this failure just is the misrepresentation. A pure covariance theory cannot say this. On a covariance account, the content of B is whatever it reliably covaries with. Since B covaries with both glimmer-berries and mimics, its content would be 'glimmer-berry or mimic', and the state would be correct whenever either is present. So the covariance theory cannot distinguish the case where B is correct from the case where it is incorrect; it makes B infallible, which is absurd. Millikan avoids this by appealing to selection: B was selected to correspond to the glimmer-berry, not to the disjunction, so the disjunction is not its content. This is why teleosemantics is superior to pure informational accounts."
