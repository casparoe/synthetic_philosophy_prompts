I teach a 400-level seminar on social choice and political philosophy — 16 students, one 75-minute session — and I want to run a game that makes them feel Sen's liberal paradox in their gut instead of watching me scribble the derivation on the board. What I want them to collide with: you can be a liberal (each person gets to decide their own private business, no one else's vote counts for anything) and you can accept the feeblest possible Pareto principle (if every single person prefers X to Y then so does the group), and those two innocent comittments, plus a scrap of consistency, refuse to sit in the same room — you wind up with a cycle, or an outcome everyone agrees is worse than one that was sitting right there on the table. I want the rules to do the work, so they walk into the wall themselves and only hear the word "paradox" at the very end.

Here's the crude sketch, build it out properly. The structure I want them to stumble into (I dont need the names to survive — change the story if you like): two people, one scandalous book, three possible outcomes — nobody reads it, A reads it, B reads it. A ranks: nobody reads > A reads > B reads. B ranks: A reads > B reads > nobody reads. Each person is boss of exactly one thing, whether *they themselves* read it. And both rank "A reads" above "B reads." Now A's authority over his own reading forces the group to rank "nobody reads" above "A reads." B's authority forces "B reads" above "nobody reads." And Pareto forces "A reads" above "B reads." Three perfectly reasonable constraints that close a loop: nobody reads > A reads > B reads > nobody reads. So the group can't produce a consistent ranking at all.

I want this staged, not lectured. My instinct is groups of three: two players whose private choices are binding on the group, and a third who's the "clerk" whose job is to write down the group's ranking of the three outcomes and who simply can't make it come out. Run several groups in parallel so the class sees it isn't one group being dense. Please give me the actual rules, the role cards and materials, a scoring scheme using points rather than money, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes.
