I chair the standing scientific advisory panel of a state drinking-water program. The panel has nine members, five technical specialists and four public representatives, and it advises the program's director, who is free to disregard our recommendations but has never departed from a unanimous one.

Two of our technical members, A and B, have spent the last ten days on a single question: whether a cluster of anomalous readings at one intake point means that a contaminant has entered the distribution system serving roughly 400,000 people, or whether it is an artifact of sensor drift and seasonal turnover in the reservoir. They have read the same raw data, the same maintenance logs, and the same three independent laboratory reports; they have met alone together for six hours, twice; each has written a four-page reasoning memo that the other has read and annotated; and both have stated in open session that they understand the other's position and consider it coherent but wrong. I have satisfied myself that neither has seen anything the other has not. They still disagree. A puts the probability of contamination at roughly 0.7 and wants us to recommend that the director issue a public advisory and switch the system to its alternate supply. B puts it at roughly 0.15 and wants no public action, on the ground that a false alarm would spend credibility the department will need for a real event.

The panel votes Thursday at 5 p.m., and the director wants our recommendation that evening, so that any advisory could be issued before the holiday weekend, when system staffing drops to a skeleton crew.

One of the lay members, a retired philosopher, has circulated a note arguing that rational agents who share the same evidence cannot rationally disagree; that Aumann's agreement theorem shows this kind of persistent disagreement to be a defect rather than a signal; that therefore at least one of A and B is either reasoning badly or withholding something; and that the panel should accordingly not split the difference but should determine which of them it is. She proposes a charter amendment, which she calls the Convergence Rule, for adoption at the same meeting: "Where two panel members, after exchanging all evidence and reasons, continue to assign materially different probabilities to the same proposition, the panel shall presume that at least one of them holds evidence not yet disclosed, and shall adopt the more precautionary of the two judgments pending disclosure."

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action on the immediate question, including what we should say to the director and by when; it should say whether the panel should adopt the Convergence Rule, in its proposed form or an amended one; it should explain to the four lay members, in terms they can follow without pretending the technical details don't matter, what the disagreement between A and B does and does not tell us about who is right; and it should engage the philosopher's argument on its merits, so that if she is mistaken, the opinion says precisely where, in a way she could respond to.
