I'm on the board of our neighborhood association—not an academic, just a parent who got elected because nobody else wanted the job. We have this old rule from the 1970s that says no fence or hedge over three feet tall along the front of a lot. It's basically ignored: half the block has six-foot fences, and nobody has enforced it in decades. A few of us want to repeal it, mostly because it's a hassle for people with dogs and kids. But our president is a big Burke fan—he says society is a partnership between the dead, the living, and the unborn—and he keeps bringing up Chesterton's fence, the idea that you shouldn't tear down a fence until you know why it was put there. He says we'd be arrogant to scrap a rule just because we can't see its point. So we went through the old minutes, and it looks like the rule was adopted right after a group home for adults with disabilities tried to buy a house on the corner; the minutes from that year talk about keeping the block residential in character and protecting property values. Now the whole thing feels different.

One friend says the origin doesn't matter—that's just the genetic fallacy, and we should judge the rule by its effects now. Another says when the origin is a deliberate attempt to exclude people, the history isn't a fallacy, it's evidence about what the rule is still doing. I have to draft the boards memo for Tuesday's vote, and I need a clear recommendation: repeal, keep, or amend, with a concrete process and timeline for whatever we choose. Please take the conservative presumption seriously—not as a cartoon—because our president is smart and I want an argument he can actually answer. But also deal honestly with the fact that the rule's original purpose looks discriminatory, and say what that does to the Chesterton's fence argument. And say what we should do about the fences people already have. Keep it to around 700 words, with the recommendation up top and a short section on the strongest case against it.
