I'm putting together the take-home final for an upper-level seminar I run called "Pleasure and the Good Life," and I'm sick of the same essay every year where students explain the usual quantity-versus-quality line or recite the sensory/attitudinal split and then stop. I want one question that actually drags them into the heterogeneity problem — why the taste of a ripe peach, the ache you sort of enjoy after a long run, the satisfaction of finally getting a proof to go through, and the weird pleasure of a sad song all get filed under "pleasure" when there's no one feeling they obviously share. To keep them from just parroting what I said in lecture, for the last two weeks I've been pushing an account I basically made up in my notes and called the "self-recommending" view: a state counts as a pleasure just in case (a) the subject is disposed to keep it going or seek it again, and (b) it presents itself to the subject as making the activity worth continuing. I want the exam to test whether they can say what this view gets right about those cases, where it either collapses into one of the older views or dies on a hard case, and whether it can really beat the two main rivals — the idea that pleasures share some common sensory quality, and the attitudinal view where a pleasure is just taking pleasure in some proposition or fact — rather than quietly renaming one of them. And it has to make them face the cases I keep harping on: the person who genuinely enjoys their own grief at a funeral, and the person who's clearly enjoying something (they got the fellowship, they're lying in bed grinning) while feeling nothing in particular in their body.

So write me the exam question itself — one long question or a question with two or three sub-parts, whatever works — a model answer of around 600 words that a strong student could realistically produce in the 90-minute window, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want five must-hit points with marks attached that add up to 20, and for each point a one-line note on how students typically botch it. I care a lot about seperating the A answers from the B answers, so tell me the specific moves that make the difference — e.g., someone who notices the self-recommending view has to say whether the disposition comes before the seeming or the other way round, versus someone who just mutters "it's about desire" and moves on. No citations, no reading lists; it's closed-book and I'd rather they argue than name-drop.
