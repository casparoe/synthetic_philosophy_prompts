I am a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on interpersonal utility comparisons, and I have been trying to formulate a principle that captures my considered judgments about when such comparisons are meaningful. A colleague and I have been arguing about this over lunch for weeks. I have a tentative view I call the anchored-comparability view: a comparison of two people’s welfare is meaningful just in case there is a shared anchor—some condition or outcome that both parties can independently locate as better or worse than their own—and the comparison does not require a common unit for welfare differences. I am not confident it survives the cases below.

Here are my verdicts. (1) Comparing a person with mild but constant arthritis pain to a person with severe but intermittent migraines, I think it is meaningful to say the migraine sufferer is worse off overall. (2) Comparing a person blind from birth to a person deaf from birth, I think it is not meaningful to say one is worse off than the other. (3) Comparing a person with severe depression to a person with moderate chronic pain, where both agree that severe depression is worse than moderate chronic pain, I think it is meaningful to say the depressed person is worse off. (4) Comparing a rich person who loses $1,000 to a poor person who gains $1,000, I think it is meaningful to say the poor person’s gain is greater than the rich person’s loss, even though there is no common unit of welfare. (5) Comparing a human to a dog, I think it is not meaningful to say the human is better off overall, but it is meaningful to say the dog’s pain is worse than the human’s mild itch. (6) Comparing two people with different value systems—one who values achievement, one who values relationships—both of whom report equal life satisfaction, I think it is not meaningful to say one is better off. (7) Comparing a person with severe tinnitus to a person with moderate chronic pain, where both agree that severe tinnitus is worse than moderate chronic pain, I think it is not meaningful to say the tinnitus sufferer is worse off. (8) I think it is not meaningful to say that Alice is twice as well off as Bob, even if we can say she is better off.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If no single principle works, give me the smallest set of candidates that together cover the cases, and say which case forces each. What does it say about cases I have not considered—for instance, comparing the welfare of a person with severe tinnitus to a person with severe chronic nausea, comparing a person’s welfare before and after a religious conversion, or comparing the welfare of two people with different disabilities? Which of my verdicts, if any, must I give up? If you propose a principle, state it precisely, give the case that motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I am especially interested in whether the principle should be formulated for levels, differences, or both, and whether it should posit a single scale or a set of dimension-specific scales.
