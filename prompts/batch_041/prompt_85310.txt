I host a small podcast about the history of ideas, and my next guest is a historian of science who holds that the great discoveries are essentially inevitable: had Darwin died young, someone else would have reached natural selection within a generation, and much the same could be said of the calculus. I would like the interview to test that claim rather than let him restate it. Please draft eight to ten questions, including one that asks what kind of claim inevitability is supposed to be and what evidence could count against it; one that presses the thought that we may be treating two achievements as a single discovery only after the fact, because they fed into the same later science, so that the retrospective grouping rather than any real inevitability is doing the explanatory work; and one on whether Newton's and Leibniz's systems were close enough in content to count as one discovery at all. For each of those three, add a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Then two or three questions on credit: whether his view makes prizes, eponyms, and priority disputes mere lotteries, and what he would say to someone who would keep them because they spur effort. End with a question about what a non-specialist like me should do differently, and whether it misleads my listeners when I say that a particular person discovered something.
