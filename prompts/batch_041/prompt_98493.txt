I teach a 15-person upper-level epistemology seminar and have one 75-minute session to spend. I want students to feel the force of the idea that the norm governing assertion is knowledge — stricter than truth, stricter than belief — before I name any position. Design a card-and-challenge game in which players make claims to one another, may challenge, and pay a small in-class penalty (points, not money) for losing a challenge. Build in three special cases the rules must handle: (a) a card whose proposition is false but which the speaker sincerely and reasonably believes; (b) a card whose proposition is true and probabilistically well-supported but could easily have been false (a lottery-like case); and (c) a card whose proposition is true, believed, and supported by the speaker's evidence, but only because the evidence runs through a false step that happens to land on the truth. Arrange the challenge rules and payoffs so that when the group actually plays, they find themselves penalizing the lottery and false-lemma assertions despite their being true, treating ordinary well-grounded assertions as beyond reproach, and being unsure what to do with the sincere false belief — and so that this discovery is theirs, not mine. Give me the rules, materials, the payoff scheme, a timeline, and a debrief that maps moments in play onto the truth, belief, and knowledge accounts and shows which verdicts each account would have to overturn. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the knowledge account the game cannot reproduce, so I know what I still have to supply myself.
