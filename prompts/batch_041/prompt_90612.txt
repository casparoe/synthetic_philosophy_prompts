I'm trying to get my head round George Eliot's attitude to religion, and there's a take I want you to argue for. The usual line is that her religion of humanity is basically Feuerbach with the supernatural bits removed: we project our own best qualities onto God, then alienate them, and Eliot just skips the projection and keeps the human sympathy. I think thats wrong, or at least too tidy. What I want you to argue is that Eliot's mature view is actually in tension with Feuerbach in a way that matters. Feuerbach thinks religious belief is a kind of self-alienation — we worship a magnified image of ourselves. Eliot, on the other hand, thinks the real religious impulse is about recognising a debt to something we didn't choose and can't fully see: the accumulated labour, suffering, and affection of people long dead, which shapes what we care about and what we feel obliged to. So her image of the dead as a continuing moral presence isn't a human projection at all; it's a causal inheritance. Argue for that reading, and say what it changes about how we read characters like Dinah Morris, Dorothea Brooke, or Silas Marner. I'd also like you to be honest about what's actually settled in Eliot scholarship versus what's a live disagreement — don't just tell me this is obviously the consensus if it isn't. And flag anywhere you're pushing the interpretation further than the texts really support. Don't worry about quoting chapter and verse or giving me a reading list; I'm after the arguement.
