I'm putting together a dataset of hard prompts and this one's for me, so I'd like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial colaboration between two camps in pragmatics that have been talking past each other for decades. Take the role of a philosopher of language with deep expertise in both Gricean pragmatics and post-Gricean/relevance-theoretic pragmatics, and who isn't invested in either side winning. Think it through carefully, and before you write the statement, spend some space brainstorming: what do the two actually agree on, where is the real disagreement, and which parts of the fight are just the two sides using the same word for different jobs?

Camp A, the Gricean. Speaker meaning is the speaker's reflexive intention to produce an effect in the audience by means of the audience's recognition of that very intention. Conversational implicatures are what the hearer works out by assuming the speaker is observing the Cooperative Principle and its maxims (quantity, quality, relation, manner). They're calculable, they're cancellable without contradiction, and there's a real, theoretically important distinction between what was said -- the truth-evaluable core, once ambiguity and reference are fixed -- and what was implicated.

Camp B, the relevance theorist. You don't need a principle plus a list of maxims; one principle of relevance does the work, and the maxims are either derivable from it or idle. The explicit content of an utterance -- the explicature -- is itself the output of inference and enrichment, so the said/implicated boundary isn't load-bearing the way the Gricean thinks, and Gricean "what is said" as a minimal proposition isn't a level worth keeping.

What I want back is the document the two of them could both put their names to. Numbered section of agreed claims first: about the data, about what needs explaining, and about which of the classic marks of implicature -- cancellability, calculability, detachability, reinforceability -- actually discriminate between anything. Then a disagreements table: each row a point of difference, with the Gricean's own wording of their position and the relevance theorist's own wording of theirs, both written so the other side would agree the wording is fair to it. Then the cruxes, ordered by how much settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument that both sides would accept bears on it -- invent small dialogues if you need them, e.g. a scalar case in a downward-entailing context, a free-enrichment case where the proposition the speaker seems to have communicated isn't the one the sentence encodes, a manner case where two ways of saying the same thing implicate differently -- and write down each side's prediction about that case in advance, before you say who you think is right. Where you think part of the dispute is purely verbal, say which part and why, and say what a neutral shared vocabulary would look like.

No references or reading lists, just the content. And if some crux can't be settled by any case both sides would accept, say that plainly instead of inventing one.
