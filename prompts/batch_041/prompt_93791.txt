I am a third-year student in a political philosophy seminar, and for the past few weeks we have been circling around the question of whether ordinary social conventions—queueing in order, leaving a tip at a sit-down restaurant, waiting for the walk signal even when no car is coming—are genuine moral obligations or merely local etiquette that one can ignore without moral fault. My puzzlement comes from a concrete institutional practice. My university has recently revised its international student orientation. One module, called Local Norms, instructs incoming students from abroad to treat a list of host-country conventions as binding: form a single queue and never hold a place for a friend, tip twenty percent at table-service restaurants, do not jaywalk even on an empty street at three in the morning, and stand on the right side of the escalator. The module's language is moralized: it says that ignoring these expectations is not merely gauche but disrespectful, and that visitors who do so wrong the locals who have to accommodate them. Yet the same orientation, in a different session, tells students that judging another culture's customs by their home standards is a category mistake, and that moral criticism of unfamiliar practices is usually unwarranted. And the surrounding institutional signals are mixed: campus security has stopped ticketing jaywalking on the grounds that it is not a real offense, while the student government has passed a resolution calling the expectation to tip a coercive imposition on international students.

I want to know what philosophical commitments this practice—the orientation module, together with the qualifications and exceptions the university actually makes—presupposes. Does it presuppose a relativist view, on which the bindingness of a convention is indexed to the local community and visitors acquire obligations simply by entering the territory? Or does it presuppose something more like a fair-play or natural-duty view, on which visitors owe compliance because they benefit from a scheme of coordination and because respect for persons requires not free-riding on shared expectations? Or is it committed to a Humean or Lewis-style account on which conventions are solutions to coordination problems and therefore generate obligations only when the coordination is actually valuable, which would make the empty-street jaywalking case a hard test? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with. In particular, is the module committed to the idea that the same convention binds locals and visitors alike, or does its asymmetry—locals who jaywalk are said to be taking a small liberty, while visitors who jaywalk are said to be disrespectful—reveal a role-based or associative view of obligation? Which rival theories does the practice quietly rule out, and is it internally consistent with the security policy and the student government resolution, or do those parts presuppose incompatible pictures? Finally, suppose we replaced the presupposed view with the view that everyday conventions are morally inert unless they protect rights or prevent harm. What would have to change in the orientation module, in how the university enforces or declines to enforce the conventions, and in what it owes to visitors who violate local norms?
