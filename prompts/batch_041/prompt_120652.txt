I've been keeping a list on my phone of cases where I have a strong gut feeling about whether two things really share a property or whether I'm just using the same word for both, and I finally have enough of them to want to know if there's one principle behind my gut. This started at a bar after my cousin's wedding, arguing with my brother (chemist) and my friend who's doing a PhD in phil of biology, and it's been bugging me ever since. The verdicts: two tomatoes that are exactly the same shade of red — I'm sure they share something in the fullest sense, the very same thing present in both. A scarlet tomato and a crimson one — both red, and I want that to be real too, but it feels different, like 'red' is a range and the exact shade is a point. Two lumps of 24k gold: same stuff, no question. An electron here and an electron on the far side of the galaxy: same mass, same charge, and my brother says there's an extra sense in which they're the same, since you can't tag them — if you swapped the two of them nothing whatsoever would differ, whereas with the tomatoes you could at least paint a dot on one. I still say that's a real shared property, maybe the realest one on the list. Two mallards: yes, both mallards, real, even though I know two mallards on different continents can be pretty different genetically — they're the same species because of where they came from, not because of how they're built. Then the other column: two chairs share nothing real, 'chair' is a job description, a wooden chair and a beanbag have nothing in common except that we sit on them. Chess and tag are both games — same deal. 'Being not a cat' isn't a property of anything, and 'being either red or a proton' obviously isn't either. But here's the one thats been bugging me: 'being heavier than my laptop.' Everything that satisfies it does share a single underlying trait, a very precise one (mass above 1.4 kg or whatever), and it's the same kind of trait that makes redness real if redness is real — a band along some measurable dimension. Yet I feel sure that one isn't a real property, it's just a fact about my laptop. So either redness isn't real in the way I thought, or the 'shared underlying trait' idea is doing no work at all.

My brother says I'm a nominalist with extra steps, which I don't think is fair, and my best attempt at a principle so far is roughly: a predicate picks out a real property when everything it applies to shares one underlying trait that's there whether or not anyone is around to care about it, rather than being held together only by what we do with them or by a list. Can you tell me what principle, or small set of candidates, actually gets my verdicts, where the two columns pull apart, and which of my verdicts I'd have to give up to get a clean principle? I'd especially like to know what it says about 'being a dollar bill' — I left that one off the list because I honestly don't know what I think — and about the electron case, because if the principle has to say two electrons share a property in a stronger sense than two tomatoes of the same shade do, I want to hear why that isn't just an artifact of physics being tidy. And tell me which case I should look at next that would really force the issue, not just one more case that fits.
