Last week a colleague and I spent most of an evening arguing about whether contractualism can survive the aggregation problem, and I would rather bring that argument into a public interview than leave it in the pub. I run a small recorded interview series (roughly forty minutes per episode), and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a variant of contractualism that she states as follows. The test of a principle is still what each individual could reasonably reject, but she allows complaints to be pooled across persons under three conditions: the complaints must be of the same kind; each must clear a low severity threshold; and each complainant must be willing to exchange her burden for any other complainant's burden of the same magnitude, so that pooling can never make one person's loss the vehicle of another's gain. She holds that this lets contractualism take numbers seriously in rescue-style cases without collapsing into a merely aggregative view, and she insists the result is individualist in the only sense that matters.

My friend's parting shot, which I want her to have to answer head-on, was that any view permitting complaints to be pooled has already conceded the consequentialist's point and is merely haggling over the exchange rate.

Draft eight to ten interview questions for me. Assume the audience already knows the standard formulation of reasonable rejection, so nothing should invite her simply to restate it, and there should be no warm-up or biographical questions: the first question should already be substantive. At minimum, cover the following, one question each:

- whether the severity threshold does any independent work or is aggregation under a different name;
- the same-kind condition, pressed with the contrast between a complaint about a minor inconvenience and a complaint about a serious loss;
- the trade-willingness condition, and whether it covertly imports consent, or wrongly excludes someone who refuses the trade out of commitment or spite;
- whether reasonable rejection is assessed before or after we know which burden will fall on us;
- the marginal-complainant problem: whether adding a person who is only trivially burdened should change what a principle may do to someone else;
- whether pooling can be permitted without the view drifting toward rule-consequentialism;
- the case that most embarrasses pooling, in which the many are slightly burdened and the one is severely burdened;
- who counts as a complainant at all, given that the test is framed in terms of complaints (future people, the dead, non-human animals, those unable to articulate a complaint).

Every question needs a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and the follow-up should leave her less room than the question it follows. End with a question about what would have to be true for her to abandon the trade-willingness condition.
