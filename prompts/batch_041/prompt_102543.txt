My brother has bipolar I and just came out of a manic episode where he spent alot of money and drove his car into a ditch. Before he was discharged he signed a psychiatric advance directive, a Ulysses contract basically, saying that if he gets like that again he can be held and medicated even if he says no. He's stable now and wants to rip it up. I'm trying to help him think it through, and I've fallen down a rabbit hole on precommitment and constitutional entrenchment.

So here's what I want from you. Not a reading list, not citations, I can't use those anyway. I want to know which open questions in this area actually matter most for someone designing or judging a real contract like his, and why. Rank them. I care about which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable for a philosopher rather than a lawyer or a clinician, and what would move them up or down the list.

A few things I keep getting stuck on, so maybe use them as test cases. First, the later self problem: when he wants to revoke, is that the same person who signed, or a different one, and does that even matter? Second, the difference between a contract that works by changing his incentives and one that works by changing what counts as a valid decision. The second seems creepier but maybe more effective. Third, the constitutional analogy: constitutions entrench things against future majorities, but they also have amendment procedures and courts. Does that map onto a psychiatric advance directive, or does the analogy break because there's no seperate institution to enforce it? Fourth, his actual contract has a clause where to revoke he has to wait 72 hours, talk to two people who knew him when he was manic, and then re-sign the revocation in front of them. Is that just a trick that presupposes his future self can't be trusted? What would a philosopher say the clause is doing?

I'm not asking you to decide for him. I want the agenda: the two or three questions that, if someone settled them, would most change how these contracts should be written and enforced, and the ones that sound deep but don't actually change anything practical. Also tell me what would change your ranking, like if it turns out the later self's preferences are stable enough, or if advance directives turn out not to improve outcomes. Be concrete and a bit ruthless about what's overrated.
