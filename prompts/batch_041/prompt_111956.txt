I'm TAing an upper-level seminar (mostly third and fourth year philosophy majors, a couple of MA students sitting in) on agency, mechanism, and free will, and I've been handed the final exam to write. The unit that went worst is the one on sphexishness — Hofstadter's term, from the digger wasp story where the wasp drags her paralyzed cricket up to the burrow mouth, goes inside to inspect, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's in there she'll haul it back to the entrance and inspect all over again, apparently forever. Every year the discussion collapses into two camps: half the room says obviously we're not like that, we can notice when we're stuck in a loop, and the other half says but the noticing is also just a mechanism. I want a question that stops them tradeing Hofstadter's anecdote back and forth and forces them onto the actual hard part, which I think is this: any system that can detect and break its own rigid routines has to do the detecting with some routine, so either that routine is itself rigid — the flexibility is bought on credit — or it isn't, and then we still haven't said what isn't-rigid means except driven by a bigger routine. One of my students last week called the second horn second-order sphexishness, being rigid about the very procedure you use to check whether you're rigid, and I want the question to make them decide whether that's a genuine distinction or just the regress in a trenchcoat.

Could you write (a) the exam question itself — it's the essay section, they get about fifty minutes and I'll give them a short crib sheet of the wasp facts, so assume they know those and little else — (b) a model answer of roughly 700 words that a strong student could realistically produce in that time, and (c) a marking scheme. For the scheme I'd like five must-hit points, 20 marks total, split in a way that tracks what's actually hard rather than what's easy to mention, and for each point a one-line note on how students usually get it wrong or half-get it. One thing to build in: I've decided to put in the crib sheet that later field work found the wasp does eventually adapt after enough repetitions, and that the repeated inspection may just be adaptive behavior anyway. I don't want the whole question resting on an ethology anecdote that may be oversold, so the model answer should be explicit about what's left of the concept once you take that away. No citations anywhere, obviously — it's closed-book, no internet, and they'd only be guessing at who said what.
