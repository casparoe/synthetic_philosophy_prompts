In a seminar on criticism I said I had skipped a much-praised film because everyone whose judgment on such things I trust told me it was a mess, so I had good reason to expect I wouldn't enjoy it. A colleague replied that there's no disputing about taste, and added that a verdict on a film is available only to someone who has actually seen it, so nothing I had been told gave me any reason at all. Later in the same session he insisted that one novel is simply better than another and that anyone who disagreed was wrong. Set out the distinct precise claims "there's no disputing about taste" could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the radical — about what aesthetic judgments express, about whether they can be true or false, about what one person can learn about them from another, and about what is and isn't worth arguing over — and say for each whether it is a platitude, defensible, or false. Then tell me which of those readings is strong enough to support his conclusion that my friends' reports gave me no reason to expect to dislike the film, which readings could coexist with his later claim that the dissenters were simply wrong, and which pairs of readings cannot both be true — noting in each case which reading the slogan is actually doing its work for when people reach for it.
