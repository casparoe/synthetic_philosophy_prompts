Two colleagues of mine fell into an argument after a seminar, and I ended up as the only other person at the table. One of them, a historian of nineteenth-century philosophy, had been talking about Henry Sidgwick's long involvement with the Society for Psychical Research — he was its first president, and his circle included figures like Frederic Myers and Edmund Gurney — and she treated it as a straightforward extension of his intellectual character. The other, a contemporary ethicist, thought that reading was too tidy, and what follows is the exchange roughly as it happened.

(1) The historian said that Sidgwick's psychical research is exactly what one would predict from the author of The Methods of Ethics: a man who had resigned a Trinity fellowship rather than subscribe to doctrinal formulas he could not honestly affirm, applying the same evidential discipline to claims about apparitions, telepathy and survival that he applied to moral claims. On her view, the research programme — the census of hallucinations, the attention to testimony, the refusal to let the desire for a result stand in for a result — is continuous with the rest of his work, and the ethics is left untouched by it.

(2) The ethicist said that continuity of method is not the interesting question. The interesting question is why these particular questions and not others. He pointed out that Sidgwick's ethical theory famously ends in a dualism of practical reason: the demands of rational egoism and the demands of universal benevolence are both defensible, and nothing in the Methods shows they must coincide in this life. On his reading, psychical research was where Sidgwick went looking for the missing harmony — for a cosmos in which the two standpoints could be reconciled — and that hope, not the method, explains the shape of the programme. He conceded that Sidgwick's evidential standards were never lowered, but said that being rigorous about the evidence is compatible with having a preferred answer.

(3) She answered that this conflates motive with warrant. Every research programme is started by someone who cares about the answer, and if having a preferred outcome discredited a line of inquiry, we would have to discard most of the history of science. What matters is whether the inquiry was structured so that the preferred answer could fail. She said it plainly could, and did: the results Sidgwick and his colleagues reported were largely negative or inconclusive, and he said so in print.

(4) He replied that this proves less than she thinks. It shows Sidgwick was honest at the point of reporting, not that the questions were framed neutrally from the start. He claimed the framing gives the preference away — why survival, why telepathy, why the specific hope that mind might not be exhausted by the body? He also put a further point, which is the one I found hardest to assess. On Sidgwick's own utilitarianism, he said, the truth of survival would not be practically idle. If consequences extend beyond death, the expected value of our present actions changes, and so does the weight we should give to things we now treat as terminal. So the question cannot be filed away as a private curiosity with no bearing on the ethics.

(5) She said that last move gets Sidgwick backwards. His ethics was built to deliver guidance that holds whether or not we survive, and he was explicit that practical reason does not wait on metaphysical discoveries. The fact that survival would alter a consequentialist calculus is a fact about a calculus he did not take himself to be running on that question. She also said that if anything, the negative outcome is evidence for her reading: a man in search of consolation would have found some.

(6) He said that is a psychological conjecture, not an argument, and that a person can want a conclusion for decades without manufacturing it, and still have wanted it. She said that is true, and that it is also true that wanting it leaves no trace in the argument, which was her original point.

I have no stake in either side and I could not referee this myself. Please adjudicate it move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is right, mistaken, or partly both, and why. I am especially interested in (a) whether the distinction between a research programme having a preferred outcome and that outcome shaping the programme is being used legitimately here, and (b) whether the ethicist's claim about survival changing the utilitarian calculus is a serious point about Sidgwick or a category error about what his ethics was for. Say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and identify which parts of the dispute turn on matters that are settled among people who know the period and which turn on genuinely contested interpretations, so that I am not left thinking a disputed reading is the consensus. Please write in British English.
