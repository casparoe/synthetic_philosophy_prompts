I sit in a joint reading group — half of us from a molecular biology lab, half from a philosophy department — that has spent a semester on a single question: is it literally true that DNA is a code, or are "code," "information," and "instruction" metaphors that do no work and should be retired? We have not moved an inch, and I would like to understand why.

The literalists hold that the genetic code is a code in the same sense that a cipher or a lookup table is. The mapping from codons to amino acids is not fixed by the laws of chemistry — the same codon specifies different residues in different lineages, as in the variant mitochondrial codes — and an arbitrary mapping between two otherwise independent domains is precisely what a code is. The ribosome does not respond to a codon the way a catalyst responds to its substrate; it reads it. On this view the information in a gene is a real feature of the world, and it earns its keep: it explains why the same sequence can be transcribed in one cell and silenced in another, and why a gene can be moved between species at all.

The deflationists reply that every one of those facts survives without the vocabulary. Arbitrariness is just the observation that the chemical details of the mapping are underdetermined by chemistry, which is true of much of chemistry and does not make it semantic; the causal story from DNA to protein can be told entirely in terms of template-directed polymerization and binding affinities, with nothing lost; and the instructions for building an organism are not in the DNA in the first place, since the geometry of the egg, self-assembly, and the rest of the cytoplasm do at least as much of the work.

We then circle the same two moves. Someone offers a test for genuine coding — that a real code admits of misrepresentation, that a message can be wrong about what it is supposed to be about, whereas a molecule cannot be wrong about its own chemistry — and the other side says that a mutation yielding a nonfunctional protein is precisely something going wrong, and that we have merely redescribed error in causal terms. Someone offers the opposite test — that a genuine code needs an interpreter, and the cell contains no interpreter, only more chemistry — and the other side answers that the ribosome is the interpreter, or that demanding a homunculus was always a mistake.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are not disagreeing about the cell but about what a biological explanation is permitted to invoke: whether the claim that the ribosome reads the gene and builds the protein is an explanation or a placeholder, and whether the availability of a lower-level causal story shows that the higher-level one was only a manner of speaking. If that is right, no further molecular detail will settle it, and yet it is not merely verbal either, since the two sides are not using the words differently — they want different things from an account of the same process.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about the words "code" and "information," a substantive disagreement about the right explanatory level in biology, two parties answering different questions under a single heading — one about what makes a description useful, the other about what the world contains — or a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work while nobody notices? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction that disarms one of the recurring moves, a case one side cannot accommodate, a result from molecular biology, or the conclusion that the question was ill-posed and should be replaced by a better one? An argument that no progress should be expected, and why, would be more useful to me than a survey of the positions.
