I'm running a session for our HPS-ish reading group next month — mixed crowd, a couple of economists, some historians, two philosophers, nobody who's done any formal demography — and I've been handed the topic can a theory be right but early. I want to teach one specific idea, not survey the literature: that a theory can stake a claim whose verdict date falls later than the evidence window we actually have, and that we need some principled way to tell that apart from a theory that's just been patched up to survive.

So plan the 75 minutes for me. Don't explain the idea to me, plan how I explain it. What has to be in place before right-but-early stops sounding like special pleading, and what depends on what — I have a hunch they need the core/auxiliary distinction (which bits of Malthus are the claim, which bits are the machinery that generates the actual numbers) before the earliness question even makes sense, but I might have the order wrong. Which single example should carry the whole session, and what does it have to have and lack? I'm torn between Malthus's population-and-subsistence race, the resource-exhaustion predictions in the Limits to Growth report, and the Ehrlich–Simon wager about metals prices — and I suspect teh obvious one is a trap for a reason I can't quite articulate, so tell me which to build on and why not the others.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. My guesses: (a) they'll start from half-remembered Popper and think right-but-early is just wrong-plus-stubborn; (b) they'll say the Green Revolution refuted Malthus, which I take to be a confusion between a check existing and a check biting on time; (c) having seen that, they'll overcorrect and say any theory with a far-off horizon is unfalsifiable; (d) someone will say the rescue is always ad hoc, and after that nobody can tell a good rescue from a bad one. Theres probably a fifth I'm not seeing.

Two things I want to build in and need help placing: a distinction I've been using with them — a slow clock versus a clock that was never set, i.e. a theory that says later versus one that never says when — and the point that vindication doesn't happen to a theory, it happens to a theory plus a decision about which auxiliary you're allowed to relax. Where does each go, and what has to come first for it to land?

Finally, one question to put to them at the end that tells me whether they got the idea or just enjoyed the discussion, and what I should openly tell them I'm leaving out at this level. No reading list, obviously — they'll ask, and I want to be able to say why not.
