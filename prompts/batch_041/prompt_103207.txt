I'm a retired geologist who got into philosophy late, and I've somehow ended up on the advisory board of a small foundation that can fund about three people for two years on conceptual work. We want to put the money into supererogation -- the whole business of acts that are morally good but not required. Which open questions in that area should we prioritize, and why? I don't want a reading list or a survey; I want a defended agenda. I keep getting stuck on a puzzle: if an act is morally better than what's required, why isn't it required? And if it's not required, what makes it better? Take a case: a doctor is the only compatible kidney donor for a stranger and donates. Most people say that's supererogatory, but if the stranger will die otherwise, is it really beyond duty? If yes, what does beyond duty mean when the stakes are life and death? If no, does supererogation just disappear whenever the stakes get high enough? I've been calling it the stakes-collapse worry. I'd like you to use that puzzle, or something like it, to say which questions are upstream of the others -- for instance, whether supererogation is a genuine deontic category or just a way of talking about reasons, whether it can apply to groups, institutions, or AI systems rather than individual humans, and whether the whole idea is coherent given the paradox above. Rank the questions we should fund, say what would move one up or down, and flag which ones are overrated or wouldnt change much downstream.
