I retired three years ago after thirty-five years in a physiology department, most of it on nociception and the descending modulation of pain, and I have spent the time since teaching myself philosophy with more appetite than discipline — a continuing-studies reading group, a stack of paperbacks, and a running argument with the ex-lawyer who sits opposite me. That group has now asked me to lead a single ninety-minute session next month, whiteboard available, no assigned reading, for about twenty adults: retired professionals, two schoolteachers, a nurse, and the lawyer. They are quick and unafraid of abstractions, but they have no philosophical training and very little patience for the appearance of it. The subject I have been handed is the one my friend keeps returning to: whether the badness of suffering and the goodness of pleasure are commensurable, in the sense of lying on a single scale along which any quantity of either can in principle be exchanged for the other, and the position he defends, which I take to be a recognised one. He holds that suffering-avoidance does not merely outweigh pleasure-production but, above some threshold, takes strict priority over it, so that no amount of pleasure, however large, compensates for suffering beyond that threshold, while below the threshold the two may be traded off much as an ordinary utilitarian would trade them. He calls the priority lexical, and the threshold is his own concession to an objection he cannot answer.

I do not want the position explained to me; I can read, and slowly I do. What I want is a plan for teaching it. Tell me the order in which the components have to arrive if the room is not to nod along to a sentence it has not understood — which claim must be in place before the next can even be stated, and where the sequence is tempted to run ahead of itself. Tell me the single example or case that should carry the whole session, what it must contain and what it must be kept free of for the trade-off to be felt rather than merely described; I have been tempted by a comparison of two whole lives, one long and mildly painful and richly enjoyed, the other short, painless and drab, but I suspect that whole lives smuggle in an arithmetic that does the work for me. Tell me which misconceptions this audience will bring or form, and whether my own list is right: that incommensurability means incomparability; that lexical priority is just the assignment of infinite weight to suffering; that if the two are not on a single scale then no comparison between them is possible at all; and — the one that worries me most, given my former trade — that the question is an empirical one about whether the nervous system computes a common currency, so that a sufficiently clever physiologist could settle it. For each, say where in the sequence it should be headed off rather than merely corrected afterwards. Then give me one question to put to the room at the end that would separate someone who has grasped the structure of the claim from someone who has merely acquired a taste for it, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — I assume the population-ethical apparatus and the formal decision-theoretic machinery, but tell me where the line should fall and why. Because you will be reasoning about what a particular room of people will do rather than about a settled question, I would also like you to say how confident you are in the plan and to single out the one point you are least sure of.
