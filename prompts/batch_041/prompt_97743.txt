So I've been chewing on this idea of "free-floating rationales" — I think it's Dennett's phrase — the thought that when you explain why a trait exists (why the digger wasp hauls the paralyzed cricket to the burrow, why your pupil contracts in bright light, why the peacock's tail is that size), you're citing a reason, just not a reason any mind ever entertained. Selection kept the conclusions and threw away the steps, so the design is rational even though no reasoner ran the reasoning. I like it as an anti-mystification move, but I cant tell if it's doing real work or is just a dressed-up way of saying selection favors the fit.

Here's the position I want you to try to break, then: free-floating rationales are real explanations, and "reason" here isn't merely a metaphor — there are genuine norms of correctness about what a trait is for, and they don't live in anyone's head. What would count against that? I don't want a generic "adaptationism is unfalsifiable" grumble; I want the actual defeaters. Does the view secretly need a unique rationale per trait, and if drift, spandrels, or several equally good designs make the rationale indeterminate — or make there be no rationale at all — is that fatal or just a boundary? Does it smuggle a reasoner back in (selection itself as a pseudo-agent, or the theorist's mind as the only place the norm is ever realized)? And is there any principled line between "there's a reason here that nobody has" and just talking loosely? Sort them from what would really sink it to what a defender could shrug off, and tell me which ones the defenders would themselves concede as counting against them. Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no intro and no wrap-up.
