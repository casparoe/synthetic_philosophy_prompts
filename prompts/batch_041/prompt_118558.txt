For several years I have been writing about the philosophical consequences of quantum mechanics, and I keep encountering an argument — a physicist I interview calls it the quantum-suicide argument — that I can neither fault nor accept. He sent it to me in numbered form; I reproduce it below essentially as he wrote it, with a few clarifications. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict of guilty: name the step that should be rejected (or, if none should be, the premise that must be paid for), explain why that step looked compelling in the first place, and say what it costs to give it up. I should record my own preferences: I would like to keep the many-worlds reading of quantum mechanics, and I would rather not have to declare the whole argument incoherent. Beyond that I have no stake in the outcome.

(1) On the many-worlds reading, the wavefunction never collapses: every outcome permitted by the amplitudes is realized in some branch, and no branch is privileged as the one that really occurs.

(2) Nothing further determines which branch's contents become my subsequent experience. Wherever a branch contains a successor of me, that successor is a future me.

(3) I submit to a device that measures a symmetric two-outcome quantum system and kills me in the branch in which one of the two outcomes obtains; in the other branch a successor of mine is unharmed.

(4) Whatever I am later aware of, therefore, will be the awareness of a survivor. After any single trial there is a branch whose occupant remembers having survived it.

(5) Repeat the procedure n times. For each of the n trials there is, by (3) and (4), a branch containing a successor of mine who survives that trial; and by (2) the successor who poses the question once the run is finished is simply whoever occupies such a branch.

(6) Hence there is a branch — and I shall be its occupant — in which I survive all n trials. My survival is assured with probability 1; death is not among the outcomes I should expect to occupy.

(7) If my survival is certain, then no stake is too great. I ought to be willing to risk my life on a fair coin for a trivial reward, and even to accept odds of ninety-nine to one against, since I cannot occupy the losing branch.

(8) That conclusion is absurd. So something above is wrong — but I cannot tell whether the argument contains an invalid step or whether it is valid and one of the premises must simply be abandoned.

Please do not rest with the observation that (7) is absurd; I already know that. Tell me where the reasoning breaks, why the break is easy to miss, and what a repaired version of the argument would and would not still establish.
