Molyneux's question — a man born blind can tell a cube from a sphere by touch; if his sight is restored, can he tell which is which by sight alone, before touching them? — got a no from Locke and Molyneux, a yes from Leibniz (extension is common to both senses), and a stronger no from Berkeley (the two senses share nothing at all).

I've been reading about the modern sight-restoration work, mainly the Project Prakash studies in India where Held, Ostrovsky, Sinha and others tested newly sighted kids and adolescents on cross-modal matching. The headline was that they failed at first and then improved over days. Ostrovsky and colleagues also found that early on the patients couldn't parse static visual scenes at all, and that motion helped. And Sacks wrote about a man who regained sight after decades and found the visual world an unparseable blur.

Analyze this as a case study in both directions. Map the experiments onto Molyneux's question: what would count as a yes or a no, who or what is the subject, what is the test, what is the evidence. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I'm suspicous of a few things. The tests are cross-modal matching tasks, often forced-choice, run after surgery rather than at the instant of first sight; the subjects often had some light perception beforehand; and if visual parsing is itself broken early on, then failing to match seen shapes to felt shapes may not be a fact about cross-modal transfer at all — it may be a fact about not yet being able to see a cube as a cube. Is that a real distinction or a distinction without a difference? Also, the improvement over days is usually read as an empiricist vindication, but does it actually favor Locke over Leibniz, or does it favor a third option — innate cross-modal links that need visual calibration, or a question that was never well-posed because it presupposes a clean split between visual and tactile ideas of shape? And what does the episode show about the theory itself: confirmed, refuted, dissolved, or replaced? Be concrete about which claims the data can and can't support. Don't just give me the history, I know the history — I want the mapping and the breakdown.
