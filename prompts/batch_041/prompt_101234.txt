I'm part of a reading group that has spent the term on algorithmic fairness, and we keep circling the same cluster of impossibility results: the ones showing that, except in degenerate cases, a predictor cannot simultaneously be calibrated within groups, satisfy equalized odds or error-rate balance across groups, and achieve balance for the positive and negative classes. Most of us arrived thinking the lesson was simply that one must pick a value—either you prioritize calibration or you prioritize equalized odds—but one member has been pushing a stranger line. She calls it the fairness lottery: when no deterministic model satisfies all the desiderata, we should not choose one model and defend the trade-off; instead we should define a distribution over the Pareto-optimal models, perhaps one that depends on group membership, and let each individual be scored by a model drawn from that distribution. Her thought is that no one can complain of being assigned to the worse model if the assignment is random, and ex ante the group-level metrics can be made to come out equal. Another member says this is just laundering an unjust choice through a coin flip, and that randomness cannot create a right where none existed.

Write an essay for the group that takes this proposal seriously and reaches a verdict. Weigh whether the impossibility results are best understood as a genuine conceptual impossibility—something about the structure of prediction when base rates differ—or as an artifact of insisting on a particular set of ex ante constraints. Then say whether the fairness lottery is a coherent response, an evasion, or something in between. I don't need a literature review, citations, or a reading list; I want the argument to stand on its own. The essay should end by defending a clear position: either the lottery is defensible, or it is not, or it is defensible only under conditions you specify. Be precise about what exactly is being equalized and what is being randomized, and do not let the conclusion rest on the mere fact that the desiderata cannot all be met at once.
