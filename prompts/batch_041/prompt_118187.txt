The charter of the Kestrel Society consists of three articles and nothing else:

I. Any article of this charter, including this one, may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members present at a sitting.
II. An amendment takes effect at the moment the chair declares the vote on it carried.
III. The chair's declaration of the numbers on a vote is conclusive as to those numbers.

No other instrument governs the Society, and no question of the following kind has ever arisen in it.

At the May sitting a member moves Amendment 7, whose text is: "Article I is replaced by the following: 'No article of this charter may be amended except by a unanimous vote of the members present.' This amendment is valid only if it is adopted by a unanimous vote." The chair declares the motion carried, 68 votes to 32 — above two-thirds, short of unanimous.

Argue that Amendment 7 is void and that the charter stands as it did in April. The argument should rest on a principle general enough to govern any rule-set capable of amending itself, rather than on anything peculiar to the Society or on the constitutional doctrine of any actual state. Address the strongest objection to your verdict, namely that a void amendment has no legal force, so the proviso requiring unanimity never bound anyone, leaving the unamended Article I's two-thirds requirement as the only standard in play, which the vote satisfied. Explain whether a motion may raise the bar for its own adoption while being unable to lower it, and say precisely what Article III does and does not settle. Close by stating what the Society would have to do at its June sitting to put the unanimity requirement into the charter, and why your verdict does not leave the charter unamendable.
