At a dinner thing last week two of my friends — call them Nora and Pete — got into it about pleasure, and I was sat in the middle of it and still can't tell who won. I'll try to give you the actual exchange and not smooth it over.

Nora kicked off saying she'd gotten interested in the "heterogeneity problem" for hedonism — the idea that hedonism says the good is pleasure, which assumes pleasures are all one kind of thing, but when you actually look at them they don't seem to share anything. A hot shower, finally getting warm after being out in the cold, a crossword clicking into place, a chord resolving in a song you love — she said these have nothing in common as feelings. So either hedonism is false or "pleasure" is just a word we slap on a pile of unrelated states.

Pete said that's too quick, because of course the *contents* are different — the smell of coffee and the smell of gasoline are different smells but they're both smells. Same with pleasure: the common thing is the pleasantness, which is the same ingredient wherever it shows up, like the same glaze on different pastries.

Nora said she'd love to find that glaze but she can't, and neither can anyone — you can't describe the common ingredient without just saying "and then it feels good," which is the thing you were supposed to explain. She said "pleasantness" is a label for the fact that we like the state, not a feature inside it.

Pete came back with: fine, then say pleasure is whatever we like, or whatever we're disposed to seek and keep going — that's a real commonality, a functional one, and it's all hedonism needs. The good is still the experience; we're just picking it out by its role.

Nora said that move costs him the thing he was trying to keep. If pleasure is "whatever you're disposed to seek," then you've made it about your relation to the state, not the state's own feel — and that's just desire-satisfaction theory with extra steps. She also said you can want something without liking it (she brought up a smoker she knows who says he doesnt enjoy it anymore, just needs it), so wanting and liking come apart, and pleasure has to be the liking one.

Pete said the smoker case actually helps him: it shows the relevant attitude is liking, not wanting, and liking is just... finding it pleasant. Yeah it's a bit circular, but he said some things are primitive and you can't define them, and that doesn't make them fake — you can't define "red" either, you point at it.

Nora said that's exactly the problem, because if you can't say what the common thing is, you can't check whether it's really common, and then "the good is pleasure" is either empty or unfalsifiable. She also threw in a distinction of her own: she said pleasures of relief (scratching an itch, finally sitting down, getting warm) are just the *removal* of something bad, while pleasures like a good bite of food are something *added*, and she can't see how a removal and an addition could be the same kind of thing.

Pete said she's just redescribing the contents again, and that relief and enjoyment both register as "good" at the moment they happen, and that's the only sameness hedonism ever needed.

That's about where it ended. They both seemed to have a point and I couldnt referee. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what — including whether Nora's relief-vs-addition point does any real damage, and whether Pete's "primitive, like red" defense actually saves him or is a dodge? And tell me if there's a version of hedonism left standing once you grant the heterogeneity, or if Nora's basically right that it collapses into something else.
