I run the philosophy club at my high school and have been handed the last hour of our Thursday meeting block — sixty minutes, fifteen students, mostly sixteen and seventeen, nearly all of whom have taken one history course and none of whom have read any philosophy of history — to run a session on the claim that history is written by the victors. I want an activity, not a lecture, because the claim has always struck me as both obviously true and weirdly slippery: everyone nods along, and then nobody in the room can say what exactly is supposed to be written by whom, or whether the point is about the evidence that survives, the story that gets told, or something deeper. I have been trying to pull apart three things and would like the activity built so that the group runs into the difference between them before anyone names it. First, the plain archival fact that the winning side's papers tend to be kept and the losing side's tend to be lost, burned, or left in a barn. Second, the fact that even where the losing side's papers do survive, the winning side's version is the default one — in textbooks, in the monument in the square, in the speech on the anniversary. Third, the one I cannot get my friends to see at all: that the winning side supplies the vocabulary, so that the argument is not about whether to call it a rebellion or an uprising but about whether "uprising" is the neutral word. That third level does not feel like bias from the inside, which is exactly why I want participants to catch themselves using somebody else's words and then notice what those words already settled for them.

My rough plan, which you should feel free to throw out and replace, is a case I invent myself so that nobody can appeal to anything they already know: a mining dispute in a valley, some time in the nineteenth century, no real place or company. I would hand three or four groups different bundles of fabricated documents — a company ledger with one line reading "paid to constable for special service, 14s"; an editorial in the county paper that refers throughout to "the disturbances in the valley"; a ballad someone collected fifty years later, ending "they hanged the three, and the mine still stands"; a missionary's diary that is sympathetic but confused about who was in charge; a petition with sixty marks instead of signatures. Each group writes a short authoritative account of what happened, for an encyclopedia, and then we compare. My hope is that the accounts differ not only in emphasis but in what they treat as an event at all, and that each group can point to its own documents and feel perfectly entitled. None of us can look anything up during the meeting, and I would rather the activity depend on no real names, dates, or sources whatsoever, so please invent whatever else it needs. What I want handed back is a runnable session: the setup, the document bundles (with the excerpts written out), the roles or rules, the timing, how the accounts get compared without it turning into a shouting match, and then a debrief that maps what happened onto the three levels above, in order, with the vocabulary one saved for last. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I would bet one group decides its bundle is the true one and digs in, and that somebody says "so all history is just made up," which I want handled rather than ignored. I also want the debrief to include the case against the saying, since I do not want to leave fifteen people as relativists: that victors' accounts are often unreliable precisely because they had reasons to lie, that losing sides write plenty and sometimes win the argument a century later, and that some things are settled by evidence no winner controls. Finally, be honest about what an activity like this cannot reproduce — I suspect it cannot show how historians actually widen a thin evidentiary base, and that I will have to supply that part myself, but tell me where else it falls short.
