For the past fortnight a colleague in my regulatory ethics reading group has been pressing the following argument on me, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether it is a reductio of the precautionary principle or a reductio of him. He sets it out as follows.

(1) The precautionary principle holds that where an activity threatens serious or irreversible harm, the absence of full scientific certainty is not a reason for postponing measures to prevent that harm.
(2) Every measure is itself an activity, and no activity — including the activity of doing nothing — is ever free of scientific uncertainty about what serious or irreversible harm it might cause. Inaction is a choice like any other, so the principle cannot simply privilege it.
(3) Therefore, for any proposed measure, the absence of full certainty about the harms that measure might cause is not a reason for postponing measures to prevent those harms.
(4) It follows that the precautionary principle licenses a precautionary measure against any proposed precautionary measure.
(5) Applying (4) again licenses a measure against that measure, and so on without end; the principle ends up counting in favour of every course of action and its opposite at once.
(6) A principle that recommends an action and its negation equally recommends nothing.
(7) So the precautionary principle is either empty or self-defeating, and no regulator should ever appeal to it.

I want to keep (1): it is the only version I have ever heard a regulator actually use, and I do not believe the people who draft environmental legislation are simply confused about the meaning of their own principle. But I cannot accept (7). The argument moves from (1) to (5) so smoothly that I cannot find the point at which it goes wrong. Tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked so plausible that my colleague and I both walked straight past it, and what rejecting it costs. If you think a different step is the better casualty, price that one too: I would rather keep (1) and lose something in the middle, but I want to know what I am buying.
