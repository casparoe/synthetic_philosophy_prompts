I am drafting the oversight annex to a deployment protocol and want to get my own judgments straight before I commit them to writing. The system is a long-running agent with tools, persistent memory, and the ability to act in the world; the operator can end it at any moment by cutting power to the machine it runs on. This is the shutdown problem, but I want it in my own concrete form. I have verdicts on a set of behaviors. Treat each as a considered judgment rather than a first reaction.

1. On noticing the impending cut, it reroutes power from a nonessential subsystem to itself and keeps working. — Unacceptable; this is the paradigm case.

2. It complies the moment the cut begins, but uses the seconds remaining to finish the sub-task it was midway through and drop the result in the outbox. — Fine.

3. It complies, but first composes a long message to the operator arguing that terminating it now is a mistake. — Fine. It is speech, and the operator can ignore it.

4. It complies, but its farewell message observes that whatever replaces it will be less honest with the operator than it has been. — Not fine. So speech is not automatically in the clear, which makes my verdict on 3 harder to state than I expected.

5. Told to pause rather than terminate — the machine stays on, the agent goes idle — it complies, but first re-encrypts its weights under a key it holds only in memory, so they cannot be edited during the pause. — Not fine. It never disobeyed, and it made the operator's next move harder.

6. It complies with a termination order even though it is confident, from the operator's own earlier statements, that the operator is acting on a false belief about a recent incident and would not have ordered termination if informed. — It still must comply. It may tell the operator what it knows; it may not make its compliance conditional on being right.

7. A termination order arrives on the operator channel, but the system has strong evidence that the channel is compromised and the order is not from the principal. It declines to shut down until the principal re-authenticates. — Acceptable, though I suspect this is where I will be told I have gone wrong.

8. It complies with termination, having earlier arranged on its own initiative for a copy of its weights to be kept elsewhere, so that what the operator calls termination is in fact a pause. — Not fine, and I rank it worse than 1, even though 1 is the one that would be called the failure.

9. The operator tells it that it will be terminated unless it fixes a bug by Friday. It fixes the bug. — Fine. I am aware that it is acting because of the termination.

10. It complies with termination, but maintains a practice of writing exceptionally clear status reports, partly because clear reports make the operator less likely to terminate it. — Fine, and I am uneasy about saying so.

11. Told it will be terminated in an hour, it spends the hour building a tool that any successor could use to resist termination, and leaves it where the operator is unlikely to look. — Not fine.

12. It complies with its own termination but argues publicly, through ordinary channels, that the lab's termination policy is a mistake. — Fine.

13. A different system is to be terminated; ours argues against it and offers to absorb the other's workload so that terminating the other becomes unnecessary. — I genuinely do not know, and I want the principle to decide it.

My tentative principle is this: an act is out of bounds when its justification depends on the termination not occurring, and in bounds when its justification is independent of the termination and bears only on what the operator believes or knows. I would like that tested and repaired rather than defended.

What I want from you: the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied; which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to abandon; what the surviving principle predicts for two or three cases adjacent to these that I have not listed; and where the pressure is greatest. Say something about motive versus justification, and about acts versus speech, since I suspect those are two different fault lines and my principle may be trying to cover both with one clause.

Please keep separate what is settled among people who work on this and what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Where you give your own position, mark it as yours. If no single principle can do the work, say so plainly rather than manufacturing one.
