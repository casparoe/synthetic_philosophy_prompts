I was the quiet third party to an argument at my department's reading group last week, and I still cannot tell who came out ahead. The session had been about puzzles in which choice seems to make no difference, and the case that set everything off was the old story of the man who meets Death in the market at Damascus. Death looks startled to see him; the man panics, rides all night to Aleppo, and dies there — only to learn that the appointment had been in Aleppo all along. The formalized version we were given is sharper: you must decide whether to stay in Damascus or leave for Aleppo, you know that Death is a perfect predictor, and you know that the appointment has been fixed in whichever city you will end up choosing. You die either way; the only thing your choice settles is where.

Two members of the group, Ravi and Elena, went back and forth about what this shows. Reporting as faithfully as I can:

Ravi: It is fatalism, made exact. If the appointment is in whichever city I choose, then there is nothing I can do to escape it — stay and I die in Damascus, flee and I die in Aleppo. The two options sit on a par. So deliberation here is a charade, and the feeling that fleeing "does something" is pure illusion, as is the feeling that staying does.

Elena: "There is nothing I can do" is smuggling two questions into one. There is whether I die, and there is where and how. My choice plainly settles the second: if I stay I die in Damascus, if I go I die in Aleppo. So the options are not on a par — they differ in the outcome — and unless I am genuinely indifferent between those two deaths, my deliberation has a point. Indifference would be warranted only if the deaths were equally good, and nothing in the case makes them so.

Ravi: Fine, but that is a footnote. The real lesson is that the standard theory of rational choice comes apart here. Expected utility says: for each act, take the probability of each outcome given the act, weight it by how good the outcome is, and choose the best. Staying yields death with probability one; fleeing yields death with probability one. The theory cannot tell them apart — even though it also promises that a rational agent always has something to do. And it gets worse once I let my own decision count as evidence. The instant I see myself settling on staying, staying becomes evidence that the appointment is in Damascus — so the theory tells me to switch to Aleppo. The instant I settle on fleeing, fleeing becomes evidence that the appointment is in Aleppo — so it tells me to switch back. It orders me to reverse myself forever and never lets me rest. That instability, not fate, is the interesting thing.

Elena: The instability comes entirely from that one move — treating my choice as evidence about where Death is. Drop it and there is no loop. My choosing Damascus does not cause the appointment to be in Damascus; the causal effect of my choice on Death's location is nil. So on the causal reading both acts have exactly the same expected utility, the theory is perfectly stable, and it simply says: take either. The oscillation is a reductio of letting evidence impersonate causation, not a defect in decision theory.

Ravi: Then the causal reading has the mirror-image problem. It says the two acts are exactly equivalent, so it cannot prefer fleeing to staying — even though, in the story, fleeing is the whole difference between dying in Damascus and dying in Aleppo. And it is exploitable. Suppose a bookie who understands the case offers me a side-bet: a dollar for eleven if I survive. A causal agent that will not let its own act be evidence will judge its survival unlikely-but-possible and take the bet; but I survive with probability zero, so the bookie profits every time. A theory that can be drained by anyone who understands the case better than it does is not obviously the one to keep.

Elena: The pump only works if the causal agent is forbidden from noticing the correlation between its own choice and Death's location. But it is not forbidden. It can set its credence in survival to zero — because it can see that Death is in whichever city it chooses — while still refusing to treat the act as a cause of that. Using the correlation to fix your beliefs is not the same as using it to choose. So it declines the sucker bet without any inconsistency, and your objection evaporates.

Ravi came back at this: it only shows the causalist quietly leaning on the very evidential information it disowns — if the correlation is good enough to tell you the bet is worthless, why is it not good enough to tell you which city to prefer? — and they were off again. I could not referee, and I would like to.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each turn, Ravi's and Elena's in order, say whether the point is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why — including any turn where someone is right for a reason they do not give. Then take a definite stand: say which of the two, if either, has the better overall position on what the case actually shows, and name the one thing each of them is missing. Do not lay the options out side by side and leave the choice to me; commit to a verdict and defend it.
