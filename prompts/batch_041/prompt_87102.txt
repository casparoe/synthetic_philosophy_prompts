I'm a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to write a short story that actually has the doomsday argument in it — you know the one, where you're supposed to treat your own birth rank as a random draw from the pool of all humans who will ever live, and since you turned out to be somewhere in the first hundred billion or so, the total is probably not astronomically large. (I think a physicist came up with it in the 80s, I can't remember the name, and please don't bother citing anything — I have no way to check references and I'd definately rather you didn't invent them.) Every draft I write collapses into two characters explaining a probability argument at each other, which is boring. So: take on the role of a philosopher who is also a genuinely good fiction writer, someone in the Ted Chiang zone where the idea is load-bearing but the people feel real, think carefully about what you're doing, and then write the story. Setting seeds, since I know blank pages are rough: mid-sized city, present day or fifteen minutes into the future; protagonist is a woman in her mid-thirties who does demand forecasting for a regional grocery chain, so she's already the person at work who tells people their forecast is a story they're telling themselves; she runs into the argument sideways — maybe in a comment thread about a Mars colony, maybe a blog post about AI timelines — and it gets its hooks into her in a way that starts leaking into actual decisions (a relationship, a mortgage, whether to keep grinding on a project she cares about).

Here's the part I actually care about. I don't want a dramatized encyclopedia entry, I want the story to do the work the argument does, and there are two spots where I got stuck when I tried to reconstruct it myself, so I'd love the story to turn on them. First, the reference class: are we counting all humans who will ever live, all human-years, all observer-moments — which would sweep in animals, and maybe digital minds later, which is a wild thought — or something narrower? It seems like you can get wildly different answers depending on where you draw that line, and nobody in the versions I've read seems to argue for their line rather than just assuming it. Second, the prior: I'm fairly sure the whole thing only bites if you start out treating humans ending after ~200 billion of us and humans ending after 200 trillion of us as roughly equally likely, and if you instead use something that falls off like one over N, the argument mostly evaporates — which feels like an awful lot to hang on a choice nobody can really justify. Have a character raise that objection and let the other character push back without being a strawman; the pushback can win, lose, or just muddy things, I don't want a dummy. Ground rules: no lecture scenes (if someone states the argument, keep it to a sentence or two in their own voice), no names of real living researchers, no citations, and don't end on a verdict where the story tells me whether the argument is right — end on something a person would plausibly actually do.

Before you start drafting, spend a few lines brainstorming four or five concrete ways this belief could realistically change someone's week rather than just their mood, pick the one with the most friction in it, and say why you picked it. Then write around 1500-2000 words. After the story, add a short bullet list of four to six lines naming the philosophical moves you snuck in, so I can check I actually got what I paid for. Tone-wise I'd rather it be dry and a little sad than twisty — no and-then-she-realized-the-aliens-were-watching stuff.
