Ok, so I've got about 18 months of funded PhD time left (epistemology, some formal stuff) and a co-author who works in social epistemology, and I'm trying to decide where to actually dig in on the peer disagreement debate. Our shared motivation is institutional rather than abstract: we care about how something like an expert advisory panel, a forecasting outfit, or a scientific consensus process should handle internal disagreement, not just about the tidy two-philosophers-one-proposition case. So: what are the open questions in the concilliationism vs steadfastness fight that are most worth working on, and in what order would you put them? Defend the ordering -- which are upstream of which, which are tractable with the tools the field already has, which are popular but wouldn't change much downstream if they got settled, and what over the next year or two would move a question up or down.

Three things I want you to fold in, because I suspect they're where the real action is and I don't see them getting alot of play:

1. Nearly every case in the literature is two-party, one-shot, and about a proposition. Real institutions have many disagreeing parties, and if everyone gets equal weight then it looks like the view just tells you to drift toward the crowd's average. Does the two-party intuition survive aggregation, or does equal weighting a hundred peers collapse into defer to the mean, and if so what's left of the position?

2. What's the evidential weight of disagreement you saw coming? If my model of you predicted your dissent before you gave it, is your dissent still evidence that I'm wrong, or have I already eaten it? Intuitively the surprise is doing the work -- but then how much disagreement counts for depends on my prior model of my peers, which seems like a strange place for the epistemology to bottom out.

3. Group-vs-group disagreement: does it reduce to person-vs-person with the group's opinion plugged in, or does the fact that each side is itself an aggregate with its own internal disagreement change the peerhood and independence math?

Also: is there a version of this debate that's about what to do rather than what to believe, and does anything in the belief case carry over cleanly to action, or does the practical case need its own treatment? I'd rather come out of this with a defended agenda than a reading list.
