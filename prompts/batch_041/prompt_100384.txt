I'm a third-year philosophy student, and this term I'm taking a course on epistemology and rational choice; we have just finished the unit on Bayesian confirmation, in which the lecturer presented Dutch book arguments as the canonical justification for probabilism. She circulated a one-page handout summarising the argument, and I have been trying to work out how much of it I should actually believe. The handout runs roughly as follows. A Dutch book is a collection of bets that leaves the agent poorer whatever happens. If an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability calculus — the handout's example is a person who thinks a coin is 0.6 likely to land heads and 0.6 likely to land tails — then a bookmaker who knows her credences can offer her a set of bets, each of which she will regard as fair or favourable, that together guarantee her a loss. Since no rational agent would accept a set of bets that she can see guarantees a loss, her credences must have been incoherent, so rationality requires degrees of belief to obey the probability axioms. The handout observes that the result is standardly credited to Ramsey and de Finetti, and then draws two further conclusions. First, it says this is the foundation of the subjective Bayesian picture on which there are no objective probabilities, only degrees of belief. Second, it says that the same style of argument extends to learning — an agent who fails to conditionalise on her evidence can also be Dutch-booked — so that the Dutch book arguments together yield a complete theory of rational credence: credences must be probabilistic, and they must be updated by conditionalisation.

A friend in the class who has read more of this literature than I have says the handout gets the theorem right and the philosophy wrong, and I cannot tell whether that is a fair charge or just the sort of thing people say when they prefer a different framework. What I would like is an audit of the handout, claim by claim: which parts a careful philosopher of probability would accept as stated, which are legitimate simplifications that a specialist would merely wince at, and which are actually mistaken or else conflate two things that ought to be kept apart. In particular, I want to know whether the two further conclusions really follow from anything true in the earlier part — does the Dutch book theorem by itself bear on whether there are objective probabilities, and is the extension to learning genuinely the same theorem, or a distinct argument with extra assumptions that might be contested? I am also curious whether the betting framing is doing any illicit work, since it seems to me that one could be incoherent without ever being willing to bet, and whether anything changes when the agent's credences are defined over an infinite space rather than a finite partition. My friend mentioned in passing that there are "depragmatised" versions of the argument that avoid talking about betting altogether, and that there is a separate Dutch book against agents who violate countable additivity; I do not know what to make of either remark, so if they are relevant I would be glad to hear how. Please do not bother with references or a reading list — I will track those down myself once I understand what the issues are — and do not feel obliged to be diplomatic about the handout.
