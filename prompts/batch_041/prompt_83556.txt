Some years ago, at a long dinner in a rented house by the sea, I listened to two friends dismantle the old quarrel between Mencius and Xunzi, and I have never been able to settle it in my own mind. I was there as a novelist, quietly taking notes for a book about how a person comes to be good, or fails to, and I have kept the exchange close to verbatim because it seemed to bear directly on what I was writing.

Ravi, who had spent years with the early Chinese texts, opened by saying the famous opposition is oversold. Mencius never claimed we are born finished, he said; he claimed only that we are born with the beginnings of compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong, and that these are as tender as seedlings. The whole point of his farmer from Song, who tugged at his shoots to hurry them along and killed them, is that growth is natural but still demands patient work. And Xunzi, Ravi went on, never denied that we have the raw equipment for morality; he denied only that it arrives already formed. So the two agree that virtue must be cultivated, and the quarrel is about the seed, not the garden.

Nadia disagreed at once. That, she said, understates the gulf. For Mencius the moral order is something we find: it is already written into what we are, and our task is chiefly to keep from ruining it. For Xunzi the moral order is something we make. He likens our nature to warped wood that grows straight only when forced against the press-frame and steamed, and to a dull blade that cuts only once it has been ground. On that picture morality is not in us at all; it is the deliberate handiwork of the sages, and our task is to build it. Finding and making are different enterprises.

Ravi came back that this is a distinction without a difference. Even Xunzi's craftsman needs material that will take the shape — you cannot straighten water — and even Mencius's gardener is doing work: clearing, watering, weeding. If the made-versus-found slogan has any bite, he said, it ought to show up somewhere. It ought to change what we say about a person who turns out badly, or about whether we may trust our own first impulses. He did not think it did.

Nadia said it shows up precisely there. For Mencius, a cruel person is a damaged person, someone whose shoots were trampled, to be pitied and repaired, and our own first stirrings are a guide we may trust if we attend to them. For Xunzi, a cruel person is simply one who has followed nature where it leads, and our first impulses are exactly what we must distrust; the good life is a construction raised against ourselves. If you want to know which of them is right, she said, ask what moral education is: watering a plant, or building a house out of raw lumber.

Neither would move, and the table broke up without a verdict. I would like to know how the argument actually stands. Please go through it move by move — Ravi's opening, Nadia's reply, Ravi's counter, Nadia's last point — and say who is right about what, where each of them overstates, and what both are leaving out. I am not asking you to crown Mencius or Xunzi in the abstract; I want to know which of my friends got the better of which specific point, and what a fair observer would say about each step.
