There's this puzzle I keep chewing on, I've seen it called the absent-minded driver problem — some game theorists cooked it up, don't ask me for the citation because I'll get it wrong. The setup, roughly: you're driving down a long road and there are two intersections. Turn off at the first and you get nothing, call it 0. Turn off at the second and you get something great, call it 4. Drive past both and you end up somewhere mediocre, 1. The twist is that the two intersections look identical and you're abesent-minded, so when you arrive at one you have no idea whether it's the first or the second. Whatever you decide to do at an intersection, you'd have decided the same thing at the other one — you can't tell them apart, and you can't carry out "stay on at the first, turn off at the second" because that requires knowing which one you're at. I'm fairly sure the best you can actually do is turn off with probability two-thirds at each intersection, which gets you 4/3 — better than always staying on (1) and better than always turning off (0) — but check my arithmetic, I might be wrong, and if I am, say so and fix it.

Here's what I want: the relevant section of a state DMV driver's handbook, something like "Section 9: Driving in Low-Recall Conditions," that teaches people how to handle this stretch of road. Deadpan bureaucratic register, like a real manual — numbered subsections, a "common mistakes" box, a warning label, a mandatory disclosure, a short FAQ. Around 700 words.

The comedy has to come from the decision theory being right, not from wacky jokes. So the manual has to be straight about the fact that the optimal plan — turn off at the second, not the first — is one no driver can carry out; that the best implementable policy makes you do the locally worst thing at each intersection some of the time (the mix has you turn off at the first a third of the time, which is the worst outcome available there, and stay on at the second two-thirds of the time, which is the worst outcome available there); that a driver who can commit before getting in the car does better than one who can't, which gives the department an obvious commercial interest in commitment devices; and that "the policy is optimal" and "this individual decision looks wrong from the inside" are two different claims.
