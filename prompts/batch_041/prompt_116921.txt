I am a novelist, and I have spent the last year reading about the making of the Oxford English Dictionary: the Philological Society's proposal, the appeal to the reading public, the thousands of volunteers who scanned books and sent in quotation slips, the small central staff who sorted and integrated them, and the decades of editorial labour that turned a mountain of slips into a dictionary. It has struck me as an almost laboratory-perfect case of what the AI-safety literature calls HCH, or the human computation hierarchy: a single overseer poses a question, the question is recursively decomposed into smaller tasks, each task is handed to a less capable or more narrowly scoped agent that does not see the whole, and the answers are aggregated back up. Factored cognition is the programme of trying to realise that picture in practice.

I want you to use the OED as a test case for the idealisation. Begin by mapping the episode onto the HCH picture in a disciplined way. Who or what plays the role of the overseer? What are the subtasks, and what makes them subtasks rather than independent projects? What is the aggregation step, and who performs it? What is the top-level question that is supposedly being answered, and is it stable across the decades?

Then turn to the places where the mapping strains, and be specific rather than gesturing at complexity. I am particularly interested in four things. First, the idealisation assumes that subtasks can be made sufficiently context-free; but the OED's readers had to know what to look for, and what they were told to look for changed as the editors' understanding changed. Does that undermine the picture, or can it be absorbed? Second, the idealisation treats aggregation as a synthesis of answers; in the OED the aggregator was not a summing device but a locus of judgement, and the dictionary's most distinctive features — its historical method, its choice of which writers to quote, its sense divisions — seem to be editorial achievements rather than emergent sums. What does that do to the claim that the cognition is factored? Third, the idealisation assumes a fixed top-level question; but the OED's question drifted, from a supplement to existing dictionaries to a wholly new dictionary on historical principles. Does the drift matter for the idealisation, or is it a feature of any long deliberation? Fourth, the idealisation assumes that the agents doing the subtasks are copies of the overseer, or at least share its values; the OED's volunteers were neither equally capable nor uniformly motivated, and some of the most valuable contributions came from people whose relationship to the project was idiosyncratic. What does that reveal about the role of value alignment in the picture?

Finally, reverse the lens. Does the OED case support the claim that factored cognition can scale deliberation, or does it suggest that the idealisation quietly presupposes a non-factored integrator whose judgement is the real locus of the cognitive achievement? If the latter, what follows for HCH as an idealisation of deliberation — is it an illuminating simplification, a misleading one, or a description of a special case that happens to be rare? Where is the idealisation silent, and what would a better idealisation look like?

Please write in British English, and do not worry about citations or reading lists; I want your reasoning, not a bibliography. Where a factual detail is uncertain, say so and set it aside rather than guessing.
