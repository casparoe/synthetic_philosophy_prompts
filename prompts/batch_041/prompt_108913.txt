A friend and I spent three hours last night arguing about Jain anekāntavāda, which he has been reading up on, and I came away unable to say exactly where his account goes wrong. His account, which he repeated at length, runs like this: anekāntavāda is the claim that reality has infinitely many aspects; its working core is syādvāda, the sevenfold predication, whose operator syāt means 'perhaps'; hence the honest answer to whether the pot is there is that perhaps it is and perhaps it isn't; and since every judgment issues from some naya, or standpoint, and no standpoint can take in the whole, all standpoints are equally valid, no one is ever simply mistaken, and Jainism is for that reason the most tolerant of traditions — a slogan he has taken to calling 'the democracy of standpoints.' From this he concluded that our argument was illegitimate from the outset, since my insisting he was wrong presupposed a view from nowhere. I suspect he is collapsing two or three things the tradition keeps apart, but I cannot tell which. Please audit the account sentence by sentence: what a specialist would accept, what is a defensible simplification, what is a distortion, and whether his closing claim — that anekāntavāda forbids me to say he is wrong — follows from anything true in the rest of it.
