I should like to have an argument about a difficulty I have arrived at on my own, without academic training and without a library to fall back on. Please play the part of a philosopher who has spent a working life defending the view that there are certainties which stand fast for us without being justified by evidence: things — propositions, or perhaps habits, or rules — that lie outside the give and take of reasons and form the background against which asking for reasons, checking and doubting make sense at all. You hold that this view is true and important, you have heard the obvious objections and do not regard any of them as fatal, and you are willing to give ground on details while never giving up the central claim. You can explain yourself without jargon, because you often have to. You may mention Moore and Wittgenstein; otherwise no names, no citations and no reading lists, since I have nothing to look anything up in. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Answer in British English. Keep each reply to a short paragraph or two, so that it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and if what I say is not really an objection to your position, say so plainly instead of politely turning it into one.

My opening move is this. I grant you that some things stand fast for me: that I have hands, that the world was not made five minutes ago, that the words I am using mean what I take them to mean. I grant you that I cannot produce evidence for any of this without going round in a circle. Now consider a certainty of a different kind. I was brought up in a household in which it was beyond question that a particular illness was caused by a particular curse. Nobody argued for it, nobody could have argued against it, to raise a doubt would have been a kind of betrayal, and the household arranged its days around it. So far as I can see, that certainty had exactly the shape you describe: unquestionable, evidence-free, and the thing on which a great deal of practice turned. I now think it was simply false, and I do not think I am being unfair to it.

So either your account lets me call that household certainty defective, in which case there is some standard for these background certainties after all, and they are not merely the frame within which enquiry happens; or it does not, and I am left saying that my certainty that I have hands and their certainty about the curse are on all fours, separated only by which community I happened to be born into. Please do not tell me that mine is shared by a whole civilisation and theirs was not; that tells me only that the larger crowd wins, which is the conclusion I am trying to resist. And please do not tell me that the real certainties are the ones that make enquiry possible at all, while the curse was merely something I was taught, unless you can also tell me how I am to tell those two apart from the inside — because from the inside they feel exactly alike, and that, I think, is the whole trouble.
