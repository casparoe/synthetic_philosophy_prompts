Hey — I write the science-and-ideas column for a small-city alt-weekly, and once a year the opinion editor ropes me in to doing one "fun" piece for the holiday dead zone. This year I've actually got an idea I like and I want you to draft it.

The concept: a deadpan op-ed, maybe 650 words, in the voice of the citys brand-new Commissioner of Public Spectacles — a guy who has just announced that we're cancelling the performing-arts center that's half built and redirecting the money into a permanent program of free outdoor festivals. He is completely sincere and not at all in on the joke. And the joke is that he has reinvented, argument for argument, Rousseau's attack on the theater — the one he wrote in 1758, when an encyclopedia article had politely suggested that his hometown might be nicer with a playhouse. Our commissioner has never read it and wouldn't care if he had.

So the comedy has to come from getting the arguments right, not from him just muttering "theater bad." Please have him make at least these moves, in his own bureaucratic voice:

- The actor's whole job is pretending to be someone he isn't. You can't build a temple to the art of convincingly faking a soul and then act surprised when the citizenry gets loose with the truth.
- The theater flatters its audience instead of correcting it. Give him the classic example: in Molière's Misanthrope the bluntly honest man is the one everybody laughs at and the smooth cynic walks away looking reasonable, so people leave feeling that sincerity is a little gauche.
- The emotions the theater stirs are real but sterile — you weep at the tragedy, and the weeping is the whole thing; nothing you felt in the dark ever has to be spent in daylight.
- His positive vision: festivals with no stage and no seats, because there's no audience left to be passive once everyone is standing in the middle of it. Dancing in the square, competitions, a bonfire. Contrast that with a room where you pay to sit quietly in the dark and watch professionals.
- And he should have to handle the obvious embarassment that Rousseau himself wrote plays and an opera, plus the fact that the article he was answering had actually been pretty warm toward Geneva and only floated the theater as a mild suggestion. Let him wave both away in a way that's funny and a little telling, not a cop-out.

The piece should still work for a reader who's never heard of Rousseau — they should clock that this is a crank with a coherent, faintly sinister program. Let his logic run one or two steps further than a normal person would, to somewhere a reader might actually shiver, and end on a line he clearly thinks is inspiring. No winking, no asides to the reader, no "as the great philosopher said." I'm a reporter, not a philosopher, so take the framing as given and run with it.
