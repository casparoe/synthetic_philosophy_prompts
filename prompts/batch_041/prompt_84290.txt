I am a novelist, and I would like your help with an argument I watched unfold at my own dinner table. Please answer as an expert in philosophical aesthetics and the philosophy of mind, with a specialist's command of the problem of our emotional engagement with fiction. Think carefully and slowly, and begin by restating the positions in your own words before you evaluate anything. I want a verdict on these particular arguments, not a survey of received opinion. Where you lean on an empirical claim about how minds actually work, flag it as an assumption rather than a finding.

The occasion: I had read aloud the chapter in which a character I invented dies. I admitted I had wept at my desk while drafting it, and that a reader had written to say she had rebooked a flight, at a real cost, after reading my plane-crash chapter. Two friends then argued about what had actually happened to us.

(1) Nadia, who teaches philosophy of mind, said nothing had. To fear or to grieve, she said, is to be in a state that takes something you take to exist as its object -- a state that answers to the world, that can be right or wrong, that presses on what you do. Toward a person who never existed nothing is at stake, so nothing of that kind can be occurring. What we do instead is make-believe: we join a game in which the character is real and grief is prescribed, and the tears are real while the grief is fictional. She proposes to call the state a quasi-emotion. Her evidence: I could stop whenever I chose, I checked the clock, I held no funeral, and I slept fine.

(2) Ivo, a screenwriter, said the belief requirement is simply false. Fear needs an apparent threat, not a believed one -- you flinch at a shape you have already identified as a coat on a chair. Grief needs a loss represented to you, not a loss believed in. On his account, what moves us is content we entertain, endorsed or not, and my tears at my desk were as real as any widow's. If belief were required, he added, we could not be moved by a possibility we are certain will not come about, nor by a memory we know to be only a memory.

(3) Nadia replied that he had confused the emotion with its cause. In every case he cited, what does the work is a belief about how things are or might be: the flinch rests on the belief that the shape might be something, the remembered grief on the belief that the loss occurred. She added that his account cannot distinguish being moved by a story from being moved by a bare supposition, and cannot explain the safety of fiction -- the fact that we seek out fictional suffering precisely because it does not cost us anything, and can even enjoy it.

(4) Ivo answered that the safety lies in the world, not in the state: what makes it safe is that no one is really hurt, not that my state is of a lesser kind. Then his test case. My reader paid a fee and rearranged her week because of a fear whose object was a fiction. And Nadia's own case: she once told me she sobbed over a character she had written and then cut before shooting -- a character who was never in any finished story and never will be. There is no game of make-believe to be played with someone who is in no fiction. If a state does that much work in a life, he said, calling it quasi- is a demotion in name only.

(5) Nadia granted the action test proves less than he thinks: children playing the-floor-is-lava leap onto the sofa, and the leaping is real, the reason fictional, and nobody calls it a real fear of real lava. She said my reader acted on a make-believe reason and merely paid a real price. She held her line on the cut character -- a fiction he still entertains in his head is still a game, even with one player -- but conceded that the case troubles her, because her account seems to need a text or an audience, and here there is neither. Ivo, for his part, conceded that he cannot say why we go looking for the grief in the first place.

I stayed out of it, and I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it. Please adjudicate move by move. Rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and identify any point where the two are talking past each other. Then tell me what both are missing; which of the three claims -- that we are genuinely moved, that we know these people never existed, and that genuine emotion requires belief -- should be surrendered, and what follows if it is; and whether any part of this dispute is merely verbal, turning on what we choose to count as real. Treat my own case, the weeping author who invented the person she mourns, and Ivo's cut character as the test cases: say what each account must say about them, and whether what it must say is credible. End with a verdict on who has the better of it overall, and name the single strongest point on the losing side.
