I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and for the past year I have watched two colleagues argue past each other about Williams's integrity objection without either of them shifting a millimetre. One is a Williams-style critic of impartialist ethics—call her the integrity theorist—who holds that a theory which requires the agent, at the moment of action, to consult a principle in order to license saving his own wife has already ruined the act, and that the deeper complaint is that impartial morality alienates agents from their ground projects. The other is an impartialist—call him the systematizer—who grants that the wife's claim is weighty but denies that the theory need figure as an occurrent step in deliberation, and who suspects the integrity theorist of smuggling in a psychological picture that no impartialist is obliged to hold.

I want the document they should have written instead of continuing to argue. Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two positions on the integrity objection and the "one thought too many" complaint. It should be a working document, not an essay: dense, unsentimental, and written so that each side can sign it without having conceded anything it does not actually believe.

Include the following.

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered. These should cover what both accept about the case itself (a man on a pier, two people drowning, time to save only one, he saves his wife); about what the case is and is not evidence for; about the distinction between a theory of justification and a theory of deliberation; about what neither party is committed to; and about what would count as a decisive objection to the integrity objection as opposed to a mere restatement of it.

2. The disagreements, each formulated in wording its own side would accept as its own view—not a strawman, and not a bland third-person paraphrase that both would disown. For each, state the exact point of divergence.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I expect at least these three, though you may add others: whether the objection concerns occurrent thoughts or the reasons a theory recognises; whether integrity is a value commensurable with impartial moral demands or rather a precondition of there being an agent who can be addressed by them at all; and whether the case has any force against a theory whose holder's own ground project is the impartial project itself.

4. For each crux, a case, argument, or construction that both sides agree in advance would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down. Both parties must accept the test as probative rather than question-begging; if no such test exists for a crux, say so and explain why.

5. Some cases that neither side may treat as already settled. Do not recycle the usual illustrations. Use at least these, and one of your own devising: the coached rescuer, an agent trained so that the licensing thought occurs to him smoothly and would have made no difference to what he did—ask whether the objection turns on the presence of the thought or on its causal role; and the converted impartialist, an agent whose ground project is impartiality itself. Your own case must be constructed so as to isolate whether the complaint is about the reason or about the reasoning.

6. A closing section separating the verbal parts of the dispute—those turning on what is to count as a thought, a reason, or integrity—from the substantive residue, and stating, for the substantive residue, what each side would have to concede in order to be moved.

The statement must be self-contained: no citations, no reading list, and no appeals to authority. Every distinction it relies on has to be introduced and explained within the document, on the assumption that the reader has not read the surrounding literature.
