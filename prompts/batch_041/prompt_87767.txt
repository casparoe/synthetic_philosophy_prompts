I've been trying to write up my own version of epiphenomenalism for a paper and I want a proper audit before I start, because I have a bad habbit of assembling views that each sound reasonable on their own and then discovering they can't all be true. Background that's probably irrelevant: I'm a historian of philosophy by training who drifted into teaching mind and metaphysics, so I'm definately not a card-carrying philosopher of mind, and most of this comes from arguments I've had in the pub after seminar rather than from any systematic reading. Anyway, here's the package as it currently stands. (1) Physicalism of the boring token-identity sort: every phenomenal event is one and the same event as some brain event, no ghost stuff. (2) Qualia are epiphenomenal in the strict sense — brain events cause qualia, qualia cause nothing, not other qualia, not beliefs, not behaviour. I like the image of the quale as a read-out on a machine that nothing else in the machine can see. (3) Introspection is a reliable if fallible source of knowledge about my current experience: when I judge that this red is a bit orange, I'm usually right about the orange-ishness, and it would be wrong to treat such judgments as mere guesses. (4) The content of a phenomenal judgment is fixed by the quale it's about rather than by the brain state that produces it — the orange-ish quale is what makes my thought have the content it has. (5) Belief content does real causal work; thoughts about red stop me at traffic lights, and so on. I don't want to be an epiphenomenalist about propositional attitudes, only about the feels. (6) Zombies are conceivable but not possible; conceivability is a useful but leaky guide to possibility. (7) On evolution, I think the selection pressure fell on the brain states that produce both the quale and the report, so I lose no sleep over the worry that evolution wouldn't bother producing something inert.

My nagging worry is the old puzzle about views like this — how can I know anything about my qualia, or even mean anything by my reports of them, if the qualia do no work — and I can't tell whether (3) and (4) actually answer it or whether I've just moved the bump under the rug. My friends are no help because they all diagnose it differently: one says (2) and (3) are flatly incompatible and that's the end of it, another insists the real collision is between (2) and (4), and a third says (6) and (7) already do the work and I should stop fussing. What I want from you is an audit rather than a verdict from on high: which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict and why, and for the genuine clashes, which single commitment I'd have to drop or weaken and what that would cost me elsewhere. Please rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, say explicitly if any of my friends is right and why the other two are wrong, and don't send me off to read anybody — I need the argument itself, not a reading list.
