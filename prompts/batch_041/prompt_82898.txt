I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've ended up in a running argument with a friend in my reading group that I'd like to settle properly rather than by volume. The argument is about the proposal, which I've seen called the fitting-attitude analysis of value, that 'valuable' is best understood as 'what it is fitting, correct, or appropriate to value or desire' — so that a thing's being good simply consists in the fittingness of the pro-attitude directed at it. I understand the standard objection: a demon threatens to torment me unless I admire him, which gives me a reason to admire him, but plainly does not make him admirable. My dissatisfaction with this objection is that it is rigged in the objector's favour. The reason it supplies is so transparently self-interested, and so transparently about the consequences of the attitude rather than about its object, that the defender of the analysis can carve it off with a rule that says 'count only the reasons that bear on the object' and move on unscathed. It's like a unit test that passes for the wrong reason: the implementation special-cases it and everyone declares victory.

What I want from you is a thought experiment built to specification, so that no such special-casing is available. The specifications are these. (i) The person who holds the attitude must face no threat, bribe, or consequence of any kind — nothing gets better or worse for them either way, including through their relationships, so that no appeal to self-interest is possible. (ii) The consideration that tells in favour of the attitude must nevertheless be a genuine reason, of a sort a thoughtful person would take seriously, and it must arise from something other than the merits of the object; the most promising route I can see is that the attitude's presence or absence will have consequences for some third party, but if you can construct the case on a different basis that still satisfies (i) and (ii), do so. (iii) The consideration must not be one that the defender can dismiss as outweighed or defeated by the object's real merits: either make it the only reason in play, or make it point in the same direction as those merits so that counting it inflates the analysis's verdict. (iv) The whole thing must be ordinary — a workplace, a friendship, a family, a hobby club; no demons, no science fiction, no thought-control, no brain implants — and no longer than two paragraphs. There is one trap I keep falling into myself and would like you to avoid explicitly: the third party's reaction must not carry any information about the object, because if it does the reason quietly becomes evidence about the object's merits and the case collapses into an uninteresting one; build the story so that it is unmistakable that the reaction is a fact about the third party rather than a sign about the object. And please do not decide what the verdict ought to be and then dress a story around it — the verdict has to fall out of the analysis's own machinery, or the case is worthless to me.

Then do the audit. Say precisely which attitude is at issue, what the fitting-attitude analysis must say about the object's value once your consideration is counted, and what the correct verdict about that value is; walk through why the analysis cannot escape by redescribing your reason as a reason to want to have the attitude, or to bring it about that one has it, rather than a reason to have it. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what becomes of the case when that feature is removed — in particular, whether removing it hands the defender back the escape hatch that the demon case leaves open. Finally, argue the other side: give the strongest reply available to someone who defends the analysis, which I expect will be some version of 'the reasons of the right kind are simply the ones that bear on the object, and that can be said without circularity', and tell me honestly whether that reply survives contact with your case or whether the case is only ever as strong as my stipulation that the third party's distress is non-evidential.
