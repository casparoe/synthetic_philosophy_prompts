I have been trying to make progress on a dispute that keeps recurring in my ethics reading group, and it has the shape of a stalemate. The question is whether there is any moral difference between lying and merely misleading — between asserting something false and arranging the truth so that someone draws a false conclusion. One faction holds that the distinction is real and morally weighty: a lie conscripts the hearer's trust in the speaker's assertion, whereas a misleading utterance leaves the hearer's own inference intact and merely exploits it, and that difference matters. The other faction holds that this is a distinction without a moral difference: in both cases the speaker intends to produce a false belief, the causal route through the hearer's mind is the same in all the ways that could matter, and any felt difference is an artefact of our squeamishness about literal falsehood. Both sides agree on the cases; they disagree about what the cases show.

I have a hypothesis about why they never move, and I would like you to test it rather than simply confirm it. My suspicion is that the argument is really two arguments wearing one coat: one side is answering a question in the philosophy of language — what does the word 'lying' pick out, and does it require an intention to deceive — while the other is answering a question in ethics — what makes a deceptive act wrong — and the two questions are being treated as one because the same examples serve both. If that is right, then some of the heat is verbal and some of it is a genuine disagreement about the wrong-making feature.

But I want you to consider a complication that I think both factions tend to ignore. There is a family of cases — the literal truth deployed precisely so that the hearer will infer a falsehood, the truth told as a trap — where the utterance is true and yet the speaker is doing, by design, exactly what the liar does. And there is the reverse family, where a false statement is uttered with no intention to deceive at all: someone recites a falsehood he believes, or states a falsehood to a hearer he knows disbelieves him. Lay these two families across the two factions' positions and tell me whether the 'verbal versus substantive' diagnosis survives, or whether the real fault line lies somewhere else entirely — for instance between those who locate the wrong in the violation of a convention of truthfulness and those who locate it in the manipulation of another's epistemic agency.

So: diagnose the disagreement, not the morality of lying. Tell me what kind of disagreement it is, what each side is actually committed to, whether my two-questions hypothesis is right or whether the impasse is instead a genuine clash of bedrock intuitions, and what would count as progress — a distinction, a counterexample, or a decision to stop using one word for two things. Where you are unsure, say so: give me your confidence in the diagnosis as a whole, and single out the claim within it that you are least sure of. Please answer in British English.
