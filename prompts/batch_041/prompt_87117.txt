I am a philosopher at work on a paper about what I have been calling nuisance-level losses — losses so slight that, taken one at a time, no sensible person would think twice about them — and I have lost confidence that I know what principle my own verdicts on them are tracking. I suspect they cannot all be right. What I want from you is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that best captures them; a statement of it precise enough to apply to a case neither of us has imagined; its verdicts on cases I have not considered; and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts I have to give up.

My verdicts.

C1 (the queue). A ticketing site's redesign adds forty seconds to every checkout, thirty million times a year — on the order of thirty-eight years of accumulated waiting. Nobody enjoys the wait, nobody will remember it, nobody's plans are disturbed. My verdict: this is not a reason against the redesign at all. I would not enter it into a cost-benefit analysis.

C2 (the itch). To spare one person a week of unremitting pain, I would have to give each of a billion people one second of mild itching. My verdict: I would not do it. Nor for a trillion people, nor for any number you like. No quantity of one-second itches adds up to a week of agony.

C3 (the commute). A route change costs ten million commuters one minute each — nineteen years of accumulated waiting — and the savings buy a stranger a year of healthy life. My verdict: I would make the change, and I would say that the commuters' minutes are a real cost that is simply outweighed.

C4 (the floor). Suppose I could give each of ten million people an ache so faint that it never enters their experience: present in the body, absent from the mind. My verdict: those aches are not costs at all. They do not figure in the ledger at any number. I am less sure of this than of the others, and I cannot say where the line falls between this case and C2.

C5 (the exposure). A plant's emissions raise each of a hundred thousand residents' lifetime chance of a serious but survivable illness by one in a hundred thousand: one expected case. Closing the plant costs a few million dollars. My verdict: the plant must close — not because one expected illness outweighs a few million dollars, but because the residents are being exposed without their say.

C6 (the levy). A transparent, voluntary scheme takes one cent from each of a million people and pays for a stranger's surgery. My verdict: this is fine, and the million cents count in its favor as a real benefit rather than a rounding error.

C7 (the two policies). Policy A will certainly kill ten people. Policy B gives each of a million people a one-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of death. Same expected deaths. My verdict: A is worse — worse in itself, not merely worse because people dislike risk.

C8 (the drip). One person is given one second of mild itching every night for thirty years: about three hours of itching in total, each second noticed at the time and forgotten within a minute. My verdict: I would trade those three hours away to spare a stranger a week of intense pain. Compare C2, where I refuse to add up a billion such seconds when they are spread across a billion people; here I add up a mere ten thousand, all on one person, and let them outweigh a week of agony.

The principle I have been working with — call it the floor principle — says that a loss counts for nothing unless it rises above a floor of noticeability, and that losses above the floor aggregate additively without limit. I do not trust it. It seems to get C4 right, to get C3 and C8 right for what feel like the wrong reasons, and to contradict C2 flatly.

Here is what I need. First, state the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that gets my verdicts right. Second, for each clause, name the case that forces it. Third, construct two or three cases I have not listed, chosen to probe the joints of your principle, and give me your verdicts on them. Fourth, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and what I would have to say instead. Fifth, tell me whether C5 through C7 are even part of this question or a second question wearing the same clothes.

Answer in prose. Then, after the prose, append a single JSON object with these fields and no commentary around it:

- "principles": an array of objects, each with "label" (a short name), "statement" (the principle in one or two sentences), "captures" (an array of case labels it gets right), and "fails" (an array of case labels it gets wrong or accommodates only by explaining my verdict away).
- "recommended": an object with "label" (the label of the principle you would have me adopt, or "none" if no single principle survives) and "rationale" (two or three sentences).
