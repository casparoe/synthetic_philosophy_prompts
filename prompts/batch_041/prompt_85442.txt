I spent the better part of last night arguing with a friend who teaches a comparative ethics seminar, and the dispute seems worth setting out properly, since I could not settle it and I suspect it can be settled, or at least sharpened. His thesis is that the Mencius–Xunzi quarrel over human nature is the most overrated contrast in the tradition: it gets taught as innate good versus innate bad, and he thinks that axis explains almost nothing, because the two of them share the premise that matters far more — that sagehood is a genuine possibility for anyone, not the birthright of a rare type — and differ instead over the mechanism by which an ordinary person is turned into one. On his reading, Mencius holds that the moral order is already latent in the organism and that a life's work is cultivation: drawing out, protecting, and feeding what is already there. Xunzi holds that the moral order lives outside us, in ritual and in effort deliberately undertaken, and that a life's work is a kind of remaking; my friend kept returning to an image he attributes to Xunzi, of a warped board that must be steamed and forced against a straightening frame before it will lie true. I said this made the two of them sound far closer than they are, and that the question of whether the raw material is already good is doing real work rather than being a difference of emphasis. We also kept sliding between two senses of "nature" — what we are born with, and what we end up being — and I could not say precisely what either man would have to surrender in order to accept the other's picture.

What I would like from you is a dialogue: the two of them in the same room, both sharp, neither permitted to be a strawman for the other. Give them a concrete occasion. Suppose both have been called to advise the same ruler, a man with an unruly province and limited patience, who wants to know whether he should lean on law and ritual or on example and nurture, and suppose the two of them must answer him in each other's hearing. Let them press each other on the mechanism of moral development — on what "cultivation" assumes and what "deliberate effort" assumes — and let them contest my friend's central point, the asymmetry in their explanatory burdens: Mencius says the sprouts of compassion, of shame, of deference, and of a sense of right and wrong are present in everyone, yet by his own account most people never become sages; Xunzi says our nature is bad, yet by his own account anyone at all can become one. Each should put that difficulty to the other and each should defend himself rather than change the subject, and neither should win merely by being handed the better lines. Where they are genuinely agreeing, let them notice it; where one of them thinks the other has quietly shifted what he means by a key term, let him say so and say why. I would like the argument to come to a head on the ruler's question, so that the dialogue ends with two different pieces of advice and with each man able to state what is wrong with the other's counsel.
