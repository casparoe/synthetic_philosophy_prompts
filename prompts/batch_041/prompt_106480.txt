I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-odd years doing insect sensory-motor work — a lot of it on how a wasp or a locust strings together a movement sequence, feedback loops, that sort of thing — and I've been filling the gap with philosphy, mostly the free will material, which I find both fascinating and maddning. I keep bumping into the Sphex wasp: the old Fabre party trick where she drags a paralyzed cricket to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it there while she goes in to inspect, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's inside she'll fetch it back and inspect all over again, apparently forever (Hofstadter coined 'sphexish' for it in a Scientific American column, Dennett turned it into 'sphexishness' in the mid-80s, as I understand it). I get the point of the story — rigidity dressed up as purpose — but every time a philosopher draws the moral from it I feel like they're using words I have better words for, and I can't tell whether the translation is faithful or whether something is being smuggled in.

So here's what I'd like. Take the debate as it actually stands — the claim that the wasp is just a machine and that this tells us something about behavior in general; Dennett's comeback that flexibility doesn't require escaping mechanism and that human flexibility may itself be built out of rigid bits; the libertarian insistence that a free act can't be subroutines all the way down; and the empirical pushback (Keijzer, I think) that the wasp does eventually adapt, so the story is rhetorically useful rather than true — and restate all of it in the vocabulary I actually think in. Releasers, fixed action patterns, central pattern generators, open- versus closed-loop control, reafference and efference copy, setpoints, gain, hysteresis, hierarchical control, interrupt handling, state machines, and so on. I know that vocabulary cold; use it properly and don't dumb it down. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: for each claim, what carried over cleanly, what carried over only with a distortion, and what has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary — and for each piece of residue, whether the fault is on my side (control theory is missing something) or on theirs (the philosophical claim was never as clear as it sounded).

One thing I'd like you to build the answer around, becuase it's the distinction I keep reaching for and never see anyone make: rigidity of the machinery versus rigidity of the policy it runs. A wasp can have hopelessly rigid wiring and yet a policy that shifts with the season; a trained network can have entirely fluid hardware running a policy that was frozen the day training stopped. My suspicion is that a good deal of the free will debate is people sliding between those two, and I want to know which philosophical claims survive when you keep them apart. Please also say where the wasp, an ordinary human, and (yes) something like you — a language model, which I gather is basically one enormous frozen policy sitting on very flexible hardware — land on that picture, and don't flatter yourself about it. Write it as prose, then finish with a JSON summary I can paste into my notes, with these fields: a "translations" array, each entry having "claim" (the philosophical claim, one sentence), "restatement" (your control-theory version of it), "status" (exactly one of "clean", "distorted", "no counterpart"), "what_is_lost" (one or two sentences), and "residue_reading" (exactly one of "control vocabulary is short", "original claim was vague", "both", "neither"); plus top-level string fields "architecture_vs_policy", "wasp_human_llm", and "overall_verdict".
