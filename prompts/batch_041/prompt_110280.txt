Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student working on perception/mind, and I've somehow ended up running teh reading group this term. Next week's session is the argument from illusion and disjunctivism, and I want to hand out a one-page decision tree instead of yet another summary of the usual five positions. Partly because everyone in the room already knows the five positions, and partly because I want to see where the views actually come apart rather than who's read which paper.

So: build me a decision tree / flowchart that takes someone from a few very plain starting commitments about perceptual experience to a named position, with every branch node labelled by the question that splits it from its sibling. Requirements:

- Build it out of the logical space, not out of a list of labels. Work out which questions actually carve the space, then place the positions on the resulting branches, then tell me which branches have nobody on them.
- Every node has to be answerable by someone who doesn't know which position it leads to. No 'are you a disjunctivist?' questions, no questions that only make sense once you've picked a side.
- The cases have to do the work. Use at least: the straight stick that looks bent in water; a Müller-Lyer pair; an afterimage; a perfect hallucination subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception; a veridical hallucination (someone hallucinating exactly the scene that is actually in front of them); a white wall seen under red light; and the blurry-vision case (is the blur in the experience or in what the experience presents?). If some of these cases pull apart in ways the others can't, say so at the relevant node.
- Keep illusion and hallucination as separate forks. I don't want them collapsed into a single 'non-veridical experience' node, because I think some positions come apart exactly there and I want the tree to show it — e.g. a view that treats hallucination disjunctively but still wants a common account of illusion, or the other way round. If a combination like that is incoherent, mark it and say why; if it's merely unexplored, say that instead.
- At the leaves I want: naive/direct realism or relationalism; sense-datum theory; adverbialism; representationalism/intentionalism, and, only if it's genuinely a different leaf, the weaker 'qualitative' version; metaphysical disjunctivism; the McDowell-style epistemic disjunctivism; content disjunctivism (disjunctive content, common phenomenal kind) if you think that's really a separate leaf; and anything you think I've left out. One line per leaf on its most awkward commitment.
- Flag any two leaves the tree can't separate given the questions you've used, rather than inventing a distinction to keep them apart. I'd rather know.
- For the empty branches, tell me whether the emptiness comes from incoherence, from unattractiveness, or from nobody having bothered.

Rough size: between 8 and 12 branching nodes, and no more than about 10 leaves. The audience is grad students plus a few brave undergrads, so no need to define 'qualia' and the like. And please don't pad it with references — no citations, no reading list, I just want the tree and the annotations.
