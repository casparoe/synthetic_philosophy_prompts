Question 3 (20 points). Dennett distinguishes three stances we can take toward a system whose behaviour we want to predict: the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance. He also argues that the entities the intentional stance posits — beliefs and desires — are real patterns, in the same sense in which the glider in Conway's Game of Life is a real pattern rather than a mere artifact of our describing the board. (a) Characterize the three stances and explain, using a chess-playing program as your running example, what each stance buys you and why anyone would bother with the riskier ones. (b) State the criterion Dennett gives for a pattern's being real, and explain how the glider satisfies it. (c) Does that criterion settle which of two rival assignments of beliefs and desires to a system is the correct one, and what does your answer imply about whether Dennett is a realist about beliefs? Answer in no more than 500 words.

Student's answer (transcribed from the scanned booklet; I have left the wording alone):

The three stances are strategies for predicting what a system will do, and they differ in how much they let you ignore. From the physical stance you predict using the system's physical constitution together with the laws of physics. If you wanted to know what move the chess program will make, you could in principle follow the current through the gates of the processor; nothing the machine does escapes physics. In practice this is hopeless, because the calculation is far too large to carry out, and this is the main reason the other stances exist.

From the design stance you predict on the assumption that the system is designed to perform its function well and is not malfunctioning, without knowing anything about its physical make-up. Applied to the chess program, you predict that it will avoid losing material and follow its opening book, and, since a well-designed program plays the best move available to it, that it will make the strongest move on the board. So the design stance already tells you which move will be played, and the intentional stance is a redescription of that same prediction rather than a new source of information. The design stance only gets off the ground where something was actually designed, with a purpose in mind, which is why it works for the thermostat and the chess program but not for the human eye, where using it would quietly assume a designer.

From the intentional stance you treat the system as a rational agent: you work out what beliefs and desires it ought to have given its situation, and predict that it will act so as to satisfy those desires in the light of those beliefs. For the chess program you attribute a desire to win and beliefs about the position, and you predict the move that a rational player with those beliefs would make. The three stances are exclusive alternatives: adopting the intentional stance toward the program means giving up the physical description of it, and the two compete with each other for the title of the true description of what the machine is doing.

Dennett's criterion for a real pattern is compressibility. A pattern is real when a description of it lets you predict the data more efficiently than the raw bit map would, so that you can say what comes next without simulating every element. The glider is the standard case: you can predict where the shape will be in fifty steps by applying the rules to the glider, instead of running the whole grid cell by cell, so the glider is not something we project onto the board but something that is there in the data.

On rival interpretations, because compressibility is an objective, measurable property of a description, the reading that compresses the behaviour best is the correct one, so competing belief-desire interpretations can be ranked against each other and there is no real indeterminacy about what a system believes. And since the criterion of reality is relative to our descriptive interests, the patterns the intentional stance finds are useful fictions rather than features of the world; Dennett's realism about beliefs is really instrumentalism, and beliefs are patterns we impose on behaviour because they are handy, not because they are really there.
