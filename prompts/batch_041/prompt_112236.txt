I'm running a film night for a first-year metaphysics unit and I've landed on Everything Everywhere All at Once, because it's the only recent movie I can count on everyone having seen that actually does something with the idea that there are other versions of you. The discussion I want to have is the old scrap between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the transworld identity line you get from Kripke, and I'd like you to write me the reading of the film I can hand out afterwards.

My version of the two views, so you can tell me where I've botched it: for Lewis you exist in exactly one world, and what we call "you in another world" is a counterpart, a different individual related to you by similarity, where that similarity relation shifts with context and isn't symmetric or transitive, so your counterpart's counterpart needn't be your counterpart. For Kripke no such relation is needed, you're just literally in other worlds, identity across worlds is plain identity, and asking for a criterion of "transworld identification" is a mistake in the first place. Lewis also insists worlds are completely isolated from each other, no travel, no causal contact, no overlap.

So: read the film through that. Where does its multiverse look like counterpart theory, where does it look like transworld identity, and where does it look like neither? Things I keep chewing on. The verse-jumping runs on doing something statistically improbable for you, which smells like a similarity relation doing real work, but the jumping goes to any version of yourself and the skills, memories and feelings flow both directions, which smells like one person spread thin across worlds rather than a crowd of similar strangers. And the worlds are obviously not isolated, since people are constantly barging into each other's, which means by Lewis's own lights this isn't a pluriverse at all, it's one world with an odd shape. Then the rock scene, which I love and cannot make fit: rock-Evelyn is still Evelyn, still in that conversation with Joy, but no similarity relation is going to make a rock a counterpart of a person, so is the film quietly haecceitist, i.e. does it think the thisness of a particular life matters and isn't just a matter of qualities? And Jobu Tupaki is a being who is every version of Joy at once, which is what you get if you take "all my counterparts are equally me" dead literally and let them all merge, so I half think the film's arc is an argument for the importance of the particular life, against Lewis, using Lewis's own machinery as the villain's power set. I might be overreading, that's why I'm asking.

What I want: a reading that takes both the film and the debate seriously, doesn't flatten the movie into an illustration of one side, doesn't distort the philosophy to make it fit a scene, and says so plainly when a scene resists the lens. No plot recap, I've seen it six times and my students have all seen it. And no citations or further reading, obviously, just the argument.

Give it to me in these sections, in this order:

The two views (about 150 words) - clean statement of the dispute, and fix my summary above if I've got either side wrong.

Where the film runs the debate (500 words or so) - the mechanics and scenes that map onto each position, and what each side would say about them.

Where the film won't sit still (350 words or so) - the parts that fit neither view, and what you'd have to change about the philosophy or about the film to make them fit.
