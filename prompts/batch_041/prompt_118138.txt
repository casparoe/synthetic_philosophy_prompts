Ninety minutes, eighteen students, fourth week of an upper-level epistemology seminar. We have just finished a unit on the ethics of belief, and the room is currently very confident that rationality is nothing more than matching your credences to the evidence — which is exactly the confidence I want to take apart. What I want them to walk into is the moment where a whole collection of verdicts, each of which it would be absurd not to accept, adds up to something they cannot accept as a whole, and where the only repair on offer is an arbitrary one. So: design the activity so that they arrive there themselves. Nothing in the setup, the rules, or my opening words should announce that a contradiction is coming, and the two paradox names and the closure principle they violate should not be spoken until the debrief. When you do name them at the end, use the labels only — the lottery paradox and the preface paradox — and do not attach names, dates, or titles to either; I can't check those and neither can you.

Here is the raw material I'd like you to build around, adapted as you see fit. A physical urn with 100 balls, exactly one gold, drawn live in front of everyone at the end; 100 cards, one per ball, each reading "ball #k will not be drawn," dealt so that the eighteen participants collectively hold all 100. Each holder decides publicly, card by card, to endorse or to abstain, and a clerk records endorsements in a ledger. Scoring in tokens, no money anywhere in it: an endorsed card that turns out true is +1, an endorsed card that turns out false is −9, an abstention is 0 — I want the break-even credence sitting at 0.9, so verify that these numbers give that and correct me if they don't. Then the sting, before the draw: the Bureau chief must sign or refuse a Clean Sheet Guarantee worth +6 to every participant if it holds and −6 to every participant if it doesn't. The two auditors have exactly one job, checking the ledger for a contradiction against the announced rules, and the guarantee cannot be signed over an inconsistent ledger. Work out precisely what happens in the two cases that matter: everyone endorses everything (my guess is that the ledger is then certainly inconsistent and the guarantee certainly doomed, and I want the arithmetic to show it), and the group withdraws endorsements until the ledger is consistent (my guess is that a single withdrawal suffices, that it buys a guarantee with 0.99 chance of holding, and therefore that the marginal value of a second withdrawal is nil and the entire repair consists of one arbitrary, unjustified exception). Keep the order fixed at endorse, audit, withdraw, sign, draw, so that nothing can be revised after the fact. Then close with a short epilogue that turns the same machinery into its preface version: the group publishes a report of about twelve factual sentences about the session, each written by a different person and each checkable before we leave, and then has to decide whether to append the sentence "at least one sentence in this report is false" — they should want to append it, and I want them to work out for themselves what the report amounts to when that is the only sentence in it, and how the situation changes as body sentences are added or taken away.

Give me the whole thing as a runnable design: materials, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, the payoff sheet, and the exact words for the three moments that need exact words (my opening, the auditors' charge, the chief's decision). Then the debrief, which should map each moment in the room onto the corresponding piece of the problem and lay out the escape routes with their prices — raise the threshold to certainty; accept every conjunct and refuse the conjunction; distinguish acceptance from belief; deny that there is justified belief about lottery propositions at all; treat the remark about one's own fallibility as a different kind of claim from the first-order ones. Then two more things I care about more than the rest. First, a frank paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to assert in my own voice rather than let them discover: I suspect it can only model acceptance as a scored bet, so it cannot establish that belief proper is closed under conjunction, and it cannot reproduce the inductive, first-person basis of the preface sentence, since my cards are fresh and perfectly symmetric. Second, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what to do about each, without bailing anyone out — I expect at least these: people insisting they merely find their card probable rather than believing it; the group quietly agreeing to sacrifice one designated card and treating that as a solution; and the auditors checking arithmetic instead of consistency. If it fits, tell me how to cut the whole thing to fifty minutes.
