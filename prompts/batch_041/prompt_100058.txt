I'm reading Plutarch's Life of Theseus and I've hit the bit everyone quotes for the Ship of Theseus, but I'm stuck on something that isn't the usual stuck-ness. Here's the passage: "The ship on which Theseus sailed with the youths and returned in safety, the thirty-oared galley, was preserved by the Athenians down to the time of Demetrius Phalereus. They took away the old timbers from time to time, and put new and sound ones in their places, so that the vessel became a standing illustration for the philosophers in the mooted question of growth, some declaring that it remained the same, others that it was not the same vessel."

What's bugging me is that Plutarch says the philosophers used it as an illustration in the question of growth, not the question of identity, or sameness, or what makes a thing one thing over time. I've only ever seen the ship discussed as an identity puzzle, so either Plutarch is using "growth" in some broad sense, or the ancient debate was about something a bit different from what we call identity, or the translators have flattened something out. I want help figuring out which.

And here's a distinction I've been chewing on, tell me if its any good or if I'm just projecting it onto the text. It seems like there are three ways the ship could be "the same" through the replacement: first, same matter, the planks are the planks; second, same form or plan, the arrangement is preserved even as the planks come and go; third, same ongoing activity or life, where the ship is the same not because of its stuff or its shape but because it's the continuation of one maintained, used, sailing thing, the way a body is the same body while it eats and sheds and heals. Reading "growth" makes me think Plutarch's philosophers might have had the third one in mind, or at least something closer to it than to the first or second, because growth is exactly the case where a living thing stays itself while its matter is totally turned over.

So: is that reading of "growth" plausible for the passage, or is Plutarch just lumping the ship in with any old puzzle about change? If the third reading is in play, does it actually solve anything, or does it just rename the problem, namely what makes the activity one activity rather than a series of similar activities? And if the philosophers really were arguing about growth, does that mean they'd treat a repaired ship and a growing animal as the same kind of case, or would they insist there's a difference between a thing that replaces its own parts from the inside and a thing whose parts get swapped out by outsiders?
