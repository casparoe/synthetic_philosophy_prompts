I have spent the past few weeks arguing with colleagues about the Allais paradox, and I want you to make the strongest possible case for a verdict that everyone keeps telling me is indefensible: that the Allais choices are rationally permissible, and that the independence axiom is not a requirement of practical rationality at all but a substantive separability assumption that a reasonable agent is free to reject. (The case as I use it: first, a choice between a sure million and a lottery giving an eighty-nine percent chance of a million, a ten percent chance of five million, and a one percent chance of nothing; then a choice between a lottery giving an eleven percent chance of a million and an eighty-nine percent chance of nothing, and one giving a ten percent chance of five million and a ninety percent chance of nothing. Most people take the sure million in the first pair and the ten percent shot at five million in the second, and that combination is said to violate the axiom.) Build the argument so that it earns the conclusion rather than merely asserting it, and make three distinctions do real work rather than getting name-dropped. First, the sure-thing principle — if two options agree on what happens in some event, your preference between them should not turn on that shared part — as against the mixture form of independence, which says that if you prefer one lottery to another you must prefer any probability mixture of the first with a fixed third lottery to the same mixture of the second; I want a verdict on whether the first really entails the second, or whether the step from one to the other smuggles in a premise. Second, the reduction of compound lotteries — the principle that a lottery whose prizes are themselves lotteries is equivalent to the single lottery obtained by multiplying through — and whether a defender of the Allais choices is forced to give it up, and what that costs. Third, the difference between certainty as a probability of one and certainty as a feature an option may or may not possess, since the whole force of the paradox seems to live in that difference, and I want to know whether that feature can be a legitimate object of preference or is merely a framing illusion.

Then confront the objections I find hardest, and do not wave them away. If the agent is permitted to treat certainty as special, what stops the same license from rationalizing choices nobody would accept — a cycle of preferences, or a violation of dominance? Construct the strongest such reductio you can, then say honestly whether it lands: if it does, concede the permissive position; if it does not, identify the premise a defender should deny and why. Take the dynamic objection equally seriously on its own terms: if you recast the first pair as two-stage lotteries sharing a common first stage, then choosing the sure million over the eighty-nine–ten–one lottery commits you, once the shared stage is stripped away, to preferring the eleven percent shot at a million to the ten percent shot at five million — the reverse of what you choose in the second pair — so that the two choices cannot belong to a single settled plan. Say exactly where that argument succeeds and where it assumes what it is trying to prove.

Close with your own verdict: keep the independence axiom as stated, replace it with something weaker, or drop it; and state the price of the verdict, in particular what it does to the representation theorems that use the axiom to derive expected-utility representation. Argue the whole thing from the ground up. I am not interested in a survey of who has said what, and I do not need citations.
