Ok so I'm writing a novel — a writing retreat, forty-odd broke and slightly grandiose writers, one long weekend — and the last act hinges on a stunt I've lifted from the Platonia dilemma and bent until it fits my story. I want the afterword to be an argument rather than a story, and I want you to make the argument.

Here's my version, and it's my version that matters, not the original. Everyone gets an identical envelope at the same moment, no talking about it, though everyone knows everyone got one. Inside: a card. Post the card back by Sunday and, if you're the only one who posted, you get £500,000. If two or more of you post, nobody gets a penny — the whole lot goes to the rival retreat down the coast, which they all loathe — and each of you who posted also has to put £2,000 of your own money into that same pot. If nobody posts, it goes back to the donor. Forty envelopes, forty cards. The donor has also let slip, casually, that he ran the identical offer at the rival place last summer, and that two people posted and he got to keep the lot.

I want you to argue that each of them should post the card. I know the standard line goes the other way — that if we're all reasoning alike we all post and all get nothing, so the clear-headed move is to tear the card up — so please go after that line rather than restating it, and say plainly what has to be true about the other thirty-nine people for your conclusion to hold.

Format:

- "The game, as written" — about 100 words, just fixing the terms.
- "Why the tidy answer fails" — about 400 words.
- "The case for posting" — about 500 words, this is the meat.
- "The best objection, and my reply" — about 250 words.
- "What I'll have my character do" — one paragraph, about 100 words, given where the argument lands.

Dont hedge into "it depends who you are". Write it like you mean it.
