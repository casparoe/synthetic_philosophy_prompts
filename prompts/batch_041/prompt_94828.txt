My closest friend and I have now spent three evenings arguing about a rule our housing co-operative votes on next week, and we have reached the point where neither of us can persuade the other or let it go. I would like a memo I can take into the meeting.

The co-op owns a hundred flats. Ten are let at a steep discount to households in serious financial need; the rest go at market rate. Until now a small committee has chosen the ten, informally and by consensus. The reform on the table replaces the committee with a points system: households score points for income relative to the local median, for dependants, for unavoidable medical costs, for caring responsibilities, and the ten highest scorers get the discounted flats.

Maya thinks this is plainly better than the committee. I think it is worse, and I cannot say why. The exchange that ended our last argument went like this. I asked whether she would call a household one pound below the cut-off needy and a household one pound above it not needy. She said of course not; need comes in degrees and no single pound can matter. I asked what happens if we slide the cut-off down a pound at a time, or a penny at a time. She said each slide is trivial and a run of trivial slides cannot produce a difference in kind, so there is nothing to worry about. I said that if we slide far enough we reach a household no one would call needy, and that the ten flats have to go to somebody. She then proposed abolishing cut-offs altogether: each household would pay a fraction of market rent set by its score, so that no one is ever on the wrong side of a line. I said the scale is itself a line, drawn as a curve instead of a step, and that a fixed budget still means someone misses out. She answered that even if some cases are neither clearly needy nor clearly not, the boundary between the cases that are clearly one thing and the cases that are merely in between must itself come in degrees, so my objection, if it worked at all, would destroy itself. A third member has proposed the simplest fix of all: pick a number, publish it, and raise it a little every year so it keeps pace with local wages. Maya likes this. I pointed out that after enough years every household in the building would qualify, which cannot be what the rule means, and she replied that each year's increase is justified on its own.

I do not care what this puzzle is called; I care what we should do. Tell me what Maya and I are actually disagreeing about, and separate the parts of her case I should concede from the parts I should resist. Is there any way to have a rule here without a line, or without a line that is arbitrary? Set out the options genuinely open to the co-op — a sharp cut-off, a sliding scale, case-by-case judgment, and anything else you think is live — with the price each one pays, and say which are defensible and which merely hide the problem. If you think the trouble dissolves once we attend to how the words are being used, say what that amounts to and whether it is an answer or a change of subject. Then tell me what to recommend on Thursday, and what to say to Maya when she asks why I am not simply being difficult.
