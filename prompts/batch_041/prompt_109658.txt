I'm a 4th-year PhD student and in about three weeks I have to give a work-in-progress talk to the late antiquity / Roman archaeology seminar in my department, and its exactly the wrong room for what I want to say. The paper's claim is that the whole "did Rome fall?" fight is stalled because both sides are running a welfare argument they won't own up to. The catastrophists keep pointing at the disappearance of an entire elite way of life — villa economies, literary culture, long-distance exchange in luxuries, monumental building, the whole apparatus — and treating that as a decline; the transformation people keep pointing at continuity in ordinary rural life, local production, the church, and treating that as proof that not much happened. I want to argue that these aren't two rival empirical claims about one quantity, they're two incompatible ways of aggregating welfare across a population and across time, so the interesting question stops being fall-or-transformation and becomes: whose welfare counts, and does a political order earn any credit for sustaining high culture at the expense of the median inhabitant? I've also got a second, more annoying claim, which is that the decline story is partly an artifact of the evidence — the things that vanish from the record (elite consumption, coin supply in the west, public building) are precisely the things that leave the most archaeological trace, and the things that carry on (subsistence farming, local exchange, household religion) leave almost none, so our sense of a rupture is partly a sense of what's visible. I don't need a defense of this, I need to survive the question period.

The room will be about a dozen people. Two archaeologists who work on rural settlement and visibly wince whenever anyone says "decline" — they'll want to know why I'm smuggling the Victorian story back in through the welfare door. An economic historian who'll want numbers and will ask how you'd even operationalize aggregate welfare for a period with no wage data; she's also a skeptic about reading skeletal heights as a living-standards proxy and thinks that literature is overread. A numismatist who will tell me the coin evidence doesn't say what I think it says. A postdoc doing climate proxies for the Mediterranean who will ask whether, if it isn't a fall, there's anything left to explain — i.e. am I just changing the subject away from causes (barbarians, climate, epidemic, lead, take your pick). One ancient historian who's a Gibbonian at heart and thinks transformation is a fad; he'll be my only ally and also the guy who makes my argument sound cruder than it is. And a philosopher of science who sits in on these things and will ask what my "aggregation" talk is even supposed to be — a claim about measurement, about ethics, or about explanation, because I keep sliding between the three.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or nine objections I'll actually get from this room, not the ones I'd get at a philosophy conference, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so I don't look evasive, which one of these I should just refuse to fight rather than try to win, and — the thing I actually want — the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Skip the reading lists and citations, I have those; I want the shape of the conversation and where I'm exposed.
