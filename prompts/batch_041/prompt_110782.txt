I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind working on the affective dimension of pain, and I'd like an adjudication of an argument I overheard rather than took part in. Two people in my program — Mira, who works on the motivational side of affect, and Tomas, who works on hedonic quality — went at each other for the better part of an hour after a seminar last week, and I still cannot say who has the better of it. The case they were fighting over, which both of them accepted as described, is pain asymbolia: after lesions classically placed in or around the insula and the adjacent parietal operculum (the localization is disputed, and the syndrome has been described after other damage too), patients are said to feel and localize a pinprick, to describe its intensity and quality, and to distinguish sharp from dull, but not to find it distressing — they do not withdraw, they may laugh, and they say things to the effect that it hurts but does not bother them; some reports instead suggest that the sensory experience itself is altered, and some argue that the indifference is not specific to pain but part of a broader neglect. Their exchange went roughly as follows. Mira opened: asymbolia shows that the badness of pain cannot be its raw phenomenal character, since here is a state everyone counts as a pain that is not bad for the person who has it; strip away the aversive demand to stop and what is left is a sensation, not a harm. Tomas replied that she was sliding from the patient not minding it to its not being bad for her: the depressed patient does not mind her isolation and the anorexic does not mind starving, and indifference of that kind is part of the pathology rather than evidence about welfare; the hurt is still present, so the badness is present, and what has been cut is the link between hurt and action. Mira came back that on his view bad adds nothing to hurt except a brute evaluation of a quale, and that a state which makes no difference to anything the person registers or should care about is not doing any explanatory work; she added that if pain were still bad but merely unmotivating, the patients would be in the position of someone who sees a threat and cannot move — distressed, not calm — and that his position faces a fork: if the imperative force is part of what makes a sensation painful, then asymbolia patients simply have no pain, and if it is not, then it is an add-on to pain and pain itself is neutral. Tomas answered on two fronts — welfare need not be registered, since a state can be bad for you without your caring, and he need not treat badness as a brute quale, since pain can present a disturbance in the body as a reason to stop, with the badness located in that demand rather than in a separate desire — and he pressed that her view makes the masochist's pain not bad, whereas the masochist's pain is the paradigm case of something bad that someone perversely wants. Mira's last word was that the masochist shows precisely that sensation and attitude come apart, and that wanting the unpleasantness does not make it good. Tomas closed by suggesting that if the patients' reports are as shaky as the literature suggests, the case may not even be one of pain, in which case it cannot show what pain's badness consists in. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't.

Please adjudicate this point by point rather than awarding the whole dispute to one side. For each of the central contentions — that badness cannot be the raw feel, that indifference is a pathology rather than evidence about welfare, that badness must make a difference to be doing any work, that pain's badness could consist in a demand internal to the experience rather than in a desire, that her account makes the masochist's pain not bad, and that the case may not be one of pain at all — say whether it is right, wrong, or simply underdetermined by the evidence as described, and briefly why. Then tell me what each of them is missing, and give a verdict on the question they never quite separated: whether the asymbolia patient's pain is bad for her, and whether the case tells for or against hedonic accounts of pain's badness. I would like you to be explicit about how much of this is settled and how much is genuinely live among people who work on it — if something I have reported as common ground is contested, or if one of the two positions is a minority view that the other has already been thought to answer, say so rather than smoothing it over; I would much rather have an answer that marks its own uncertainty than one that presents a contested reading as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the verdict first, then the reasoning — and skip any restatement of the setup.
