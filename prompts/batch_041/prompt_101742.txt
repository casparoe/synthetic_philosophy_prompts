I'm a master's student putting together a proposal for a one-semester independent research project — roughly 5,000 words, with a supervisor who will check the formal machinery — and I'd like you to help me turn a half-formed idea into a proper proposal document. The idea came out of an argument with friends after a probability seminar. We were going round in circles on Bertrand's paradox, the business with chords drawn at random in a circle, where 'at random' seems to yield a third, a quarter or a half depending on how you parameterise the chord. What struck me was that everyone in the room was treating this as a single problem, whereas it seemed to me to be at least two. One is a question about chance: what a physical device for generating chords actually does, and whether the phrase 'a chord drawn at random' picks out any such device at all. The other is a question about credence: what an agent who knows nothing about the chord beyond that it is some chord ought to believe about its length. The standard replies seem to me to slide between the two — the invariance or maximum-entropy line associated with Jaynes effectively stipulates a physical symmetry and then calls the resulting distribution the rational credence, while the deflationary line that the problem is merely under-specified tells us the question is ill-posed without telling us what to do when we genuinely have the evidence the question presupposes. I also recently came across the notion of dilation in the imprecise-probability literature — the surprising phenomenon whereby learning something can leave one's credal set wider rather than narrower — and I have a suspicion, which may well be wrong, that Bertrand's paradox is a dilation phenomenon in disguise: that the evidence 'the chord is random' is simply too weak to pin down a single distribution, that the honest object is a convex set of them, and that the various classical answers are its extreme points.

Please write the proposal. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer, and the question I want is not 'which of the classical answers is right' but something like this: when an agent's evidence is invariant under a family of transformations but no single measure is, is the correct representation of that evidence a single distribution or a set of them, and does the Bertrand case force the second? Set out why the existing positions leave this open, taking care to distinguish the chance question from the credence question and to say which existing responses to the paradox answer which of the two. Then give the method: what I would actually do, month by month across the semester, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives — for instance, rather than simply re-running the maximum-entropy argument or writing a survey of the paradox's history. Say what a result would look like: what would count as showing that the paradox is, or is not, a genuine case of dilation, and what sort of theorem or worked example would settle it. Say too what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that the two-tier distinction I am fond of collapses under scrutiny. Finally, give an honest assessment of scope — what parts of this could not realistically be done in a semester and should be flagged as future work rather than promised.

I shall add the citations myself, so please do not provide a bibliography or a reading list, and do not lean on particular papers or dates; refer to positions and arguments by description instead. Write the proposal in British English.
