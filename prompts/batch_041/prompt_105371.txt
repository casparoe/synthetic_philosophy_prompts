I'm in a small reading group on mental content and we've spent the last three weeks circling the symbol grounding problem, and last Tuesday two of the others had a proper row about it that I couldn't referee and it's been nagging at me since. The trigger was Ana's side project: she's trained a model on nothing but text about wine — tasting notes, reviews, forum arguments about whether a 2016 Barollo is overrated — and it turns out that if you ask it which of two wines is more similar to a third, its answers line up surprisingly well with what sommeliers say, and it even predicts tasting notes from 2020 that it never saw. Her line is that this is grounding enough. (1) Grounding isn't all-or-nothing; what matters is that the model's wine-words stand in the right relations to each other and are answerable to something outside the text, and the sommelier verdicts are exactly that, so the corpus inherits its grounding from the tasters. Ben came back hard. (2) Inheritance is the one thing that can't happen: if none of the symbols is hooked onto anything, adding more symbols just gives you a bigger ungrounded system — a dictionary isn't made meaningful by being a larger dictionary — and the sommelier labels are just more text. Ana wasn't having it. (3) She said the labels aren't more text in the relevant sense, because they're the output of a causal process (people actually drinking the stuff), so the model's predictions about them can be right or wrong about what tasters will in fact say; that's the same relation a weather model stands in to rain, and nobody says weather models have ungrounded 'rain' symbols. Ben said that's the tell. (4) The weather model's symbols are numbers hooked onto measurements, whereas the wine model's words are hooked onto other words; the fact that the whole loop terminates in human tasters doesn't ground the model, because it's the humans who have the grounding and the model is just a very good filing cabinet for their reports. And he added that on Ana's criterion you can't distinguish her model from a system that merely predicts what a thermometer will read, so her criterion either grounds everything or nothing.

Then came the bit that actually ended the evening. Ana said (5) fine, maybe the model isn't grounded — but then 'grounding' is doing no explanatory work in the argument at all, and what Ben really cares about is that the model isn't having experiences, which is a claim about consciousness and not about symbols. Ben said that's a cheap escape, and that he can say what's missing without mentioning consciousness: nothing in the system is a state that can be wrong about a wine, only states that can be wrong about what people say about wine.

I don't have a horse in this race and I'd rather not just pick a winner by vibes, and I'm definately not confident I've even got their positions straight. Can you go through it move by move — five moves, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification each — and then say what both of them are missing? I'm especially interested in whether (3) and (4) are really contradicting each other or just talking past each other, and whether (5) rescues Ana's position or changes the subject.
