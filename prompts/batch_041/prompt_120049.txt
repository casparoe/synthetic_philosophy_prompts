my decision theory reading group has spent three weeks on what we've started calling commitment races and i think we've argued ourselves into something stupid. the toy version: two firms are deciding which of two incompatible formats to back. before the market settles, each can spend money pouring a factory that can only ever make its own format -- once its poured it cant be un-poured, and everybody can see it. if only one firm pours, the other folds and the pourer takes the market. if both pour, both lose everything. if neither pours, they split it. we also tried a variant where the pour is invisible to the other side, and noticed it does literally nothing, which is where premise (1) below comes from.

then a friend in the group handed us this and i cant find the bad step. (1) A commitment only does any work if the other side knows you can't back out. (2) You can't back out only if, at the moment of truth, backing out isn't really available to you -- because if it were available, and it were the better move, you'd take it, and that's just what it is to be rational. (3) So a commitment is credible only in an agent that, at the moment of truth, will act against its own best judgment. (4) An agent that acts against its own best judgment at the moment of truth is not a rational agent. (5) So only non-rational agents can commit, and by (1) only agents who can commit win these races. (6) So the race is won by whichever side is less rational, and the more rational you are the worse you do in it. (7) Which is absurd -- rationality can't be a handicap, and a rational agent obviously wouldn't be better off lobotomizing itself. I'd rather keep (2) and (4) than (7), and i don't believe (7) is secretly true, but I also can't see where the thing slips.

So: which step goes, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me? I especially want to know whether the argument is still alive for the case with no factory at all -- where the commitment is pure resolve, nothing physical has changed, you've simply decided in advance that you won't reconsider. If it survives there, tell me exactly how much of it survives and what that means about what kind of agent a rational agent has to be. And don't just name the fallacy and move on, definately price it: tell me what I have to give up elsewhere if I take your fix. Finish with a last line that reads exactly "Verdict: <step number> -- <short reason, under ten words>".
