I serve on the internal safety review board of a mid-sized AI lab, and I have spent enough years in rationalist and agent-foundations forums to have become the board's designated skeptic. On Thursday we vote on whether to authorize deployment of a successor system — call it S2 — that our current system, S1, designed, trained, and vetted. The safety team is asking us to approve, and their central exhibit is a formal argument: S1 can prove, from axioms it already accepts, that S2's policy will never take an action S1 would classify as catastrophic. They present this as a proof that S2 inherits S1's safety.

I believe the argument is circular in a way the rest of the board will miss unless I spell it out, and I want the written opinion I circulate to do the spelling. Two things trouble me. First, S1 does not stand outside the world it reasons about: it is one of the things in that world, S2 is another, and any proof S1 gives about S2 is itself produced by a system that S2 may later rewrite. Second, there is the self-trust problem — an agent that reasons from a fixed set of axioms cannot, without already deciding the question, derive the schema that whatever its axioms prove safe is safe. That is the Löbian obstacle, and it is why successive generations of self-modifying agents cannot simply tile their trust in one another indefinitely. So the exhibit may prove considerably less than the team claims.

Please draft the board's written opinion. I need a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — carrying a time frame the lab can actually act on, and I need it to engage the strongest version of the case for approval rather than dismissing it. The safety team's best reply, I expect, is that I am demanding the wrong thing: that no one requires a human engineer to prove her own reliability, that assurance rightly comes from outside the system — external testing, staged rollout, an independent verifier working from a stronger theory — and that treating S1's inability to certify S2 as disqualifying simply confuses S2's being safe with S1's being able to prove it. That reply deserves a genuine answer, not a hand-wave: say whether an external verifier escapes the obstacle or merely relocates the circularity, and say what the board's approval standard should therefore be. Keep it short enough to circulate the night before, end with the bottom line the chair can put to a vote, and flag anything the board itself is in no position to verify. No citations or literature review — argue from the setup.
