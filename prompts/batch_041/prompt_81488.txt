I have spent the term defending heterophenomenology in a seminar on the science of consciousness, and a friend in the department has finally produced an argument that I can neither answer nor accept.

Heterophenomenology, as I understand it, asks the scientist to record everything the subject says and does, to treat that record as a text, and to interpret it the way we interpret a novel: we attribute beliefs, desires, and experiences to the subject as we would to a fictional character, and we hold the interpretation accountable to the subject's further behavior. The method's selling point is that it never has to settle whether the subject's experience is really the way she reports it. That question is set aside; what gets explained is the pattern of her reports and responses.

His argument runs as follows.

(1) The method's authority rests on its neutrality: it takes no stand on whether the subject's reports are true.
(2) A method that earns its authority by neutrality cannot exempt anyone from that neutrality; it must be applied evenhandedly, to every reporter.
(3) The scientist who applies the method is herself a reporter — she reports what the subject said and what it means.
(4) So the method must be applied to the scientist as well: her reports are a text whose content is her beliefs, not a window onto the subject's mind.
(5) Applying it again yields a text about the scientist's beliefs, and again a text about whoever interprets that, without limit.
(6) At no level does the method deliver a fact about the world; it delivers only narratives about narratives.
(7) A procedure that never delivers a fact cannot be a scientific method.
(8) Therefore heterophenomenology cannot be a scientific method for consciousness — and by the same reasoning, neither can any inquiry that treats human reports as its data, history and anthropology included.

He takes this to show that the method eats itself. I would rather keep the method than accept (8), but I cannot locate the misstep. Name the step you would reject — or, if you think the argument is valid as it stands, the premise that is least costly to give up — explain why that step looks so plausible, and say what rejecting it costs: what I would have to give up or explain away in order to keep the method. Open by naming the step, then justify. No more than 500 words.
