I grew up in a rural working-class community and have spent the last several years in a professional-class urban one, and I have come to hold a position I want tested: the two outlooks are not morally rankable, but the professional-class outlook does have one genuine, non-moral advantage -- it is structurally better at detecting and correcting its own errors, because it institutionalizes criticism, mobility, and the separation of a person's identity from their beliefs. I want to know what would count against this. What would have to be true for that advantage to be illusory or parasitic on the very class position it claims to transcend? Which criticisms would defenders of my view themselves have to concede, and which could they dismiss as mere class resentment? Rank the defeaters from fatal to awkward, and tell me whether the position is in danger of insulating itself by redescribing every counterexample as a failure of the other side to understand the professional-class outlook. I am especially interested in whether the comparison survives the fact that neither outlook is a single thing, and in whether the professional-class outlook's capacity for self-criticism is a genuine epistemic achievement or merely another style of class-bound self-presentation.
