ok this is a weird request but this topic has been living in my head rent free for two weeks and I need to get it out as a piece of writing.

Background: my younger brother got a DUI back in March. Big fine, some community service, no jail time. My aunt paid the fine. Just wrote a check and it was done, he never paid a cent, and nobody in my family thought twice about it, including my dad who is the most law-and-order guy I know. Then two Sundays later the sermon was on the atonement and the pastor did the whole penal substitution thing, Jesus taking the punishment we deserved, and afterward in small group this guy Kevin said that's just incoherent, you can't punish an innocent person in place of the guilty one, it's a category error, and everyone nodded along. And I'm sitting there thinking, Kevin, my aunt paid my brother's fine nine days ago. And Kevin would say that's different, that's just money. But is it???

Anyway apparently David Lewis wrote a short essay about exactly this, I think it's called Do We Believe in Penal Substitution, sometime in the late 90s, and his move is that we're of two minds. In the abstract we say punishing an innocent volunteer is never a reason to leave the offender unpunished. In practice we do it constantly, fines being the obvious case. And once you see that, the real question isn't whether penal substitution is absurd, it's which substitutions we allow, why, and whether that line is principled or just wherever our intuitions happen to sit.

So here's what I want from you. Write me a mock FAQ / customer support script for a fictional company that runs an actual marketplace for this, like TaskRabbit but for taking on someone else's punishment. Call it whatever you like, I keep calling it Vicarious in my head. Aim for 600-700 words, deadpan chirpy corporate voice, no winking at the reader, no jokes that are just about prison being bad. The comedy has to come from getting the philosophy right. Let the fine print quietly take positions on:

- fines, obviously transferable, no questions asked, this is the boring case
- community service, also fine
- actual prison time, which is where it gets interesting. Does it matter that the substitute consents? Does it matter that the offender consents? My aunt paying the fine didn't need my brothers permission, but I feel like serving his sentence should
- the innocent substitute problem: if the point of punishment is that the guilty one suffers, a volunteer suffering in his place seems to miss the point entirely; if the point is deterrence or restitution it seems to work fine. Have the company be a little slippery about which theory of punishment it's actually operating under, in a way a philosophy person would recognize as the real issue
- double punishment and the refund policy if the state goes ahead and punishes the original offender anyway
- the premium tier for debts too large for any finite substitute to cover. Yes this is the God one, but don't say that, let the reader get there on their own
- and at least one category of offense the company flatly refuses to substitute, with a reason that sounds bureaucratic but is actually philosophically motivated, something about the punishment being tied to who did it

End with a testimonials section, one from someone who paid another persons fine and now feels weirdly guilty about it, and one from someone who served three months for a friend and can't quite explain why it felt like it worked.

Don't quote Lewis or anyone else, don't cite anything, don't put in any real book titles or dates. Just make it stand on its own so someone who's never read a word of philosophy can follow it and someone who has read the essay would grin.
