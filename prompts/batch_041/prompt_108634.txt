I am a philosopher of science whose recent work has been on the epistemology of adversarial verification, and next month I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on scalable oversight. The thesis I will defend is that the debate protocol — the now-familiar proposal that we can keep a superhuman system honest by having two instances argue a question before a weaker human or model judge — is unsound in principle for a broad class of questions, and that the culprit is the phenomenon usually called obfuscated argument. This is not the usual complaint that debate is hard to implement or that the judge may be biased; my claim is that there is a structural obstacle that no amount of engineering or training can remove.

The argument has three steps. First, soundness for debate requires that for any false proposition, no dishonest debater can produce a line of argument that a bounded judge will accept as settling the matter. Second, I claim that there are true propositions whose truth cannot be certified to a judge of bounded computational power by any argument the judge can check within its budget, even when the honest debater has unbounded access to the facts: the honest debater knows the answer but cannot transmit the warrant for it. Third, I model the exchange as a game between a polynomial-time judge and two debaters, and I argue that closing the resulting gap would require the judge to decide a language outside P — a problem that no polynomial-time algorithm can decide — or to possess a certificate that in general does not exist, so the protocol is sound only for restricted classes of questions, roughly those whose members admit short certificates. I connect this to the difference between interactive proof systems in which the verifier is computationally bounded and those in which it is not, and I contend that the debate literature has tacitly assumed the unbounded case.

The audience will be about twenty people: a dozen empirical machine-learning researchers who work on reinforcement learning from human feedback, red-teaming, and debate-based oversight; three complexity theorists from the theory group; two formal epistemologists; and one economist who works on mechanism design. I have argued this through with a colleague in the theory group, who tells me with some relish that I am reinventing a known separation, and I want to be ready for that charge in particular. I know the objections I would get from the AI-safety community, and I do not need those. What I want is a preparation document that anticipates the eight or so challenges that will actually come from this room, ranked by how badly each would wound the paper if I fumbled it, each written in the idiom of the person who would raise it and labelled with the part of the audience it comes from — the complexity theorist's challenge in complexity-theoretic terms, the mechanism designer's in mechanism-design terms, the epistemologist's in epistemological terms — and each accompanied by a two-minute response and an honest assessment of the response's soft spot. Tell me what I should give ground on at the outset and what I should refuse to give ground on, tell me which step of my own argument is most likely to break, and save for the end the one challenge I am dreading. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or references; I know the literature, and a list of titles would be useless to me.
