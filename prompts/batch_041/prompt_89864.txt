I've ended up in a long argument in a forecasting group chat and I want to be able to lay it out properly instead of just sniping at my friend. His whole method is: don't ask whether America (or the EU, or whoever) is in trouble, ask what the base rate is. Polities are like organisms, he says — born, grow, mature, get old, die — so go find how long past empires lasted, put us on the clock, and stop being special about it. The number everyone in the chat throws around is 250 years for a big one, and Rome is the reference case. What bugs me is that when I ask what counts as Rome's founding date he says 753 BC, and when I ask why not the start of the republic or the principate he says it doesn't matter, and it seems to matter enormously — the lifespan moves by hundreds of years depending on which event you pick, and the picking looks like something the analyst just decides. Then the other half: Gibbon's point, in the general observations he tacks onto the end of the chapter on the fall in the West (ch. 38, I think), is that Rome didn't die of anything you could have seen coming from outside — it declined because it had gotten so big and rich and easy that the decay was baked into the greatness itself. If that's right, then greatness isn't a neutral clock ticking in the background, it's the hazard, and pooling Rome with every other empire into one survivorship curve looks like pooling things whose hazard rates aren't the same at all.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Start with the correspondence table — organism, lifespan, birth, maturation, aging, death, and what each is supposed to be in the case of a polity — then tell me where the mapping breaks. I care most about three things: (a) whether there's any shared mechanism of senescence in the target the way there is in the source, or whether "age" is just an accounting convention, (b) whether a reference class built out of past empires is being assembled by looking at which ones fell, and if so whether that poisons the base rate, and (c) whether the outside view my friend thinks he's taking survives at all once the cause of decline might be the very property that got you into the class. Then say whether the conclusion he actually wants — that we're overdue so expect it soon — is something this analogy can carry, or whether he's getting the conclusion from the analogy and the analogy is quietly doing all the work. And if there's a better analogy for what he's trying to do, tell me what it is; I'd rather show up with that than with another round of nitpicking.
