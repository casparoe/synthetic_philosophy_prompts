I'm in a seminar on rational choice and cooperation, and I've written up a discussion note around an argument of my own that I now suspect proves too much. Every premise looks defensible to me and I can't accept the conclusion, so what I want is a diagnosis rather than reassurance.

(1) Two agents can gain by cooperating whenever each can predict what the other will do, even if neither can causally affect the other: if I know you will reciprocate exactly when I cooperate, cooperation is available to us with no channel between us.
(2) The predicting need not run through any physical signal. It can rest on the fact that we implement the same rule for deciding, so that each of us can read off the other's choice by working out its own.
(3) So the set of agents I can trade with is fixed by similarity of decision procedure and information, not by distance or causal reach.
(4) Similarity of procedure is a matter of pattern rather than stuff: an agent that would reason as I do is relevantly similar to me whether it is across the galaxy, in another branch of the wavefunction, or merely possible, in the way an uninstantiated algorithm is possible.
(5) If I give non-zero credence to such agents existing and to their choices being correlated with mine, their welfare enters the payoffs of my actions as ordinary expected value.
(6) Nothing rules them out, so my credence is non-zero; and if they are there at all, they are not few. There is no more reason to think there are finitely many possible trading partners than to think there are finitely many possible numbers.
(7) So the expected value of what I do is dominated by agents I have no evidence for, and the effect of my actions on actual people is a rounding error.
(8) But then the theory cannot guide me at all: whatever I do, the same hypothetical term dominates, so no option comes out ahead of any other.
(9) So something in (1)-(7) is false.

If I can, I'd like to keep (1)-(3): the whole interest of acausal trade, as I understand it, is that cooperation needs no causal channel, and I don't want to give that up. I'd rather not give up (5) either. So which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? If it comes down to trading (2) against (5), tell me which is cheaper to abandon. And please say what would change your mind: which consideration would push you to reject a different step instead, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
