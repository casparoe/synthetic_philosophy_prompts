ok so I'm in a third-year philosophy of perception module and we're doing a calibration exercise — the prof put up a question from a past paper along with an anonymised answer that a real student handed in, and we have to mark it ourselves before Thursday's seminar and then compare our marks with what the original marker gave. I'd rather not tell you what I thought of it, I don't want to anchor you. So: could you grade it, and then give me the comments you'd actually write next to each part? I mean point by point — what they got, whats missing, where an argument step doesn't hold — rather than a general impression of the whole thing. Marks were out of 20, split roughly 6 for part (i), 7 for (ii), 7 for (iii), and we've been told to be generous to people who state the right thing in the wrong words. Also, if there's something wrong in there that a marker skimming it would probably wave through, please say so explicitly, because finding those is basically the point of the exercise.

The question:

(i) Set out the argument from illusion — in the version that turns on the possibility of an experience of the very same kind occurring both with and without its object (you can treat the argument from hallucination as the same argument here) — in a form that applies to the following pair of episodes, making every premise explicit. S is fitted with a device that records the state of her brain while she veridically perceives a red cube on the table in front of her, and can later reproduce that state exactly, with no cube present and nothing else in the room altered. Call the first episode V and the second R. S cannot tell the two apart, every test available to her comes out the same, and the state her brain is in during R is stipulated to be a duplicate of the state it was in during V. (ii) Explain how someone who is a disjunctivist about perceptual experience should respond to the argument as you have set it out. (iii) Does the response work? Argue for your answer, and say what you take to be the strongest objection to it.

And what the student wrote:

(i) The argument runs: (1) In R something seems to S to be the case, namely that a red cube is present to her, but no cube is there. So either S is aware of nothing at all, or she is aware of something that is not a mind-independent object, an appearance or sense-datum. (2) R and V are subjectively indistinguishable: nothing available to S tells her which one she is in. (3) If two experiences are subjectively indistinguishable then they are experiences of the same fundamental kind — whatever it is like to have one is what it is like to have the other, and that shared character is what fixes what kind of experience it is. (4) In R, S is not aware of a mind-independent red cube. Therefore (5) in V, S is not aware of a mind-independent red cube either; what she is aware of is an appearance, and the cube itself is at best the cause of it. Premise 3 is doing the work: without it, 1 and 4 tell us only about R.

(ii) The disjunctivist grants 1, 2 and 4 and rejects 3. They don't have to deny that S can't tell V from R — that is the whole difficulty of the case — what they deny is that not being able to tell them apart tells you anything about what kind of experience each one is. On this view V is a case of S being directly confronted by the cube, whereas R is not: the two experiences have different natures even though they are indiscriminable from the inside. The difference shows up in S's epistemic position. In V, S's experience puts her in a position to know that there is a red cube in front of her, and her warrant for that belief is the fact that she sees the cube; in R she is in no such position, since there is nothing there to see, and any belief she forms about a cube being there is at best accidentally true. So the two episodes do not share a common experiential factor, and premise 3 is false.

(iii) I think the response works. The replay device only guarantees that S's brain is in the same state on both occasions; it doesn't guarantee that the same experience occurs, because the disjunctivist denies that having an experience is just a matter of what is going on inside your head. So the fact that the device can duplicate the state is not evidence that V and R are the same kind of thing, and the argument never gets going. The strongest objection is that this makes the difference between V and R completely undetectable and therefore mysterious — we are told there is a difference in the nature of the two experiences, but S could never in principle discover it, and a difference that makes no difference to anything S could ever do starts to look like a difference we have no reason to believe in. But the disjunctivist can reply that undetectable is not the same as non-existent, and that we already accept differences of this sort elsewhere: two people can be in states that feel exactly alike to them, one of whom knows something and the other of whom merely believes it. So I don't think the objection is fatal, though it does show the view is costly.
