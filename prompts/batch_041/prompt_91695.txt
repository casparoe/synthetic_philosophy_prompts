I’m a graduate student in history, and I’ve been arguing with my advisor about how to attribute causes in cases where an outcome seems to have been produced twice over. My chapter concerns a fictional but realistic decision — a city council passing a controversial ordinance — where two independent coalitions each had the votes and the will to pass it, and either would have succeeded alone. My advisor says I’m confusing causes with background conditions and that the ordinary counterfactual test still does the real work; I say the case shows the ordinary test breaking down. I want a clean thought experiment to separate the theories, not a verdict about my chapter, and I don’t want to lean on any famous historical example.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. It should be realistic and historical rather than science-fictional: no time travel, no magic, no perfect predictors, no brain-in-a-vat machinery. It must contain two distinct causal chains, A and B, each independently sufficient for the same outcome O, with the same background conditions and actors held fixed across two versions. In version 1 — symmetric overdetermination — both A and B run to completion, neither preempts the other, and O occurs. In version 2 — preemption — A runs to completion and brings about O before B can, while B would have been sufficient had A not intervened; only the timing or preemption relation changes between versions. Keep each version to one paragraph, invent the case rather than borrowing a real event, and make it mundane enough to be credible: a committee vote, a factory decision, a legislative logroll, or something similar.

After the scenarios, walk through what each of the following says about whether A and B caused O in version 1 and in version 2: the but-for test; NESS, understood as the idea that a cause is a necessary element of a sufficient set of conditions; INUS, understood as an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition; and contrastive causation, understood as the idea that causation is always of the form “A rather than A* caused O rather than O*.” For each theory, state the verdict in both versions and explain exactly why it follows from the theory rather than from your stipulation. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work — preemption, symmetry, or the sufficiency assumption — and what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed. Finally, if a defender of whichever theory comes out worst wanted to attack the case, what would the strongest line of attack be? I care more about the structure of the disagreement than about which theory wins.
