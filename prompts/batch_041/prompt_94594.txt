I am applying for a three-month summer research grant from an institute that funds work at the boundary of philosophy and computer science, and I need a two-page proposal. The subject is the idealisation of deliberation known as HCH, in which a human who is asked a question may delegate any sub-question to a copy of themselves, each copy may delegate in turn, and so on without bound — so the limit is a human with unlimited recursive help from copies of themselves. It belongs to the same family of ideas as factored cognition, the thought that a hard task can be broken down into many small and largely independent ones.

My half-formed idea is that this construction is normally treated as a single idealisation when it is really a bundle of at least four separable assumptions, and that different conclusions in the surrounding discussion lean on different members of the bundle. First, that a large cognitive task can be decomposed into subtasks independent enough for a bounded agent to settle one at a time. Second, that depth of delegation is unlimited and costless, or at least that only the limit matters. Third, that the base case — the unaided human — is held fixed, so that the construction amplifies rather than improves the reasoner. Fourth, that the limit is a well-defined object, that there is a fact of the matter about what this idealised deliberator would answer. I suspect the fourth is where the difficulty lies, and that the sharp form of the question is whether a construction that may be queried about its own rules for decomposing questions — as opposed to one that merely consults copies through a fixed interface — is coherent at all, since the first looks uncomfortably like asking a system to certify its own reliability.

I also want the proposal to take seriously a distinction I have not seen drawn cleanly: between an idealisation in the physicist's sense, a deliberately distorted but tractable model whose distortions must be tracked, and an ideal in the normative sense, a standard we could be wrong to fall short of. If the idealised deliberator merely amplifies whatever systematic biases survive recursion, it supplies no normative standard, and the proposal should say what would settle whether it does.

Please turn this into the proposal: a precise statement of the question, in a form that has a determinate answer rather than a merely rhetorical one; why the existing treatments leave it open, put in terms of positions and arguments rather than a survey (I shall add the citations myself, so please do not supply any); the method — what I would actually do, week by week, across twelve weeks — and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives; what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a candid assessment of what might make this too much for three months, with a fallback that would still be worth doing. I would rather have a small question that is genuinely open than a large one that amounts to a summary of a debate, so keep the conceptual work in the framing, mark clearly which parts are reconstruction of other people's views and which are claims of my own, and do not assume that the construction is well-defined in the course of asking whether it is.
