My roommate and I got into a stupid argument about how many things are in our apartment — like, literally, how many objects — and it escalated way more than it should have, and now I want to know whether there's actually a principle behind my gut answers, because I keep catching myself contradicting myself. Here's where I land. A brick: one thing. A wall made of bricks and mortar: also one thing — if you asked me to count what's in the room I wouldn't say "four hundred bricks plus some mortar," I'd say "a wall." But two bricks I glued together in the garage: that's two things, or at best one object made of two things, but it's not *a* thing the way the wall is, and I can't articulate the difference to save my life. A heap of sand: not a thing, just sand. A sandstone rock I picked up on the beach: a thing. A cloud: not a thing. A river: a thing, sort of, even though it's different water every second, which I realize opens a whole other can of worms. A swarm of bees: not a thing, many things. But a Portuguese man o' war — I looked this up after seeing one washed up, it's actually a colony of separate little animals that can't survive on their own — that is one thing, one animal, and I feel weirdly strongly about that. A cup with a handle that was glued on at the factory: one thing, the handle is part of the cup, no question. My two glued bricks: two things. A cat: one thing. The top half of a cat (god forbid): not a thing. Me and three friends holding hands: not a thing. A human pyramid: not a thing. A corporation, though, feels more like a thing than the pyramid does, and I suspect that's just me being spooked by legal paperwork rather than anything real.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? My hunch is it's something about the parts being joined *in the right way* — not mere contact, not even glue, but doing something together, having a job as a unit. But then I worry that's just "being alive" wearing a costume, and if I commit to that I have to give up the wall and the rock and the cup, which I definately won't do. So maybe I should go the other way and say anything whose parts are stuck together counts as one thing — in which case my glued bricks become one thing, and a wet clump of sand becomes one thing, and those verdicts have to go instead. I genuinely don't know which loss is less bad.

So: what principle, or small set of rival principles, actually captures this pattern, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't seen? For each candidate, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and be honest if the answer is "you can save everything only by adding a clause that does no work." Then tell me what your preferred principle says about three things I haven't thought about: a lichen (which I'm told is a fungus and an alga living as a single thing), a species, and a hurricane. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no throat-clearing, and don't restate my question back to me.
