I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book whose main character is a neuroanatomist, and she holds a view I've ended up half-believing myself: that brain size carries zero moral weight. Not that it carries less weight than we assume — zero. Absolute volume, mass, whatever you like; you can know every gram of someone's brain and it tells you nothing about what you owe them. Her set-piece is a thought experiment she calls the Cranial Audit — imagine an agency that can weigh every brain on Earth and has to hand out a fixed pot of moral consideration in exact proportion to the grams, no other information allowed. She thinks the Audit is obviously monstrous, and that the monstrosity isnt in the arithmetic but in the premise. I want to stress-test the whole thing. What would count against the claim that brain size is morally irrelevant? Not the obvious correlation-isnt-causation stuff, but the actual defeaters — cases, results, arguments — ranked from decisive to merely awkward. I'm especially interested in the spots where the defender has to bite a bullet: what does she have to say about brains that are small because of injury or disease, about animals, about whatever our extinct relatives were carrying around? And where a size difference tracks something else that does look morally relevant, does she get to say the something else is doing all the work and size is just along for the ride, or does that quietly smuggle size back in as evidence after all? Her position, and mine, is that brain size is irrelevant both intrinsically and as evidence — useless as a proxy for anything moral. If that's too strong, tell me where the line should go. And I'd definately like to know whether the view can be falsified at all, or whether it's been hedged into something no evidence could touch, and whether that's a problem or just the price of the claim. Say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
