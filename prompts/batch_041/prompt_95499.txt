I'm a journalist who writes about philosophy and the human sciences, and I've been asked to open a new discussion series for a group of intelligent generalists — a barrister, an oncologist, a couple of retired engineers; the sort of people who read the books but have never sat in a seminar. The topic for my session is Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and what I want from you is a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself: the order in which the pieces have to arrive so that the difficulty emerges from the audience's own commitments instead of descending as philosopher's apparatus, and an account of what depends on what — which idea is doing the load-bearing work, which is mere stage-setting, and which can be dropped at this level without loss.

I've decided the whole thing should hang on a single worked example, and I'm torn between the usual case of two things standing in a relation and something more tangible — a knot in a length of string, say, as against a loose heap of the same string, or the way the notes of a chord are heard as one sound rather than as a succession. Tell me which of these should carry the session, and what the chosen example must have and must lack if it is not quietly to presuppose the very unity it is meant to explain. The audience will arrive with things half-learned, and I'd like you to anticipate them: I'd guess that some will treat any infinite regress as automatically fatal and others as automatically harmless; that several will hear the problem as being about words and grammar rather than about what makes a proposition the one proposition it is; and that most will assume the conclusion that relations are not real is the upshot of the argument rather than a further step that can be refused. Say where in the sequence each of these should be headed off, and what I should actually say to the person who insists that a regress which never terminates is a triviality rather than a problem. I'd also like the session to close on a case I've invented rather than borrowed: ask the group to consider a proposition that would, if true, state its own unity — whether such a thing is even intelligible, and whether the regress simply begins again one level up. Then give me the single question I should put at the end to tell whether the difficulty has genuinely landed rather than merely been admired, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — I would rather say plainly that we are not going to settle whether the unity of the proposition is primitive or constructed than bluff my way through it.
