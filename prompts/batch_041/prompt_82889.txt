My friend and I spent most of an evening arguing over whether violence is in long-run decline, and we ended up further apart than we began — largely, I suspect, because we kept sliding between different things we meant by 'violence' and different numbers we took to settle the question. Write the joint statement we should have produced. Assume two signatories: a declinist who thinks the long-run trend is real and downward, and a definitional skeptic who thinks the trend is largely an artefact of shifting definitions and denominators; neither may help himself to the other's measure.

Open with a numbered section of claims both can sign: what the evidence for and against a long-run decline can and cannot support, given that the quality of the evidence itself changes across the centuries, and the distinction between a falling rate and a falling toll. Then set out, as a table, the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept: where the line between act and omission falls (famine, preventable disease, harm that accumulates slowly rather than in an event); whether a decline in lethality per assault reflects declining violence or improved trauma medicine; and whether per-capita rates spanning centuries are even well defined. For each disagreement, name the crux, give a case or body of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Close by separating the parts of our dispute that were verbal — artefacts of definitions adopted for different purposes — from those that were genuinely empirical or moral, and say whether, once the verbal parts are set aside, there is a fact of the matter about the original question or only a family of narrower questions with different answers.
