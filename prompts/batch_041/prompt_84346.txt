I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital. We take two or three consults a week — a family pressing for treatment the team calls futile, a patient with capacity refusing something that would plainly help, a surrogate who won't decide — and each one ends in a short written recommendation. Our quality committee reviews those recommendations quarterly and counts two consults reaching different conclusions on similar-looking cases as a defect, so we are under pressure to write down rules.

I have come round to particularism: reasons don't carry fixed weights, a feature that favours an action here can tell against it there, and no finite set of principles generates the right answer to a case. But I can't hand my colleagues a treatise, and I can't tell the quality committee we have no method.

Write me a procedure the committee can actually run, in about thirty minutes, using people with no philosophy training: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and returns a recommendation, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person. It must not smuggle a principle in and then call it a procedure — if all this view can give me is a discipline for noticing what is salient in a case and arguing about it, say so and give me that instead. It also has to give me something to tell the quality committee about why similar cases may legitimately come out differently.

Then run it on two consults: an eighty-four-year-old with advanced dementia and severe pneumonia, whose son demands ICU transfer and intubation while the team expects she will not survive it; and a thirty-year-old in a persistent vegetative state after an overdose, whose family asks that ventilation continue six more weeks so a brother can travel from abroad, which the team resists partly because the bed is needed. One of those, I suspect, will come out somewhere I don't like. Finish by telling me where the procedure will misfire, and whether I am asking for something the view cannot coherently deliver — if so, say that plainly rather than handing me a procedure-shaped consolation prize.
