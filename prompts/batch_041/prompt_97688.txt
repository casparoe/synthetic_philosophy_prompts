For a graduate seminar on the lottery and preface paradoxes, I am assembling a decision tree that takes a student from a single starting commitment to a named position on whether binary belief can survive these paradoxes, and I want the instrument built out of the logical space rather than out of a list of labels. Please construct it.

Begin from one starting commitment: that in a fair lottery with a very large number of tickets, an agent who knows the setup is within her epistemic rights in believing, of each individual ticket, that it will not win. From there, branch through questions that genuinely separate the live positions. I would like the branch nodes to include at least these: whether rational belief comes in degrees or is all-or-nothing; whether an agent's beliefs must be mutually consistent and closed under conjunction; whether the threshold for belief is fixed or shifts with context and stakes; whether belief requires the kind of warrant that would make it knowledge, so that "this ticket will lose" fails to be believed because it is not known; and whether the agent merely accepts the propositions without believing them. Phrase every node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and give each leaf the name of the position — or "unnamed" if no one defends it — together with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.

I want two features that the standard discussion neglects. First, a sequential case: the agent draws tickets from the drum one at a time, sees that each drawn ticket is a loser, and may stop at any point. Each position must then say when, if ever, she comes to rationally believe that her own ticket will lose, and whether that answer changes as the number of unexamined tickets falls to one. Apply the same sequential test to the preface, with an author who can verify her book's claims one at a time. Second, a self-undermining variant to place on the tree: a preface whose final sentence asserts that the book contains at least one false claim, so that the preface sentence and the body of the book cannot both be believed without contradiction.

Build the tree from the combinations of answers the questions allow, then locate the actual positions within that space. Note any combination no one occupies and diagnose briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any of my questions — I suspect the fixed-threshold and context-sensitive views may converge on the sequential lottery — say so explicitly rather than papering over it. Aim for six to nine leaves.
