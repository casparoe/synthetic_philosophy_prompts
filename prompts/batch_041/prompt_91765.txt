I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and I am setting the final essay. I want one question on Avicenna's flying man that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar claim that the argument anticipates Descartes or by paraphrasing the scenario. The question should force candidates to separate two claims the argument is often used to support: the epistemic claim that a subject's awareness of its own existence does not depend on any sensory or bodily input, and the stronger metaphysical claim that this awareness discloses a substantial immaterial self. To keep it from being a stock exercise, build the question around the following variant — the doubled flying man: two subjects are created at once and suspended side by side, qualitatively identical, each with no sensory input, no memory, and no awareness of the other, yet each, on the argument's own telling, would affirm its own existence. Candidates must say whether this doubles the argument's force, undercuts it, or merely relocates it — in particular whether the affirmation rests on the content of the thought that one exists or on the sheer indexicality of first-person awareness — and must draw out what this implies for the metaphysical reading. They should also engage the objection that the scenario begs the question because we imagine the subject trading on our own self-awareness, and judge whether the doubled case makes that objection better or worse.

Please write the exam question (no more than 120 words), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks that separates a first-class answer from a merely good one by naming the specific moves each must contain — the epistemic/metaphysical distinction, a definite verdict on the doubled case with a supporting reason, a position on the borrowed-consciousness objection, and at least one consequence for how the argument should be classified — and, for each must-hit point, one line on how candidates typically get it wrong. The candidates have no internet access, so the question must be answerable from the scenario and the argument alone and must not require citations.
