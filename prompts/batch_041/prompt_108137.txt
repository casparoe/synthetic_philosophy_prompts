I spent my career in the natural sciences and came to moral philosophy only in retirement, and I am temperamentally a consequentialist, so I want Anscombe's view of Truman tested rather than admired. Take the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki as the episode and her absolute prohibition on the intentional killing of the innocent as the lens, and map the two onto each other carefully. Under what description were the deaths of the inhabitants chosen, and does her distinction between the intended and the merely foreseen — or the doctrine of double effect — rescue the bombings? Then press the comparisons I cannot get straight: the firebombing of Japanese cities and the naval blockade, where the deaths are arguably foreseen rather than intended. Does her absolutism condemn the bombs while licensing the blockade, and if so, is that a genuine moral distinction or the theory's reductio? Tell me whether the usual defense that the bombings averted a worse catastrophe is even available to her, and whether her position can be held without collapsing into moralism. I know the historical dispute over whether the bombings were necessary is contested; say whether it is relevant on her view at all. End with a verdict — does her absolutism survive this case intact, need repair, or fail — and argue for it rather than surveying the options.
