I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, trying to state precisely what I think about when aggregating individual judgments is epistemically reliable. Here are my verdicts on a run of cases; I want the principle behind them, if there is one. A panel of a hundred members, each independently right on a yes/no question with probability 0.6 and all equally competent: I trust the majority far more than any one member. The same panel, once I learn their mistakes all flow from a single unreliable memo they each read beforehand: I stop trusting the majority and might as well consult one member. One expert who is right with probability 0.9 versus a three-member majority of members at 0.6: I take the expert. A panel of a thousand members each at 0.51: I expect the majority to be almost certainly right. A thousand members each at 0.51 whose votes are correlated: I don't. And when the question has three possible answers rather than two, I don't think the most popular answer is reliable in the way majority rule is for yes/no.

Is there one principle that delivers all of these? I suspect reliability has to turn on independence and on competence clearing chance, but I can't make the last two cases fall out cleanly, and I don't know what to say when competence is heterogeneous — say, half the panel at 0.7 and half at 0.45, so the average clears chance but the median doesn't. Please state the principle, or the candidates, precisely; tell me which of my verdicts it makes me give up; and say what it predicts for a case I haven't put to it — a panel whose members are individually mediocre on every question, but each is an expert on a different sub-question.
