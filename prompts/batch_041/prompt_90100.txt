I've talked myself into the self-indication assumption — the line that when I work out what my own existence tells me about the world, I should weight hypotheses by how many observers they contain, so a hypothesis with a trillion people is a trillion times better supported than one with just me. The usual grumbles (it kills off the doomsday argument, it makes the presumptuous philosopher sound sane) dont especially bother me. So what should actually move me off it? I want two specific things taken seriously. One: I've begun to suspect the real villain isn't indexicality at all but that SIA is simply an improper, unnormalisable prior over observer-moments, and the presumptuous philosopher is just the symptom of that rather than a problem about selves. Two: here's a case I cooked up — a god offers to make a trillion duplicates of me, each with a life identical to mine, but only if I press a button now. SIA seems to tell me that my present existence already counts as evidence that the duplicates exist, which is evidence I can't possibly have while the button is still unpressed. Real counterexample, or just evidential decision theory doing its ordinary work?

Sort the objections from decisive to merely embarrassing, say which ones a committed SIA defender would themselves grant rather than only what critics throw at them, and be straight with me about whether the view has any exposure left or has been walled off by patching. I'd personally be most swayed by something showing SIA can't be stated without quietly fixing the measure in advance — but tell me if that's actually the weaker line. British English, please.
