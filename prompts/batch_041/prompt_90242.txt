I got into a long argument with a couple of friends after a grad seminar on testimony, and I came away more convinced than I expected by the anti-reductionist side: the idea that testimony is a basic source of justification, that we're entitled to accept what someone tells us by default rather than having to independently vouch for each source before trusting it, and that the reductionist demand for prior evidence of reliability is both psychologically unrealistic and epistemically way too strict. I want you to stress-test that position for me. Not a summary of the debate and not a both-sides tour — a map of its exposure.

So: what would actually count against it? Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or cases should genuinely move me, and which are just noise that people dress up as objections? Sort the defeaters from fatal down through merely awkward, tell me which ones anti-reductionists themselves would grant as counting against them versus which they'd wave off, and say what the view predicts that its rivals don't. And be honest about whether the position has quietly been qualified into unfalsifiability — every counterexample met with "well, a defeater was available there" — and whether that's a real problem for a view of this kind or just the price of any default-entitlement story.

Here's the thing that's really bugging me and the reason I'm asking. I keep catching myself half-believing what chatbots tell me, in roughly the way I'd half-believe a person. If anti-reductionism is right that we're entitled to take an assertion on trust unless we've got a defeater, does that entitlement reach a machine that isn't a person, has no stake in getting things right, and was optimized to sound fluent and confident? If it does, I have a feeling that might be a reductio. If it doesn't, I want the principled line that excludes it, because "it's not a person" cant be the whole story, and I suspect whatever line does the work will end up telling us something about why we trust people at all. Work that case into your ranking rather than treating it as a footnote.
