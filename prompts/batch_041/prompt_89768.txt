I'm putting together a session for my philosophy of science reading group on the demarcation problem, and instead of lecturing at them I want a decision tree they can walk through themselves and land on a position. So: build the tree from the logical space first, and only then put names on the leaves. Start from things a person can answer without already knowing which position they're picking — is a sharp line between science and non-science something we should even expect to find, or is it a matter of degree; is the thing being judged a single hypothesis, a whole research programme, a field, or the conduct of a particular scientist; when a prediction fails, does that refute the theory or only the theory-plus-auxiliaries; and what is the criterion *for* — saying what science is, telling us how to do it, or deciding what gets taught and funded (that last distinction matters most for what I'm doing, so please make it do real work in the tree rather than being decoration). Leaves: naive falsificationism, the sophisticated version where you judge progressive vs degenerating research programmes, a paradigm-based Kuhnian account, a Bayesian degrees-of-credence account, the institutional or legal checklist (the kind a court or a school board would end up applying), and the view that the whole demarcation question is a psuedo-problem. Phrase every node as a question with a yes/no or a short menu of answers, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Two things I really want back: the combinations of answers nobody occupies, with your guess as to whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and any leaves your tree can't actually seperate — I suspect the two falsificationisms will collapse into each other and I'd rather know than pretend otherwise. Before you hand it over, run it on three cases and tell me where it strains: natal-chart astrology, a mathematically elegant theory whose predictions nobody can currently test but might be able to later, and a claim that makes no testable predictions at all but whose defenders insist it explains a lot. Last line: \answer{...} with the names of the leaves you couldn't tell apart.
