I'm trying to pin down when it is acceptable to settle a choice by an arbitrary procedure — a coin flip, a lottery, alphabetical order — and my verdicts refuse to line up.

Picking between two identical yogurts by coin: fine. Picking between two apartments I've inspected and like equally, by coin: fine, but only after I've looked for the detail I might have missed (one is on a noisy street, say); skipping that check and flipping feels wrong. Choosing between two equally qualified job candidates by coin: not fine, though I grant I have no preference between them. Choosing between two equally appealing partners by coin: not fine, and not even close. Choosing which of two drowning strangers to pull out when I can save only one: arbitrary choice is not merely permitted but required — refusing to pick is the worst option, and picking on a trivial tie-breaker, like who is wearing the nicer shirt, is also wrong. Choosing between medicine and music when the two seem genuinely on a par — neither better, yet not interchangeable the way the yogurts are — feels fine to settle by coin, but it also feels different from the yogurts, and I can't say how.

I keep reaching for two thoughts: that some options are equal while others are merely on a par, and that some choices merely allocate a good while others commit me to a person. I don't know which of these is doing the work, or whether the two are doing different work in different cases.

Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that captures all of these? State it precisely, say which verdict motivates each clause, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — settling by lottery which of two equally suitable patients receives a kidney, and which of two equally promising research directions to pursue — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.
