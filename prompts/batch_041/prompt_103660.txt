Me and my friend Dana argued for like two hours last night about superrationality and I'm still annoyed this morning because I'm pretty sure her version proves way too much and I couldn't pin down where it breaks. She laid it out like this:

(1) In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, if the other player is a perfect copy of you — same code, same inputs, deciding at the same moment — you should cooperate. That's the Hofstadter move.
(2) The reason is that if you cooperate, so does the copy, and R beats P. Her point is that the copy's choice is a function of the very same thing your choice is a function of.
(3) But physical sameness is irrelevent to (2). All that's doing the work is that your choice and theirs are both outputs of one shared procedure on the same inputs.
(4) So it should apply whenever your choice and theirs are both outputs of a shared cause, whatever that cause is — same training data, same culture, same genes, standing in the same room.
(5) Two people always share tons of causes, so their choices are always at least a little positively correlated.
(6) So in any one-shot PD against another person you should cooperate, even a stranger who's just going to take the money and run.
(7) Which is obviously wrong, you'd definately get eaten alive.

So which step do I give up, and what's it cost me? I really want to keep (1) — I've been a cooperator in these things since college and I don't think the fix is just "don't cooperate with strangers," because that feels like throwing out the whole idea. Dana thinks the problem is down at (5), I think it's higher up than that.

Give it back to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up. First point is the step I should drop; the rest are why that step looked fine and what I'm paying if I give it up — or what it costs if I keep it and drop something else instead.
