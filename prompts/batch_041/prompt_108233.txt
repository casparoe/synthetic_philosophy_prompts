You are a political philosopher and social epistemologist with a deep knowledge of Athenian institutions, democratic theory, and the epistemology of collective decision-making. I want you to think carefully rather than produce a survey. Begin by brainstorming several candidate readings of the material below — including at least one that treats the Athenians as having a coherent implicit theory and one that treats their practice as simply inconsistent — then settle on the reading you can best defend and argue for it.

Here is the pattern I want you to explain. In 427 BCE, the Athenian Assembly voted to put to death every adult male of Mytilene and enslave the women and children, then reconvened the following day and reversed the decree; a second ship was sent to overtake the first, and it reached the island only just in time, as the commander was on the point of executing the earlier order. In 399 BCE, a large jury of Athenian citizens convicted Socrates by a narrow margin, and then, after he responded to the prosecution's proposed penalty by suggesting that the city owe him public maintenance for the rest of his life, voted for death. Athenian juries had no appeal; once the Assembly had voted, its decree stood as law unless the Assembly itself chose to take the matter up again.

What conception of legitimacy does this cluster of practices presuppose? A natural first pass says the Athenians were pure proceduralists: a decision is legitimate because it was reached by the prescribed procedure, and there is no further question of whether it was right. But I cannot make that reading fit the whole pattern. The Mytilene reversal is usually treated as a credit to the democracy — an institution capable of correcting itself within a day — while the condemnation of Socrates is treated as its indictment, and the same proceduralism seems to underlie both. Is the difference merely in the outcomes we happen to approve of, or does the practice itself presuppose something that distinguishes the two cases: a distinction between a body's capacity to revisit its own decisions and a court's finality, or between collective deliberation and the judgment of a sworn jury?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Say what the practice strictly requires, what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it is underdetermined, so that several rival theories of legitimacy would license the same institutions. Then tell me what would have to change in Athenian practice if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which legitimacy also depends on the decision's revisability or its epistemic quality — and whether such a replacement could have preserved the virtues of the Assembly's reversal without the vices of the jury's finality, or whether the two are two faces of a single commitment.
