I am a high-school senior taking an elective in political philosophy, and my teacher has us mark one another's mock examination papers before he marks them himself. The paper he set was on precommitment and constitutional entrenchment, and I have been assigned a classmate's script to mark out of fifteen. I would like a full assessment rather than a general impression, because the comments go back to the writer and the teacher will compare my mark with his own.

The question he set was this (Section B, 15 marks):

(a) Explain what a Ulysses contract is supposed to do for a single agent, and why a political community is often said to need the same kind of device. (6 marks)

(b) The constitution of the Republic of Valdania, adopted in 1991 after a period of hyperinflation, contains Article 47, which fixes the formula for indexing pensions. Article 47 can be amended only by a two-thirds majority in two successive parliaments, followed by a confirmatory referendum. In 2019 a government with a clear electoral mandate tried to amend it and lost the referendum. Discuss whether Article 47 can properly be described as the Valdanian people binding itself. Say what the Ulysses analogy is meant to establish, where it breaks down, and what follows from the breakdown. Use the individual case to test the collective one. (9 marks)

The answer I have to mark reads as follows.

(a) A Ulysses contract is an arrangement by which an agent who knows that her future self will have different preferences takes steps now to stop the future self from acting on them. Ulysses has himself tied to the mast so that he can hear the Sirens without steering for the rocks. The interesting case is not ordinary weakness of will but preference reversal: at t1 the agent wants not to do X, at t2 she will want to do X, and binding herself is rational at t1 because the t2 preference is the one she has most reason to distrust. The device can work either by making the later action physically impossible or by making it expensive.

Communities are said to face the same structure. A government under the pressure of an election or a panic will want to do things that the same public, at a cooler moment, would reject, and a constitution that is harder to amend than ordinary law is supposed to be that cooler moment made permanent. Article 47 is a good example: it removes the indexation formula from ordinary politics, and the two-successive-parliaments requirement forces the question to be put twice, at an interval, with a referendum at the end. That is what happened in 2019: the government had a mandate and still could not change the rule, because the referendum defeated it.

(b) The analogy is meant to establish that a decision taken at one time can legitimately bind a decision taken at a later time by the same agent. It seems to me to break down in three places. First, in the individual case the binding is entirely self-imposed: nobody else's will is involved, and the agent remains free to release herself if she changes her mind. In the political case the constraint is imposed from outside, by the 1991 assembly and then by the courts, on citizens who took no part in it. Second, an individual's preferences are hers alone, whereas a people has no single will: in 2019 a parliamentary majority wanted the amendment and a referendum majority did not, and there is no fact of the matter about which of these is the people. Third, the individual at t1 and the individual at t2 are at least the same person, whereas the later generation is a different set of people, and it is unclear why the dead should govern the living.

The second of these seems to me fatal to the analogy, and it also shows why Article 47 cannot really constrain anyone: since no legislature can bind its successors, a determined parliamentary majority can always change the rule in the end, and the referendum requirement adds little, since a referendum is only another majority and merely relocates the decision instead of entrenching it. The clause is therefore best described as the dead hand of the 1991 legislature. The counter-majoritarian objection, that entrenchment lets a past majority defeat a present one, is the strongest argument against it. And since the analogy breaks down, entrenchment cannot be justified by appeal to a people's own long-term interest.
