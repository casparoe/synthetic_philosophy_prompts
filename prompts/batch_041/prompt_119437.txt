Two weeks ago my partner and I were given two different numbers for the same unborn child in the space of an afternoon. The first counsellor put us in the class of women my age and said one in a few hundred; the second put us in the class of pregnancies with this particular measurement on the scan and said something closer to one in forty. Neither was being careless. Each was answering the question, what is the frequency of this outcome in this group, and the groups were simply not the same group. I am in the final year of a philosophy degree, so I recognised the shape of it at once: a probability for a single case only exists relative to a class somebody has chosen, we can always choose a narrower class, and if we narrow far enough the class contains exactly one member, at which point the frequency is either nought or one and the number has stopped telling us anything. What I did not expect was how little the recognition helped. This has stopped being a puzzle about the foundations of probability and become something I turn over at three in the morning.

I would like a poem out of it, and I want the poem's form to carry the argument rather than describe it. Please write me a pantoum: six quatrains, the second and fourth lines of each quatrain returning as the first and third of the next, and the final quatrain taking its repeated lines from the opening quatrain so that the poem ends by handing back its own beginning. The form is the whole point, because a pantoum repeats lines word for word while their sense shifts as the context around them changes, and that is precisely what happens to us as we are sorted into one class and then another. So I want the repetitions to do real work. At least one line should recur in such a way that on its first appearance it reads as a claim about everybody and on a later appearance as a claim about one person, the words unaltered. The poem should move the way the afternoon moved: from the wide class, to my age band, to this measurement, to my own history, and at last to a class with a single member, where the statistic dies in my hands. Then the circular close should feel like a trap rather than a comfort — the wide class swallowing the narrow one again, with nothing settled. Please do not let the poem resolve which class was the right one, or suggest that more data would settle it, or end on consolation. Do not use the term of art and do not have the speaker explain the problem to the reader; the two numbers and the narrowing should be enough. It should be in the first person, in my voice or in the voice of someone sitting in that room, and a specialist in the foundations of probability should recognise the problem without being told its name, while someone who has never heard of it should still find it a good poem. Rhyme is optional; the recurrences are not.

Begin the response with the poem itself, with no preface, no note on the form before it and no explanation of what you have done. If you want to add a few sentences afterwards about which recurring lines are doing which work, that is fine, but keep them brief and put them after the poem.
