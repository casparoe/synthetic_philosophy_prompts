I am a software engineer designing a decision-making feature for a collaborative project management tool. The feature lets a team of, say, 8 to 15 people choose among a set of proposed project directions. Each person submits a ranked list of the proposals, but they can also mark any proposal as unacceptable—meaning that if that proposal is selected, they would rather the team select nothing and continue deliberating. The system then applies a rule: it selects the proposal that is ranked highest on average, but only if no more than one person has marked it unacceptable; otherwise, it selects nothing. I have been reading about the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and want to understand its most important implications for this design. Specifically, I am trying to figure out: (1) whether the theorem applies to this rule at all, given that the rule can output nothing and is therefore not obviously onto the set of proposals; (2) if it does apply, what the theorem implies about whether users can benefit from misrepresenting their true rankings or their true acceptability judgments; and (3) what practical lessons follow for designing a mechanism that is as resistant to strategic manipulation as possible, even if strict strategy-proofness is unattainable. I do not need a formal proof, but I would like a careful conceptual explanation that distinguishes between the theorem's exact conditions and the looser, real-world goal of making a system hard to game. Please also comment on whether adding the unacceptable option changes the strategic landscape in a way that is not captured by the standard theorem.
