I have recently taken a seat on the ethics advisory board of a public agency that is about to deploy a package of behavioral interventions: an automatic-enrollment default for a savings plan, a simplified benefits form, and — the item I am least comfortable with — an "adaptive" default that revises itself as a participant opts in and out over time. Before we vote on any of it, I want a specification of what an adequate theory of permissible choice-steering must deliver, rather than another pile of examples.

Set out the desiderata such a theory has to meet: the cases it must get right, each with the case that motivates it; the distinctions it must respect; and the empirical findings it must be consistent with. I am especially interested in five things. First, how it draws the line between steering a choice and coercion, deception, and the plain provision of information. Second, whether a covert nudge and a disclosed one fall under a single justification or must be ranked, and on what basis. Third, whether an adaptive default that learns from a person's own opt-outs is a different kind of act from a fixed default — and if so, what makes it different. Fourth, what the theory says when the preference it is supposed to respect is itself the residue of earlier steering, so that the justification threatens to regress. And fifth, whether it must reckon with the fact that a nudge can help most participants while imposing costs on a few, and that it tends to work best on those least equipped to resist it.

Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a commitment of one camp, and tell me whether any pair of them — transparency and demonstrated efficacy, say — is jointly unsatisfiable. A short scorecard against the leading accounts is welcome, but the list is what I need.
