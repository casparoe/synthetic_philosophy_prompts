I've been volunteering for about six months as a behaviour assessor at a mid-sized animal shelter, and I've slowly become convinced that the form we fill out rests on a mistake I can feel but can't articulate. Each dog gets a forty-five-minute assessment: a stranger walks past the kennel, a fake hand comes near the food bowl, a decoy dog is walked by, someone reaches for a toy. We then score the dog on things like stranger-directed aggression, resource guarding, and handling tolerance, and the scores feed a one-page sheet that the director and the rescue-transfer coordinator use to route the dog to the adoption floor, to a breed rescue, or onto the euthanasia list. Volunteers with a two-hour training do the scoring; the director signs off. The sheet is written as though each of these were a simple if-then fact about the animal — if you reach for the bowl, the dog bites — and after a stretch of bad outcomes (the dog I ended up adopting came with a clean sheet and turned out to be terrified of men; a dog I argued hard to save was flagged as aggressive and, I'm fairly sure, was just miserable in a kennel) I went looking for something better and stumbled into the metaphysics of dispositions. I'm now convinced the simple conditional is just wrong in ways that matter for us. A glass is fragile even if it never breaks; a sugar cube dipped in wax is still soluble even though it will not dissolve; and a glass can be fitted with a mechanism that keeps it intact at the very instant it is struck, so that it never breaks even though it seems to have been fragile all along. I gather the standard vocabulary distinguishes masking, where something stands between the disposition and its manifestation without removing the disposition, from finkishness, where the disposition itself is removed (or its basis acquired) exactly when the stimulus arrives, and from the further case of something that behaves the way the disposition would have made it behave without having the disposition at all, because some one-off mechanism produced the behaviour. I have not read the literature carefully and I do not want a reading list or citations — I want the distinctions turned into something my team can use.

Please write me a decision procedure our assessors can actually run on a single dog, in the time and with the expertise we have. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure is entitled to stop with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person — the director on the risk question, a staff behaviourist on the cases a volunteer cannot settle. It has to tell the difference between three things that currently look identical on our sheet: a dog whose aggression is genuinely absent, a dog whose aggression is present but masked by the assessment conditions themselves (the strange room, the handler, the kennel stress), and a dog whose aggression is finkish — present, but suppressed by the very act of assessing it, so that a clean score tells us nothing or worse than nothing. It also has to catch the mimic case, the dog that bit once because of an ear infection or a slipped harness rather than because of any standing trait, and say what evidence would license calling a single episode a disposition rather than an accident. Where a discriminating test exists that a volunteer could run, say what it is and how to run it; where it doesn't, say so plainly and tell me what to do instead. Then run the procedure end to end on three cases: a dog we scored as guarding food that I suspect was masking, a dog that scored perfectly and, I think, was finking us, and a dog where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — I'd rather see the case where the machine tells me my favourite is not safe than three cases where it flatters me.

Two things I care about more than elegance. First, be explicit about which distinctions have to survive the compression into a form and which can be dropped without loss — I'd rather lose a step than keep a step that pretends to be a test when it is really a vibe. Second, tell me honestly where the procedure will misfire, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, what it cannot possibly settle (I suspect the question of how much residual risk is acceptable is not a dispositional question at all, but say so), and what a volunteer should write on the form when the honest answer is that the disposition is present but the conditions of assessment made it impossible to observe. Assume the person running it has the two-hour training, not a degree, and assume the director has to be able to defend the resulting decision to a board that will ask why we put a dog down or sent it home.
