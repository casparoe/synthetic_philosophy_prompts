I have been working through a puzzle on my own, without any training in philosophy, and I would like you to build me a case rather than tell me about one. The puzzle concerns two distinctions that almost everyone treats as a single distinction but that I have come to suspect are two. The first is the difference between doing something and merely letting it happen. The second is the difference between killing someone and letting someone die. In ordinary speech the two lines are drawn in the same place: if you do it, you kill; if you let it happen, you let die. I want a case that pulls the two lines apart, so that a single person's single piece of behaviour falls on one side of the first distinction and the opposite side of the second.

Please construct one realistic situation — a hospital ward, a workplace, a household, nothing with levers, runaway vehicles, or devices that make the choice on the agent's behalf — in which either (a) the agent plainly does something, and yet what the agent does is, on the best reading, only letting die, or (b) the agent plainly refrains from doing anything, and yet that refraining is, on the best reading, a killing. Give the scenario in a single paragraph, with the death's actual causal history laid out clearly enough that nothing needs to be stipulated. If you think one of these two directions cannot be made to work while the other can, say which and why, rather than manufacturing the harder one.

I have a guess about what does the work, and I would like you to test it. My guess is that the hinge is not whether the agent moved but whether the lethal process was already under way before the agent became involved, and, relatedly, whether the agent removed a barrier that had been holding the threat back or instead failed to supply a barrier that was never there. Tell me whether that guess survives contact with your case or whether the real hinge lies elsewhere.

After the scenario, please do the following, in order. First, describe the agent's behaviour twice, once in the vocabulary of someone who thinks the important line is doing versus allowing, and once in the vocabulary of someone who thinks the important line is killing versus letting die, and show that the two descriptions classify the same behaviour differently. Second, state the verdict each of those two outlooks gives, and make plain that the verdicts follow from how the death comes about rather than from my say-so or yours. Third, name the one feature of the case that is doing the work. Fourth, remove that feature, or reverse it, and show the two lines snapping back into agreement. Fifth, say how someone convinced that the two distinctions really are one would attack the case — whether by redescribing the behaviour as an act rather than an omission, or the reverse, or by disputing which event was the death's cause — and whether that attack succeeds. Finally, tell me whether the case shows the two distinctions to be genuinely independent, or only that our words for them are loose.

I am not after references, names, or a reading list; I have no library at hand and would rather you reason it out from the case itself.
