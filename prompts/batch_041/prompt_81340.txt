I'm setting the final paper for a masters module on the foundations of inference — mostly Bayesian stuff, a bit of information theory, the usual. I need one essay question, 25 marks, 90 minutes, on whether Occam's razor is objective, and I'm sick of the answer I keep getting. It's always the same two pages regurgitated: short programs are a priori more probable, so the razor is derived rather than assumed, thank you very much, see you next term. I want a question where that's the first sentence of a good answer and nowhere near the last.

So I'm going to bake a made-up case into the question itself so they can't just recite. Here's the shape of it. Fix some universal machine U. Now build a rival, U*, which is U with a big table of hints bolted onto the front: the table lists a thousand particular data sets and gives, for each one, a very short program that prints it out. On those thousand data sets U* says "simple" where U says "hopelessly complex". U* isn't a cheat — it's as legitimate a universal machine as U is; it just pays for its hints by being a fixed number of bits worse everywhere else. And the trick cuts both ways: for any finite batch of data at all, you can build a machine under which your pet hypothesis comes out shortest. So "shortest program" only fixes an ordering once you've fixed a machine, and nothing in the formalism fixes the machine.

I want the question to force three things. First, say precisely what the apparatus does give you — the mixture over all programs, the invariance result (the claim that complexity judgements under different machines agree up to an additive constant), and what that constant is actually allowed to be. Second, use the U/U* business to work out what that buys you when your data set is finite, which is the only kind anyone has ever had. Third, make a call: does this vindicate the razor, relocate the assumption somewhere else, or neither — and if it relocates it, say exactly where it's gone. There should also be a bit on the fact that the guarantees are all asymptotic, i.e. about what happens in the limit, and whether "in the limit" is the sort of thing that could ever guide anyone who has to make a decision on Tuesday.

And I want the other lazy answer blocked as well: "well, simplicity is subjective, so the razor is just taste" is no good either, and the question should definately make that obvious without saying it.

1. the question as it'd actually appear on the paper;
2. a model answer, roughly 600 words, pitched at a strong masters student;
3. a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a first-class answer has to make, and — this is the bit I care about — a one-line note for each on how students typically miss it. Don't pad it out with "clear structure" and "good spelling".

Two things. No asking them for names, dates or reading — I'm not testing bibliography and half of them will get there attributions wrong anyway. And write the whole thing in British English, model answer included.
