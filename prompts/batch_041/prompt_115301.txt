I'm in a third-year political theory course and we've just done the Ulysses thing — the guy has his crew tie him to the mast so he can hear the sirens without jumping in, and they're under orders not to untie him no matter what he says — and the prof used it to introduce constitutional entrenchment (rights that a bare majority can't touch, supermajority amendment rules, that kind of thing). My study group has an argument that I think proves way too much, but I can't find the bad step. Here it is:

(1) A precommitment is rational when you know that at some later time you'll be in a state — a craving, a panic, a passion — that will make you act against what you now, calmly, judge to be your own good, and you can arrange things now so that the later you can't act on it. (2) What justifies the arrangement is that the later state distorts the later judgment, so the calm judgment is the better guide to what the later person actually wants over the whole of their life. (3) Nothing in (2) depends on how much later the later time is; a craving next Tuesday and a craving in thirty years get the same treatment. (4) So the same reasoning justifies a founding generation entrenching constraints that later generations can't easily remove: the founders are the calm ones, the future citizenry will have there own panics and passions. (5) And it justifies any current majority entrenching constraints on the next majority, which then does the same to the one after it, and so on forever. (6) So no generation can ever be governed by its own considered judgment — it's always governed by the calm judgment of some earlier generation, all the way back. (7) But a people that can never revise its fundamental law isn't self-governing at all; that's just the dead hand ruling. So something above has to go.

I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, because without them I don't see how any precommitment ever works, and I do think the guy tying himself to the mast was being smart. So: where's the bad step, and what does giving it up cost me? I have three suspects and I can't tell which one is the real one. Maybe it's the move from one person to a whole people, i.e. the analogy is just broken somewhere around (4), though I can't see why the structure would care about the number of people involved. Maybe it's (6), which sneaks in "can't" where the truth is only "can only with a lot of trouble," and if that's it then is there any principled place to stop entrenching, or do we just entrench "as much as is worth it" and hope. Or maybe it's (2), where "what the later person actually wants over the whole of their life" is quietly presupposing a single unified subject whose good can be added up over decades, and that's exactly the thing the intergenerational case can't supply.

The other thing that keeps nagging me, and I don't know if it's the same problem or a seperate one: if the later me is the same person as the earlier me, then the binding is just a decision I'm allowed to revisit and it isn't binding at all; if the later me is a different person, then one person is coercing another, which sounds like exactly the paternalism we're supposed to be objecting to. I feel like the whole thing lives in that fork and I can't tell which horn my prof wants.
