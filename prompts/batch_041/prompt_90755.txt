So a couple of friends and I have been running a little Lakatos reading group that has quietly turned into a writing group — three of us, weekly, and we've basically committed the next two years to it with a vague plan of producing two or three papers and maybe one co-authored thing. None of us are a historian of science by training; I do philosophy of science, one of us is a mathematician, and the third works on ML interpretability. We've gotten past the obvious scaffolding (hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristics, progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts) and we keep landing on the same pile of half-formed worries, and I'm worried we'll spend two years circling them without ever deciding what we're actually claiming. What I want from you is an agenda: given that this is three people for two years, which open questions in the Lakatosian framework most deserve our effort, ranked, with the reasoning spelled out — which ones are upstream of the others, which are tractable with the tools we actually have (close reading of case studies, some formal modeling, no archival work), which are already being chewed over so thoroughly that a small group adds little, and which sound deep but wouldn't change anything downstream if they were settled.

Let me seed you with the things we keep arguing about so you can react to them instead of starting from scratch. (1) Whether a novel fact should be understood temporally, as something predicted before it was known, or in terms of use, as something not used in constructing the theory — and whether that choice even changes the progressive/degenerating verdict or just changes which episodes get the label. (2) Whether degenerating is a retrospective historiographic judgment or something a working scientist could in principle detect in real time, i.e. whether it's a normative claim about rationality or just a description of a pattern of resource allocation under uncertainty. (3) Whether the hard core is held by convention or by commitment — the Popperian versus the Quinean reading — and what actually hangs on picking one. (4) Whether the whole apparatus survives being pointed at a computational research programme; the ML person keeps asking whether degenerating just means a field is throwing compute at a anomaly, and whether we'd be forced to say the same about hyperparameter tuning. (5) Whether rival programmes are ever really compared on a common measure, or whether the methodology quietly presupposes that they share enough of a problem-set for the comparison to mean anything. For each question you keep, tell me what a two-year project on it would concretely look like — what case study or formalization would move it, what the deliverable would be, and what argument or piece of history over the next couple of years would push it up or down our list. I'd rather have three questions defended properly than ten gestured at, so feel free to tell me some of these are dead ends; just say why.
