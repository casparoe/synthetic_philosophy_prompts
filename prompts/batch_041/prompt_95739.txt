I'm assembling a humor feature for the newsletter of a political-economy reading group, and what I want is sustained irony rather than a string of gags. Please write, in the deadpan register of an internal vision document — the kind of memo a founder fires off to staff the night before a rebrand — a numbered manifesto (a short preamble plus roughly a dozen principles) issued by a fictional startup that has automated almost its entire operation and now presents itself, entirely in earnest, as the living realization of the "general intellect" that Marx sketches in the section on machinery in the Grundrisse, the stretch of the 1857–58 notebooks usually called the Fragment on Machines. Aim for about 700 words and format it as clauses. The founder is not a cynic and never breaks character: he takes himself to be a Marxist, and every boast he makes should be one that a reader who knows the relevant political economy can watch cutting against him. Have him sincerely celebrate having abolished necessary labour time while the revenue model quietly depends on a standing reserve of wage-dependent subscribers; have him conflate real subsumption with formal subsumption, as though putting a dashboard on a process were the same as transforming it; have him call the machine the accumulation of social knowledge while his terms of service patent that knowledge as private property; and have him price the product in a currency he calls labour-time credits and report, as his headline metric, the socially necessary labour time each seat has saved. At least once, let a fictional works-council delegate get a sentence in edgewise, and let the founder quote that sentence approvingly in a way that inverts its meaning.

A few constraints. Do not include any citations, reading lists, dates, or page references, and do not put any real passage in quotation marks — if you need Marx's ideas, paraphrase them in your own words rather than reaching for a line you'd have to reconstruct. I'd rather the piece stand on the argument than on erudition, so nothing should depend on a specific text you'd have to look up; the Grundrisse material is there to be used, not footnoted. The target of the satire is the practice of tech capital dressing its automation up in Marxian language — not Marx, and not any living person — and the ideal reader is someone who finishes the memo recognizing that the author understands the contradiction being staged, while a reader who has never opened Marx can still follow what is happening.

When you're done, add a short note outside the manifesto itself. In it, say how confident you are that the parody is fair to Marx's actual position on machinery and automation, and single out the one interpretive claim in the piece you're least sure of — for instance, whether the Fragment on Machines really licenses the automation-optimism the founder voices, or whether that reading is a later imposition on the text.
