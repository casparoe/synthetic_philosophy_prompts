Ok so this is a slightly odd one. I'm in my third year doing philosophy and I do two days a week at a materials testing lab as a placement — they certify stuff like coatings, foams, fabrics, seals — and my metaphysics module has basically ruined the job for me. We did dispositions, finks and masks, and now I can't unsee it: the lab's whole method assumes that if a sample fails the test, the sample doesn't have the property. Which is exactly the thing the seminar spent two weeks dismantling.

The lab has this in-house shorthand for a failure: 'gone', 'blocked', or 'never there'. 'Never there' is a real absence, 'blocked' is when the property is still in the specimen but something else stops it showing up under the test conditions, and 'gone' is when the act of testing itself destroyed whatever would have done the job. Nobody can definately tell me how they decide which one it is — as far as I can work out it's vibes plus 'run it again twice'. And 'gone' and 'blocked' aren't even really the same phenomenon, I think, because in one case the test is part of the trigger and in the other the test is just an obstacle, and that seems to matter for what we're allowed to put in the certificate.

What I'd like from you is a procedure I can actually hand to the technicians — questions to ask in order about a failed sample, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it's allowed to stop and say the disposition is genuinely absent, where it has to say 'this test can't tell us' and send the sample back for a redesigned test, and where it has to go to the lab manager because it's a judgement about what we're willing to certify rather than a fact about the specimen. Keep it in their vocabulary — trigger, conditions, specimen, certificate — not the seminar's, otherwise I'll just be That Student. It needs to be runnable in about twenty minutes per sample, because we do maybe a dozen a week, there's alot riding on each one, and the customer is waiting.

Then run it on three, and be specific:

1. A water-repellent coating on tent fabric. It passes the beading test on a fresh sample but fails after the abrasion step in the standard cycle. The abrasion is part of the same test protocol.
2. A flame-retardant foam. Small coupons pass easily; full-size panels of the same batch fail. Same material, same protocol, different geometry.
3. A shock-absorbing helmet liner. Passes everything at 20 °C, fails everything at −10 °C. The spec sheet just says 'shock absorbing'.

I'd bet the third one is the interesting one and I suspect the procedure is going to tell us something we don't want to hear about the spec, which is fine — I'd rather find that out now than in a meeting. At least one of these should come out undecidable, so please don't fudge it into a clean verdict.

The conditions we impose are in the trigger, and once you've noticed that, I don't know whether the fink/mask vocabulary even has a place to put it, or whether it quietly assumes there's a clean seperation between the disposition, the trigger and the interference. If it doesn't have room, say so rather than patching it. And tell me where the procedure just stops working and someone has to make a call.
