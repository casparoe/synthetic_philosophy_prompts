I've been going back and forth with a friend in my uni's animal-ethics reading group about something that's been bugging me. Where I study, if you want to run a study on mice or zebrafish you have to get it past the animal care committee — justify the numbers, show you're using anaesthesia where you can, spell out humane endpoints, the whole rigmarole. But a lab down the hall that rears millions of fruit flies, or breeds crickets as feed for the fish facility, doesn't need approval for any of that, and nobody I've asked thinks it should. And honestly, most people who are pretty serious about animal welfare — vegetarians, people who'd never touch veal — will swat a mosquito or drown a wasp without a second thought, and it doesn't strike them as hypocrisy.

What I keep chewing on is an argument another friend made: if there's even a small chance insects are sentient — say you put it at 5 percent, or 1 percent — then the sheer number of them means the expected suffering dwarfs everything we care about for mammals, so one percent times an astronomically large number is still enormous and should dominate our priorities. If that reasoning goes through, then our actual practice of drawing the line at vertebrates (and cephalopods, if we're being fancy) looks like it's presupposing something pretty specific — maybe that uncertain sentience gets zero weight rather than a discounted weight, or that moral standing requires clearing some threshold probability, or that this is a taxonomic question we can settle once and be done with. So what I want from you is this: reconstruct the theory our actual practice — committees for mice, nothing at all for insects, casual mosquito-killing — has to be commited to in order to be coherent. Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Is it committed to a probability threshold, or could a view that genuinely takes expected numbers seriously end up licensing the very same practice, just for different reasons? And is there a real inconsistency with the way the same institutions happily invoke the precautionary principle elsewhere, where a tiny chance of something catastrophic really does drive policy? Tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, and what — if anything — would have to change if we took the scale argument at face value.
