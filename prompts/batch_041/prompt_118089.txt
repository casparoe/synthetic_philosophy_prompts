I'm making a handout for a first-year ethics seminar on white lies -- the 'fine, thanks' when you're not fine, the haircut compliment, that kind of thing. A Dutch friend of mine insists that hedging is just dishonesty and a colleague who grew up in Osaka thinks bluntness is plain rude, and I've realised they're running different theories of what politeness is even for, so I want the handout to sort the positions rather than just list them. Please write it as a taxonomy in tree form: each branch labelled with the question that splits it from its siblings, each leaf carrying the position's name (or 'unnamed' if nobody actually defends it) plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Seven leaves max.

The distinction I most want the tree to make is between 'it isn't a lie at all', 'it's a lie but permissible', 'it's wrong but outweighed here', and 'there's no separate rule, tact is just part of honesty'. So the branch nodes should include whether convention can change what a sentence means, whether politeness is a moral requirement or only a social one, whether the hearer's expectations or consent matter, and whether what matters is being truthful or being someone people can rely on. Nodes have to be answerable by someone who doesn't already know the labels -- use concrete cases: 'fine, thanks', 'we're busy that night' when you're not, a translator softening a blunt assessment for a client who'd be humiliated by the full version, a doctor being cheerful at a bedside, a kid's terrible drawing.

Flag any leaves the tree can't seperate, and for combinations of answers that nobody occupies, say whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unexplored. One case I want it to handle: a chatbot instructed to be warm saying 'great question' to a terrible question -- is that a white lie on any leaf, or does the consent-to-the-register line make it not a lie by definition, and if so is that line just a machine for making everything permissible?
