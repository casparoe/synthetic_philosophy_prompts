I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a political economist whose book argues that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is routinely over-read as a counsel of despair: he holds that each of the familiar escape routes — restricting the domain of admissible preferences, letting the mechanism also hand out money, weakening the demand that honesty be best no matter what everyone else does to the demand that misrepresentation pay only in configurations the designer can steer away from, and keeping participants ignorant of others' reports — is genuinely available, so the theorem's real content is a price list rather than an impossibility. Draft eight to ten questions that make him name those prices. Include at least one question each on whether the monetary escape is an escape or a change of subject; on whether trading dominant-strategy incentive compatibility for an equilibrium-based notion merely relocates the problem into the selection of which stable outcome occurs; and on deliberative democrats who deny the fixed-preference premise, asking whether deliberation is a counterexample to the theorem or just one more mechanism inside its scope — and for each of these, a follow-up aimed at the reply he is most likely to give. Then put one concrete new case to him: a citizens' assembly choosing among budget packages whose members care not only about the outcome but about whether their final vote matches the position they argued for during deliberation, and ask whether that kind of preference makes manipulation more attractive, less attractive, or beside the point, and what it does to the price-list reading. Close with what would count, for him, as an escape route too expensive to take — that is, what would show the theorem is not merely a price list.
