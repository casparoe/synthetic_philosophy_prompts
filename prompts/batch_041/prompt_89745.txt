I'm a second-year law student in a jurisprudence seminar, and my paper is on the legitimacy of judicial review. I don't want to write another essay on whether it is democratic; I want the prior specification — what would a theory of judicial review have to deliver in order to count as adequate for a particular purpose? My purpose is narrow and practical: a theory good enough to guide a judge who is deciding whether to refuse to apply a statute on constitutional grounds, and good enough to guide a legislator deciding how much weight to give a court's constitutional ruling. Give me the desiderata list — the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the institutional facts it must not contradict — with the consideration behind each requirement, and say explicitly which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp in the existing dispute.

To keep the list concrete, it has to cover at least the following, and for each I want you to say what exactly it demands of a theory. First, a theory may not assume that the constitution authorises courts to have the last word, since that is the very thing in dispute. Second, the countermajoritarian objection, and with it the question of why judicial error should be preferred to legislative error, if it should — which forces the theory to say something about the direction in which each institution is likely to err. Third, the remedial question: refusing to enforce a statute, suspending its operation, declaring it incompatible while leaving it in force, and reading it down are different acts with different justifications, and a theory that licenses one need not license the others; the same goes for the norm of avoiding constitutional questions and for severability, which seem to trade off against any duty the court has to declare the law rather than dodge it. Fourth, the fact that the same disagreement is settled differently in different institutional settings, including arrangements in which the legislature can override a rights ruling by ordinary majority and arrangements in which the court's only remedy is a formal declaration addressed to the legislature. Fifth, the recurrence problem: what the theory says when a legislature re-enacts substantially the same statute after a court has struck it down, or when a lower court is convinced the highest court got a constitutional question wrong. Sixth, the distinction between provisions that protect individuals against the state and provisions that allocate power among organs of government, and whether the justification for judicial enforcement is the same in both. Then tell me which requirements do the most work in excluding the main candidate positions — originalist accounts, living-constitutionalist accounts, the view that courts should police only the openness of the political process, the view that each branch should construe the constitution for itself, and the various arrangements in which courts lack the power to invalidate legislation outright, sometimes called weak-form review — and, above all, whether any pair or subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and what that shows. I would rather the list itself be the result than a scorecard, but a short closing paragraph on how the main positions fare would help me see which requirements are doing the work. No citations or reading list, please; I will supply those myself.
