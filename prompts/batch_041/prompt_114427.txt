I spent thirty years doing bench chemistry before I retired, and I've been reading moral philosophy for about two years now. There's a move I keep running into that I can't find a name for. Somebody shows that a theory requires the agent to have a certain thought at a certain moment — the parent who stops to ask whether partiality to her own child is permitted before she runs to help; the friend who checks whether loyalty obliges him to do this particular favor; the person who forgives a wrongdoer because a principle says forgiveness is owed — and the objection is never that the thought produces the wrong answer. The act is usually exactly what the theory prescribes. The objection is that needing the thought at all is already the failure: that a person who deliberated this way would be standing outside her own life, or would have turned the relationship into something else by the very act of weighing it. It isn't the complaint that the theory asks too much of you, and it isn't the old point that a right act done from a cold motive earns no credit — here the motive is one the theory itself hands you, and the claim is that being the sort of agent who has it is itself the loss. Is there a settled name for this? A name alone won't satisfy me. If it's one thing rather than several, what makes these instances instances of it, and where does it stop — does it survive the standard reply that the theory doesn't ask you to deliberate this way in the moment, that it's only an acount of what's permitted afterwards? That reply seems to kill some of my examples and not others, and I want to know why.
