I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on formal epistemology, and I've been sitting in on a seminar on the philosophy of mathematics. For our next session — on whether the incompleteness theorems have any bearing on computational theories of mind — a colleague in cognitive science circulated a one-page handout summarising the theorems and their philosophical upshot. He is a careful reader but not a logician, and I would like to know how much of the handout I can take at face value before I let it shape what I say in the seminar. The relevant passage reads:

'Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that any consistent formal system rich enough to express arithmetic contains true sentences that it cannot prove. The second theorem tightens the screw: such a system cannot prove its own consistency, so no system can underwrite itself, and Hilbert's programme of securing mathematics by finitary means is finished. Gödel's original proof needed the strong assumption of ω-consistency, but Rosser showed shortly afterwards that ordinary consistency will do, so the result now holds for any consistent system that can do arithmetic, however it happens to be axiomatised. The proof turns on a sentence that, in effect, asserts its own unprovability, which makes it a precise version of the ancient liar paradox. Since that sentence is unprovable, it is true, and we are left with a mathematical truth that no rational being could ever establish. And because a human mathematician can see that the sentence is true when the machine cannot, the theorems vindicate the Lucas–Penrose argument that the mind is not a machine.'

Could you go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what a logician would accept as stated, what they would wince at as a defensible but loose simplification, and what is simply false? Then I would like a verdict on the closing claim: does the conclusion about minds and machines follow from whatever survives the corrections, or does it need further premises that the handout does not supply? I am inclined to think the Lucas–Penrose move is the weak point, but I cannot tell whether the technical claims are also shakier than they look.

Please be explicit about your own confidence, and say where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong: which technical or philosophical point, if you were mistaken about it, would change your verdict most, and what consideration would move you to a different overall assessment.
