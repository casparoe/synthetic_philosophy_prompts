I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, and the concept that has aged best for me is the one that treats a whole family of large-scale coordination failures as a single recurring phenomenon with a single name — the competitive dynamic, sometimes called a multipolar trap, in which every participant acting reasonably moves toward an outcome none of them wants. I want an essay that puts that framing under real pressure instead of admiring it.

To keep the discussion honest, work with the following four-way distinction and keep the categories separate throughout:

1. Equilibrium traps: the game has a unique Nash equilibrium that is Pareto-inferior to some feasible outcome, and no unilateral deviation helps.
2. Enforcement traps: a Pareto-superior outcome exists and is even common knowledge, but nothing decentralized can hold participants to it, because the enforcement mechanism would itself have to be a participant with its own incentives — and would itself be the sort of thing the trap makes dangerous.
3. Selection traps: the participants are not fixed. Agents, firms, or lineages that decline to play the losing strategy are replaced by ones that do play it, so the bad outcome is produced not by anyone's choice but by the changing composition of the population.
4. Epistemic traps: the better outcome is available and enforceable in principle but is not jointly representable — participants misestimate each other's preferences or cannot say aloud what they all believe — so the bad equilibrium is sustained by mutual ignorance that no one can unilaterally correct.

Now write the essay on this question: does one concept earn its keep across all four of these, or is it doing genuine explanatory work in at most some of them while functioning as rhetoric in the rest?

Requirements:

- Make the strongest case for the unifying view: say what all four cases share that a bare statement of the game plus an equilibrium concept does not capture, and why that shared feature has predictive content — for instance, what the framing predicts about the failure of local fixes, about the ineffectiveness of moral appeals, and about where blame will be assigned.
- Make the strongest case against it: that it is game theory plus a story about which game we happen to be in, and that its distinctive contribution is the transfer of responsibility from identifiable agents to a pseudo-agent, which is attractive precisely because it excuses the people who chose. Note what is lost by lumping the four categories together: they have different remedies, and a diagnosis that cannot distinguish them will recommend the wrong ones.
- Take seriously the possibility that the framing is right about the diagnosis and wrong about the remedy — that the same analysis which explains why decentralized coordination fails also undercuts the appeal to a central coordinator, since a coordinator is just another participant with the same problem one level up, or is itself a large irreversible bet.
- Test the framework against cases of apparent escape, including ones not usually discussed in this literature: the phase-out of ozone-depleting chemicals, the moratorium on commercial whaling, the removal of lead from gasoline, and the various arms-control agreements. For each, say which of the four categories it was, what actually broke the trap (a hegemon, a technological substitute, a change in the unit of selection, a focal moral shock, or something else), and whether that mechanism generalizes. Then say whether the concept survives these cases with refinement, or whether they show it doing less work than advertised.
- Do not treat the AI-race case as the only application, but include a short section on whether the escape mechanisms you identified are available there and which of the four trap types that case most resembles. Keep this section brief; I care more about the concept than about AI policy.
- No citations, no reading list, no bibliography. Argue from the structure of the cases.

About 1200 words. The last line must be in exactly this form:

Verdict: <one of GENUINE KIND, USEFUL BUT OVEREXTENDED, RHETORICAL DEVICE> — <one sentence, and no more, saying why.>
