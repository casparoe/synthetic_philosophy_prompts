A friend in my history-of-econ reading group has an argument about Malthus that I can't shake, and I'm hoping you can tell me where it goes off the rails. The setup: we were reading the Essay, and the standard complaint came up — he predicted mass famine, it didn't happen, so he was just wrong. My friend says that's naive, and here's his case.

(1) Malthus's central claim is conditional: left to itself, population tends to outrun subsistence, and the gap gets closed either by positive checks (famine, disease, war) or by preventive checks (delayed marriage, restraint, fewer births). (2) In the twentieth century the catastrophe didn't arrive: food output rose and fertility fell. (3) A fall in fertility is a preventive check — the very escape route the theory names. (4) So the non-catastrophe doesn't refute him; it's the theory working, with the gentler of the two checks doing the job. He was right, just early, or right and lucky about which check turned up. (5) And the same move is available for any failed long-run prediction: the world didn't do what was foretold, therefore some counteracting factor was at work, therefore the underlying tendency is still there and was merely offset. (6) If every failed prediction can be converted into evidence that the tendency was real and merely counteracted, then the theory fits any history you like. (7) A theory that fits any history makes no claim and can be confirmed by nothing. (8) So (4) is false, and "right but early" is never a real verdict — it's always a rescue dressed up.

I can't accept (8), because it seems to prove way too much: it would kill any conditional, mechanism-based prediction in the social sciences, and it would mean an intervention that works for exactly the reason a theory said it would can never count as confirming that theory. But I also can't just wave the argument off, because if I let the rescue stand then every failed prophecy and every missed economic forecast is "right but early" too, and that can't be right either. So: which step do I give up, why did it look so plausible in the first place, and what does giving it up cost me? If the honest answer lives in the difference between a rescue specified in advance and one invented after the fact, or between a theory being merely consistent with a history and that history being made likely by it, or in Popper's line about ad hoc saves vs. Lakatos's progressive/degenerating shifts, use whatever is actually doing the work — I just want to know which of (1)–(7) is the illicit step and what I lose by dropping it. I'd most like to keep (3) if I possibly can, since without it I don't see what's left of the original insight.
