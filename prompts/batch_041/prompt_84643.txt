Compose a satirical press release from the International Bureau of Historical Standards (IBHS), an invented regulatory body, announcing the outcome of its periodic review of three European periods: the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution. Aim for about 750 words in American English, formatted as a press release with a dateline, a headline, a subhead, two or three quoted officials (all invented, with invented titles), and a closing section headed Stakeholder Questions and Answers. Keep it deadpan from first line to last: the Bureau is entirely sincere, treats periods as load-bearing administrative objects with specifications, tolerances, and compliance obligations, and never acknowledges that anything about this is funny.

The humor must come from the actual historiography rather than from invented absurdities, so that a historian of historical writing would recognize that the author understands the literature being satirized. Work in the following, without footnotes, citations, or a reading list:

- The Bureau's central doctrine is that periods are specified intervals rather than discoveries, and it holds to this doctrine while repeatedly behaving as though the intervals had been found in the ground. Give the Bureau an invented piece of internal jargon for this failure.
- Boundary trouble: any date for the onset of the Renaissance is arbitrary, so the Bureau publishes tolerances instead of dates, and speaks of local variance, transitional zones, and grace periods.
- The name problem: the Middle Ages began as a filing term for the stretch of time between two admired eras and carries a judgment inside it. The Bureau handles this as a branding issue and commissions a rebranding study, with candidate replacement names.
- Continuity: whatever the Renaissance is supposed to have accomplished was already under way, and the supposed novelties of the Scientific Revolution were already being pursued by fourteenth- and fifteenth-century natural philosophers. The Bureau's reviewers cannot locate a rupture and respond by widening the interval.
- The Scientific Revolution is a pending docket item; the Bureau downgrades it to provisional status and allows that it may be a slow accumulation rather than an event.
- Observer effects: the Bureau concedes that its labels determine what gets catalogued, preserved, taught, and funded, so that the periods partly manufacture the evidence later cited on their behalf, and it processes this as a compliance matter.
- Objections: each objection that periods are conveniences rather than entities is answered by adding a qualifier to the label rather than by changing the ontology. At least one such exchange should be quoted at length in the Q&A, and it should be clear that the Bureau has no account of what would count as evidence against its scheme.
- Scope: the Bureau's remit is European. A quoted official notes that the same intervals do not fit other regions and that the Bureau has elected to treat the difficulty as out of scope.
- Value disguised as time: Renaissance ranks as much as it dates. The Bureau insists its rankings are purely descriptive, and a quoted official defends the continued use of early modern as a hedge against all of it.

Two structural jokes to land: the Bureau's own style guide forbids using a period as the grammatical subject of a verb of intention, as in saying that the Renaissance rediscovered antiquity, and the press release itself should violate this rule at least once, in a way a careful reader will catch. The piece should end on a note that reveals, without ever stating it, that the Bureau cannot describe what a falsified periodization would look like.
