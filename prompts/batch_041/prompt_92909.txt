I work on machine learning infrastructure, and I keep having the same argument with a colleague. Neither of us has any formal training in philosophy, so we have been improvising the vocabulary, and I would like a proper treatment of it. We spend our days looking at the same trained model and disagreeing about what is actually there.

Here is the disagreement. When we describe the model, we can do it at three levels. At the bottom, there is a very large array of floating-point numbers and the arithmetic the hardware performs on them. In the middle, there are modules, attention heads, and a training objective: parts that function, in the way a thermostat functions, whether or not anyone is home. At the top, we talk as though the thing wants things — it "tries" to finish the sentence, it "prefers" one continuation to another, it "avoids" certain topics. Some philosophers call these the physical, design, and intentional stances. My colleague says the top level is a convenient shorthand and nothing more, and that the only things really present are the weights and the arithmetic. I say that when a description lets you predict the model's behavior far more cheaply than the low-level description does — when it actually compresses the data rather than merely relabeling it — then it is picking out a real pattern, and it would be a real pattern even if no one had ever found it useful.

Write me a dialogue that works through this properly. It should be a conversation between two people who are both competent, with neither serving as a strawman: one who holds that a pattern is real exactly when it earns its keep by compressing, and one who holds that "compresses" always means compresses-for-someone, so that the reality of a pattern is relative to an observer's purposes, interests, and computational budget. They should respond to each other's actual points rather than trade monologues, and neither should win by fiat or appeal to a result the other cannot check.

I would especially like them to get into the following, because this is where it stops being hand-waving:

- Whether there is a fact of the matter about the right level of description, or whether choosing a level is like choosing a map projection — a matter of what you happen to want to do with it.
- The difference between a pattern that is real because it supports counterfactuals (had the weights been slightly different, the system would have behaved otherwise) and one that merely summarizes what happened to occur.
- Whether the middle, design level survives if the top, intentional level is demoted to a fiction, or whether the two stand or fall together.
- The worry that "compresses the data" hides a parameter — how much description length you are willing to spend — and whether that makes the criterion circular or merely interest-relative.
- Whether anything changes if the pattern is discovered by an algorithm rather than by a person, so that no human purpose is in the loop at all.

About 1,000 words. American English. Please give me the argument itself rather than a bibliography — no citations or reading lists, since I cannot check them anyway.
