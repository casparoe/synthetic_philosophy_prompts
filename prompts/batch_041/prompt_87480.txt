Ok so I need help with something for a film-and-theory night I somehow got roped into hosting. We're screening Fritz Lang's Metropolis and I want to pair it with the "Fragment on Machines" bit of Marx's Grundrisse -- the notebooks he scribbled in the late 1850s, that nobody could even read until the 20th century. You're a philosopher who knows Marxist theory of technology and also actually watches films closely, so please take this seriously and don't give me the standard "Metropolis = class struggle + scary robot" spiel. Start by brainstorming the points of contact and friction before you commit to a reading, and be honest when the reading starts to strain.

Here's the Marx I want you to work with, stated plainly: capital constantly drives toward automating production, replacing living labour with fixed capital, and in doing so it undermines the very thing its value rests on -- labour time as the measure of wealth. Once direct labour stops being the main wellspring of wealth, labour time has to stop being its measure, which is a contradiction capital keeps positing and can't escape. And the knowledge that ends up stored in the machinery -- social knowledge, what he calls the general intellect -- becomes a power over the worker rather than the worker's own power, so the worker ends up as a mere living appendage of a system that moves on its own.

Now the film: the machine halls and the Moloch hallucination, the workers' city down below and the gardens up top, the ten-hour clock, the Heart Machine, Rotwang's robot wearing Maria's face, the false Maria whipping up the revolt, the mob smashing the Heart Machine and flooding the workers' quarters, the children nearly drowning, the burning of the false Maria, and then that last title card about the heart mediating between the head and the hands.

What I want from you: a reading that takes the film and the fragment equally seriously. Where does Metropolis actually dramatize Marx's argument -- the workers as appendages of a self-moving machine system, the city above living off the labour below, machinery as a power standing over its producers? And where does it get things backwards or dodge them? Two things bother me in particular. First, the film's horror is aimed at a machine that counterfeits a person, when Marx's point is that the system already treats persons as machinery -- so is the false Maria a displacement, an anxiety about automation that the film can't state in economic terms? Does the film end up siding with Joh Fredersen without meaning to? Or is it doing something allegorical and religious that my Marxian frame is just flattening?

And the other direction: does the film see something the fragment misses? I keep thinking about Rotwang building the robot to resurrect Hel -- automation as grief and revenge rather than as a tendency of capital -- and about the fact that what terrifies everyone isn't lost disposable time but the loss of the meaning of work, and of being replaceable. Does Marx have anything to say to that, or is that a blind spot?
