I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on combinatorial games, and the midterm has landed on my desk. The first unit of the course was on the strategy-stealing argument — the reasoning that shows the first player has a winning strategy in Hex, in Chomp, and in Bridg-It, and the general question of which games it does and does not reach. One exam question, worth 20 points, read as follows:

Question. "State the strategy-stealing argument as it applies to Hex, being explicit about what the argument assumes about the game. Say precisely what the argument establishes and what it does not establish. Then explain why the same reasoning does not yield a win for White in chess."

I'm marking against a three-line rubric: accurate reconstruction of the argument and of its assumptions (8 points); an accurate account of what the argument does and does not establish (6 points); a correct treatment of the chess case (6 points).

Here is one student's answer, reproduced in full.

"Strategy stealing applies to a finite two-player game of perfect information with alternating moves in which the rules give the two players symmetric roles, the game cannot end in a draw, and having an extra move of one's own can never be a disadvantage. Hex satisfies all four conditions: it cannot end in a draw, since a filled board always contains exactly one monochromatic chain joining the two opposite sides, and a stone of one's own colour can never hurt, since a winning chain that uses it would still be a winning chain if the stone were removed — the extra stone can simply be ignored.

Suppose for contradiction that the second player has a winning strategy S. The first player begins with an arbitrary move, say the centre, and thereafter pretends to be the second player: whatever the opponent does, the first player consults S and plays the move S recommends. If S ever recommends a cell already occupied by the first player's extra stone, the first player plays any unoccupied cell instead and carries on as if that had been the recommendation. Every subsequent move the first player makes is a move S would have made in the corresponding position of the game in which the first player had not moved first, so the first player wins; but the second player was supposed to have a winning strategy, so we have a contradiction, and the first player must have one instead.

The argument is non-constructive: it tells us that the first player has a winning strategy without telling us what it is or which opening move wins. For this reason it establishes only that a win exists in the abstract, not that anyone could exhibit one; indeed, for Hex on a large board no winning strategy is known, and it may be that none exists. What it shows in the first instance is that the second player cannot have a winning strategy, and it is only because Hex has no draws that this rules out a second-player win outright.

The argument also transfers to Chomp and Bridg-It, and indeed to any symmetric no-draw game with alternating moves, including Go: Go has no draws and its rules treat the players alike, so in the absence of komi the first player must have a winning strategy. In chess the same reasoning fails, because chess can be drawn; a stolen strategy would at best show that the first player does not lose, and the standard conclusion, that White wins, is unavailable."
