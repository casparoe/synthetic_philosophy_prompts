I'm a highschool student and for my philosophy club presentation I'm trying to get my head around Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment — the imaginary frictionless box full of tiny magnetized marbles he calls simms, which bounce around and sometimes clump into big slow lumbering clusters called simmballs, where the whole point of the setup is the question of who is actually shoving whom in there: whether the simmballs push the simms around, or whether it's only ever simms pushing simms and the simmballs are just a convenient way of talking. I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts on the real-world versions of this and I'd like you to act like a philosopher of mind and metaphysics who works specifically on levels of description and downward causation, think carefully, and brainstorm a bit before you commit to anything. Here are my verdicts. Traffic jam: when a jam builds up on the highway and the cars behind it slow down, I think the jam is genuinely doing the causing, not just each car's brake lights. Corporation signing a contract: I think the corporation is a real cause of the ink going onto the page, not just the people in the room. Person believing the stove is hot and yanking their hand back: I think the belief really pushes the neurons around, it isn't just neurons pushing neurons. Hurricane flattening a town: yes, the hurricane is a real cause even though it's just molecules. Chess engine deciding to trade bishops: here I flip — I don't think the engine's plan pushes the transistors around, I think the transistors do all the pushing and the plan is just a description we happen to like. A gene making an organism behave in a way that spreads the gene: also no, I think that's a redescription and not a cause at all. Joke punchline making someone laugh: I think the structure of the joke is doing real work there, not just sound waves.

So my problem is that the corporation verdict and the gene verdict look like the same kind of case to me and I have opposite feelings about them, and it's the same story with the belief and the chess engine. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close, and what does it actually say? I don't want a survey of positions, I want the principle stated precisley enough that I could apply it to a new case on my own. Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't thought about — a market price rising because of a shortage, where I want to know whether the shortage is pushing the price or the traders are, and a word's meaning guiding my mouth when I speak — and finally tell me which one of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up, because I'd honestly rather know that than have you paper over it. If no single principle can do the job, give me the smallest set of candidates and say what each one costs me.
