Right, I'm putting together a case for a first-year seminar and I want you to actually build the thing for me rather than just hold forth about it. The point of the exercise is to show the students that the received wisdom on this is a bit too quick, so the case has to genuinely earn its verdict rather than me rigging it.

Two labels to work with, so we're not talking past each other. Call someone bubbled if they've simply never been exposed to the relevant counter-evidence — nobody's hiding anything, it just hasn't reached them — and crucially they don't distrust the people who hold it; the thought of them being wrong just doesn't come up. Call someone chambered if they have been exposed, repeatedly, and they treat the sources of that counter-evidence as discredited, so it slides off them. The usual line is that chambering is the worse condition and the harder one to fix, becuase you can fix a bubble just by putting the information in front of someone, whereas a chamber has to have its trust rebuilt from the ground up.

I want a case that runs the other way. Two people, same small town, same false belief about something mundane and checkable — keep it apolitical and non-medical, something like whether a local factory is polluting the river, or whether a particular neighbour's dog is the one getting into everyone's bins. One of them is bubbled and not chambered; the other is chambered and not bubbled. Same belief, same town, comparable intelligence and good faith on both sides. Build it so that the bubbled one is clearly the worse epistemic position and the harder case to turn round — the opposite of the usual ranking.

Constraints: no social-media algorithm, no cult, no manipulative genius pulling strings, no science fiction, no exotic technology; ordinary adults in an ordinary place; keep the whole thing under a page; and the asymmetry has to fall out of the structure of the situation, not out of me quietly stipulating that one of them is more stubborn or dimmer than the other. And don't let the bubbled one's problem be simply that they're busy or that they missed a one-off conversation — I want something that keeps regenerating the exclusion rather than a single gap in the record.

Then walk me through it. For each of the two, say which label fits and why. Then say what the exclusion story and the discrediting story each predict about which of the two is worse off, and why the case makes the exclusion story come out on top. Tell me plainly which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict if I delete that feature — I'd expect it to snap back to the standard ranking, so tell me if it doesn't. Finally, put yourself in the shoes of someone who thinks chambering is always the worse of the two and say how they'd try to deflate my case, and whether they'd get away with it.
