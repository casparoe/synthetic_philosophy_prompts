I am a novelist — three books, the most recent a quiet one set in a mill town — and for two years I have sat on the board of a small independent press that publishes translations and a literary quarterly. There are six of us: a poet, a retired accountant, two academics, the publisher, and me. We meet by video on Thursday evenings for ninety minutes and vote at the end. This year most of our agenda has been automation proposals, and we have been handling them the way I imagine most small boards do — by instinct, by whoever speaks last, and by a private sense that some of these things feel like a betrayal and others feel like arithmetic. I have been rereading Marx's long chapter on machinery to see whether it gives us anything better than instinct, and I think it does, but I cannot get it into a form the accountant could run in ten minutes, which is the actual difficulty. What I want from you is a decision procedure my board could actually use on a proposal.

What I take the argument to be, in my own words — and I would rather the procedure follow from whatever of this survives your scrutiny than from the loose version in my head: machinery is never introduced merely to save labour; under capitalist relations it is introduced to extract more surplus from the same hours, so the first question about any machine is not whether it saves labour but who gets the time it saves. It tends to take the skill that lived in the worker and put it into the employer's equipment, so that a person meets his own competence as someone else's property. It lowers the skill threshold, which makes the remaining labour cheaper and easier to replace. It can intensify the working day rather than shorten it. And the people it displaces do not vanish; they become the pool that keeps the rest grateful. The strand I find most interesting and least usable in a board meeting is the one about time: capital drives the labour time needed to make things toward zero while still measuring wealth by labour time, so it works against its own foundation and cannot really want full automation — which seems to mean that the interesting question about any given machine is not whether it replaces workers but what it does to the hours, the skills, and the bargaining position of those who remain, and that the answer depends on the relations of production rather than on the machinery. Please write the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a vote because the remaining question is about who should bear a loss rather than about what the machinery does. It has to be usable by five people who have not read Marx and will not read him, it has to fit into a ninety-minute meeting, and it must not collapse into the generic ethics-of-AI checklist our publisher keeps circulating. One of us will say this is politics dressed up as procedure; I would like the procedure to contain its own answer to that objection. I would also like it to say what difference it makes if the press were a worker cooperative rather than a private company, since the whole analysis seems to turn on that and I do not know whether the procedure itself should change or only its verdicts. Do not give me a reading list; I will find the references myself.

Then run it on three proposals we are actually facing. First, replacing the human narrators of our backlist audiobooks with synthetic narration at roughly a tenth of the cost, with a one-time payment to each narrator and no continuing royalty. Second, machine-translating four backlist novels into Spanish and Portuguese for a cheap e-book edition with a human proofreader on a flat fee, presented to us as a way of reaching readers we would otherwise never reach — I should say that one of the four is mine, so I am not a clean judge of it, and the procedure should tell me so. Third, running the quarterly's slush pile through a model that passes about five per cent of it up to us, cutting our first readers from six to two. I expect the procedure to land somewhere I do not like on at least one of these; say plainly which way it goes and do not soften it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, which judgements it disguises as steps, and where the honest answer is that we are not deciding about machinery at all but about who is allowed to keep what.
