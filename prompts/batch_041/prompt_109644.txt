Write a pantoum in which a single conditional sentence is carried through both grammatical moods. Choose a case where the indicative version is plainly true and the counterfactual version is at best doubtful — the familiar pair about whether someone else would have killed Kennedy had Oswald not is the standard illustration, but any case with the same asymmetry will serve. The form should do the argumentative work: as the pantoum recycles its lines, the sentence should return each time in a new position and, at least once, in a new mood, so that the reader hears the same words slide from something assertable to something merely conjectural, and the closing return of the opening line should land with a different force than it had at the start. The poem should make the divergence legible without adjudicating between theories of conditionals — it should register that the counterfactual asks after the world's dispositions while the indicative asks only what follows from what we already accept, and that neither can be read off the other. A reader who works on conditionals should find it accurate; a reader who does not should find it a poem.
