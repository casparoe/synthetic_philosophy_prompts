Last night my friend Ana and I got into it for about two hours about Nyāya epistemology, which is not how I expected to spend a Tuesday. Her line was basically this: Nyāya says there are four pramāṇas, and that śabda — testimony — is one of them in its own right, not just a shadow of inference. Fine. But Nyāya also says validity is extrinsic, parataḥ prāmāṇya: you dont get to assume a cognition is correct, something else has to come along and certify it. And her point was, if the only way I ever find out whether your testimony was knowledge is by going and checking for myself, then perception is doing all the work and śabda is just a post office. I couldn't get out of it in the moment and I still think theres an answer, I just couldn't find it.

Anyway, I'm setting the final for my third-year seminar on Indian epistemology. They've done perception, inference, and the basic pramāṇa material; they can all recite the four pramāṇas, and they know roughly what svataḥ and parataḥ prāmāṇya mean. I want one essay question, 90 minutes, out of 20, that puts them exactly where I was standing. What I do not want: a question they can answer by listing the pramāṇas and saying śabda is irreducible because Nyāya says so. I want them to have to actually build the reductionist case — testimony collapses into inference, or into perception plus inference — and then say what Nyāya has left to say, and whether what it has left is a real defence or just a relabelling. If upamāna fits without bloating the question, put it in, because the gavaya case has the same shape: you're told a wild cow looks like a cow, later you see one, and the question is whether anything happened there that wasn't perception plus memory plus inference.

One more thing I care about: the question has to force them to say where the regress stops. If every valid cognition needs another cognition to certify it, something has to stop that chain, and I want to see whether they realise what Nyāya actually uses as the stopper and whether that stopper can certify a piece of testimony as well as it certifies a perception.

Lay it out in this order:

1. "What this question is testing" — 3 or 4 sentences, written to me, not to the students.
2. "The question" — the text exactly as it would appear on the exam paper, about 100 words, including the marks and the suggested time.
3. "Model answer" — around 700 words, written the way a strong student would write it under time pressure, not as a polished essay.
4. "Marking scheme" — the must-hit points with marks that total 20, then a short list of the specific moves that should cost marks, and for each must-hit point one line on how students typically get it wrong.

No citations please, I hand out the readings separately.
