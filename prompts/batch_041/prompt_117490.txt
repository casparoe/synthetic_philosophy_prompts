For the past four months I have been clearing out my grandmother's house, and in between the boxes I have been sitting in on the collections committee of the small historical society where I volunteer, and the combination has left me with a set of convictions about originals and copies that I cannot get into any single shape. I had a lab-grown replica made of her engagement ring so that I could wear it every day without risking the real one, and I love wearing it; looking at the replica gives me most of what looking at the original gives me. But it is not a substitute. If the original were stolen I would be devastated in a way that the replica's continued presence would do nothing to repair, and if the replica were stolen I would be mildly annoyed and order another. Her great-grandfather's chair, by contrast, has a replaced leg, new upholstery, and a refinished frame, and it is still his chair; a workshop that built me an exact new one to the same measurements would not be giving me his chair, and I would not pretend otherwise. At the society, a portrait long catalogued as the work of a named eighteenth-century painter has turned out to be a nineteenth-century copy of an original that no longer exists. Nothing about the object has changed, and I still enjoy looking at it, but the new label changes everything, and my verdict is that we should keep it on the wall and that its value has collapsed, and that both of those are true at once. I am also, for what it is worth, a person who would pay a great deal for a bottle that has been topped up and recorked at the estate over the decades and would feel cheated by a bottle that turned out to be a blend of younger wines sold under an old vintage, even though in both cases the liquid in my glass is not what the label implies. A friend who collects watches showed me a piece assembled from the movement of one watch and the case of another of the same model — every part period-correct, running better than an all-original example with a corroded movement — and I think it is a fake, and I think he is right to think so, though I cannot point to anything visible or measurable that makes it one. A pub fitted with fake Tudor beams and distressed plaster, presenting itself as a surviving old building, strikes me as straightforwardly objectionable, and the objection seems to live entirely in the claim rather than in the beams, which are charming. A restoration service plausibly in-painted the torn corner of a family photograph; I am happy to keep it in the album and uneasy about it standing in as the photograph itself. And I have forty seconds of my grandmother's voice on an old tape, and a service that will synthesize her reading anything I type; I tried it once and hated it, and not because it was inaccurate, since it was uncannily accurate.

The principle I keep reaching for is that what I value in an original is not its appearance but its history — specifically, that it is the numerically same object that was present at the events that give it its meaning — and that where copies go wrong, the wrongness lies in the false claim their presentation makes about that history. I would like you to test that against the cases above and repair it where it breaks, and to tell me whether it is really one principle or two, one about where value lives and another about when misrepresentation is wrong, since the watch and the pub seem to be about deception while the ring and the chair seem to be about something else. Say what the repaired principle predicts for a case I have not put to you: the house itself is to be demolished, and a developer has offered to rebuild it to the original plans and call it a heritage replica of the family home, which my mother finds grotesque and which I half agree with and half think is no different from the chair. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up, and be honest about how much complexity you are buying to save the rest.
