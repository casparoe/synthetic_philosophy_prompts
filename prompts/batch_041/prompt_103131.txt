I've been covering the surveillance and privacy beat for about six years now, and there's one panel I keep getting invited to and it always ends the same way. Three speakers: a German data protection lawyer who says the state may only collect about a citizen what the citizen could in principle see about itself, an American civil liberties person who talks about the public's right to know and the government's duty to be legible, and someone from a Beijing-adjacent policy shop who says the whole Western framing misses the point, because the goal is social trust and stable credit, not privacy versus security. Everyone agrees the technology works, everyone agrees it can be abused, and nobody has moved an inch in six years of these panels. What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a scorecard — I don't need you to tell me which regime is right, I need you to tell me what kind of arguement this actually is.

A colleague of mine who does comparative constitutional law has a hypothesis I can't shake. She says the three positions aren't three points on one axis at all, but three different answers to a question nobody has stated out loud, namely: who is the official author of you? On the German view the citizen is the author and the state may hold only a copy; on the American view the public is the author and the state holds the pen but has to let you read over its shoulder; on the social-credit view the collective is the author and you are a draft that keeps getting revised by your own behaviour. If she's right, the fight was never about how much hiding is permitted, it's about authorship, and no amount of privacy-versus-security evidence will ever move it. Is she right, or is there a real empirical or normative dispute underneath that a sharper framing would expose?

I'd also like to know how much of this is secretly an empirical quarrel — whether mass data collection actually improves the things states claim it improves, and whether the mosaic effect (lots of individually trivial data points adding up to something none of them reveals on its own) is a genuine qualitative shift or just aggregation with a scary name — and whether the Chinese case is even one thing, since everyone I know who actually works on it says it's a patchwork of local pilots and seperate regulatory blacklists rather than the single unified score the op-eds describe. So: substantive disagreement, verbal disagreement, three groups answering different questions under one word, or bedrock values that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a formal result, or nothing at all?
