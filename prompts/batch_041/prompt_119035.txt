I'm three weeks into a graduate seminar on modality and identity, and a classmate has handed me an argument that I have now read more times than I care to admit. The occasion was our session on the necessity of origin — the thesis, standardly traced to Kripke, that a person could not have come from a different sperm cell or egg than the ones they actually came from, on the grounds that whatever was formed from different cells would have been someone else, not you — and he maintains that the thesis, taken at face value, silently proves something none of us can accept. Here is the argument as he circulated it. (1) If an individual came from certain individuals, then it could not have come from different ones; so I could not have come from a different sperm cell or egg than the ones I actually came from. (2) A sperm cell is itself an individual, and it too came from something: a particular meiosis in my father, with a particular set of chromosomes going into it rather than some other set. (3) By (1), applied to the sperm cell, it could not have come from a different meiosis, or with different chromosomes in it. (4) If X could not have existed without Y, and Y could not have existed without Z, then X could not have existed without Z. (5) So I could not have existed without that meiosis and those particular chromosomes. (6) Those chromosomes were assembled in my father out of what he had inherited from his parents, and what he inherited was settled by which of his parents' sperm cells and egg cells met; by (1) his origin is essential to him, and by (4) it is essential to me. (7) Iterating, I could not have existed without every conception and every meiotic division in my entire ancestry. (8) Which sperm cell wins a race, and when a germline cell divides, depends on an unbounded mass of ordinary circumstance: what my ancestors ate, when they slept, whether a storm delayed a journey. (9) So in any world in which I exist, all of that went exactly as it actually went; and by the same reasoning the same holds for every other person. (10) Therefore every world containing any person agrees with the actual world on every detail of its entire history, and there is exactly one possible world.

He concludes that the necessity of origin is refuted and that we should all stop being essentialists about origins. I would rather keep (1) if I can — the intuition behind it seems to me firmer than most of what we have been reading this term, and (10) strikes me as plainly false — but I have gone through all ten steps and I cannot find the one that is doing the damage; each of them looked innocent when I first read it, and each still looks innocent now.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Tell me where the argument breaks down — whether the trouble is a step that does not follow or a premise that has to be surrendered — and explain why that step looked fine when it was written down and what makes it suspect on inspection. Then price the repair: tell me what I would have to add or give up in order to block the argument, and whether the fix leaves (1) intact or quietly converts it into a weaker thesis that no longer says what it seemed to say. I am less interested in being told which conclusion is more palatable than in knowing exactly which commitment I would be buying, and what it would cost me elsewhere.
