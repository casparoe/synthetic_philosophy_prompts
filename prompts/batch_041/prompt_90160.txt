A colleague on our lab's oversight project keeps justifying the whole approach with the same comparison: debate between two trained models, he says, is just an adversarial trial. You seat a judge who cannot answer the question himself, let one system argue for each answer, and the truth surfaces the way it does in a courtroom, because the structure rather than any single participant does the epistemic work. He leans on this whenever someone raises the familiar worry that the judge will be weaker than the debaters and therefore fooled: judges and juries, he points out, are laypeople deciding between expert advocates, and we do not conclude that trials are worthless. I find the comparison seductive and probably too cheap, and I would like a proper audit of it before I put anything like it in a write-up.

First, lay out the correspondence the trial picture commits us to — what plays the role of the two advocates, the judge or jury, the rules of evidence, the burden of proof, the record, the appeal, and the independent fact of the matter that the verdict is supposed to track — and then tell me where the mapping fails. I am most interested in one failure I can feel but cannot state cleanly. In a courtroom, the losing advocate's dishonesty is checked by things outside the argument: exhibits, witnesses, the judge's authority to strike testimony as irrelevant or to refuse to credit a line of reasoning that has become too tangled to follow. In our setting there is no exhibit and no striking. One debater can win simply by making the case too long and convoluted for the judge to untangle, so that the honest side must produce something the judge can actually follow while the dishonest side need only be confusing. Is that a difference of degree from courtroom obfuscation, or a difference of kind — and if it is a difference of kind, does it defeat the conclusion the analogy was carrying, namely that we do not need a judge who could have answered the question alone? If the trial picture is the wrong one, say what comparison would do better, and what it would predict that the trial picture does not.
