For the past three years I have been running a small monthly discussion group — nine of us, none with any academic training — and for several months now we have been circling a single question: when we say that someone is ill, are we reporting a fact about their body or passing a judgement on it? I have no academic background myself; what I know of this I have picked up from reading and from arguing with the group, so I would like you to help me build the material for our end-of-year session, which we run as a mock exam. I need three things: the essay question itself, a model answer I can read aloud afterwards, and a marking scheme I can genuinely apply, since we mark one another's papers and I would rather not do it by impression.

The background, as plainly as I can put it. On one side are people who say that being healthy is simply a matter of the body doing what bodies of that kind are built to do, so that calling something a disease is a claim about biology which we can get right or wrong; on this view the fact that a condition is unpleasant or unwanted is a separate matter, and may explain why we treat it but not why we call it an illness. On the other side are people who say that nothing counts as an illness unless it is bad for the person in some way, and that badness is never a biological fact; on this view medicine's categories carry our values inside them and cannot be scrubbed clean of them.

What keeps me up, and the reason I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting either side's slogan, is something I have come to call yardstick-switching. Whenever somebody insists that a condition is really a disease, I find I can always ask which of three yardsticks they are measuring against: how common the condition is among people of the patient's age and background; how the body is built to work, whether or not it is working that way; or how the patient wants to live and what she is prepared to put up with. In the cases that interest me these three yardsticks point in different directions, and the person I am arguing with quietly swaps between them — reaching for the first when they want to say a condition is abnormal, the second when they want to say it is broken, the third when they want to say it is suffering. I would like the question to make that swapping visible.

Please build it around these four cases, which are my own, invented to pull the yardsticks apart:

1. The miner's ear. In a coal town where most men work underground, nearly every man past fifty has substantial hearing loss. None of them thinks of himself as ill. An audiologist visiting from the city says the whole town is diseased.
2. The quiet bone. A woman of sixty-two has bone density well below average for her age. She has no pain, no fracture and no limitation. She is offered a drug to lower the odds of a break that may never come; she takes it, and begins to think of herself as having a condition.
3. The beardless man. A man in his thirties seeks treatment because he cannot grow a beard and it distresses him. By the design yardstick nothing is malfunctioning; by the distress yardstick something plainly is.
4. The shortsighted classroom. In a school where more than half the children are shortsighted, glasses are cheap and handed out at the door. Is shortsightedness a disease there — and does the answer change if the glasses are taken away?

The question should require the student to say, for each case, what someone who holds the first view consistently must conclude and what someone who holds the second must conclude; to identify where each view is pushed into an awkward corner; and then to take a side on whether yardstick-switching is a fatal weakness in the first view or a legitimate response to different questions. I also want it tested whether a student can say what the first view would have to add in order to handle the quiet bone — the case where nothing is wrong now and the whole complaint is about a risk — without turning into the view it was meant to oppose.

Practical requirements: the question should be answerable in about forty-five minutes at the level of a strong final-year undergraduate (our group is mixed, but I would rather aim high than low); the model answer should run to roughly six hundred and fifty words; and the marking scheme should be built on five must-hit points worth twenty marks in total, with a one-line note against each about how people typically get that point wrong. Please add a short note on what separates a top answer from a merely competent one. Do not require citations, and do not let the marking scheme reward name-dropping: I want the papers marked on whether the argument holds together, not on whether the student knows who said what.
