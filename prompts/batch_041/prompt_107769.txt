I write a column on science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and next month I'm giving the keynote at a small interdisciplinary workshop on science and religion, which means my usual sympathetic readers are replaced by a room I'm already nervous about. The argument I've been developing is this: the two halves of Einstein's worldview that everyone treats as an embarrassing inconsistency — that he denied free will in the ordinary sense while insisting on an ethic of responsibility and a religious reverence for the universe — are actually one position, and the determinism is the load-bearing part. My claim is that his denial of free will is not a moral or psychological thesis at all but a metaphysical one: it says a person is not a causally isolated origin of their own acts, and that this very fact is what dissolves the picture of a personal, interventionist God and leaves in its place the attitude he described as a cosmic religious feeling — awe not at a designer but at necessity itself. On my reading, the relief of no longer being the ultimate author of oneself is the affective core of his religiosity; the determinism is doing the work that grace does for a theologian, and the ethic of solidarity follows from our being continuous with nature rather than set over against it.

The room is not neutral. There will be four Spinoza scholars who hear the phrase cosmic religious feeling and immediately ask whether I have simply collapsed God into Nature and then renamed the collapse an emotion; two analytic philosophers of religion who work on divine action and free will, and who will want to know how a deterministic universe can ground anything that deserves to be called religious rather than merely aesthetic; one historian of physics who knows the Einstein correspondence and will object that Einstein was just inconsistent and that harmonizing him is a genre of wishful reading; and a theologian interested in naturalistic religion who will press on whether awe at necessity is worship or just sublimity with a straight face. The seed of all this came out of a long argument with a friend who teaches Spinoza, so I'm aware my reading has a bias toward him — I need you to hear it the way his critics will.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than fight, what I should refuse to concede even under pressure, and which single objection, if I lose it, sinks the whole talk. I don't need citations, a reading list, or pointers to the archive — I need the argument and its soft spots. And include the question I am privately hoping nobody asks.
