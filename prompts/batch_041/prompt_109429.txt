Next month I'm on a panel at the media-ethics day our J-school runs with the philosophy department, and I've got twenty minutes plus a long Q&A to defend a paper that will annoy basically everyone in the room. My line, roughly: there is no moral difference between lying and misleading as such. Whatever people think they're tracking with that distinction is downstream of two other things -- how much inferential work the speaker makes the hearer do, and how much the speaker is entitled to assume about what the hearer already believes and is entitled to assume. I think the distinction is trying to seperate two things that come apart in both directions. When you run what I've been calling the naive-hearer test (what would someone who has no idea how implicature works take away from this utterance?), misleading usually comes out worse than lying, not better: a lie is at least a discrete false thing the hearer can eventually catch you in, while a misleading utterance leaves a true trail the speaker can hide behind and quote later. So the standard ranking is backwards a lot of the time. The only distinction I'll grant that survives is evidential -- lying and misleading differ in what the hearer can later prove, not in what was done to them.

The room has three blocs. Four or five philosophy of language people, Gricean to the bone, who think "what is said" is a real level of content and that my test just relabels the semantics/pragmatics boundary. Two Kantians who work on veracity and think misleading is worse than lying because it enlists the victim's own reason against them -- they may end up agreeing with my conclusion while despising the route I take to it, and I have no idea how to handle that. And the rest are working reporters, a couple of desk editors, and the standards editor, whose code of conduct forbids "misleading" in so many words and who is convinced that any argument blurring the line is a gift to liars.

I don't need the objections I'd get from a seminar full of grad students; I need the ones this room will actually ask, in their words. Prep sheet, please: order them by how much damage they'd do me live, phrase each one the way the person who'll ask it would phrase it, give me an answer I can say out loud in about ninety seconds, and tell me where that answer is thin. Flag anything I should just concede on the spot and move past. If two of the objections are really the same objection wearing different hats, say so. Don't bother with citations, I know the lying/misleading literature well enough (Saul, Stokke, Fallis and that whole crowd). And finish with the question thats I'm dreading.
