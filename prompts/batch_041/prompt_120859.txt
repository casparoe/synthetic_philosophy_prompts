For thirty years I built interferometers and worried about systematic error, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in the philosophy of perception, where I have discovered that I am a poor philosopher and an enthusiastic one. The topic I cannot get any purchase on is the dispute over whether a case of genuinely seeing something and a case of merely seeming to see it are episodes of the same fundamental kind. The argument for the negative answer looks trivial when I read it and yet evidently moves people, and I have come to suspect it is one of those arguments whose force is invisible in print. I have talked the seminar leader into letting me run one session, and I want it to be an experiment rather than a lecture: twelve to fourteen graduate students, roughly half from philosophy and half from cognitive science, ninety minutes, in a seminar room with movable chairs, decent blackout, a whiteboard, and a small budget (say fifty euros) for props, plus whatever I can salvage from the lab's discards. Please design the session for me.

What I want them to have felt by the end is a particular inferential step and its cost, not a doctrine, and I would rather the names of the positions were not spoken until the debrief. Early on, the activity should get them to make the move themselves, in their own persons and without being told to: from the fact that two episodes cannot be told apart from the inside, to the conclusion that the two must therefore have something of the same kind at their core, with the difference between them lying somewhere outside the experience — in how it was caused, or in whether an object happened to be there. The rules should make that commitment something they have actually recorded (a vote, a written verdict, a classification they must sign) before anything undermines it, and the staging should be genuinely first-person wherever possible: the old trick of crossing the fingers and touching the tip of the nose so that one nose is felt as two is cheap and repeatable, and I want at least one moment where a participant cannot tell, by any means available to them, whether what another participant is reporting is a perception or a mistake, and must nonetheless act on it. Then, in the second half, the ground should shift under the commitment they made. I want three things built into that shift. First, a case where the causal history is thoroughly abnormal but the report happens to be correct — I have in mind the sort of thing where direct stimulation of the relevant cortex yields an experience matching what is actually in front of the subject — because that puts pressure on the idea that what makes the good case good is merely its causal pedigree. Second, the disjunctivist's negative characterisation of the bad case: the hallucinating subject is not in a state of a distinctive kind that the perceiver also occupies, only in one that cannot be told apart from it. Third, the two worries that this immediately provokes, which I want the room to generate rather than receive: that it leaves the difference between the cases brute and inexplicable, and that it threatens the perceiver's justification, since the hallucinator seems to be as well placed as the perceiver with respect to how things seem.

Give me the whole thing as a runnable package: a materials list with a fallback for every prop, the roles (including whether I need a confederate and how to brief that person so they do not give the game away), the sequence with timings, the precise points at which participants commit to something in writing, and the moment at which I should expect the room to divide. Then a debrief that maps each stage of what actually happened onto the corresponding step in the reasoning, keeping the argument from illusion — the mismatch between how something appears and how it is — clearly separate from the argument from hallucination, where there is no object at all, since I gather these are routinely run together and that the heavy lifting is done by the second. It should also keep separate the version of the disagreement about what experience is and the version about what justifies a perceptual belief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect the illusion to fail on two or three people, someone to announce that this is all just misfiring neurons and therefore not philosophy, and someone else to refuse to commit because they can see where I am steering them — and how to handle each without sliding into a lecture. Finally, be straight with me about what the activity cannot do: I assume it can stage indistinguishability but not sameness or difference of kind, and that a staged illusion is not a hallucination, so tell me which premise the room will simply have to take on my word and how I should flag that at the close. I would rather they leave dissatisfied and clear-headed than satisfied and confused, and I want them to see what the disjunctivist's move costs, not merely that it is available.
