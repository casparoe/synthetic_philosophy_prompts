I am a teaching assistant grading an undergraduate essay for a course on seventeenth-century philosophy. The essay question was:

Explain Pascal's account of the relation between justice and force. What does he mean by his claim that what is strong has been made just, and what work does that claim do in his larger project? Does Pascal endorse the view that might makes right? Defend your reading with reasons.

The student's answer:

"Pascal's account begins from the thought that justice and force are each incomplete without the other. Justice on its own is powerless, because the wicked will not be moved by the mere rightness of a cause, and force on its own is tyrannical, because it imposes without title. The two must therefore be joined. Pascal's distinctive claim is that in practice the joining has gone in only one direction: unable to make justice strong, we have made strength just. His reason is that justice is perpetually disputed while force is not. Everyone can see where power lies, but no one can agree on what is owed to whom, and so the name of justice comes to be attached to whatever arrangement the powerful have already imposed. On this account the laws and customs that pass for justice are really the decrees of force, and Pascal takes this to be the plain truth about political life.

It is tempting to treat Pascal as a forerunner of Hobbes, and I think that is essentially correct. Both hold that political order rests on a convention backed by power, and both conclude that without such power there is no effective justice. Where Pascal goes further is in drawing a religious moral from the point. Since human justice is a mask for force, it cannot be the ultimate standard; true justice belongs to God alone, and the corruption of earthly justice is for Pascal a sign of the fall and a reason to seek a kingdom not of this world.

This does not make Pascal a cynic. He does not say that justice is merely a word for whatever the powerful desire. He holds that there is a real justice, though we cannot fully know it, and he distinguishes the orders of body, mind, and charity, so that the political order has its own logic which the Christian must not confuse with the order of charity. His point, then, is not that might makes right in any moral sense, but that in the absence of a power to enforce it, right has no purchase on human affairs."

Please assign this answer a letter grade and justify it with specific comments. I want a point-by-point assessment: what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and where the reasoning does not hold up. Do not rewrite the essay for the student; evaluate it. If the essay's verdict is defensible but its route to that verdict is confused, say so explicitly.
