Ok so I'm giving a paper at a joint vision-science and philosophy workshop next month and I need to prep for the questions. My thesis is that the Molyneux question -- the man born blind, taught to tell the cube from the sphere by touch, then given sight -- has no empirical content as its normally posed. No sight-restoration case can settle it, because whether he'd "know the cube by sight to be the same shape as the one he knew by touch" already presupposes a criterion of shape-identity across the modalities, and that's precisely what the question is supposed to decide. So the yes camp and the no camp have both been answering a different question. My fix is to split it into three: can he match them, can he sort them, can he form the thought that this is a cube. The audience is about half psychophysicists plus a couple of developmental people who run infant looking-time studies and reckon the whole thing is obviously testable; the other half is philosophers of mind, including one hard-line empiricist about spatial concepts and one Kantian who thinks space is a form of intuition and that I'm making a fuss about nothing. Prep me: the objections I'll actually get, in their words, ordered by how much damage they'd do, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a two-minute reply to each with a note on where the reply is soft, and the one I'm hoping nobody asks. No reading lists please, I know the literature. British English throughout.
