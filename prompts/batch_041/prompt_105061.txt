I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, and I open the unit on functional explanation with a piece of fiction rather than a survey of the literature. I would like you to write that piece for me.

Write a short story of roughly two thousand words. Set it aboard a generation ship whose routine maintenance is carried out by a lineage of self-replicating repair machines—machines that copy themselves with occasional errors, so that the population aboard has something like a heredity, and a history of differential persistence. Your protagonist is a ship's engineer responsible for keeping these machines running; a second character is the archivist who keeps the record of how each machine's components have changed across the generations. The two disagree, repeatedly and concretely, about what a given component is for.

The story should dramatize the disagreement rather than name it. Do not let either character win by fiat, and do not let the narration adjudicate. I want the reader to feel the pull of both positions through the cases themselves.

At a minimum, the plot should turn on three components whose status is contested in different directions:

First, a component that was originally introduced for one purpose but now performs a different, load-bearing role that the ship depends on daily—so that one character insists its function is the old one and the other insists it is the new one.

Second, a component that was reliably favored in the lineage for a role it no longer performs, and that now contributes nothing, or is mildly harmful—so that one character treats it as having a function and the other does not.

Third, a component that arose as a copying error and now contributes to a capacity the ship needs, but whose spread through the lineage is explained by its having been carried along with a component that was itself favored for an unrelated reason—so that neither character can comfortably grant it a function, yet the engineer must decide whether to preserve it.

Then add a third voice: a junior crew member who argues that the whole quarrel is about what the two of them want to explain rather than about any fact of the matter, and that calling something its function is just a way of flagging which explanation one finds useful. Make this third position genuinely tempting, but also show it under pressure—include at least one scene in which the engineer must judge that a component has malfunctioned, and in which the deflationary reading seems to lose its grip.

Finally, let the disagreements matter practically: each verdict should issue in a different decision about repair, replacement, or preservation, with real stakes for the people aboard.

You need not cite or name any philosophers, and I would prefer the story to carry the ideas without lecturing. What I care about is whether the cases are sharp enough that a student who has read the story will already know what the two standard accounts of function disagree about—and why a third, deflationary option is hard to dismiss and equally hard to accept.
