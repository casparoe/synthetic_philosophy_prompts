I sit on the resource-allocation committee of a regional health authority, and I am trying to find out what I actually believe about the decisions we make. Our budget is fixed, so every choice is between two policies affecting determinate, non-overlapping groups of people; take it that the facts about who is affected are known in advance, and that no one is responsible for being in the group they are in. What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best fits the verdicts below, together with a plain statement of which of those verdicts I have to give up. I am not asking for a survey of what has been written about cases of this shape; I want the principle that fits these votes.

1. A single dose of an antidote can be given to one of two groups of people exposed to the same poison, each of whom will otherwise die within the day. One group has two members, the other five. I give it to the five. Each of the two has precisely the complaint that each of the five has, and the count is the only thing left to break the tie.

2. The same dose could instead be given to one patient, whose life it would extend by twenty-five years, or divided among forty patients, each of whose lives it would extend by one year. I give it to the forty. Twenty-five is less than forty.

3. A different drug would save one patient from dying within the month, or restore the sight of three hundred people who would otherwise be blind for life. I save the one. Death outranks blindness, however many blindings there are.

4. Five patients will die within the month unless each receives a marrow transplant. The only matching donor is a healthy man who has refused. A colleague proposes to sedate him and take the marrow anyway; he would be in severe pain for a week and would then recover completely. I refuse. I would still refuse if the procedure left him permanently disabled.

5. Had the five needed only a blood sample, an hour of his time and some bruising, I would authorize taking it against his refusal. So there is a line somewhere between an hour of bruising and permanent disability, and I cannot say where it falls.

6. This is the case I keep returning to. Sealing a leaking shaft will save five thousand workers who would otherwise die, but the blast will certainly leave one trapped worker permanently paralyzed. He is not killed, and he is not used as an instrument; he is simply caught. My instinct is to refuse, and yet I know that at some larger number of workers I would stop refusing, and I cannot defend the number at which I would stop.

7. The authority is deciding whether to introduce a vaccine. One hundred thousand people each face a one-in-two chance of contracting a disease that is not fatal but leaves them debilitated for months. The vaccine eliminates the disease and carries a one-in-one-hundred-thousand risk of death. Adopting it means that one person will certainly die who would otherwise have lived, and that fifty thousand will be spared months of debility. I vote to adopt.

I notice that 2 and 3 look like the same shape and get opposite answers from me, and I cannot yet say what the difference is.

Here is the principle I have been using, which I take to be in the spirit of contractualism, the thought that a rule is defensible only if no one could reasonably reject it, and that complaints are weighed one person at a time rather than added up. Call it P. (i) An option may be chosen only if no one has a complaint against it stronger than the strongest complaint anyone has against the alternative. (ii) Where complaints are of the same kind and equal in strength, the greater number decides. (iii) Complaints are not otherwise summed. (iv) No one may be used merely as a means.

P gives me 1, 3, 4 and 5. It seems to fail on 2, where the forty complaints are each weaker than the single twenty-five-year complaint, so there is no tie for clause (ii) to break; on 6, where the deaths are of a different kind from the paralysis and clause (iii) forbids summing them; and on 7, where the one who dies of the vaccine has a complaint against adoption at least as strong as anyone's complaint against refusing.

Tell me whether P can be repaired, or whether it is one of my verdicts that has to go. State the resulting principle as precisely as you can, clause by clause, giving for each clause the case that forces it. Say what it implies for cases I have not listed, and in particular for this one: a policy that leaves one person permanently paralyzed but whose savings certainly and completely restore her within the year, so that she loses a year and nothing else. Does my way of thinking permit that, and does it permit it because the harm is undone or because the harm was never what mattered? Finally, tell me plainly which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
