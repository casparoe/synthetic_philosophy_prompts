I've been reading about people who feel pain but it doesn't bother them at all — I think it's called pain asymbolia or something — where someone can feel a burn or a pinch and even agree "yes, that's pain", but it doesn't hurt in the usual way and they don't pull away. It got me trying to work out what I actually think makes pain bad, going by my gut on cases, and I can't get them to line up. Bad headache: clearly bad, I'd take a pill. Someone who never feels pain at all: I think they're worse off, not better, because they keep getting hurt. Someone who feels the pain but genuinely doesn't mind it and laughs it off: my gut says it isn't really bad for them, or not the way the headache is. A soldier who's badly wounded and in agony but says he doesn't mind because it means he's still alive: I still think that's bad for him, even though he doesn't mind it. A masochist who seeks pain out and enjoys it: bad for him or not, I can't decide. Same injury, but one person thinks it's just gas and the other thinks it's a heart attack: I think the frightened one is worse off, which bothers me, since the pain itself is the same. And pain while you're asleep or under anaesthetic isn't bad at all, because there's nobody there to feel it.

Is there one principle about what makes pain bad that gets all of these, or close? I lean towards "pain is bad because we mind it", but the soldier seems to break it, and maybe the masochist does too. If you have to drop one of my verdicts to keep a clean principle, which one should go, and what does your principle say about a case I haven't thought of — say, someone who says the pain is terrible but definately wouldn't trade it for numbness, because they'd rather feel something than nothing? Please write it in British English.
