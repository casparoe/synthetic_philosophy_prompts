You're a philosopher of science with a real soft spot for theatre, and I'd like you to think carefully before you write anything. I teach a second-year moduel on chance and credence, and this term I'm doing a joint session with the English department on Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead — the one where the two of them are stuck in the wings of Hamlet, betting on a coin that comes up heads 92 times in a row before it starts behaving itself. What I want from you is a proper reading of the play through the philosophy of objective chance, and in particular through the Principal Principle: the idea that your credence in a future event ought to match its objective chance, so long as none of your information is inadmissible, because chance is supposed to be the thing you defer to rather than second-guess.

Before you start, brainstorm a bit — ask yourself what in the play is actually about chance, as opposed to fate, or luck, or simply being a bit-part in somebody else's tragedy. Then give me the reading, prose please, and make sure it covers the following:

- The coin run itself. A fair coin is still fair on the hundredth toss, and the gambler's fallacy is a fallacy, so is Guildenstern's unease even an unease? Or is the real problem that he has no story about admissible information that would let him keep deferring to the chance while staring at a run that long?
- His hunch that they're caught up in something un-, sub- or supernatural. Take that seriously as philosophy rather than as a joke. Is the supernatural horn a live option, and what becomes of the Principal Principle if it is — can you rationally defer to a chance you suspect is being managed?
- The bit I actually want for the seminar: the play's world is written. Hamlet's plot is running, the two of them are minor characters with a fixed ending, and the Player keeps telling them so. If determinism is true there's arguably no such thing as objective chance at all, just 0s and 1s all the way down, so what is left of the Principal Principle in a world with an author? Is deference to chance a rule that only makes sense when nobody is dealing the cards, and does Stoppard dramatise that or merely presuppose it?
- Where the play pushes back. If you think it shows us something about setting a credence that the chance-credence literature can't get at — something about what it is like to be the one who has to pick a number — say what that is, and be honest about whether it's an argument or just atmosphere.

Don't turn the coin into a tidy illustration of a formula, and don't bend the philosophy out of shape to make it fit the play; if the lens doesn't fit a scene, say so and move on. No references, reading lists, footnotes or page numbers — I know the literature and I'm not asking you to cite anything, and I don't need long quotations from the play either, I know the scenes. British English, please, and I'd rather have your own reading than a survey of what other people have said about Stoppard and probability.
