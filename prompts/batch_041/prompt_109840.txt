I belong to a fortnightly reading group that has spent its last three meetings on the sea-battle chapter of Aristotle's De Interpretatione and on some recent work about whether the future is open, and at this week's session two other members, Nadia and Colin, began an argument that outlasted the pub's closing time. I was a bystander throughout and have been deputised to bring back a verdict next time, which I am not competent to give. I would rather not rehash the texts; what I need help with is the logic of what they actually said.

Nadia opened: 'The whole argument is a reductio of the assumption that a prediction is true or false before the event it predicts. If a statement such as "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is true today, then tomorrow's battle is fixed and everything downstream of it is fixed with it; the only way to keep the future open is to deny that contingent statements about it have truth values yet, and to let them acquire truth values when the events settle.' Colin answered: 'You are running two laws together. Excluded middle says that a statement of the form "p or not p" holds; bivalence says that every statement is true or false. The standard fix keeps excluded middle and drops bivalence: "there will be a battle tomorrow or there will not" is true already, because no way the world turns out falsifies it, while neither disjunct is true yet. That gives you an open future without giving up a law of logic.' Nadia replied: 'That is a trick with the word "true". If the disjunction is true while neither part of it is, then "or" has stopped doing the work it does in proofs, where from "p or q" together with "not p" we are entitled to conclude q; and your view makes the past depend on the future, since one disjunct becomes true only once the battle happens, so what was true yesterday is settled by what happens tomorrow. That is not an open future, it is an unstable past.' Colin came back: 'Instability is just the price of confirmation; we say a forecast turned out right, and nobody supposes we have retroactively altered yesterday. And the alternative is worse: if you deny excluded middle you are committed to holding that "there will or there will not be a battle" might be false, and no possible future makes it false. If instead you let predictions be true or false but deny that truth carries necessity with it, you owe an account of what truth adds to correspondence, and the fatalist will simply repeat that whatever is true now was always going to be true.' Nadia closed: 'Then the mistake is the idea that truth is a property a statement has at a time, full stop. On the view I favour, truth is relative to a context of assessment as well as a context of utterance: yesterday's prediction is neither true nor false as assessed from yesterday, but it is true or false as assessed from tomorrow. The future is open from where we stand and settled from where we will stand, and nothing is contradicted, because "true" is indexed to different standpoints.' Colin: 'That changes the subject. If one and the same sentence, meaning what it means, is true as assessed at one time and not true as assessed at another, then "was the prediction true?" has no answer, and a claim that cannot be mistaken is no longer the kind of claim we make about the future. What we wanted was for the world to be unsettled, not for our verdicts to wobble.'

Neither of them would budge and nobody at the table could adjudicate. What I would like from you is a point-by-point verdict on the six contributions in the order they came, rating each one on a scale running from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly correct, with a short justification for each rating, rather than a general verdict on which of them had the better position overall. Where a contribution is half right, say which half; where the two are talking past each other, say what distinction would dissolve the impasse; and say plainly if any of their claims is simply a mistake about what a view entails. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing about the underlying question, whether the openness of the future is a fact about the world or a fact about what we can know or assert, and if you had to say who came out ahead, who that would be and why.
