I have been keeping a private list of judgments about which of two unpleasant experiences I would rather undergo, and I have reached the point where I can see that the list cannot be entirely right. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, lies behind it, what that principle implies about cases I have not listed, and which of my judgments I have to give up.

By better I mean: given a choice between the two, I would rather undergo the first. Intensity is rated on a scale from 1 to 100, where 1 is barely perceptible and 100 is the worst pain I can imagine. Durations are given in minutes and seconds.

My list:

1. The local trade. I will give up 30 seconds of duration in exchange for a 5-point increase in intensity, anywhere on the scale. So I judge 9 minutes 30 seconds at intensity 10 to be better than 10 minutes at intensity 5, and 9 minutes at intensity 15 better than 9 minutes 30 seconds at intensity 10, and so on. The trade seems right to me at every step, not only at the mild end.

2. The chain. Applying that rule repeatedly, I judge 5 minutes at intensity 55 to be better than 10 minutes at intensity 5. Ten steps of the trade above connect the two.

3. The endpoint. I judge the opposite. Ten minutes at intensity 5 is better than 5 minutes at intensity 55; the second is much worse.

4. Tolerance. Two experiences at the same intensity that differ by two or three seconds feel equally bad to me, and two experiences of the same duration that differ by two or three points of intensity also feel equally bad. I do not register differences that small at all.

5. The floor. No duration of intensity-1 discomfort, however long, is worse than one minute at intensity 100.

6. The generalization. The same pattern appears outside of pain. Comparing apartments, I will give up a slightly longer commute for a slightly better kitchen, and I will make that trade repeatedly, but I will not accept a two-hour commute for an excellent kitchen. I suspect this is the same phenomenon as in 1 through 3, but I would like you to say whether it is.

I recognize that 1 through 3 cannot all hold if better than is transitive, and I suspect 4 makes trouble of the same kind by itself. My own attempt at a principle — that worse simply means more total suffering, intensity multiplied by duration — is refuted by 1, 4, and 5.

What I want from you:

(a) The principle, or the two or three best candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a new case without asking you again. For each, say which of my judgments it preserves, which it forces me to abandon, and which judgment forces each clause of it.

(b) Whether the best principle makes better than non-transitive. If it does, tell me whether that is a defect in my judgments or a defect in the demand for transitivity.

(c) What the principle says about a case I have not listed: choose a comparison in the middle of the range, state both options with numbers, and give the verdict. Then give one case in which your principle's verdict strikes me as plainly wrong, and say whether that is a reason to reject the principle.

(d) If the only principle that keeps every judgment on my list is a complicated one — say, one with a built-in tolerance, so that some pairs come out neither better nor worse nor equal — tell me so plainly, tell me what the simplest principle is that preserves transitivity, and tell me what that simplification costs me.

(e) Finally, the practical question. I compare options in order to choose. If better than turns out not to be transitive, what should I actually do? Is there a rule I can run that will give me a defensible choice, and what do I lose by adopting it?
