I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry and there's one idea from it I can't stop chewing on: that a diagnostic category isn't just a label you stick on a person who was already there. You write up the criteria, clinicians start asking the questions the criteria imply, patients read the criteria and recognize themselves in them, insurance and support groups and internet forums reward certain ways of describing your suffering, and then the manual gets revised on the basis of how all these newly-minted patients actually behave — so the category and the people in it keep chasing each other and neither one holds still. My professor calls it a looping effect and every example in the reading is either super famous (multiple personality, hysteria) or contested, so I keep understanding the idea in the abstract and never feeling it. I want fiction where I have to watch it happen in the details.

Could you write me a short story that does that. Here's the setting, feel free to bend it: a mid-sized American city maybe fifteen years out, and a national diagnostic manual has just released a draft entry for public comment on a newly recognized condition — give it a boring, technical-sounding name of your own invention, the kind of thing that sounds like it came out of a committee, and give it a checklist of around eight criteria, written the way real criteria are written (duration thresholds, "clinically significant distress or impairment," that register). Tell the story in three braided strands: the draft criteria themselves as printed, with a note about which ones the work group fought over; a clinician's intake and follow-up notes on one patient over about eight months; and that patient's own journal, where you can literally watch the manual's vocabulary leak into how she describes her life. I want the loop to be visible in the mechanics, not announced — she starts doing something the criteria don't mention yet, and by the end the leaked revision has folded it in, and a forum she posts on is full of people scoring themselves against the draft. Don't let the story resolve whether she has a real illness or has been handed a script; both readings should stay live. And please don't have anyone in the story name the phenomenon or explain it to the reader — no lectures, no epilogue where a doctor sums it up. Write it in American English.
