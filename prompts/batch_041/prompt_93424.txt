I'm a novelist — three books, the last one about a farm family — and for the past two years I've been a volunteer EMT on our town's rescue squad, which is basically research with a pager, because the book I'm actually trying to write is about a squad like ours. Here's the problem I've backed into. We have no idea what to do about members who go beyond what the squad requires. Last winter one of our guys went into a house that was already fully involved to pull out a dog, and we gave him a plaque at the banquet. In March a different member, same fire, stood on the lawn and did chest compressions on the owner and didn't go in, and two of our officers have been quietly treating him like a coward ever since. Our chief keeps saying "we can't require that, but we sure can expect it," which even I can tell is doing something sneaky with the word expect. We get maybe a dozen calls a year where somebody's conduct is even in question, but a real one every couple months, and the verdict gets told to the member and goes in the file, so it isn't academic. I need a decision procedure we can run after an incident: what to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where it stops with a verdict of required, forbidden, or optional-but-good, and where it has to stop and say this is a value judgment and a human being has to own it. It has to be usable by a chief who has never read any philosophy, in a kitchen, in about twenty minutes, while everyone is still rattled, and it has to survive our lawyer, who only cares whether we're creating a duty. Ground it in the philosophy of acts that go beyond the call of duty — the word for it is supererogation, I think, my daughter's philosophy TA said so — and I know there's at least one tradition that denies such acts exist at all, that every act is either required or not, so make the procedure say out loud where it's taking a side in that fight and why.

It also has to handle the stuff that actually trips us up. The same act isn't the same for everybody — our medical director climbing a ladder to a patient is one thing, a seventeen-year-old probie doing it is another — so there needs to be a step for the person's role, training, and what they'd already agreed to when they signed up, and the procedure has to be able to say "supererogatory for her, required for him" without falling apart. There needs to be a step for the case where somebody did the brave thing for terrible reasons (the dog guy, honestly, wanted the plaque) and a step for the case where the brave thing was also stupid and put two other people at risk. And it has to tell me what to do about precedent, because the minute we commend something, everybody on the squad starts hearing it as the new standard, and I genuinely dont know if that's a flaw in the procedure or just how institutions work. Then run it on three cases and show your work: the dog guy; the guy who stayed outside; and a third one you invent where the procedure comes out against the person everyone in the room is rooting for — I want to see it tell us something we don't want to hear. Finish by telling me what this thing can't do, and specifically which of the verdicts are just me and the chief making a call with philosophy's name taped to them. Keep it in language a person could say out loud in a scene, because I'm stealing it for the book, and don't give me a reading list, I won't read it.
