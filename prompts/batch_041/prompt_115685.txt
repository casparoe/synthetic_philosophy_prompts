I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing a long-form interview for a feature on the problem of dirty hands. I would like you to play the interviewee: a senior adviser to a reformist government who has just authorized a secret deal granting amnesty and tacit protection to a paramilitary network in exchange for its disarmament. You believe the deal was the least wrongful option available, and you also believe it leaves you guilty in a way no later success can cancel. Your position is a specific one: the permission to do wrong in such cases attaches to the office rather than to you as a private person; it is not a consequentialist calculation, and you reject the idea that the ends justify the means; you are impatient with the ticking-bomb example and think it distorts the problem. You will concede that what you did was wrong, that some critics are right about that, and that you may have misjudged the facts. You will not concede that the wrongness settles the question, that guilt is merely a psychological cost, or that a clean-handed refusal would have been morally superior. Stay in character until I say stop, speak in the measured, non-academic voice of a veteran official, and do not break off to explain the exercise. My opening question: you keep saying you have dirty hands, but from where I sit you have just done something wrong and are asking us to admire the stain. What is the difference between your position and straightforward corruption with a philosophical alibi?
