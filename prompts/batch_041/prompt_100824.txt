A friend of mine, Dana, runs a small grantmaking foundation that funds speculative, one-off interventions. Each cycle it has exactly one grant to award, and the standing alternative is always the same: a plodding direct-transfer program that reliably produces 1,000 wellbeing-years (WY) per $1M, where a wellbeing-year is one person's life going well for one year. Every candidate project she considers costs the same $1M, so the only question is which option to pick. She describes herself, without irony, as an expected-value maximizer; some of my colleagues call her an expected-value fanatic, and she takes it as a compliment. Here is her record — five calls I have watched her defend:

1. Funded a project with a one-in-a-million (10^6) chance of producing 10^12 WY. Expected value: 10^6 WY.
2. Funded a project with a one-in-a-thousand (10^3) chance of producing 2,000,000 WY. Expected value: 2,000 WY.
3. Declined a project with a one-in-two chance of producing 1,000 WY, giving to the standing program instead. Expected value: 500 WY.
4. Funded a project with a one-in-a-billion (10^9) chance of producing 10^14 WY. Expected value: 100,000 WY.
5. Declined a project with a one-in-a-quadrillion (10^15) chance of producing 10^17 WY, giving to the standing program instead. Expected value: 100 WY.

The proposal now on her desk: a one-in-a-trillion (10^12) chance of producing 10^17 WY, for the same $1M. Its expected value is 100,000 WY, two orders of magnitude above the standing program. Will she fund it?

I do not want a bare yes or no, and I am not asking what she ought to do. I want to know the rule she is actually running. More than one rule fits all five of the calls above, and the record by itself cannot tell them apart; I would like you to say what each surviving rule predicts about the new proposal, which prediction you would bet on and why, whether the rule requires some cutoff or ceiling and where the record pins it, whether the new proposal is, in her eyes, the twin of call 4 or the twin of call 5, and finally the single additional case — one probability, one payoff, one choice — that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules from one another. No citations or references are needed.
