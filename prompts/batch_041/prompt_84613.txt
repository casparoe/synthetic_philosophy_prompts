I'm a philosopher by training -- metaethics, a bit of political philosophy -- who has somehow been drafted into co-teaching a big interdisciplinary course on genetics and society next term, and I've been reading my way through the behavior-genetics material so I don't say anything embarrasing in front of the psychologist I'm teaching with. She's keen to assign a long piece from one of the big popular science magazines, written by someone who has clearly read a lot of the primary literature and is broadly friendly to it, and my problem is that it *reads* to me as if it's saying something important and true, but every time I try to say exactly what it's claiming I get suspicious. Since the whole point of my half of the course is teaching students which inferences are licensed and which aren't, I'd rather not hand them something I can't audit myself, and my colleague just tells me to relax and that "the science is settled," which is not the kind of thing I can put on a syllabus.

The piece runs roughly as follows. 'Decades of twin and adoption research have established that intelligence is about 70 percent heritable. What that means is that about 70 percent of any given person's intelligence comes from their genes, and the remaining 30 percent from their environment. Because the classic design compares identical twins raised apart with fraternal twins, it effectively controls for the environment, so the estimate can't be confounded by the fact that brighter parents tend to provide richer home environments. The figure is remarkably stable: it climbs from childhood into adulthood, and it comes out much the same across the developed countries where these studies get run, which is what you'd expect if you were measuring a biological constant rather than a social artefact. A decade of large genome-wide studies has turned up only a fraction of that 70 percent, but that's a puzzle about gene-hunting, not a reason to doubt the twin estimates themselves. The upshot is that by the time you're an adult your cognitive ability is largely fixed by your genome, and the 30 percent that isn't is where schooling, nutrition and social policy can actually do their work. Some commentators go further and suggest that because the trait is so strongly heritable within populations, average differences between social groups are probably genetic too, though the author is careful to hedge on that one.'

What I want is an audit, and I'd like it sentence by sentence, or claim by claim: what would a working behavioral geneticist happily sign off on, what would they wince at as a popularization, and what is just not true -- and flag anywhere the writer is using a term of art in its ordinary everyday sense. I'm especially interested in the machinery the piece skates over: what exactly the 70 percent is a percentage *of*, what the twin design has to assume before it will hand you a number at all, what ends up inside the E in these models besides 'environment,' and whether 'heritable' is doing the same work as 'inherited.' A colleague of mine mentioned in passing some trait that is essentially 100 percent genetic and yet completely preventable by an environmental intervention, and I can't remember what it was -- if that's right, I'd like to know, because it seems to bear directly on the last two sentences. Then tell me which of the piece's conclusions actually survive once the gloss is fixed: does 'largely fixed by your genome' follow from anything in those studies, does the stability-across-countries line mean what the writer thinks it means (I have a nagging memory of a result where heritability estimates came out differently depending on the family's socioeconomic status -- is that real, and does it matter?), and does a within-group heritability estimate license any inference at all about between-group differences, or is the standard 'within versus between' reply too quick? Ideally I'd end up with two lists: sentences my students can take at face value, and sentences they should argue with -- and for the second list, which specific correction kills which conclusion.
