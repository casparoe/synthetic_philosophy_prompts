I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and this spring I'm running the discussion section for an upper-level seminar on mechanism, mind, and mathematics; the last stretch of the term is on the Gödelian argument against mechanism in the Lucas–Penrose line, and the argument has gone stale in the room. The students can produce the whole shape of it and the whole shape of the standard replies, and it all comes out sounding like slogans: a consistent formal system can't prove its own Gödel sentence, a human mathematician can see that sentence to be true, therefore no such system captures the mathematician; and then the replies, equally glib. What's missing is the experience the argument trades on — the felt asymmetry between working inside a system that cannot justify a negative and stepping outside it and simply seeing. So I want a session built around that experience rather than around the argument: sixteen students, seventy-five minutes, one seminar room, a whiteboard and movable chairs, nothing on screen, and nothing in the activity itself labelled with Gödel's name or with either philosopher's, so that they walk into the debrief already holding the thing. What they should come away seeing is that this asymmetry is real as an experience and yet does not by itself mark any difference between minds and machines. Give me a runnable plan: roles, materials, rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, a scoring scheme that needs no money but that they will actually care about winning, the debrief, and a frank statement of what the activity cannot reproduce.

Here is the seed I've been playing with. Keep it, repair it, or replace it with something isomorphic, but please check my logic rather than taking my word for it. Two teams. The first team is the system. It starts with the single string X on the board and may write a new string only when that string is obtained by exactly one application of one of four rules to a string already on the board: (1) if a string ends in X, append O; (2) any string may be doubled; (3) any occurrence of XXX may be replaced by O; (4) any occurrence of OO may be deleted. Their target is the string O, a single letter, and they must either exhibit a derivation by the time the round ends or else say plainly that they cannot produce one. They are not permitted to reason about the rules as a whole — only to apply them one at a time, with every move written down, so that the negative answer, if they give it, is one they cannot justify from inside. The second team is the mathematicians: no restrictions at all, and they may reason about anything, including the rule system taken as a whole. I am fairly confident the invariant that does the work is that the number of X's is never a multiple of three — it survives rule 2 because doubling a number not divisible by three keeps it that way, and rules 3 and 4 change the X-count by three and by zero — while the target contains no X at all; verify that before you build anything on it. What I want the room to feel at the moment team two finds it is the gap: team one has been meticulous, has generated a great many strings, has even produced OO and then watched rule 4 eat both letters, and still cannot say why the target is out of reach, whereas team two can just see it. Then I want to take it back. In a short second round I write the invariant on the board as an added rule, say out loud that the machine has now been handed everything the seers had, and give the augmented system a question the invariant does not settle, so the same structure reappears one level up. Materials as I imagine them: index cards with the four rules for team one, a second board for the derivation ledger, a hidden sheet for me with the invariant, a timer.

For the debrief I want something more than a lecture on Lucas. Map particular moments onto particular pieces of the argument: team one's inability to justify its negative is the difference between not having derived something and its being underivable; team two's seeing is the mathematician's grasp of the Gödel sentence; and then the deflation, in three steps the activity should have made available on its own. First, the seers' insight was ordinary mathematics about the rules, and it was absorbed by the system in a single stroke, so whatever team one's blindness demonstrates, it demonstrates something about that rulebook and not about machines as such. Second, in the real case the system already contains the conditional form of the human's insight, so what the human adds is only the antecedent — the assumption that the system is consistent, or sound — which is precisely what the anti-mechanist needs the human to know and the mechanist denies anyone can know. Third, the regress: each fresh insight can be taken on as an axiom, so the anti-mechanist needs the human to keep producing insights that no consistent machine could produce, not merely to outrun one system at a time. Stage those three steps as questions to the students rather than assertions, and tell me where in the seventy-five minutes each belongs. Then the usual warnings: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect at least one student to recognise the underlying puzzle and short-circuit team two, and I expect team one to try to win by declaring the target unreachable on a hunch — and what I should do in each case. Finally, be blunt about what the activity cannot reproduce. My worry is that in the toy setting the seers' insight is transparently formalizable and the target is not a truth of anything, so a well-run version of this game will make the mechanist look right for reasons that have nothing to do with the real argument; if that is so, say so, and tell me what I have to supply from the front of the room to keep the session honest. I don't need references; I need the plan.
