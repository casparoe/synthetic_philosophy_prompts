I chair a citizen charter-review commission in a mid-sized city, and in three weeks I have to present my proposal to the commission itself before it goes to a public hearing. I am not a political theorist; I am a nurse-anesthetist who got appointed after the last redistricting fight, and I have read enough to be dangerous but not enough to be quick.

My proposal: replace the nine-member elected city council with a forty-five-member Citizens' Assembly chosen by stratified random selection (stratified by ward, age, and housing tenure), serving a single four-year term with no possibility of re-election, meeting in public, with deliberations minuted and the reasons behind each vote recorded. The offices we keep — mayor, city auditor, and school board — would retain ordinary elections and ordinary re-eligibility.

The thesis I must defend is not simply that sortition is more democratic. It is narrower and, I think, harder. The standard objection to lot-based legislatures — that they destroy accountability — conflates two different things: accountability by sanction (voters can throw the rascals out) and accountability by condition (publicity, recorded reasons, rotation, and recall by a lot-drawn jury). I want to argue that for legislative bodies only the second is normatively required, because the first is precisely what produces careerism, name recognition as a qualification, and the permanent campaign; whereas for executive and auditing offices the first is indispensable, since someone must be answerable for the exercise of discretionary power over individuals. Sortition for legislatures, elections for executives — that is the asymmetry, and it is the whole of my case.

The audience is not a seminar. It is two sitting council members whose seats would disappear; a political scientist on the commission who studies democratic accountability, is broadly sympathetic to lot-based bodies, and thinks I have misdescribed what elections do; a civil-rights lawyer who will argue that random selection cannot substitute for elections because it cannot guarantee an organized minority a seat at the table; a union officer who will ask whom she negotiates with next year and who can be held to a deal; and a deputy city attorney who will warn me that state law may not permit an unelected governing body with taxing power. We do have the wave of citizens' assemblies and permanent citizens' councils convened in Europe and Canada over the past two decades to point to, though I do not want to lean on them.

Act as a veteran who has prepped witnesses for hostile public hearings and charter fights, and who also knows the democratic-theory literature well enough to tell a conceptual objection from a merely practical one. Think carefully, and before writing anything, brainstorm privately about what each of these five people actually cares about and what each would regard as a concession versus a capitulation.

Then give me the prep sheet. Objections in the order they will actually come, and separately ranked by how much damage they do to my thesis — these two orders will differ, so say where. Each objection phrased the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine. Each with a two-minute reply I can deliver out loud, and a candid note on where the reply is soft, question-begging, or works only on a friendly audience. Tell me what to concede up front rather than fight, and what I must not concede even under pressure. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.

Do not give me a reading list, citations, or gestures at what scholars have argued — I have no library access before the hearing and nothing to fall back on but the argument itself. Close by stating how confident you are in the prep sheet as a whole and in your ranking of the most dangerous objection, and single out the point you are least sure about — including whether my distinction between accountability by sanction and accountability by condition is really doing the work I think it is.
