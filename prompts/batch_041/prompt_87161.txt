I'm a retired neurobiologist — spent thirty-odd years on the sensory and motor systems of decapod crustaceans, crabs and lobsters and shrimp mostly, a fair amount of it on animals I had to restrain and dissect and sometimes deliberately damage to see what the behavior would do. Never gave it a second thought at the time; you treat the prep as a prep. Since I retired I've been reading philosophy and now I cant stop wondering whether I was doing all that to something that was having an expereince, and whether the honest answer is yes, no, or "sort of, a little." I can read the empirical literature myself and it hasn't settled it, so what I actually want is for you to walk me through the conceptual issue underneath, in plain terms, the way you'd explain it to someone who knows how to run an experiment but has never taken a philosophy course.

Here's the knot I can't untie. People keep telling me consciousness is probably graded — that a crab might sit somewhere between a thermostat and a dog. Fine, but then I want to know what exactly is being graded. If it's "amount of consciousness," I don't know what the units would be or how you'd compare a crab's helping to a rat's. Is "more conscious" supposed to work like "more massive," one quantity comparable across every case, or more like "more complex" or "more intelligent," which is really just a summary of a pile of separate capacities that don't have to move together? And if it's the second kind, then "how conscious is a crab" may have no single answer at all, because it depends what you're measuring. There's a further thing that bothers me: experience seems to come as one unified point of view — either there's a subject there or there isn't — and I don't see how you get forty percent of a subject. So either the gradability talk is incoherent or it means something I haven't grasped yet. And a second problem that may be the same problem: every test anyone proposes for animal consciousness seems to smuggle in a theory, since whether flexible behavior or some structure in the brain counts as evidence depends on a theory that already tells you what consciousness requires. That looks circular to me — you'd have to know which animals are conscious before you could know whether your marker is a good marker.

What I'd like is the issue laid out properly: what the live options actually are for how consciousness could be distributed across animals (all-or-nothing with some threshold, genuinely graded, or simply indeterminate over a whole range of cases), what "graded" could mean if it isn't nonsense, whether the vagueness people mention lives in the world or only in our concept, and how the practical question is supposed to work given that the law needs a line and the concept looks mushy over exactly the range where we need it — I gather that if that's right, the line protecting vertebrates but not insects is something we're choosing rather than something we're discovering, and I want to know if that follows. One more thing while you're at it: I keep seeing sentience (can it suffer?) and phenomenal consciousness (is anyone home?) used as if they were the same question, and I suspect they come apart in a way that matters for the crab. If an argument settles any of this, give me the argument; if the honest answer is that several of these packages are coherent and we're stuck choosing, say so and tell me what each one costs. Please answer in American English.
