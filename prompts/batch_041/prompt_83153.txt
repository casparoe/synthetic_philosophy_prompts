Two objections to utilitarianism are commonly treated as separate criticisms, and I would like them compared as arguments rather than summarized as positions.

The first is that the theory fails to take the distinction between persons seriously. Because it identifies the good with the greatest total of satisfactions, it allows one person's loss to be justified by another person's larger gain, and so treats a plurality of separate lives as though it were a single life whose welfare is to be maximized as a whole. The second is that the theory alienates the agent from her own life. In requiring her to regard her deepest commitments as no more than one set of claims among others, to be set aside whenever the overall balance favours doing so, it makes her a conduit for the general good rather than the author of her own conduct.

First, state each objection in its strongest form, making explicit what it alleges to be wrong with utilitarianism and what a theory that answered it would have to look like.

Second, observe that each can be cast as a complaint about a kind of merging — of many persons into a single subject of welfare, and of one agent into an impersonal standpoint — and say whether the resemblance is substantive or merely verbal. Relatedly, consider whether a utilitarian can turn the first objection against the second: if persons really are separate, does that give the agent any licence to weight her own commitments more heavily than anyone else's, or does separateness instead reinforce the demand for impartiality?

Third, test the two objections against a single case. Suppose a scheme funds one person's central project by spreading its cost so thinly across so many others that none of them notices the loss. The objection from separateness appears to condemn the arrangement, since it justifies some persons' losses by another person's gain; the objection from alienation appears to forbid requiring the beneficiary to give the project up. Apply each objection to the case, and say whether the two verdicts can be reconciled, and if so, how.

Fourth, give a verdict: which of the two objections is the stronger criticism of utilitarianism, and what does the weaker one nevertheless get right that any adequate theory would have to accommodate?

Argue each point on its merits. A survey of what has been written about these objections is not what is wanted.
