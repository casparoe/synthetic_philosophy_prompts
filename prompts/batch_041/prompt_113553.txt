I'm a science journalist who spent a decade on the health and epidemiology beat before moving to a column on science and ideas, which means the causal-inference toolkit is now the native language in which I think: directed acyclic graphs, backdoor paths, structural equations, exogenous nodes, the do-operator, the modularity and invariance assumptions that make an arrow an arrow, identifiability, transportability, target trials, counterfactuals. When someone tells me that X causes Y, I hear a claim about what happens to Y under an intervention on X, plus a claim that the relation is stable across settings.

I'm now writing a long piece about al-Ghazālī and the critique of causation in The Incoherence of the Philosophers, and I want to understand the position by translating it into that vocabulary. Here is the position as I have come to understand it, stated without any of the apparatus I'm asking you for. The philosophers hold that fire burns cotton in virtue of a nature the fire has, and that causes bring about their effects by necessity. al-Ghazālī denies that any created thing has causal power. On the positive picture, what we call a cause is a reliable sequence and nothing more: God creates the burning at the moment the cotton meets the fire, and the regularity is God's custom, which he is free to vary, so that what we call a miracle is not the violation of a law of nature but a departure from a habit. I know there is a live dispute about how much of this he asserts and how much he is merely arguing that the philosophers cannot prove their necessity claim, and I want the translation to survive that dispute rather than take a side in it.

What I want: restate that position as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, one commitment at a time, and then give me the residue report — what survives the translation, what survives only in distorted form, and what has no counterpart at all. Concretely:

1. Does the occasionalist picture have a clean formulation as a causal model — say, one exogenous node, God's will, with no arrows among the created variables, so that the whole observable world is a single confounding structure in which every association is a common-cause association? If that is the translation, tell me whether it is faithful or whether it quietly changes the position, and what the position looks like when drawn as a graph.

2. The do-operator is where I expect the interesting failure. Occasionalism can say what happens when you intervene — put the cotton to the fire and it burns — so it seems to license arrows at the level of interventions while denying that anything passes along them. Is that a stable position in my vocabulary, or does the vocabulary forbid it? And what becomes of the modularity and invariance assumptions, which for me are what make an arrow more than a correlation?

3. What has no counterpart at all? My candidates: power, efficacy, agency, the idea that an effect is somehow owed to its cause, and the theological motive — that a creature with causal power would be a co-author of creation, which is why the doctrine exists at all. Tell me which of these are genuinely inexpressible in causal-inference terms, which are expressible but pointless to express that way, and which I only think are inexpressible because I have confused a claim about the world with a claim about who is responsible for it.

4. Finally, the verdict I actually want. Where the untranslatable remainder is a hole in my vocabulary, say so plainly. Where it is a sign that the occasionalist claim was never a claim about causal structure but about authorship — something my vocabulary was never built to record — say that too. And answer the question that decides whether the exercise was worth doing: if I conclude that the arrows are real at the level of the do-operator and that the rest is theology, have I conceded the substance of the dispute, or have I only changed the subject?

No reading list and no citations — I want the translation and the residue. End with a single final line in exactly this form: \residue{LABEL}{PHRASE}, where LABEL is one of DEFECT, OPACITY, BOTH, or NEITHER — DEFECT if the remainder is chiefly a gap in causal-inference vocabulary, OPACITY if it is chiefly a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked, BOTH if both, NEITHER if the translation is complete — and PHRASE is at most twelve words naming the single biggest casualty of the translation.
