I'm a science journalist working on a feature about moral bioenhancement, and I want to do something a bit unusual: instead of asking a living ethicist, I want to ask what Darwin would have thought. Not the usual would he approve of gene editing question, which is boring. The specific proposal is this: a future society offers safe embryo screening or a mild neurotropic intervention that shifts the distribution of fellow feeling upward and in-group favouritism downward. It does not create saints. It just makes the average person a bit more sympathetic to strangers and a bit less fired up by tribal markers. Suppose also that a secular movement grows up around this, treating the intervention as a kind of sacrament, required for full membership, and promising a kind of salvation by chemistry. What would Darwin have made of that? I'm interested in the tension in his own account of morality: he thought the moral sense grew out of social instincts and sympathy, and that it could widen from family to tribe to nation to all humanity, but he also thought tribes with more cohesive, self-sacrificing members would tend to beat tribes whose members were more selfish. So would he see engineered sympathy as accelerating a trend he already thought was underway, or as pulling out the selection pressure that kept morality a living, contested thing? And would he treat the secular sacramental movement as just another religion in his functional sense, a tribal survival strategy with a new ritual, or as something genuinely novel because the ritual actually changes the biology rather than just symbolising it? I want a prediction with reasons, grounded in what he actually wrote about conscience, sympathy, religion and agnosticism, not a generic summary of his life. And please write it in British English.
