Consider the sequence of events that runs from the executions of the Hébertists and then of Danton and his allies in the spring of 1794 to the fall of Robespierre that summer, and treat it as a test case for the old claim that revolutions devour their children. I am not asking for a narrative of the Terror, which I know. I want the sequence analyzed through one specific mechanism, which I will state so that we are working with the same object. Call it the loyalty-signaling spiral: once public denunciation becomes the cheapest available proof of revolutionary commitment, each faction's safest move is to strike first, the winners of one purge become the losers of the next, and no one can credibly promise not to denounce the others, because the promise itself looks like a conspiracy. Take this mechanism seriously and work out whether it actually fits. Identify who the relevant actors were at each stage, what options were open to them, why preemption was or was not rational for each, and whether the spiral predicts the specific order of the victims -- Hébertists, then Dantonists, then Robespierre -- or merely predicts that someone will be purged and cannot say who.

Then tell me where the mechanism breaks. The coup of Thermidor is the hard case: was it a further turn of the same ratchet, or a different thing altogether -- a defensive coalition of the frightened, in which men who had every reason to distrust one another acted together because each feared he would be next? If it is the second, say what that does to the spiral story: does it survive as the explanation of the earlier purges, or does it turn out to have been describing a symptom rather than a cause?

Finally, set the mechanism against the three standard ways of reading the Revolution -- as class struggle, as the working-out of an ideology, and as contingency. Which of the three does the signaling story most resemble, and where does it cut against each? In particular, a class-struggle reading locates the motor of events in the conflict of social interests, an ideological reading in the logic of a doctrine that cannot tolerate dissent, and a contingency reading in the play of accident and individual decision; the spiral I have described seems to need none of these, and to explain the same events from a bare strategic logic that could in principle run anywhere. Say whether that appearance is real. Does the sequence, read this way, favor one of the three interpretations, or does it reveal that the interpretive debate has been miscast -- that the interesting question is not which motor drove the Revolution but why the same self-consuming dynamic recurs across revolutions with entirely different social bases and creeds? And be explicit about what the mechanism leaves unexplained, rather than forcing it to account for everything.
