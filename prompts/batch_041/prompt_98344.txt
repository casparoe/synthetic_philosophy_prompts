I'm not a philosopher by training, but the topic below has become personal. In March I was driving home late and looked down at the radio for what I still insist was a second. I was in a collision; another person was hurt. Since then I've lived inside a particular pair of sentences. One of them — if I had not reached for the dial, none of this would have happened — I rehearse constantly, and no evidence settles it, because it is about a world that never occurred. The other — if the other car was already across the centre line, then the crash was not mine to avoid — is a matter for the accident report: given what actually happened, it is true or false, and I can find out which. I've slowly understood that these two sentences differ neither in tense nor merely in feeling, and that the difference between them is one of the oldest unsettled problems in the philosophy of language. I want a poem that makes that difference visible.

The distinction I need the poem to embody is the one between indicative and counterfactual (subjunctive) conditionals. The standard illustration is the pair about Kennedy: the indicative — if Oswald did not kill Kennedy, then someone else did — is almost certainly true, since Kennedy was killed; the counterfactual — if Oswald had not killed Kennedy, then someone else would have — is not something we have reason to accept, and it smuggles in a conspiracy. Same antecedent, same consequent, opposite verdicts. The going story, roughly, is that indicative conditionals are tied to our evidence, so that the probability of a conditional tracks the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent (the Ramsey test, and what is often called Adams's thesis), whereas counterfactuals are about how non-actual possibilities would have gone and are judged by something like how close those worlds are to ours. There are also triviality results that seem to show you cannot keep the probability equation and still treat conditionals as ordinary propositions with truth values — which is why some philosophers deny that indicative conditionals have truth conditions at all. I don't need the poem to take a side in that dispute, but I need it not to get the dispute wrong: no treating the two as simply different tenses, and no treating the difference as mere rhetoric or tone. Do not present the poem as a lecture; let the philosophy live in the structure.

Here is the form, and I want it followed exactly. Write a poem of fourteen lines in seven rhymed couplets. Each couplet is a minimal pair: the first line an indicative conditional, the second its counterfactual twin, sharing the antecedent and the consequent but not the mood — the same content under two different relations to the world. The two lines of a couplet must rhyme, and the rhyme should be the only thing holding them together: the couplet should insist that the two lines belong as one utterance, while the sense slowly pulls them apart. Early couplets should feel like the same sentence said twice; by the later ones the two halves should be saying things that cannot both be true. In the final couplet, break the rhyme — a slant rhyme or none at all — so that the form itself records the split that the poem has been tracking. Metre is yours to choose, but keep the two halves of each couplet close enough in shape that the difference registers as a difference of mood rather than of length, and make at least one couplet turn on the truth-value gap directly, where the indicative is settled by what happened and the counterfactual is unmoored from it. Write it in the first person, about the accident — the dial, the road, the other car — but let the personal material be the vehicle rather than the subject; it should not become a confession or a moral. You may allude to the Kennedy pair if it earns its place, but do not make the poem about it.

The test I'm holding you to: a philosopher of language should not wince, and someone who has never heard of the distinction should finish the poem understanding that the two conditionals differ in kind, not in degree.
