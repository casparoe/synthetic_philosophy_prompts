I run a monthly thing for our department's science-and-society reading group -- mixed bunch, around 18 people, mostly grad students, a couple from econ and public health. I've got 80 minutes and I want to do something that makes them *feel* Goodhart's law instead of nodding at it, because everyone nods at it and nobody flinches. But I don't want a generic "people game a metric" game; those write themselves and the group sees it coming.

Here's the wrinkle I actually care about, and it's why I'm being fussy. I want the rules arranged so they walk into two different ways a proxy comes apart from the thing it's meant to track, and only realise at the end that these are seperate things:

(a) the proxy was only ever correlated with the goal. The correlation was a fact about how the world happened to be arranged, and as soon as you select on the proxy you erode the background conditions that made it track -- no bad faith, no cheat, maybe no agent at all;
(b) the proxy stays a perfectly good measure in the normal range, but optimising it hard shoves you into a regime where more proxy means less of the goal.

If somebody spots a loophole and exploits it on purpose, fine, that's a third thing (the adversarial one), but I don't want the whole thing to hinge on someone being a bastard, because then it's just a story about cheaters and everyone tells themselves they'd never.

Setting should be deliberately mundane -- not a trolley problem. Teams, a scoreboard, a handful of rounds, a payoff in fake currency or chocolate, and a hidden second number the facilitator tracks that the players never see. That hidden number is the real goal. They should be trying hard to win and feeling good about it right up until the debrief. If the mechanism can work through selection -- who gets to keep playing, who gets more resources next round -- rather than through any one person deciding to cheat, that's much better. I want them to see it can happen with no villain in the room.

What I need:
- the rules, materials, round structure, scoring -- concrete enough that I can run it cold;
- how you bake in (a) and (b) as two distinct moments rather than one mushy "haha they gamed it";
- the debrief: map what actually happened onto the two (three) ways a proxy fails, name the law only at that point, and get them to see that (a) isn't about anyone's motives;
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to steer it back;
- and be honest about the part I can't stage. I suspect the statistical flavour of (a) -- the whole thing being about a sample from a distribution and many instances over time -- is really hard to reproduce with 18 people in 80 minutes. Tell me what I'd be faking, and what I'll just have to assert as facilitator.

Nothing in the activity can require them to know any stats or econ going in. And I dont want to stand up and explain the distinction first -- if they don't hit it themselves it's a waste.
