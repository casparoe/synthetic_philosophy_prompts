So I've been lurking in a forecasting forum and its Discord for about six years, and there's this moderation policy I keep chewing on because I can't tell if it's philosophically coherent or if I'm just bad at reading it. Roughly, the rules are:

- When two people disagree on a factual question in a thread, a mod asks both to state their number and their reasoning, and whoever's estimate is further from the thread's current median has to go first.
- If the two of them trade replies more than three times without either publicly moving their number, the thread gets locked with a note saying "no update, no continuation."
- But if the disagreement shifts from what will happen to what should be done, the norm flips 180: the person being pushed to update is told they're being coercive, and the community line is that you don't owe anyone a change in your values just because they disagree with you.
- There's a carve-out: if one of the disagreeing parties is credentialed in the domain, rule 2 is suspended and the thread can run forever.
- And there's an independence checkbox on every forecast: you have to tick a box saying you arrived at your number without looking at anyone else's estimate in the thread. If you don't tick it, your number is excluded from the median — even though the whole thread is public and everyone reads it anyway.

What view of peer disagreement is this whole apparatus presupposing? I think the pieces aren't pulling in the same direction and I want someone to tell me whether that's a bug or whether I'm misreading it. Specifically:

Reconstruct the implicit theory. Is it conciliationist, steadfast, or a hybrid that switches depending on the subject matter? Does rule 2 presuppose that the mere fact of disagreement is evidence — that it should move me halfway toward the other person — or only that refusing to engage is evidence, which is a way weaker claim? If it's the weaker claim, is the rule still doing any epistemic work at all?

Take the fact/value split seriously. Does the policy presuppose that disagreement about values isn't evidence about anything, so there's nothing to conciliate toward? And is that consistent with the rest, or is it just a peace treaty that happens to have an epistemology stapled to it?

The credentialed-expert carve-out seems to presuppose that conciliation only triggers when the two parties are roughly epistemic peers, and that credentials are a decent proxy for peerhood. Is that a stable place to stand, or does it collapse into "defer to whoever the community already trusts"?

The independence checkbox is the one that really gets me. It presupposes that how you came to hold a credence changes how much evidential weight it carries — that a number reached by reading the thread counts for less than the same number reached in isolation. Is that compatible with straight evidentialism, where only the evidence matters and not the causal history of your belief? Or does the checkbox quietly commit the forum to a non-evidentialist view of what justifies a credence?

Then tell me where the practice is underdetermined — which parts strictly presuppose one view and which parts would be licensed by several rival views equally well — and what the rules would have to look like if the forum replaced the implicit view with full-on steadfastness, or with full-on conciliationism. What would actually have to change, and what would stay the same because it was never about disagreement in the first place?
