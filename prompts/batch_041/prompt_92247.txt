I'm putting together materials for a small reading group on confirmation theory, and I need a decision tree I can hand people at the start of the session on the problem of old evidence. You're a philosopher of science who works on Bayesian confirmation and the epistemology of theory choice — please build the thing properly rather than giving me the standard tour.

The setup: if evidence only confirms a hypothesis by raising its probability, and you already knew the evidence before anyone had even formulated the hypothesis (you were certain of it, so its probability was 1), then the hypothesis can't get a boost out of it. And yet we all feel like a theory that derives something we already knew — an anomaly that sat there unexplained for decades — earns real credit for it. Your tree should start from someone who accepts that confirmation is probability-raising and work them down to a definite position on what's gone wrong.

What I want from the tree:

- Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of famous replies. Work out the fault lines yourself (I've listed the ones I suspect below), then place the standard views in the resulting slots, and tell me about slots nobody occupies.
- Every node has to be a question the reader can answer without already knowing where the branch leads. No nodes like 'do you think the problem is dissolved?' — each node should be a concrete commitment about a case, or about what confirmation is.
- Leaves should be positions, each with a one-line gloss of the view and one line on its most awkward commitment — the thing it forces you to give up or swallow elsewhere. Don't lean on names. If there's a standard label you're confident of, add it, but the tree has to work for someone who's never heard the name.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't separate, and say whether that's because the views genuinely coincide or because I'd need another node.
- For combinations of answers that nobody takes, say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Cases I want the nodes to be able to lean on (add your own too, but these should all be reachable from the tree):

1. The classic historical one — a new theory of gravity that entails a long-known anomaly in a planet's orbit.
2. A made-up linguistics case: someone has carried the same dozen grammaticality judgements around in their head for years, then builds a grammar that derives all twelve. Does the grammar get confirmed by the judgements?
3. A case where the theory was built by staring at the very data in question, so there's a double-counting smell to the whole thing.
4. A case where the 'old' evidence isn't certain at all — the person only half-believed it, or believed it on testimony they never checked.

Fault lines I think are in play. Check these and tell me which ones aren't real:

- Whether confirmation is agent-relative at all — is 'E confirms H' a fact about the evidential relation that holds independently of anyone's actual credence?
- Whether the culprit is the idealisation of logical omniscience (nobody is really certain of the consequences of what they know, so old-evidence cases are just cases of a badly idealised prior) or the updating rule itself.
- Whether the fix is to conditionalise on something other than E — the fact that H entails E, or the fact that the old datum turns out to be derivable — and what that does to the probability axioms.
- Whether you're allowed to measure the boost using a hypothetical prior in which the evidence wasn't known, and if so whether that prior is still your prior (i.e. whether you're still doing subjective Bayesianism at all).
- Whether the whole thing is better seen as a problem about explanation rather than confirmation, in which case the Bayesian machinery is the wrong tool and the credit comes from explanatory virtue.
- Whether old evidence is even the interesting problem, or whether its mirror image (evidence you'd already expected, which also can't budge you) is the real one — and whether your tree should treat them as one issue or two.

Length: 5–7 branching nodes, 8–12 leaves. Audience: grad students who know what conditionalization is but not the literature. I care most about the diachronic question — what should actually change your credence when the news is old — so weight the tree that way rather than toward a taxonomy of published replies.

Think carefully and brainstorm the fault lines before you commit to the tree; I'd rather see three good nodes then ten that don't cut.
