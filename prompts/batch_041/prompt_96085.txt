A mate and I are writing a long piece together about self-improving AI, and he keeps reaching for Escher's Drawing Hands. His argument runs like this: the two hands draw each other, so it looks like a system that makes itself, but the whole thing only works because something sits outside the loop — the paper, the pen, Escher, the conventions of drawing. He calls that the inviolate level. So, he says, any system that rewrites itself still needs a level it doesn't rewrite: the interpreter, the weights, the hardware, us. Which means the idea of an AI hauling itself up by its own bootstraps, or being the genuine author of its own values, is a category error, and 'recursive self-improvement' is a phrase that sounds profound and says nothing.

I dont think the picture will carry that much weight, but I can't quite put my finger on why. Can you do the mapping for me — hands, paper, pen, Escher, the sheet its all on — and then say plainly where it cracks? Especially what the picture smuggles in just by being a picture: a thing somebody made, frozen at a single moment, looked at from outside, whose parts are depictions rather than causes. I want the strongest case against him, not a list of nits. My hunch is that the inviolate level is quietly doing three jobs at once — a level that this particular process happens not to rewrite, a level that can't be rewritten at all, and a level that's logically out of reach from inside — and that the drawing only ever supports the first of them. Then tell me what's left standing of his conclusion, and whether there's a better image for whatever it is he's actually getting at. He'll say I'm just being pedantic about a metaphor, so the answer needs to show why it isn't pedantry — which disanalogies bite and which are just scenery.
