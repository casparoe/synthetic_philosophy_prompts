I'm giving the opening remarks at a one-day memorial workshop for my doctoral supervisor, Ruth Calder, who died in March at seventy-eight. She spent her career on the frame problem — on how a system that acts can represent what it has left alone. The room will be split. Roughly forty of the attendees are her former students and collaborators in knowledge representation and non-monotonic reasoning; they will notice instantly if I get the technical content wrong, and they will be mortified for me if I make her sound like a mystic. The rest are her two sons, her brother, and a handful of friends from her climbing club, none of whom know what a frame axiom is, and several of whom are braced for an hour of computer science before the coffee. Her older son asked me, before he knew what I was planning to say, not to turn his mother into a thought experiment; I have promised him I won't.

I want about eight minutes of remarks that hold both halves of the room at once, and I want the frame problem to do the structural work of the speech rather than be its subject. Here is the shape I have in mind. To describe anything that changes — a world, an institution, a person — you have to say two things: what the change alters, and what it leaves alone. The first is easy; the second is the whole of the difficulty, because there is no finite way to write down everything that stays the same. Any inventory of what doesn't change is either incomplete or absurd. She used to say that this was not a defect in our formalisms but a fact about the world, and that the largest part of anything is the part you cannot list.

That is what I want these remarks to be about. The standard move — the list of what she was, her kindness, her stubbornness, her handwriting — is the list everyone reaches for, and it is always incomplete and slightly false. The honest thing is that what did not change is the largest part of her, that it cannot be enumerated, and that this is not a failure of love but the shape of it.

A few constraints. Don't teach the audience the frame problem; assume the collaborators know it and let everyone else absorb it from the shape of what I say. I would like the words frame problem to appear once, early, and never again. No she-would-have-wanted, no she-lives-on-in-her-students-and-her-code. End on something concrete rather than consoling. For the last fifteen years of her life she kept an index card taped to the edge of her monitor, headed things that don't need updating; she rewrote it every January. I would like that card to be the last thing the room sees.
