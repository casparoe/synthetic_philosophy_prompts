I am a novelist, and I am writing a book set in two villages that face a common problem and have solved it in opposite ways. Both draw irrigation water from the same channel, and in both each household is entitled to take water in turn. On the north bank the order is fixed by the date on which a household first broke ground on its fields, and it has not changed within living memory. On the south bank the order is fixed by the sequence in which households arrive at the channel head on the morning in question, so that it rotates. Neither village can give a reason for its rule beyond the fact that it is the rule. In both, a household that takes water out of turn is shunned until it makes amends, and shunning is the only sanction, since no one has the power to stop a determined thief.

I want to write a long scene in which two of my characters argue about what these practices actually are. The first is a game theorist who works on the theory of convention and thinks in the idiom of Lewis: coordination problems, common knowledge, multiple equilibria, arbitrariness. The second is a historian of the region who has spent years in the archives of both villages and is suspicious of that entire vocabulary. I want them equally informed and equally formidable; neither is there to lose.

Let the argument be specific rather than programmatic. Let the historian press the things that make the word "convention" feel wrong to her: that the villagers experience the rule as an obligation rather than as one option among several; that the north bank's rule is the residue of a dispute settled long ago in favor of the households that were then strongest, so that calling it arbitrary launders a distributional outcome; and that the rules have shifted when drought or migration made them costly, which suggests they are adaptive rather than arbitrary. Let the game theorist answer on his own terms: that arbitrariness means the situation does not single out a solution, not that the solution is unimportant or just; that an asymmetric equilibrium is still an equilibrium; that a felt obligation may be downstream of an expectation rather than the ground of it; and that "it does something useful" underdetermines the rule, which is exactly what the two banks demonstrate.

Then let each of them find the place where the other's framework runs out. I am especially interested in whether the north bank can be said to have a convention at all if no one there has ever entertained the south bank's rule as a live alternative, and in whether an evolutionary account of why such rules persist competes with the convention analysis or quietly presupposes it.

I don't want a tidy resolution or a synthesis at the end. I want the two positions to end sharper and more uncomfortable than they began, and I want each character to have conceded something real along the way. They should sound like people talking, not like a seminar, even when they are being technical.
