I help run a small research group — myself, two doctoral students, and a semester of protected time each — that wants to make a genuine contribution to the computational modeling of analogy and conceptual slippage, working in the tradition of Hofstadter's Copycat and the Fluid Analogies Research Group. Our starting point is the architecture that group built: a coderack of many tiny codelets that run in parallel and compete, a slipnet of concepts whose activations shift as a run proceeds, a workspace in which temporary structures are built and torn down, a global temperature that governs how wildly the program explores and that falls as the program converges, and a microdomain of letter strings — the canonical problem being abc is to abd as ijk is to what?, where the intended answer, ijl, competes with readings that yield ijd or something else entirely. We are persuaded by the underlying philosophical claim, that analogy-making just is high-level perception and that what looks like rule-following is really the fluid perception of sameness and slippage, but we are also aware of the standing complaints: the slipnet is hand-built, the program never learns, the domain is tiny, and success is judged by a handful of hand-graded examples.

Which open questions in this line of work matter most, and in what order would you have us take them up? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for a small group with modern tools (we can afford far more compute than the original authors could, and we can call on large language models either as instruments or as foils), how neglected it is, and what concrete result over the next year would move it up or down our list. Please weigh, among others, questions like these: whether conceptual slippage is best explained by probabilistic parallel search at all, or whether the explanatory work is really being done by something else in the architecture; what it would take to give such a model genuine concept learning without sacrificing the fluidity that makes the approach distinctive; whether the letter-string microdomain still earns its keep or should be traded for a richer one (the group's later letterform, Bongard-problem, number-sequence, and melodic microworlds, or something else); how one would even evaluate a model's slippage against human behavior rather than against the designers' intuitions; and whether the whole enterprise has been made redundant by what large language models now appear to do. Push back on my framing where you think it is wrong — if the most important question is not on my list, say so and say why. Assume I know the literature, so no reading list and no citations; I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
