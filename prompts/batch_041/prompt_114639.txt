I was arguing with a friend who's become a hard eliminativist about the propositional attitudes, and he produced the following argument to convert me. I've written it out the way he did. (1) To explain someone's behavior is to cite the causes of that behavior. (2) The only causes that appear in our best-confirmed causal account of the world are neural and physiological states. (3) Beliefs and desires just are the states we posit when we give folk-psychological explanations of behavior. (4) No neural or physiological state is true or false, is about anything, or is sensitive to reasons as such. (5) So beliefs and desires are not identical with any neural or physiological state. (6) Whatever causes behavior is a neural or physiological state. (7) So beliefs and desires do not cause behavior. (8) A posit that does no causal work should be eliminated from our ontology. (9) Therefore we should eliminate beliefs and desires. I'm fairly sympathetic to the conclusion — I've never been happy with the idea that a brain state is "about" Paris — but the argument bothers me because it seems to prove far too much. If (4) and (5) are doing the work, the same reasoning should rule out pains, itches, and moods, which are no more "about" anything or "sensitive to reasons as such" than beliefs are, and which I have no intention of eliminating. And if (2) and (6) are doing the work, they seem to eliminate every higher-level property as a cause — being an infection, being a drought — and thus to abolish the special sciences wholesale. So either the argument is valid and the eliminativist owes me a principled stopping point between beliefs and itches, or one of these steps is doing something illegitimate and I can't see what. Please identify the step I should reject, say why it looked compelling in the first place, and tell me what it costs to give it up — in particular whether I can keep eliminativism about beliefs while retaining the mental states and the special sciences I want, or whether the costs force me to abandon the conclusion too. If more than one step is defective, say which is the load-bearing one and why the others are less serious.

I'd like you to state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and at the end flag the single point in your answer you're least sure about — the one most likely to be wrong or to depend on a contested reading.
