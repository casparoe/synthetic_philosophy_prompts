I sit on the ethics committee of a transplant hospital and I am drafting a brief in favour of a small pilot programme for compensated kidney donation. A senior colleague on the committee is opposed, and the backbone of her objection is an analogy rather than an argument about outcomes.

She puts it this way. The country's supply of whole blood rests on unpaid volunteers who give because they want to give. When money was allowed into that system, the gift was degraded: the donors who had been giving out of solidarity drifted away, those who remained were the ones who needed the money, and the whole arrangement came to be seen as a transaction between a hospital and a body rather than a gift between strangers. Kidneys are the same kind of good as blood, she says, and would be subject to the same erosion. Any scheme that compensates donors will therefore end by destroying the very disposition — the willingness to give a part of oneself to a stranger — on which our organ supply depends. Better to keep both on a voluntary footing.

Two things make me want to test this rather than concede or dismiss it. The first is that plasma is a partial counterexample: donors are paid for plasma in the United States, Germany, Austria, Hungary and Czechia, and those systems have not collapsed into a caricature of exploitation — or at least, the people who run them do not think so. My colleague's reply is that plasma is simply a different case. The second is the shape of the scheme I am actually proposing. To keep the transaction from looking like a purchase, the pilot would give donors no cash: instead, a contribution of a fixed sum to a charity of the donor's choosing, and a modest tax credit. Donors would also be offered, as an alternative to the tax credit, priority on the waiting list should they themselves ever need a kidney. My colleague says that dressing the payment up does not change what it is, and that the blood analogy shows why.

I am not asking you for a survey of the literature on organ markets, and I am not asking whether compensated donation is a good idea in the abstract. I want an audit of the analogy as an argument — what it is entitled to establish about the scheme above, and what it is not. Please write it in the following sections:

The mapping. A correspondence table, one row per element, pairing what the blood system contributes to the argument with what the kidney case would have to contribute for the analogy to run. Keep the table tight and follow it with roughly 100 words of commentary. (~200 words)

Where the analogy earns its keep. The respects in which the two cases really are alike, stated as precisely as you can. (~150 words)

Where it breaks. The disanalogies, with a clear flag on which ones are load-bearing for my colleague's conclusion and which are incidental. Say explicitly where the plasma case belongs — whether it is a counterexample to the analogy, a different cell in the same table, or something else. (~400 words)

Does the conclusion survive? Whether, once the breaks are on the table, the analogy still supports the claim that my scheme would erode the willingness to donate, and what independent evidence that claim would need if the analogy cannot supply it. Address the design features of my scheme — charitable contribution, tax credit, waiting-list priority — separately: does the analogy have anything to say about whether the form of the compensation matters, or is that a question it cannot reach? (~250 words)

A better analogy. If there is one that carries the weight my colleague wants carried, name it and sketch the mapping. If there is none, say so, and say what that implies for how the argument should be run. (~150 words)

Bottom line. My position, in the form of what I can and cannot say to the committee on the strength of this analogy. (~100 words)
