I sit on the model-review committee at a public agency, and I have been asked to draft the fairness section of a memo about a risk-scoring tool we are preparing to deploy. To orient myself I read an explainer that a colleague circulated, and I have reached the point of not knowing how much of it to trust. The passage that concerns me runs roughly as follows.

"Several research groups proved, a few years ago, that a fair algorithm is impossible. The setting is simple: a model assigns each person a risk score, and a threshold converts the score into a yes-or-no decision. The theorem concerns three properties one might want. First, the scores should be calibrated: a score of 0.7 should mean a seventy percent chance of the bad outcome in every group. Second, the model should catch the people who go on to do the bad thing at the same rate in every group, so that false-negative rates are equal. Third, it should wrongly flag the people who would have been fine at the same rate in every group, so that false-positive rates are equal. The theorem says that you may have any two of these three, but never all three, unless the groups have identical base rates. This is precisely what happened in the recidivism dispute: the journalists showed that the tool wrongly flagged one group more often, the vendor replied that the tool was calibrated, and the theorem shows that both sides were right and that there is nothing to fix. The moral is that fairness and accuracy are in tension, that a model can be fair only in the sense of choosing which group to sacrifice, and that the practical remedy is to leave the protected attribute out of the features and let the data speak for itself."

I would like an audit I can act on. Please work through the passage claim by claim and tell me which statements a specialist in algorithmic fairness would accept as written, which are loose but defensible, and which are wrong; where something is wrong, give the accurate version and say whether the surrounding argument survives the correction. Four questions matter most to me. First, is the "any two of three" slogan being used correctly here? I want to know exactly what the three conditions are, whether accuracy is among them, and whether the trade-off is genuinely between fairness and accuracy. Second, if the calibration defence is sound, what does it show about the claim that the tool treats the two groups unequally — can both claims be true at once, and if so, what is the committee actually being asked to decide? Third, we currently apply a single global threshold to the score, and a colleague proposes group-specific thresholds chosen so that the error rates match; is that a genuine remedy, what does it give up, and does it bear on the slogan in the passage? Fourth, what does the result not show? I would rather my memo state plainly what has been proved and what is being inferred from it. I am inclined to accept the closing advice, since omitting the protected attribute is what our counsel recommended in any case, but I would like to know whether it follows from the theorem or merely sounds prudent. If it helps, work a small numerical example with two groups, unequal base rates, and explicit false-positive and false-negative rates, to make the conflict concrete. No citations are needed; I am after the substance, not the literature.
