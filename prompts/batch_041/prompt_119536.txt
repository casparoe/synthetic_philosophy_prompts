I'm the instructor for a 400-level metaphysics seminar, and I'm writing the final exam. Every year the unit on Bradley's regress produces the same essays: students narrate the regress in two paragraphs, announce that it either shows that relations are unreal or that it is a harmless infinite regress, and then stop — none of them see that the argument is really about the unity of the proposition, that is, about what makes a proposition with several constituents one thing rather than a mere aggregate or list of those constituents. I want one exam question that cannot be answered by that narration. Act as an experienced examiner and metaphysician, and think carefully about where the real difficulty lies before you draft anything.

To force the issue, I am going to hand the students a position they cannot have read, because I invented it: call it the brute-unity view. On this view a proposition such as the proposition that the cat is on the mat is genuinely composed of its constituents — the cat, the mat, and the relation of being on — and is one thing rather than a mere plurality because it possesses a primitive unity that is stipulated to be neither a further constituent of the proposition nor a relation. Its defender claims that the regress never gets started, since the unifying feature is by hypothesis non-relational and non-constituent, so no further relation is needed to tie it to the items it unifies. The question I want should require students to (a) state precisely why the regress is supposed to threaten any view that locates a proposition's unity in a relation among its constituents, (b) decide whether the brute-unity view genuinely escapes the regress or merely relabels the difficulty by fiat, and (c) identify what the view costs — what it leaves inexplicable, what it forces one to give up about composition or about propositions being complexes of their constituents, or which more familiar position it quietly collapses into. Pitch it at 60 minutes and 25 marks, so roughly an 800- to 1000-word essay.

Please give me the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words that a strong student could plausibly write under time pressure, and a marking scheme that attaches marks to the specific moves a top answer must make (totalling 25) and says, in one line each, how students typically get those moves wrong. The scheme should draw a sharp line between an A answer that sees that the words non-relational and non-constituent may be doing all the work by stipulation — and goes on to ask what independent reason there is to accept such an entity — and a B answer that simply reports that the view avoids the regress. Build the question so that it stands on its own: do not anchor it in any named author's published reply to the regress, and do not ask for citations or a reading list. Begin with a few bullet points sketching the lines of attack on the brute-unity view, then write the question, then the model answer, then the marking scheme.
