I recently read a short popular essay on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and I would like to know how much of it I can trust. The essay runs roughly as follows:

'Illusionists hold that phenomenal consciousness — the felt redness of red, the sting of a pain, the what-it-is-like of experience — is an illusion. There is no inner show and no qualia. The brain processes information and issues reports about experiences, but the experiences themselves, understood as private, ineffable, introspectively accessible feels, are a trick the brain plays on itself. In this respect illusionism is a form of eliminativism: it says that consciousness does not exist, and that the whole vocabulary of experience is a folk-psychological fiction that a mature neuroscience will replace, much as phlogiston was replaced by oxygen. The genuine puzzle, on this view, is not the hard problem but what illusionists call the meta-problem — which is, in effect, a new name for the easy problems of consciousness, attention, introspection, and report, repackaged; get those right and the hard problem simply drops out of the picture. Nor is illusionism a piece of armchair metaphysics: it is an empirical hypothesis about the mechanism of introspection, and more specifically a version of the higher-order thought theory, on which a higher-order representation is what makes a first-order state conscious, the illusionist adding that this representation systematically misrepresents its target as possessing phenomenal properties. The upshot is that whatever confidence we have that other people, or we ourselves, have experiences rests on a misrepresentation — though illusionists cheerfully grant that we behave as though we do, and that something is going on in the brain.'

I have no background in this area and no stake in the outcome, though I suspect the essay's tone is more confident than the position can really support. What I want is an accuracy audit I can act on. Please take the essay claim by claim and, for each, say whether a philosopher who actually defends illusionism would accept it as stated, would treat it as a harmless popularization, or would say it is something illusionists explicitly deny — and, in that last case, what the real position is. Please flag any place where a term is being used in a non-standard or question-begging way, and any place where the essay conflates two claims that the view is careful to keep apart. Finally, tell me whether the closing claim — that our confidence in other minds rests on a misrepresentation — follows from any version of illusionism worth defending, or whether it is an overstatement the illusionist can simply decline. I am not asking for reading recommendations or citations; I want to know where the essay goes wrong, and why.
