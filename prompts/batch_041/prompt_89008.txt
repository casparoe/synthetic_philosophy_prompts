I am a novelist at work on a book in which a dying woman, a lapsed physiologist, tries to articulate a view of death that is neither consolation nor stoic resignation. I have been reading the James Legge translation of the Zhuangzi, in the chapter "The Great and Most Honoured Master," and the following paragraph has become central to her idiom:

"The True men of old knew nothing of the love of life or of the hatred of death. Entrance into life occasioned them no joy; the exit from it awakened no resistance. Composedly they went and came. They did not forget what their beginning bad been, and they did not inquire into what their end would be. They accepted (their life) and rejoiced in it; they forgot (all fear of death), and returned (to their state before life). Thus there was in them what is called the want of any mind to resist the Dao, and of all attempts by means of the Human to assist the Heavenly."

I need help understanding how this paragraph is supposed to hang together. In particular, I want to know what work "forgot" and "returned" are doing: why is forgetting the fear of death paired with returning to a state before life, rather than with simply accepting death as natural? And how does the final clause—about not using the Human to assist the Heavenly—bear on that pairing? I am not asking for a survey of Zhuangzi's view of death; I want a close, conceptually patient reading of this specific passage. Please explain the distinction between the Heavenly and the Human as it operates here, say what kind of "return" is at issue, and tell me what a character could plausibly take from this without turning it into either a deathbed platitude or a doctrine of annihilation. If the passage invites a tension—say, between "no joy" at birth and "rejoiced" in life, or between forgetting and not forgetting one's beginning—I would rather you bring that tension out than smooth it away.
