I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I'm opening next week's seminar on the new riddle of induction with a poem rather than a summary, so that the group has to diagnose for itself what has gone wrong. I'd like you to write it. Take the role of a poet who has also spent real time with the philosophical literature on induction, and think carefully before drafting: begin by setting out for yourself exactly what the grue example is meant to establish and what it is not meant to establish, then decide how the verse form can carry that. Please don't gloss the puzzle in a preface or an epigraph; the poem has to stand on its own, and it should be accurate enough that someone who knows the argument recognizes it without being told its name.

The content I want in the poem is this. An emerald examined before some future time t is green; a second, and a third; and so on. Now let 'grue' apply to a thing just in case it is examined before t and green, or not examined before t and blue. Every observation we have is equally evidence for 'all emeralds are green' and for 'all emeralds are grue', and the two hypotheses diverge only over emeralds first examined after t. What I want the poem to locate is not that we cannot know the future -- that is Hume's problem, and it is not this one -- but that we have no account of which predicates are fit to be projected from observed cases to unobserved ones, and that the familiar repairs, that grue is disjunctive or time-dependent or otherwise ill-formed, are unsatisfying, since 'green' can be defined from 'grue' and 'bleen' by appeal to the very same t, so that the oddity is perfectly symmetric and each vocabulary looks primitive from inside itself. What is left to separate the two hypotheses is entrenchment: a fact about our verbal habits and our past inductive practice, not about emeralds.

I want a pantoum. Its rule is that the second and fourth lines of each quatrain become the first and third lines of the next, and that in the closing quatrain the opening quatrain's first and third lines return as its second and fourth, so the poem loops. That circulation is why I am asking for this form and not another: in a pantoum a line keeps its words and changes its sense as the frame around it changes, and a poem that can be read round again is a poem in which the 'observed' and the 'unobserved' readings cannot be told apart from the inside. Five quatrains at most, and please make the returns exact.

Constraints I care about:

- The recurring lines should be the ones carrying the argument, and they should be neutral between the two hypotheses in the way the evidence is neutral. A reader who has gone through the poem once should be unable to point to a line that forces 'green' rather than 'grue'. The effect I am after is that reading it a second time, from the loop, feels like re-running the same observations under the other hypothesis.
- The time t has to fall somewhere in the poem as an event rather than a date, and the poem's sense should turn there without announcing that it has turned.
- Let the entrenchment point emerge from the form instead of from a thesis sentence. The reader should be able to notice, without being told, that the poem's preference for one reading is a matter of which line came first -- of habit -- rather than of anything the stones have shown.
- No uplift, no mystery, no 'we can never know'. The last line should be as flat and as examinable as the first.
- No footnotes, no explanation of the puzzle, and no name-dropping. If the poem works, a reader who knows the literature will supply the name.

When you have finished, add two or three sentences, separate from the poem, saying which line you take to be the pivot and what the repeated lines do on their second appearance. I want to be able to check whether the form is doing the work or merely decorating.
