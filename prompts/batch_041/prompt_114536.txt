I work on the theory of consent, and I am looking for a case analysis that my graduate seminar can fight over. The theory to be tested is the standard one: a token of consent is valid only if the consenter understands what is being consented to, is competent to consent to it, and consents voluntarily, where voluntariness excludes coercion and the forms of manipulation that bypass or subvert deliberation. The episode I want you to take is the consent apparatus that has grown up around European data-protection law: the banner, the one-click accept-all placed beside a many-click refusal path, the granular toggles that no one reads, the subscription tier that buys the same service with the tracking removed, and the regulators' recurring finding that making refusal harder than acceptance is itself unlawful.

Analyze the episode through the theory, and then the theory through the episode. On the first pass: say what is actually being consented to, which is less obvious than it sounds; say who the consenter is when the data also concern a household or a third party who never appears at the banner; and sort the regime's failures into failures of information, of voluntariness, and of competence, marking which of them are failures of the surrounding architecture rather than of any individual transaction. On the second pass I want the case to test the theory rather than illustrate it, so take seriously a distinction I will put in my own words: a consent requirement can function either as a moral transformer, whose point is that the consent makes permissible an act that would otherwise wrong the consenter, or as a procedural gate, whose point is only to record that the consenter was asked. The digital regime looks to me like a gate that has kept the rhetoric of the transformer; tell me whether that is a corruption of the practice or a discovery about the theory.

Press on two things the theory was not built for. The first is consent at scale: repeated, low-salience, unread, and given by people who are not the only ones affected. The second is consent-or-pay, where declining leaves you facing a price rather than a threat; say what the theory's account of coercion implies there, whether it can reach an answer without quietly moralizing the baseline, and whether the answer survives once the baseline is moralized. Close by telling me what the theory cannot settle: whether any of this shows that consent is the wrong instrument for the goods at stake, or only that this instrument has been badly used, and what a defender of consent as the central gate should be willing to concede. Where the theory is silent, say so rather than making it speak.
