I'm TAing a value-theory seminar next term and the one session I always fumble is the wrong kind of reasons problem, so I want a handout I can give the students instead of lecturing off the cuff. Please build me a decision tree that takes a student from a commitment they'll accept on sight to a named position on what the problem actually shows. The audience is mixed: about half are ethics students who know the fitting-attitude machinery, and about half are epistemology students who'll insist the belief case is the real one, so the tree needs to work for both without assuming either.

Seed case, which the students will recieve without argument: a demon will make your life go badly unless you admire him; you now have some reason to admire him, but he hasn't thereby become admirable. Branch the tree on the commitments that force you one way or the other — whether the demon gives you a reason *for* the attitude at all, or only a reason to want it, to bring it about, or to cultivate the disposition; whether you hear 'he isn't admirable' as a claim about reasons or about something else (fittingness, correctness, merit); whether you're treating 'there are reasons to admire him' as straight-up identical with 'he is admirable'; whether you fix things by restricting what counts as a reason of the right kind, and if so whether that restriction is principled or quietly circular; whether you keep any analysis of value in terms of fitting attitudes; and whether the admiration case and the belief case (a pill that pays you to believe something false, or a bet that pays off only if you believe) seperate, since plenty of people think belief has a wrong-kind problem too.

Phrase every node as a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — no 'are you a buck-passer?' style nodes. Where the tree can't separate two positions, flag it in a note rather than papering over it. And tell me which answer-combinations nobody seems to occupy, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Skip citations and reading lists, I'll add those myself.

No more than 900 words, and lead with one sentence saying what the tree is a tree of, then the tree, then a short table of the leaves with each leaf's most awkward commitment.
