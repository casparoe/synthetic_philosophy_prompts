Like: I finish a proposal convinced it's strong, then a colleague whose judgment I trust says it's thin; or I recall that panels like ours reliably over-fund anything that sounds interdisciplinary and under-fund the plodding-but-solid work; or the dean circulates a calibration sheet showing that, across past rounds, each of our confidence ratings barely predicted whether the project ever produced anything, and that a couple of us are actually below chance on the "will this get cited" call. And we still have to vote — we cant suspend judgment and go home, somebody has to rank the pile and send it up.

So dont give me a seminar on conciliationism, give me a procedure the panel can actually run: turn what we know about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia into a checklist for a panelist who's just gotten evidence that their own read is unreliable. The questions to ask in order, what a yes and a no to each look like, where it tells you to discount your own read and where to stand pat, and what to do when you have to cast a score while thinking your own scoring is garbage. Run it on two of my cases — the disagreeing-colleague one and the calibration-sheet one where someone has to act on a judgment they think is junk. Say where it'll misfire, and give me one case where following it forces a panelist to do something they'll hate.

Give it back as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
