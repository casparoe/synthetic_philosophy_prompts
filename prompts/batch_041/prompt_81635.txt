Next month I am presenting at a departmental seminar a paper arguing that the everyday conventions of the places we visit — how much to tip, how to queue, whether to cross against the light — are not moral obligations, and that the familiar maxim about following the customs of whatever place one is in is, as usually stated, a confusion. My positive claim is that whatever reason a visitor has to conform is never owed to the culture (cultures are not right-holders) but only to particular persons with whom the convention coordinates; and that once one asks whose claims a given convention actually mediates, the intuitive ordering of cases inverts. Queueing and jaywalking mediate determinate claims — of the people behind you in the line, of the drivers around you — and so bind the visitor comparatively firmly, whereas tipping, in its pure form as a price set by convention rather than by any claim of the person served, mediates no one's claim at all and binds only where and because it is in fact how the server is paid. I mark this with a pair of terms I will use throughout: conventions that are load-bearing, in that some determinate person's claim runs through them, and conventions that are decorative, in that they carry only the marks of belonging.

The room: two normative ethicists who work on promising and on the ethics of social practices, who will go after the difference between an obligation and a liability to social sanction and will ask whether I have simply relabeled blameworthiness; two behavioural economists who model norms as equilibria and will ask whether decorative is just my name for low-stakes equilibria, and whether my distinction survives once norms are equilibria all the way down; an anthropologist of gift and hospitality exchange who will say that decorative is what an outsider calls a practice whose point he cannot see; and a legal scholar interested in custom as a source of private-law rights, who will want to know what my view implies when custom is legally incorporated. I speak quickly and will have roughly twenty minutes of questions.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from a general audience, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it, each with the reply I can deliver in two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and a clear indication of what I should concede up front rather than defend. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Then separate what is settled among people who work on convention and social normativity from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus; where my own framing is idiosyncratic rather than a recognized position, say so plainly. I do not need citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
