Two weeks ago a friend of mine — a union organizer, the kind of person who has actually had to move votes for a living — and I spent most of a long evening arguing about Simone Weil's note on the general suppression of political parties, the one she wrote near the end of her life and that was published only after her death, and we ended in the sort of disagreement that keeps generating new questions days afterward. The version of Weil I put to him was the one I had always found compelling: that a political party is a machine for manufacturing collective passion; that it is an organization built specifically so as to bring collective pressure to bear on the thinking of each of its members; and that its first aim, and in the end its only aim, is its own unlimited growth. From those three claims she drew a conclusion much stronger than the usual complaint that parties sometimes go wrong — that belonging to a party always constrains one to untruth, and that the existence of parties is therefore an unconditional evil, not a cost to be weighed against the goods parties deliver but a structural incompatibility between the party form and truth and justice. And her alternative was not coalitions, or factions, or better internal democracy; it was the revue, a group gathered around writing and reading that one is near rather than a member of, and on whose behalf no one stands for office. My friend, who has spent fifteen years in a union he loves and that has plainly been worth what it cost him, said three things I could not answer. First, that parties are the only machinery anyone without money has ever used to make power answer, and that abolishing them is a counsel of despair which leaves the field to those who never agreed to be bound by the rule. Second, that the revue is a party with a masthead — editors, a canon, an orthodoxy of taste, a small group of people who decide what is serious. Third, and worst, that Weil's definition is not diagnostic at all: every group that has to act together, from a congregation to a research lab to a family to his own union local, exerts pressure on the thought of its members, and a criterion that condemns everything tells you nothing about when the thing you are already inside has turned. We ended up agreeing, roughly, that the interesting question is not whether parties should exist but whether there is a way to tell, from inside a group that is doing necessary work and mostly doing it decently, that the machine has started running. That is the question I want you to answer, and I want the answer as a procedure rather than an essay.

I have a reason to want it, and a deadline. I chair the internal standards committee of a state-level political party — about four thousand members, four of them in the legislature — that began six years ago as a single-issue campaign and is still, in most of the ways that matter, that campaign with a mailing list attached. The committee is three volunteers, none of us lawyers; we meet monthly for two hours and usually have two or three disputes referred to us, and our recommendations go to the steering committee for ratification, with expulsion ultimately requiring two thirds of the convention. In ten weeks we hold our first convention and adopt bylaws, including — because two of our legislators keep breaking with the platform on the issue that founded us, and because four hundred members have signed a petition to expel someone over a remark he made on a podcast — a discipline and expulsion clause and a rule about endorsing candidates. I have no interest in the legislative half of Weil's title; the first readers of that note took it to mean driving parties out by persuasion rather than banning them by law, and either way it is not available to me. I want the diagnostic half. Write me a decision procedure my committee can run on a case in ninety minutes, that a member who has never read a word of Weil can be walked through, and that will survive being argued about by people who will assume any rule we write is aimed at them. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions stop the process with a no and which merely slow it down, and — the part I care about most — the points at which the procedure hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than of fact, with a note on which of the earlier steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It should be mechanistic rather than moralistic: it should name what has actually happened in the organization, in terms someone could dispute with evidence, rather than asking us to assess how the group feels about itself. Where Weil's positive alternative can be turned into something usable inside a party that intends to contest elections — what it would mean to stand near a position rather than be bound to it, what a loyalty test looks like when it is wearing the costume of a request for message discipline — build that in; where it cannot, say so plainly rather than inventing a step to fill the gap.

Then run it on three cases in order, showing the procedure working rather than just announcing a verdict. First, the draft unity rule: members of our legislative caucus would be required to vote with the platform on a list of core votes drawn up by the steering committee or lose party funding and staff support; the two legislators who break with us say the list is drawn to catch their dissent and not the leadership's past deviations, and I suspect they are right. Second, the expulsion petition: a member said something about the founding issue that some members read as a slur and others read as clumsiness, there is no rule that covers it, and the petitioners argue that the petition itself is evidence of what the membership considers beyond the pale. Third, the endorsement: the only candidate who can win a district we need is quietly on the other side of the founding issue, and the alternative agrees with us and loses by thirty points. I expect the procedure to give me an answer I will not like in at least one of these, and I would rather have it now than at the convention; when it does, tell me what the answer costs, because I will have to argue it in front of people who will hear it as a betrayal. There is one more thing I want the procedure turned on, and I want you to be unsentimental about it: our founding pledge, which members have signed for six years, promising never to support any candidate who voted for the bill that started us. I have defended that pledge in every argument I have ever had, including this one. If the procedure says it was the first loyalty test we ever ran, say so, and tell me what follows. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — whether a diagnostic built out of Weil's critique can ever prescribe anything positive or can only tell you that you are ill, whether the revue is a real alternative or a beautiful thing that has never held a ward, and what a procedure of this kind is blind to that my union organizer friend would see immediately. Do not send me a bibliography; I will not read Weil and neither will the other two members of the committee. Write the one-page version they can use, and keep the reasoning for me.
