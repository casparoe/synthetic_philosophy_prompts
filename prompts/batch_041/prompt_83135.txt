At our school charity carnival I got drawn into a dollar auction: the auctioneer sells a one-dollar bill, the highest bidder pays what they bid and keeps the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays what they bid but gets nothing. I bid three dollars against a classmate who had bid two, so we both walked away poorer. When I asked my economics teacher what the lesson was, she said it came down to not throwing good money after bad, and a friend said I should have quit while I was ahead. Both phrases sound wise and neither tells me anything I can use. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that not throwing good money after bad could be making, from the trivial to the radical, and say for each whether it is plausible on its own terms and whether it actually supports the advice my teacher gave me? I would particularly like to know how much of my situation is ordinary reluctance to write off a loss and how much depends on this auction's peculiar rule that the runner-up also pays, and whether either slogan has anything to say about the point, if there is one, at which continuing to bid would be rational rather than a mistake.
