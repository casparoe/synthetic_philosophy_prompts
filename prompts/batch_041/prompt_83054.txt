I've got an odd request for you. I spent thirty-five years as a isotope geochemist and took early retirement a few years back, and since then, mostly to stop myself from just watching cricket, I've been running a Sunday morning Stoicism reading group out of the back room of the local library — we did the Handbook first, then a big chunk of the Discourses, and this year we've been grinding through Marcus. One of the regulars turns out to be a classics lecturer, and she's asked me to give the work-in-progress paper at her department seminar in three weeks. It's a much tougher room than my library group: fifteen or so people, nearly all of them people who read Greek for pleasure, one of them apparently finishing a commentary on Epictetus; a philosopher of mind who works on cognitive behavioural therapy and knows the Ellis and Beck lineage cold; an emeritus ancient philosopher with a reputation for standing up at every seminar to insist you can't lift Stoic ethics out of Stoic physics; and a grad student who is a serious practising Stoic, journals morning and night, has a blog, and gets visibly annoyed when academics treat the modern revival as a marketing phenomenon. I'm the only person in the room without a classics degree and I'd rather not get filleted.

What I'm defending: first, that the distinction Epictetus opens the Handbook with — some things are up to us, some aren't, and the ones that are up to us are our judgements and impulses — is the load-bearing idea of his practical philosophy, and that the revival's habit of calling it the dichotomy of control is a legitimate piece of translation-by-adaptation rather than the distortion the classicists say it is; second, that the ethics survives without the providential cosmology, i.e. you don't have to think the universe is a rational animal looking out for you for the practice of confining your concern to what's yours to have its point. There's a section where I argue the moderns are wrong to drop the Stoic habit of attaching a caveat to every commitment — the bit where you resolve on a course of action but add that it happens only if nothing prevents it — and another where I lean on the doctrine of preferred indifferents to argue that caring about outcomes in a conditional way was always part of the package.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not another essay. Give me the eight or ten objections in the order they'll actually arrive, in the vocabulary of whoever will make them — I want the Epictetus specialist's version and the CBT philosopher's version even when they turn out to be the same objection wearing different clothes — ranked by how much damage each does to my two claims, each with a reply I can deliver in two minutes without slides, plus a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so I'm not defending ground I don't need, and which one exchange I have to win for the rest of the paper to survive. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. I dont need a bibliography, I need the conversation.
