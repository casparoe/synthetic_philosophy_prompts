I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy of history. At a workshop last week another participant used the proverb 'history is written by the victors' to argue that the standard account of the Armenian genocide is simply the account that won, and that the so-called revisionists who dispute it deserve the same scholarly hearing as the historiography they reject. I think the proverb is doing illegitimate work there, but I can't yet state the objection without either denying an obvious grain of truth in it or sounding as though I'm policing a boundary I can't justify. Please separate the precise claims the sentence could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical: about who controls archives, curricula and monuments; about selection and silence in the surviving record; about whether the content of an accepted account is systematically distorted by power; about whether historical truth is constituted rather than merely influenced by power; and about who counts as a competent knower. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive thesis a working historian could accept, or something false or self-undermining. Then tell me which of the readings my colleague actually needed in order to reach his conclusion about the genocide, and whether any independently defensible reading delivers it. Two further questions: does the proverb have any special application where the surviving documentary record is largely the perpetrators' own bureaucracy rather than that of the victims or the victors; and, granting the proverb its strongest defensible reading, on what basis should the line between legitimate historiographical revision and denial be drawn? Lay this out as a numbered menu I can bring to the next session.
