Here's a view I've talked myself into and want stress-tested. I think interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing are genuinely true or false — there's a fact of the matter about whether my life is going better than yours, over and above what either of us prefers or would say — and I think those facts are knowable enough to base policy on (tax this, fund that). The bit I actually like, and which I suspect is doing more work than I admit, is that a comparison only becomes well-defined once you fix a currency, and I'd say the right one is how much of what matters to a person is going their way, weighted by how much it matters to them. Which I know sounds like restating the problem.

So what counts against this? I don't just want "ordinalists say you can't add utilities." I want the specific stuff: which formal results, which empirical findings (the measurement work, adaptation, the QALY/DALY fight in health econ), which intuitions, and which consequences I'm now stuck with (utility monsters, the repugnant conclusion) actually bear on it. Rank them from "this would genuinely kill the view" to "awkward but survivable," and tell me which ones I have to concede myself rather than wait for a critic to point out. If it helps, the thing that would spook me most is two equally defensible measures ranking the same two people oppositely.

Be honest about whether that currency line makes the whole thing unfalsifiable — if every failed measure just shows we haven't found the right currency yet, is that a bug or just the cost of doing business? If it turns out well insulated, say so, and say whether that's a problem here or not. I'd rather have a definate verdict than a menu of options, so take a position and defend it.
