ok so I'm running a third-year seminar on transcendental arguments — 14 students, 90 minutes, and honestly they'll have done no reading for it. What I want is an activity where they feel the force of the thing in their bodies, the bit where you try to deny some claim and find the denial quietly helping itself to the very thing it's denying. No lecture up front, they shouldn't hear the term until the debrief.

Here's my rough idea, tear it up if there's better. Everyone gets a card with a "commitment" they have to keep exercising all session — making claims about the past, telling seem from is, using "I" to refer to themselves, giving reasons for what they say, doing sums, treating one thing as causing another. Then each of them gets a target claim to deny (the past is real / there's a world independent of us / other people have minds / there's a persisting self / some things are genuinely reasons for anything). Rounds of about ten minutes: the denier has to state their denial out loud and then defend it under questioning while everyone else hunts for the moment their own speech relies on the target. Gotcha = you either drop the reliance and admit you're stuck, or you pay a token. Tokens are biscuits, no real money needed.

One card in the deck is different: whoever draws it may keep using the practice while flat-out denying the belief — a "useful fiction" licence, they can't be gotcha'd. Watch what that does to the room. That's the whole crux, because it prises apart "you can't stop doing X" from "X is true", and the second is what everyone had been quietly assuming the game had proved.

Then the debrief, which should map the concrete moments — the first gotcha, the first person who just went silent, the licence — onto the pieces of the idea, and be honest about what the game can't do: it shows that we in this room couldn't wriggle out, not that nobody could, and that gap is one the facilitator has to close out loud rather than pretend the game closed it.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and let the debrief seperate what's actually settled from what's a live fight among people who know this literature — don't sell me a contested reading as the consensus. British English please.
