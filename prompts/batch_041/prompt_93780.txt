For the last several months I have been sitting in on a reading group that has been working through the literature on disjunctivism, and I have come away with a frustration I suspect is not mine alone. The exchange has settled into a ritual. Someone presses the argument from illusion, or its hallucination cousin: a veridical perception of a red apple and a hallucination of one can be subjectively indistinguishable, and since we cannot tell them apart from the inside, there must be some common mental core they share. The disjunctivist replies that indistinguishability from the inside is not the same thing as sameness of kind, that the veridical experience is essentially a relation to a mind-independent object while the hallucination is not, and that the argument illicitly reads a fact about our discriminatory capacities into a claim about the nature of experience. Everyone nods, everyone has heard it before, and no one moves. I want a diagnosis of this impasse rather than another round of it. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of experience; a verbal one, in which each side means something different by its talk of sameness of experience, the opponent meaning indistinguishable to the subject and the disjunctivist meaning the same kind of mental event; or two parties answering different questions under a single heading, one about phenomenal character and one about epistemic warrant? I am inclined to think a good deal of it is verbal, but I do not trust that instinct, because the two readings are not obviously independent: if warrant supervenes on phenomenal character, the verbal reading collapses back into a substantive one, and if it does not, that is itself a substantive thesis that neither side has bothered to state.

To make the diagnosis bite, I would like you to test it against a case I have not seen discussed in the group. Imagine a subject fitted with a neural interface that delivers an experience subjectively indistinguishable from seeing a ripe tomato, though there is no tomato and no light anywhere in the causal chain. Now imagine a second subject who looks at a real tomato through a distorting lens to which she has long since adapted, so that her experience is systematically caused in an abnormal way, yet her perceptual judgments about tomatoes are reliable. If the disjunctivist's central claim is that the veridical and the hallucinatory differ in kind because only the former is a relation to an object, does the adapted perceiver count as perceiving or as hallucinating, and does the answer turn on causal history, on the object-relation, or on the reliability of the resulting judgment? If the two sides answer this differently for principled reasons, that is evidence the disagreement is substantive; if they answer it the same way and still disagree about the apple case, that is evidence the dispute is really about something other than the cases. I would like your diagnosis to engage the actual positions concretely, and to end with an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, a stipulated disambiguation, a formal result about the supervenience of warrant on phenomenology, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and that no progress should be expected. Please do not give me a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want the diagnosis and the reasoning behind it.
