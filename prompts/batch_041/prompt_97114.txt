I run the interpretability team at a small AI lab, we're mostly people who came out of the rationalist blogosphere, and the recurring fight is over the word "represents." Someone trains a linear probe that decodes X from the residual stream and says "the model represents X," someone else says "thats just correlational, nothing downstream reads it," and we go in circles because nobodys written down what would actually settle it. So I want a decision procedure we can run on a claim before it goes into a paper or an internal report: the questions to ask in order (does the probe work at all, does it survive shuffled-label and random-direction controls, is it selective rather than picking up everything, does intervening on it move behavior in the right way, does it hold up across prompts or across model sizes), what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no — you're not allowed to say the model represents this — and where it hands off to a person because the dispute is really about what we mean by representation rather than about a fact. Be upfront about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and about the stuff the machinery just cant decide: polysemanticity, superposition, a direction that decodes perfectly but that nothing downstream reads.

Then run it on three claims and tell me what the procedure lets us actually assert in each: (1) there's a single sentiment direction in the residual stream; (2) the model has a representation of a fact it will deny when you ask it directly; (3) a steering vector that's extremely useful for controlling outputs but flunks your causal test — I suspect the honest verdict there is "you found a knob, not a representation" and I want the procedure to say so out loud. And tell me what the procedure can't do, because I'd rather know where it misfires than pretend it's a machine for turning probes into knowledge.
