I've been running a small reflective-equilibrium exercise on myself for a while: I list cases of agency and record, one by one, whether the agent's action is free in the sense I actually care about — the sense worth wanting — as opposed to the sense philosophers mostly argue about. Here are my verdicts. A man who, after a decade of deliberate habit-building, wants to run and runs: my paradigm case, free. A man who wants to run because a hypnotist planted the desire an hour ago, and who would endorse that desire and would have chosen it anyway: not free in the sense I care about, even though everything at the moment of action looks identical to the first case. A man coerced at gunpoint whose will resists throughout: not free. A man in a deterministic world who weighs his reasons and acts on the best of them: free, and I find I don't care that no alternative was ever physically open to him. A man whose choice is settled by a quantum coin flip: not free, even though an alternative was genuinely open. A man whose entire character was fixed by an upbringing he never examined, who now acts in character: free — and this is the verdict that keeps me up at night, because it looks like the hypnotist case with a longer fuse. A company that acts through its deliberative procedure: free in the same sense, which surprises me. And a language model fine-tuned to be helpful, whose outputs reliably track reasons and which can be argued with after the fact: not free in the sense worth wanting, though I can't articulate what it lacks.

The principle I keep reaching for is that the freedom worth wanting is neither openness of alternatives nor the absence of causes but answerability: an action is free when it is the kind of thing its agent can be met with reasons about, and can take those reasons up. So I have two candidate components pulling apart — moment-freedom, control at the time of acting, and provenance-freedom, the right relation to the history that produced the act. My verdicts seem to want provenance-freedom, yet the deterministic case and the company case seem to want only answerability, and the two diverge on the hypnotist and on the long-fuse upbringing.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If it takes an extra clause to save every verdict, give me the clause and tell me what it costs in simplicity; if a simpler principle would force me to give one verdict up, say which and why. Then tell me what your account predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a person who betrays his own settled values in a moment of rage, and a market whose prices encode information no participant holds. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
