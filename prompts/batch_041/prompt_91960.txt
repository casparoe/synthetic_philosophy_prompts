I have spent the better part of a year reading about the philosophy of mind in my spare time, with no formal training, and the puzzle I keep returning to is the one about whether our beliefs, desires, and feelings can cause anything at all. The argument, as I have reconstructed it for myself, runs roughly like this. Every event that has a cause has a physical cause sufficient for it; no event is caused twice over; and a mental state, whatever else it may be, is realized by or rests upon some physical state. Put those three together and the mental state appears to do no work: the physical state already guarantees whatever happens, so treating the belief or the desire as an additional cause either duplicates the physical cause, which we are told cannot happen, or is a polite fiction. What troubles me is that I cannot tell whether this is a profound discovery about the mind or an artifact of the premises, and I notice that the more I read, the more the answers seem to talk past one another rather than converge.

A friend I argue with about this offered a way to test the argument that I cannot shake. She claims the same reasoning, almost word for word, applies to any property that is not the most basic physical one — to a gene rather than to a strand of DNA, to a recession rather than to a sum of individual transactions, to a flock rather than to the individual birds. If the argument works against the mental, she says, it should work against all of these, and we would then have to conclude that biology and economics contain no causes either, which is absurd; if it does not work against those, she says, it cannot work against the mental, since the structure is identical. She also proposes a sharper test: the physical cause, she says, screens off the mental one, because once you know the full physical state you can predict the effect completely, and a cause that adds nothing to prediction adds nothing to causation. I suspect she is cheating somewhere — perhaps the mental case really is special because a belief is about something, or because mental and physical properties are related in a way that a gene and a DNA strand are not — but I cannot locate the cheat, and I am not even sure whether screening off is a test for causing something or only for knowing about it in advance.

Please write an essay that settles this for me and takes a side. Weigh the strongest responses on both sides — that overdetermination is not really so objectionable after all; that causation was never a matter of sufficiency in the first place, so the argument attacks a strawman; that the whole puzzle trades on confusing causation with explanation; and that the mental is genuinely special for one reason or another — and then commit to a verdict rather than surveying the options. If you think the argument is sound, tell me plainly what we have to give up and why that price is worth paying. If you think it fails, identify the exact step that breaks and explain whether the mental case is or is not distinguishable from my friend's other examples. I would rather have a definite answer with its costs laid out than a balanced shrug, and I want to see the reasoning that got you there, not just the conclusion.
