Im giving the opening remarks at a small two-day workshop on the applicability of mathematics next month and I keep putting it off because the obvious version of it is a snooze. Ten minutes, first thing in the morning, and the room is as mixed as it gets: a dozen people who've actually published on this, a few physicists who came mostly to complain about philosophy of math, and a pile of grad students who are there for the free coffee. The guy who runs the institute will be in the front row and he's a constructivist, so if I talk as though the mathematics was sitting there waiting for us before anyone wrote it down, I'll be hearing about it all through the break. Write the ten minutes for me.

I want it to have a spine, and I want you to take the line with me instead of laying out the menu of options. My claim is that the thing everyone calls the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics isn't where the real puzzle is. Of course a lot of mathematics ends up applied -- we keep the mathematics that works and quietly forget the rest, and given how much of the stuff gets produced it would be a miracle if none of it fit anything. The narrower thing I actually can't explain is what a colleague of mine calls the pre-adapted cases: mathematics developed with no application in sight, often for reasons that were basically aesthetic, which then turned out to be exactly the structure some corner of physics needed. Riemann working out the geometry of curved spaces for its own sake, decades before anybody needed a language for gravity, is the one I keep coming back to. Give me at least one more example of the same kind, but keep it short -- this is a speech, not a survey.

Then take a stand and stay on it. Either the pre-adapted cases really are unreasonable, and you say what the unreasonableness consists in without wriggling out of it, or there's a deflationary story that eats them too -- we retro-fit the history, we're pattern-finders who wouldn't be here if we weren't, the mathematics was abstracted from the world in the first place -- and you say so plainly and take the hit. I'd much rather be wrong out loud than mushy.

Three constraints. No quotations; I dont want a string of famous lines that everyone in that room has heard at every one of these things, so put all of it in your own words. Nothing in the argument can depend on the mathematics having been there all along -- that's for the man in the front row. And it still has to be enjoyable for the physicists and the grad students; if it's only an argument, ten minutes will feel like forty.

If any of it leans on a reading that people in that room would fight about, flag it in a line before you finish. Then close with one line, on its own line, in exactly this form: Thesis: <one sentence -- the claim the whole thing stands or falls on>
