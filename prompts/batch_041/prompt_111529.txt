I teach an upper-level seminar on the ethics of the city: twenty students, roughly half philosophy majors and half from the planning school, one ninety-minute session, no projector, though I can print handouts. I keep failing to convey by lecturing a split that I want them to feel instead: that a right to the city is really two claims wearing the same clothes — a right to a voice in how a neighborhood changes, and a right to be in the city at all — and that a procedure can be scrupulously fair on the first count while producing a systematically exclusionary result on the second, with nobody behaving badly.

Design a staged activity that does this. The idea must arrive through the rules, the incentives and the roles, not through anything I announce; I want the room to run into it and only afterwards hear it named. Build it around this scenario, adjusting numbers but not the shape: a mid-sized city, rising rents, a proposal for a sixty-unit apartment building — fifteen units below market — on a site that is now a surface parking lot and a shuttered laundromat, on a commercial corridor with a bus line, with single-family blocks behind it. The neighborhood body's recommendation on the conditional use effectively decides the matter.

Include, and specify fully enough that I can type the materials in an hour:

- A one-page site brief and ten role cards for voting members of the neighborhood body, each carrying a private stake (a landlord with two nearby fourplexes, a recent buyer, a long-time renter, a parent worried about school crowding, a corridor shopkeeper who wants foot traffic, someone whose real stake is that the process look fair, and so on), plus two applicant roles who present but do not vote, and three roles who may testify but not vote — a nurse with a fifty-minute commute, a local teenager, and someone currently doubled up with relatives who would apply for one of the fifteen units.
- A rule that the body may record findings only in a fixed form: the proposal's consistency with the adopted neighborhood plan, and impacts on the immediately surrounding blocks. Testimony about a commute or about a doubled-up household has no box to go in. Someone should notice this and object; that moment is one I want to be able to count on.
- Midway through, a swap: every participant exchanges location cards with someone across the room, so a homeowner becomes a renter three blocks away. Their arguments need not change, and I want to see whether their votes do.
- A sealed envelope, opened only at the end, holding tokens representing the households that would have lived in the building and were never in the room.
- A second phase in which the group may amend exactly one rule, from a menu of three: add three at-large citywide seats to the body; keep the body as is but attach a cash payment to the neighborhood association; or take the recommendation out of the decision path and let a citywide body decide, with neighborhood comment on the record only. Then re-vote the same proposal.

Give me a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps each mechanic — the findings form, the swap, the envelope, the rule menu — onto the corresponding part of the idea, in language I can use out loud. Add an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, including what to do if the swing vote becomes obvious too early or if the three non-voting roles go quiet or go meta. Then tell me what this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself — I suspect it cannot show that in reality the excluded are invisible rather than seated in the room, that the exclusionary work is done by hundreds of small default rules nobody ever votes on, and that the process runs over decades rather than one evening, but say what you think the real gaps are.

End with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing a single short phrase naming the one idea the activity is engineered to make participants feel. That line is for me, not for the students, so use the ordinary name of the concept even though they should not hear it until the debrief.
