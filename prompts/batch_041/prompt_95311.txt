I am assembling the specification for a position I may have to defend, and I want the requirements before the candidate theories. Set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of mathematical truth and mathematical objects must satisfy — not the theories themselves but the criteria, each accompanied by the case or consideration that puts it on the list.

The purpose that fixes adequacy for me is this: the theory must let me take mathematicians' assertions at face value — when someone proves a theorem and asserts it, the theory should say what she has asserted and that she is correct in asserting it — while also letting me remain a naturalist, granting existence only to what earns its keep in our best empirical science and distrusting any special faculty for intuiting abstract objects. I am aware that these two pulls are not obviously compatible. That is precisely why I want the list of requirements rather than a winner.

Include at least the following, with the motivating case stated for each:

- Semantics. What truth conditions the theory assigns to ordinary mathematical sentences, including quantified statements about numbers, and what, if anything, number-expressions refer to.
- Determinacy of open questions. Many mathematicians treat an unproved conjecture as having a definite answer and a statement independent of their axioms as true or false rather than as neither. The theory must say whether that attitude is warranted and what would make it so.
- Reference and the reduction problem. Consider the two familiar constructions of the natural numbers as sets, which disagree already at the number two, so that a theory identifying numbers with sets must either select one construction arbitrarily or concede that neither is what we meant by the number. The theory must handle this case.
- Sameness of content across foundations. The same theorem is proved within set theory, within type theory, and in category-theoretic terms. The theory must say whether these are one result or several, and what makes the translation between them truth-preserving.
- Introduction of new objects. When a new kind of object is introduced by a definition or by a principle of abstraction, what makes the introduction legitimate; what becomes of the theorems derived from an introduction later found inconsistent; and whether a device posited merely to shorten proofs and later shown eliminable counts as existing.
- Epistemology. How mathematical belief can amount to knowledge, and whether the answer is compatible with a causal or otherwise naturalistic account of knowledge. State plainly how much of the standard epistemic demand is a genuine requirement and how much is the commitment of one camp.
- Error and disagreement. What the theory must say about a proof that stood for years before a gap was found, and about two mathematicians who disagree over whether a disputed method is legitimate.
- Applicability. How the very same objects can figure both in pure proofs and in the equations of empirical science.

Then, in a separate section: mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps; identify any pair or subset that cannot be jointly satisfied and explain what forces the conflict; and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive theories. Close with a few lines only on how platonism, structuralism, and fictionalism fare against your most discriminating requirements. If you judge that a theory of mathematical truth also owes an account of what a proof is, add that as a further requirement and say whether it is separable from the rest.
