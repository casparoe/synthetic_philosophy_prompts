I've been reading about al-Ghazālī and the occasionalsts — the view that nothing created really causes anything, God produces every event directly, and fire is just the occasion on which God does the burning. I tried to write the argument out properly and now it looks like it eats itself:

(1) If A causes B, then A's happening makes B happen — a cause necessatates its effect.
(2) But fire touches cotton and I can conceive of that with no burning; God could always hold the burning back. That's the whole point.
(3) Whatever I can genuinely conceive of is possible.
(4) So fire doesn't necessitate the burning, and by (1) it doesn't cause it — same for everything else created.
(5) So only God causes anything.

The trouble is I can run (2)–(4) on God's own act. If God brings about the burning by *willing* it, then by (2) I can conceive of God willing it while the cotton stays cold — otherwise his will is tied down, and I've lost the very freedom that made (2) work in the first place. So (4) takes out God's willing too, and (5) is false. And if you say instead that God's willing just *is* the burning, then there's no gap left to conceive of, nothing satisfies (1), and 'cause' ends up with no instances at all — which also kills (5).

People usually attack (3), and I see why, but I don't want to drop it; it's my only route to knowing that anything is possible. I don't want to drop (1) either — a cause that doesn't necessitate sounds like Hume's constant conjunction, and then I've lost the theism rather than saved it. So which premise actually goes, and what does that cost me? Also tell me what would change your mind, and where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong.
