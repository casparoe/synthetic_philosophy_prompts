Two friends of mine, Dan and Rosa, went at each other at dinner last week over something that's been rattling around my head ever since. It started because a third friend, who wasn't there, has been offered a genetic test for a condition there's no treatment or cure for, and she keeps flip-flopping about whether to take it. I write novels for a living, so I spend alot of my working life deciding how much to tell a reader and when, and I'd said something vague about her situation being basically a plot problem. That was enough to set them off.

Dan (works in insurance, thinks in probabilities) said declining is basically irrational. His argument: there's a theorem — Good's, he thinks — that the expected value of information can never be negative, because whatever you find out you can always ignore it and act exactly as if you'd never been told. So "I'd rather not know" is always a bit of a confusion.

Rosa said that theorem is about a very particular animal: someone with fixed preferences and a fixed decision problem, getting information that costs nothing and that they can genuinely set aside. She said every one of those breaks here. She won't set it aside, she'll think about it every day, and the cost isn't in anything she does about it, it's in the knowing itself. And she said Dan's theorem doesn't even have a slot for that, because in it what you care about is what happens, not what you know.

Dan said fine, but that's not a counterexample, that's a scope note — if you build the dread into the model then you're calling two different things "information," the evidence and the punch in the stomach, and you should seperate them. The evidential part can't hurt you. If she wants to pay to avoid the punch, that's a preference, and a perfectly respectable one, but then she's saying knowing feels bad, not that knowing is bad for her decision.

Rosa said that's exactly the problem — the theorem then can't call her irrational, which is what he'd been doing. Then she added it isn't only dread. She can't promise herself she won't act on it: she'll start checking things, planning, reading, and "you can always ignore it" just hands you a discipline the theorem never earned. And she said it doesn't only change what she believes, it changes what she wants — she'll talk herself into not wanting the things she wanted, and that'll be the information doing it.

Dan said if she's that fragile, that's a fact about her and not about information, and that people who dodge tests get blindsided. Then he turned to me and said I make my living off this — nobody pays to be told the ending on page one. Rosa said that was her point exactly. They asked me to settle it and I honestly couldn't.

Can you go through the argument move by move — who's right at each step, not just who's right overall, and what each of them is missing? And please be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on it and which are genuinely contested, rather than presenting one side's view as the consensus.
