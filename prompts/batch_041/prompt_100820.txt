I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a blind art critic who has never seen a painting and whose judgments about paintings rest entirely on the descriptions and testimony of a trusted friend; the plot turns on whether his judgments can be knowledge. I have been reading the debate between aesthetic testimony pessimists, who hold that you cannot genuinely form an aesthetic judgment on testimony, and optimists, who hold that you can, at least in some cases. Acting as a philosopher of art and an expert in the epistemology of testimony, and thinking carefully rather than reaching for familiar slogans, draft the joint statement that a committed pessimist and a committed optimist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Begin by brainstorming the possible cruxes; then give me a numbered section of claims both sides accept—about what aesthetic judgment is, what testimony can transmit, the difference between knowing that a work is good and being able to appreciate it, and what the blind critic case does and does not show—followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the neutral formulation of each side's position, the prediction each side makes about the blind critic case and about a more ordinary case of a friend's recommendation, and the evidence or argument both sides agree would bear on that crux. Where you judge that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I want the conceptual reconstruction itself.
