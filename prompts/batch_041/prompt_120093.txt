Two people in my reading group had a proper row about Carroll's tortoise last week and I've been turning it over ever since. I'm not going to say which of them I thought had the better of it, mainly because I kept changing my mind, so please judge the exchange as it actually went rather than trying to work out where I stand. Roughly how it unfolded:

Nadia kicked off. Her line was that the tortoise isn't just a clever puzzle, it's a proof that no deductive rule can ever be justified. Take modus ponens. You accept "if P then Q", you accept "P", and the tortoise says fine, but I haven't yet agreed to accept "Q". So you hand him one more thing to accept: the conditional that says if you've got both of those, then you've got Q. But then he wants that one too, and the next, and so on. Every attempt to justify the rule either runs forever or quietly assumes the very rule it's supposed to be supporting. So deduction bottoms out in something we can't justify. We just do infer; we're trained into it. That's a real result about the groundlessness of logic, she said, not a party trick.

Rob said that's a misreading, and that the whole thing trades on a confusion. What the tortoise shows is only that you can't justify a rule of inference by handing over one more proposition to accept. But nobody ever claimed you could, and the demand is confused rather than unsatisfiable. Asking for a reason to infer by modus ponens is like asking for a reason to move the bishop diagonally — the rule isn't a claim that stands in need of evidence, it's part of what it is to reason deductively at all. You can show a rule is a good one by proving it sound, or by pointing to its record of never carrying you from truths to a falsehood. That's a justification of a different kind, and the tortoise never asks for it because he's fixed on the wrong kind.

Nadia's reply: that's just relabelling the gap. Chess is something we made up and can walk away from; nobody says a person who moves a knight like a bishop has made a mistake about chess, they've simply stopped playing. But if someone reasons by affirming the consequent we say they've reasoned wrongly, full stop. So the rules of logic aren't optional conventions, they're norms we hold each other to, and the regress shows we can't ground those norms in anything. Her arguement stands, just restated.

Rob went a different way after that. He said the tortoise isn't even a coherent thinker, because accepting "if P then Q" just is, at least partly, being disposed to infer Q from P. Someone who says yes, I accept the conditional, and yes, I accept P, but I'm still not prepared to accept Q hasn't actually accepted the conditional — he's just printing the words. And if you want the fancier version: for a transition to count as an inference rather than an associative jump, the thinker has to take their premises to support the conclusion, and that taking isn't one more premise, it's the act of inferring itself.

Nadia pounced. If the taking is a genuine act, what makes it correct? Either it's another state you can be right or wrong about, in which case we're back to needing a justification for it and the regress is back — or it's just a bare psychological fact, in which case anything counts as inference and there's no normativity left. Pick a horn: groundless or vacuous.

Rob said that's a false dilemma, that a capacity can be answerable to correctness without needing a separate premise to licence each exercise of it, and that the regress only bites if you insist that justifying must always take the form of adding something to accept. Nadia said that's just refusing to answer the question. They went round twice more and then the pub closed.
