In my epistemology seminar the phrase 'belief comes in degrees' has become a conversation-stopper. It surfaces every time we discuss the lottery paradox — the case in which it seems rational to believe of each of a thousand tickets that it will lose, while also believing that exactly one of them will win — and again whenever someone raises the preface paradox, in which an author seems entitled both to believe every claim in her book and to believe that at least one of those claims is false. Whoever says the slogan treats it as a dissolution: the paradoxes, on this view, are artifacts of a confused, all-or-nothing picture of belief, and once degrees of belief are admitted there is nothing left to explain. I want a careful disambiguation rather than a defense or a refutation. What are the distinct precise claims that 'belief comes in degrees' could be making, arranged from the nearly trivial to the frankly radical? At the trivial end I expect things like: ordinary belief talk admits hedging, or people are sometimes more and sometimes less confident. At the radical end I expect things like: there is no such state as outright belief at all, and 'believing' is at best a coarse-grained way of speaking about credences. In between I want the psychologically substantive readings, the normative ones about which states the norms of rationality actually govern, and the reductive one that identifies outright belief with credence above a threshold. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive thesis, or false, and — this is the part I actually need — what it does to the two paradoxes. Does it dissolve them, leave them exactly where they were, or make them worse? My suspicion, which you should treat as a hypothesis and not as a premise, is that the reading doing the work in my seminar is stronger than anyone there would defend if it were stated plainly, and that the threshold reading, which is the one the slogan most naturally suggests, simply restates the lottery problem instead of solving it. I would also like to know which reading the people who repeat the slogan probably have in mind, and whether the slogan is being asked to do work that only a much stronger claim could do. Lay the readings out as a menu with a verdict on each, but write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; just paragraphs, the way you would explain it in a long email.
