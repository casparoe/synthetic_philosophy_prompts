I help run a monthly philosophy-ish meetup at my office — mostly software engineers like me, about 12 people, 90 minutes, and a mix of folks who know some history and folks who know none. I've been reading about the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials and the thing that keeps bugging me is the retroactive-law problem: the winners set up the court and wrote its rules after the acts had already happened, then tried people under those rules, and when the defendants said you can't do that because it wasn't illegal when we did it, the answer was basically that it always was, morally. I want an activity where the group has to actually do this, not hear me explain it.

So design me a runnable role-play where they sit as judges (maybe with prosecution and defense too) deciding a handful of cases, with the rules arranged so that the person everyone agrees is the worst did something that wasn't against the law on the books at the time. I want them to hit the wall themselves — to feel the pull of acquitting someone monstrous on a technicality, or of inventing a law after the fact, or of reaching for some higher law nobody wrote down — before I name any of it. No lecture up front, and please don't make the historical names the point; fictional defendants are fine as long as the shape is right. Work in the "you did it too" problem somewhere, since the winners weren't clean either and that's half of why this is hard.

Give me the roles, the rules, the materials (one page of "law as it stood" plus the case facts), a rough 90-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the real problem. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce so I have to say it out loud myself. No reading list or citations — nobody's going to look them up.
