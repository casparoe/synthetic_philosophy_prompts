For the last six months I have been running a fortnightly lunchtime reading group at the robotics company where I work, and next session falls to me. The group is eleven people: eight ML engineers, two of whom have doctorates in computer science and none of whom has read any philosophy since an undergraduate ethics elective, plus two people from our policy team and one colleague from a partner lab who does formal verification. My topic is side effects — specifically the proposal, which I keep meeting in our own design reviews, that a good agent is one that accomplishes the task while avoiding unnecessary side effects. What I want the room to come away with is a single idea, and it is a negative one: that 'side effect' does not name a natural kind of event in the world but encodes a judgement about which changes were wanted, so the injunction to avoid side effects cannot be made precise without first settling what the agent is supposed to be bringing about — which is to say, without the specification of value that the injunction was meant to spare us from needing. Whether that makes the phrase merely vague, or circular, or still serviceable as a heuristic, is the discussion I want them capable of having at the end. Please do not explain the topic to me; I think I understand it. Plan the hour.

Three things should be treated as fixed, since I have already committed to them, and two of them are mine rather than anything from the literature. First, the carrying case: a warehouse robot instructed to bring a pallet from the far aisle to the loading bay, which on its chosen route (a) closes a fire door that a human had propped open, (b) crushes a flattened cardboard box left in the aisle, (c) passes within a metre of a colleague's mug on a shelf and could stop, wait for someone to move it, or take a four-minute detour, and (d) must disable a small sensor to fit through the gap at the end of the aisle. Tell me whether this is the right case to hang the whole hour on, and if it is not, what properties the carrying case must have and lack — I suspect it needs at least one change that is a necessary means to the instructed end, one that is neither intended nor foreseen, and one where the question of whether it was a side effect is genuinely contested, but I want your reasoning rather than my own returned to me. Second, the objection I want to organise the session around instead of batting away at the end: one of the engineers has already told me he does not see the problem, because impact can be defined in a value-free way as the degree to which the agent reduces the set of futures still reachable by the humans around it, whereupon any reduction can be labelled a side effect and penalised. I want the arc of the hour to be the slow discovery of what that proposal does and does not buy you. Third, one of the policy people keeps insisting this is simply the old distinction between what you intend and what you merely foresee, from the ethics of double effect, wearing new clothes; I want the plan to say where the comparison illuminates and where it misleads, and to say where in the sequence to raise it.

So: the order in which the pieces have to come, and which piece must be in place before the next one can even be heard as a question; the misconceptions they will arrive with or form, in the order those will surface, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off — I expect 'a side effect is just an unintended consequence', 'if the robot did nothing there would be no side effects', 'impact is just how much the world changes', and 'a good enough proxy is fine in practice' to be the main four, but tell me if I have missed one; the single question I can put to the room at the end that would show me whether they have grasped the idea or merely learned to say the words; and what I should announce at the outset that I am not going to cover in an hour, so that nobody leaves thinking the hard part was quietly skipped. Do not send me readings — nobody in this group will do them.
