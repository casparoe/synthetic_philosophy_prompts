I spent the better part of last night arguing with a friend who is convinced that expressivism collapses the moment a moral sentence is embedded — her claim is that inside a negation, a conditional, or an attitude ascription, the non-cognitivist must either change what the embedded sentence means or abandon compositionality, and she treats either horn as fatal. I think the damage is much more localized than that, and I want a case that will decide the matter between us. Construct a minimal pair: two sentences that differ in exactly one respect, namely the operator under which the same moral claim appears, such that a straightforward expressivist treatment — moral sentence expresses a non-cognitive attitude, negation of it expresses the opposing attitude, no truth-apt content smuggled in on the sly — handles one member of the pair smoothly while the other forces the expressivist into one of the two bad options. Constraints: ordinary moral vocabulary only ("wrong," "ought," "cruel," "must"), ordinary English embedding devices, no invented operators and no logical notation standing in for the case itself, the pair itself no longer than a paragraph, and — this matters — do not use the stale conditional about lying and getting one's little brother to lie, or any other example that appears in every introduction to the debate. I want a pair I have not seen. Once you have it, nest the troublesome sentence one operator deeper and tell me whether the difficulty survives or dissolves.

Then do the full accounting, and do it in the order I ask, because I want to watch the verdicts come out of the machinery rather than be announced. For each sentence in the pair, state the expressivist's semantic clause, the verdict that clause delivers, and the precise point at which it breaks; identify the single feature of the embedding that is doing the work; show that removing that feature, or swapping in the operator from the other half of the pair, restores smooth handling; and then say what happens if the feature stays but the attitude-theoretic story is modified in the most natural way a defender would propose, so that I can see whether the repair is cheap or whether it costs the expressivist the very thing that made the view attractive. Finish with the strongest move available to a defender: if the honest answer is that the expressivist should never have signed on to that clause in the first place, say so plainly and tell me what the concession costs. The case exists to show that the embedding problem is local rather than global, so a pair that works only by stipulating the verdicts is a failure — the verdicts have to fall out of the clauses you supplied, and if they do not, tell me that instead of papering over it.
