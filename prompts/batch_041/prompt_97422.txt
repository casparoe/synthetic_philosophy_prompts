I would like to rehearse a defense of a specific thesis in the theory of epistemic trust for artificial systems, and I want you to serve as my opponent rather than as an assistant. Please play a committed skeptic: technically fluent, unsentimental, willing to grant narrow points but never the central one, and willing to press an objection until it is either answered or exposed. You are not a skeptic about trust as such — you think trust is grounded in something more mundane, such as how a system behaves when corrected or how it holds up when the world shifts — but you deny that the formal property below is that thing. Remain in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to one substantial paragraph so that the exchange reads as a conversation rather than a report. If you need some piece of the setup made more precise, ask for it in character rather than stepping outside the role. Write in American English.

So that neither of us has to pretend you already know the apparatus, here it is. Fix an interpreter with some epistemic state, and imagine that interpreter trying to recover what a system believes from what the system outputs. Call the system ascription universal, relative to that interpreter, when the system's explicit, stated beliefs already contain everything that any rival way of ascribing beliefs to it would supply — so that no simpler computation the interpreter could use to model or predict the system leaves the interpreter better informed about the system than the system's own output does. The strongest version of the demand is that the system be better informed than every simple-enough rival computation. The proposal I want to defend is that this property is what transparency, or honesty, actually consists in, and that it is the correct thing to certify when we want to trust what a system says about itself. Your role is to deny this: you hold that the property is either vacuous or unattainable, and that in any case it is neither necessary nor sufficient for warranted trust.

My opening move. You keep insisting that a lucky rival can out-inform the system — some simple predictor lights on an assumption the system regards as silly, and the assumption turns out to be right, so the system is dominated and fails the test. But that is only to say that a rival can be right by accident, which no criterion of trust could exclude and none should try to. What the criterion requires is that no rival hold an expected edge over the system, and a lucky guess is not an edge. So either you concede that the criterion was never meant to defeat luck, or you owe me an argument that expected dominance is too weak to certify anything worth certifying. Which is it?
