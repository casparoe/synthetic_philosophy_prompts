A friend in my reading group has been reconstructing the strategy-stealing proof that the first player wins at Hex — the game in which two players alternately place stones on a rhombus of hexagons, each trying to connect her two opposite sides — and he now believes it generalises. His version runs:

(1) In any finite game with perfect information, no chance and no draws, exactly one of the two players has a winning strategy.
(2) Hex is such a game, and it is symmetric: exchanging the two players leaves the rules unchanged.
(3) Suppose the second player had a winning strategy. The first player could then make an arbitrary opening move and afterwards take over that strategy; her opening stone is simply one more stone of her own on the board.
(4) Hence the second player cannot have a winning strategy, and by (1) the first player wins — without our ever having to find the strategy.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(3) turns on anything peculiar to Hex, so no finite, chance-free, draw-free, symmetric game can be won by the second player.

He then offered this game as satisfying everything in (5): three squares in a row; players alternately claim an unclaimed square; whoever claims the middle square loses. The rules distinguish no player, and the game cannot end in a draw. It does appear to satisfy (5), yet the second player wins — if the first player takes an end square, the second takes the other end, and the first is then obliged to take the middle.

If I must choose, I would rather keep (4) than (5), but I want to know the price. Which step is at fault, why did it look harmless, and what precisely must be added or restricted if the Hex result is to be preserved? Please answer in British English.
