Okay, I need help with something I'd normally just muddle through on my own. I'm the outgoing director of a small interdisciplinary research centre -- long-run social change, comparative history, a couple of economists, one person who does quantitative work on premodern demography -- and in three weeks I have to give the after-dinner speech at a two-day symposium for the centre's fiftieth anniversary. Sixty-odd people, round tables, too much wine, everybody's already given their paper and nobody wants a lecture. The problem is the founding premise. The founder, who died in 2019, built the place on the conviction that history has discoverable laws of development in roughly the sense that physics has laws; his line at every seminar, whenever someone showed him a trend, was 'show me the curve.' Half the room joined in the eighties and nineties because of that promise and still believe it. A quarter of the room are grad students who think the programme was always a bit of a fantasy and who will be watching me very carefully. And the founder's widow is at the head table and she is sharp, and she'll know exactly what I'm doing the moment I start hedging. I've come to think the founding ambition can't be met -- not because we weren't clever enough, but in principle, because you can't predict the future growth of knowledge and the future of a society is largely downstream of what it knows. But I can't stand up and tell a room full of my friends that their life's work was a mistake, and I don't actually think it was a mistake; I think it was the right instincts pointed at a target that isn't there.

What I want the speech to do: honour what was genuinely good about the place -- the long horizons, the patience, the refusal to be impressed by the news cycle -- then make the case that the thing they thought they were building isn't available, and make it by drawing a distinction rather than swinging a hammer, because I don't want to say there are no patterns. I want to say the patterns are conditional and time-bound, and that a trend is not a law, and that the difference is not pedantry -- it's the difference between what we can honestly promise a funder and what we can't. That's the part I'm least sure how to say without it sounding like I've conceded the whole game, so don't let me off the hook if the position I end up in is just the founding ambition with the word 'law' filed off it. Then I want to leave them with something to do next that isn't the old programme in a new hat. Two things to build in structurally rather than just mention: first, the founder had a long-running grudge against Popper -- the details don't matter -- so if I cite Popper as an authority, half the room stops listening; whatever case I make has to stand on its own footing, in our own vocabulary, and land as an argument rather than as a name. Second, we have a house rule that started as a joke and hardened into law: in our seminars nobody may utter a future-tense claim without attaching an explicit condition to it. 'X will happen' is banned; 'if A, then X' is fine. I want the speech to obey the house rule visibly, so the audience can catch me doing it and be a little pleased about it. That's going to bite hardest exactly where the speech wants to be warmest, which is the forward-looking bit at the end, and that's why I want it -- it should feel like a discipline the occasion is forcing on me, not a gimmick.

Six to eight minutes, spoken not read, no slides, and don't put any quotations in my mouth -- I am not going to get someone else's words wrong at my own anniversary dinner. When you're done, flag the two or three sentences most likely to lose the room and tell me what to say instead, and give me one line to keep in my pocket in case the tribute to the founder goes badly.
