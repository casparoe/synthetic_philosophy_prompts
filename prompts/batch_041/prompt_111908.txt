I have been keeping a notebook of my own verdicts about dispositions — which things are fragile, soluble, live, brittle, and so on — and I have reached the point where the verdicts no longer fit the account of dispositional ascription I would once have offered. I should like you to tell me what principle, or small set of rival principles, best captures the pattern, what it implies for the cases I have not yet adjudicated, and which of my verdicts it obliges me to abandon. Please take the devices I mention to be reliable, instantaneous, and external to the object in question unless I say otherwise.

1. A wine glass stands on the table. Struck, it shatters. Verdict: fragile.

2. The same glass, now fitted with a snug rubber sleeve. Struck, it would bounce and survive. Verdict: still fragile. The sleeve does the protecting; the glass is what it always was.

3. A wire carrying current from the mains, with a sensor that would cut the current the instant any conductor drew near. Touching it would never shock you. Verdict: the wire is live.

4. A wooden ruler, secretly rigged with a small charge that would snap it the moment you tried to bend it. Verdict: not brittle. The charge would be doing the snapping, not the ruler.

5. A key that opens one particular lock and no other. Verdict: the key is disposed to open that lock — although I notice that whatever grounds the disposition seems to lie as much in the lock's pins as in the key.

6. A lump of sugar into which a fixative has been worked. The sugar's own crystal structure is intact, but the fixative would harden into an impermeable shell the instant the lump met water, so the lump would not dissolve. Verdict: not soluble. The lump as it now stands has been altered through and through; I cannot see the fixative as mere packaging.

I am confident of 1 to 4. Cases 5 and 6 are where I feel the ground shift under me. My own tentative principle is this: a thing is disposed to M in C just in case, were it in C under normal conditions, it would M. That appears to save all six. But I am uneasy, because I cannot specify normal conditions except by saying the conditions in which the disposition would manifest, which is circular, and I suspect the circle is doing all the work. The austere rival is this: a thing is disposed to M in C just in case it possesses the intrinsic structure whose business it is to produce M in C, whatever the surroundings would do. That saves 2, 3, 4 and 6, but it makes a liar of me about 5, and it threatens to render dispositions unknowable from the outside.

Please do the following. State precisely the principle, or the small set of candidates, that you think best captures my verdicts, and identify for each clause the case that motivates it. Say which of my six verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and if a verdict must be given up, say which and why. Then apply your preferred principle to two cases I have not adjudicated: (a) a wire whose own substance is such that it would go dead the instant it were touched — the interfering device built into the wire rather than bolted onto it; and (b) a glass whose microstructure is that of ordinary glass but which generates, from within itself, a field that would keep it from shattering when struck. Finally, if you can, construct one case of your own that would force a choice between your principle and its nearest rival, and say how each would rule on it.

Please write your answer in British English.
