I'm a PhD student working on the epistemology of self-locating belief, and I'm putting together the handout for the second meeting of a graduate seminar on the Sleeping Beauty problem. The usual halfers-versus-thirders framing flattens everything — students pick a side by temperament and then reverse-engineer the arguments — so I want a diagnostic instrument instead: a decision tree that takes someone from the plain description of the experiment to a specific, named position on how credence should work in self-locating cases, with every branching question phrased so that it can be answered by someone who has never heard of any of the positions.

Act as an expert in formal epistemology and the metaphysics of probability, and think carefully before you commit to a structure. Begin by brainstorming, in a scratch space, the space of possible combinations of answers before you draw anything, so that the tree's shape is driven by the logical space rather than by a list of positions you already have in mind. If a branch turns out to do no discriminating work, cut it and say so.

Requirements for the instrument:

- At least four branching nodes, each a question a student can answer without knowing where it leads. Candidates you may use or replace, justifying any replacement: (i) On waking, does the subject's total evidence differ from what it was on Sunday night? (ii) If it differs, is the difference exhausted by facts about where and when she is, or does it include ordinary non-self-locating facts as well? (iii) Does her rule for self-locating cases come from indifference over her own possible awakenings, over all the observers and observer-moments in the world, or from something else — and if something else, what? (iv) Is her credence answer constrained by what a bookie could do to her, by a reflection principle, or by nothing beyond the evidence? (v) Does she hold that waking gives her no new evidence at all?

- The tree must route through all of the following cases, not just the textbook one. (a) The standard setup: fair coin; heads means woken on Monday only, tails means woken on Monday and Tuesday, with amnesia between awakenings. (b) The twin-subject variant: the same coin also schedules a second subject, of whose existence the first is aware but with whom she cannot communicate, and whose awakenings are tied to the coin exactly as hers are — so heads yields four awakenings in total and tails two. Each is asked on waking for her credence that the coin landed heads. I suspect this variant splits the thirders into two camps with different numerical answers, but I want the tree to establish that rather than assume it. (c) The informed variant: on each awakening the subject is told truthfully which day it is. (d) The many-observers variant: a fair coin decides between a world containing exactly one observer and a world containing a vast number of observers, and you are one of the observers, unsure which world you are in.

- Between eight and twelve leaves. Each leaf should carry the position's standard label where there is one and be described in plain words where there is not; one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment; and, where the position makes a definite prediction for case (b) or case (d), that prediction.

- Flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to separate them.

- Note any branch of the logical space that no leaf occupies, and diagnose the vacancy: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

- Where two positions differ only in their labels, say so rather than presenting them as distinct.

Please do not include a reading list, citations, or attributions of views to authors; the handout will be self-contained, and I would rather have the arguments reconstructed plainly than attributed. Do not use any position's name inside a branch node.

Finish with a short paragraph on which single question in the tree does the most discriminating work, and which of my candidate nodes you discarded and why.
