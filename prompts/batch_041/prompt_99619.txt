for my philosophy of science thing at school I have to referee a fake-but-serious "adverserial collaboration" between two people in my debate club. one is a hardcore frequentist (probability = long-run relative frequency in a repeatable collective, single-case probabilities are just stand-ins for ignorance) and the other is a hardcore subjectivist/Bayesian (probability = degree of belief, coherent betting odds, no spooky objective chance you can peek at). write the joint statement they could both actually sign.

so: the claims both sides accept, then each side's remaining position in wording that side itself would sign off on, then the real cruxes. I'm pretty sure the cruxes are at least these -- what to do when there's no repeatable collective at all (one coin, one reactor, one football match); the reference class problem (which collective?); and whether the exchangeability result (the one where an exchangeable sequence of 0s and 1s acts like draws from some fixed but unknown coin, averaged over your prior) is a genuine vindication of one camp or just a translation between them. also: is calibration plus proper scoring rules (the rules that reward you for being honest about your credence) something they both accept as the operational test, and does agreeing on that quietly give the frequentist's game away? throw in whether the Dutch-book/coherence argument is something the frequentist has to admit cuts both ways.

the bit I care most about: I made up my own compromise definition and want to know if it's anything -- "a probability is the long-run frequency in the narrowest reference class you can actually justify, and when there's no such class it's a degree of belief." ask them whether both can sign it, and if not, which exact clause each one has to strike and why. and if part of their fight turns out to be just about words, say which part.

give it to me as exactly five numbered parts, no intro or conclusion, each a few sentences: 1) agreed claims, 2) each side's view in its own words, 3) the cruxes, 4) for each crux the case or argument both accept as bearing on it plus what each side predicts about it, 5) the verbal part and what both would count as decisive.
