I help run a small, long-lived social virtual world — a persistent shared space with a few thousand members, no combat, mostly people building things — and I sit on the volunteer panel that hears appeals against moderation decisions. There is a case I keep going round in circles on, and what I want from you is advice on how to think about it rather than a ruling.

One of our members, Nadia, spent about eight months building a careful reconstruction of her late grandmother's garden on a plot she had been given. It was visitable; other members held small gatherings in it; she wrote on our forum that walking through it was how she marked the anniversary. Another member, Tomas, who had building rights on her plot from an earlier collaboration, flattened the whole thing in about ten seconds — cleared the terrain, deleted the objects — and there is no backup, because our world does not keep one. He is unrepentant and rather pleased with his argument. Nothing was harmed, he says, because there was nothing there to harm: the garden was made of information rather than matter, information is copiable, what Nadia lost was a feeling, and a feeling is not a harm unless it tracks something that really existed. He has offered to rebuild it from her screenshots, exactly as it was, and says that if the replacement would be indistinguishable from the original then there was never anything there to lose. Nadia's reply is that the garden was as real as anything else in the world we actually share: it persisted when nobody was looking at it, it had a location, other people could find it and stand in it, and the fact that it was made of code rather than soil no more makes it a fiction than the fact that this message is made of pixels makes it unreadable. Two other members have weighed in. One says the platform's economy settles it, since the plot and the plants had market prices and could have been sold, so this is simply property damage with extra steps. The other says that both of them are describing the wrong thing: no object was damaged, and what was damaged was Nadia's and the community's ability to remember in a particular way — a real loss, but not an injury to a thing.

What I would like is a way of adjudicating this that does not simply back whichever intuition is loudest, and in particular a clear statement of how much of the answer turns on the world being shared: would Tomas be right, or nearer right, if Nadia had built the same garden in a private, single-player copy that no one else could ever visit, and if so, which of the arguments above survive that change and which collapse? Please also tell me what our panel should actually do to Tomas, if anything, and whether the reason that justifies a sanction is the same reason that shows he did something wrong, or a different one — I have a suspicion that we are quietly sliding between the two. If you can, end by naming the one or two further facts about the case that would most change your answer, so that I know what to ask him. Please write this as continuous prose rather than a bulleted checklist, about 800 to 1,000 words, in British English.
