I'm setting the final exam for a senior seminar in metaphysics of mathematics, and I want one essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard two-horn summary of Benacerraf's dilemma. The seminar has read the usual platonist and nominalist responses, and most students can already say that platonism buys truth at the cost of knowledge while nominalism buys knowledge at the cost of truth. What I want is a question that forces them to separate two demands that the textbook treatments often run together: the semantic demand that mathematical statements have a uniform truth-conditional semantics, and the epistemic demand that our mathematical beliefs be reliably connected to whatever makes them true. I also want to inject a wrinkle of my own. A colleague in the department has been pushing what she calls inferentialist platonism: mathematical objects are posits whose acceptance is justified by their role in our best mathematical theories, and our knowledge of them is supposed to be like our knowledge of theoretical entities in science, warranted by explanatory success rather than by any causal contact. She claims this dissolves Benacerraf's dilemma by giving up the causal theory of knowledge while keeping a realist semantics. I suspect it merely relocates the problem, because it still owes an account of how a causal-free warrant can be about a particular abstract structure rather than merely about our theories of that structure. But I do not want to stack the deck; I want the question to make students decide.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes without notes and should require students to do three things: state the two horns in a way that shows why they are not just the same worry twice; assess whether inferentialist platonism is a genuine solution or a relabeling of the access problem; and say what a solution would have to accomplish in order to be more than a stalemate. The model answer should take a definite position on whether the inferentialist proposal succeeds, and it should defend that position against the strongest objection, rather than surveying the options and leaving the verdict open. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points totaling 25 marks, with a clear line between an A answer and a B answer: an A answer must, for example, distinguish the semantic and epistemic horns and identify a specific residual problem for the inferentialist, while a B answer may conflate the horns or treat explanatory success as automatically securing reference to abstract objects. For each must-hit point, add a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students marks. No citations or reading lists are needed; the students will not have books in the exam, and I will handle any attributions in the question stem.
