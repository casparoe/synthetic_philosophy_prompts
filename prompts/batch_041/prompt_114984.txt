Question 3, as printed on the paper:

"(a) Set out the instrumental account of voting, and explain why, for a large electorate, the standard calculation of the probability of casting a decisive vote makes that account's prediction look absurd. For the calculation, assume the electorate has an odd number N of voters and that every voter other than the one under consideration votes for one of the two candidates independently with probability 1/2. (b) In week 7 we distinguished pivotal influence from marginal influence. Explain the distinction and use it to say whether the instrumental account is refuted by observed turnout. (c) Does the expressive account resolve the paradox, or does it relocate it? Defend your answer."

The script's answer:

"The instrumental account treats voting as an investment: you bear a cost — the time, the information, the trip to the polling station — in exchange for a chance of bringing about your preferred outcome. Its prediction is that turnout should be close to zero in any large electorate, because the chance that your single vote decides the election is vanishingly small. That is the paradox: national elections routinely see turnout above 60%.

The probability that my vote is decisive is just the probability that the other N−1 voters split exactly evenly, so that my vote breaks the tie. Each of them independently votes for one candidate or the other with probability 1/2, so the electorate's pattern of votes is one of 2^(N−1) equally likely patterns, and only one of those patterns is an exact split; the probability is therefore 2^−(N−1). For a national election that is a number with tens of millions of decimal places, and the conclusion is robust: I could relax the 1/2 assumption and it would still be true that nobody could have a reason to vote on these grounds.

The week-7 distinction is supposed to rescue the instrumental account. A vote is pivotal when it decides the election; it has marginal influence when it changes the size of the winner's margin. My vote always has marginal influence, since the margin is one vote larger or smaller depending on whether I voted. So my vote does make a difference to the outcome even in a safe seat, and the instrumental account was never in as much trouble as the pivot calculation suggested.

The expressive account says that people vote in order to express solidarity or civic identity rather than to change anything. That is a concession that voting is irrational, and it abandons the rational-choice framework instead of repairing it. It is true that turnout rises when an election is expected to be close, which is awkward for a purely expressive story, but I think the account genuinely resolves the paradox rather than relocating it: once we allow that people get something out of the act of voting itself, there is no longer any tension between the theory and the facts. A prediction this makes is that if the cost of voting fell — all-mail ballots, say — turnout would be unchanged, because cost is not what people are responding to."
