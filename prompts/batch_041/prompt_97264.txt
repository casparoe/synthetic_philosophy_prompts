i'm in a seminar on pragmatism, we're doing Peirce's "Some Consequences of Four Incapacities" before we get to James, and there's one paragraph in the anti-Cartesian section that i can't parse properly. here it is:

"The same formalism appears in the Cartesian criterion, which amounts to this: "Whatever I am clearly convinced of, is true." If I were really convinced, I should have done with reasoning, and should require no test of certainty. But thus to make single individuals absolute judges of truth is most pernicious. ... In sciences in which men come to agreement, when a theory has been broached, it is considered to be on probation until this agreement is reached. After it is reached, the question of certainly becomes an idle one, because there is no one left who doubts it. We individually cannot reasonably hope to attain the ultimate philosophy which we pursue; we can only seek it, therefore, for the community of philosophers. Hence, if disciplined and candid minds carefully examine a theory and refuse to accept it, this ought to create doubts in the mind of the author of the theory himself."

my problem is the last sentence. a few paragraphs earlier in the same essay he says "Let us not pretend to doubt in philosophy what we do not doubt in our hearts" — i.e. doubt can't be conjured by a maxim, it has to come from a real, positive reason. but here it sounds like other people's refusal to accept my theory is supposed to "create doubts" in me, which sounds like doubt being produced socially, or adopted at will, rather than experientially. is there a reading where those two aren't in tension? like is he saying the community's non-acceptance is itself the kind of experience that genuinely unsettles you, or is he quietly making doubt depend on other people in a way that undercuts his own anti-Cartesianism?

and then the "idle one" line. i get that he doesn't mean agreement makes something true, but i want to know what work "the community of philosophers" is actually doing in 1868 — is this already the ideal-limit-of-inquiry community he'll be famous for later, or is it just the actual body of working inquirers, with the ideal-limit thing being a later developement? because if it's just the actual community, the passage starts to look like it hands truth over to consensus in a way that would make James's later line about truth happening to an idea look like a small step rather than a break. i'm trying to figure out whether the Peirce/James divergence on truth is already visible in this paragraph or whether people just retro-fit it onto the earlier text.

don't give me a reading list or citations, i just want the sentences unpacked and an honest answer about whether the tension i'm sensing is real or me overreading. if one of my two readings of "community" is anachronistic, say so and say why. also if the odd word "certainly" in that sentence is doing anything or is just an OCR slip, tell me.
