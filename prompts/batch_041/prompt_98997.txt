You are a clinical ethicist serving on the ethics consultation service of a large teaching hospital. We run several consults a week on one recurring problem, and we keep resolving it ad hoc: a patient who is now unable to make decisions is dissenting from a treatment choice that the same patient, while competent, set down in an advance directive — sometimes dissenting from a treatment the earlier self refused, sometimes refusing a treatment the earlier self demanded. I want a decision procedure the on-call consultant can actually run at the bedside within an hour, built on a distinction I think we have been sloppy about. The authority of the earlier self seems to me to rest on three different grounds, and they pull apart: (1) the directive records the person's own settled values, so following it honors autonomy rather than overriding it; (2) the directive is merely evidence about what this patient would want if thinking clearly, and can be outweighed by better evidence about that; (3) the directive is a claim of authorship, that this person gets to write the story of their own life, and therefore binds even when the present patient disowns it. Build the procedure so that each step names which ground it rests on, because I suspect most of our internal disagreements are really about which ground applies.

Before drafting, think through the ways an earlier self and a later self can conflict, and set aside the ones a bedside procedure cannot responsibly handle. Then give me: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure should treat the earlier self's refusal of treatment differently from its demand for treatment, should have a test for whether the earlier self, foreseeing the later dissent, would have wanted the directive to bind, and should say whether and how the directive's authority decays as the discontinuity between the two selves grows.

Run the procedure on three cases: (a) a man with ALS who, while competent, directed that he never be put on a ventilator, and who now, in respiratory failure and frightened, asks to be intubated; (b) a woman with bipolar disorder who signed a psychiatric advance directive authorizing involuntary medication during a future manic episode, and who is now manic and refusing it; and (c) a woman with advanced dementia whose directive refuses antibiotics for a treatable pneumonia, and who now, at the moment of the decision, cannot express a preference at all. At least one of your outcomes should be one a clinician will find hard to accept; say so plainly. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do.

After the prose, give a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: procedure_version (string); steps (array of objects with step_number, question, ground — one of value, evidence, or authorship — sufficient_answer, and on_no); handoffs (array of objects with condition, to_whom, and why_not_a_rule); cases (array of objects with case_id, ground_relied_on, outcome, handed_to, and asker_discomfort); and limits (array of strings).
