I have no formal training in philosophy, but I think in terms of data structures and comparisons, and I would like to use that to pin down a question about pleasure and pain that I have never gotten a straight answer on.

Suppose we represent the value of a world as a pair of totals, pleasure and pain, and compare two worlds the way a sort function compares tuples: pain first, and pleasure only as a tiebreaker. That is a lexicographic ordering, and it is one way to make precise the idea — sometimes called negative utilitarianism, or lexical priority for suffering — that above some level of pain, no amount of pleasure, however large, can compensate for it. Call the resulting claim [T]:

[T] There exists a finite amount of suffering S* such that, for any two worlds identical except that one contains S* and the other contains no suffering at all, no finite amount of mild pleasure added to the first world makes it at least as good as the second.

A weaker view holds that the exchange rate between mild pleasure and suffering is finite everywhere: for any amount of pain, some finite amount of mild pleasure offsets it, even if that amount grows without bound as the pain worsens. I want that distinction kept sharp, because a curve that gets arbitrarily steep is still the weaker view; [T] requires a point at which the compensating quantity of pleasure stops being finite altogether.

Please give me your credence that [T] is true, broken out rather than asserted. At a minimum I want a probability for each of the following, with a line of justification apiece:

1. That pleasure and pain are commensurable at all — that there is a single scale on which quantities of each can be compared, rather than the comparison being a category mistake.
2. Conditional on commensurability, that the ordering is lexicographic in the sense above rather than continuous.
3. Whether [T], if it holds, is a fact about the structure of value or a fact about how minds like ours happen to be wired (satiation, adaptation, diminishing returns), and which of those you take yourself to be estimating.
4. Whether the threshold, if there is one, sits at a magnitude that shows up in ordinary life — a bad day, a toothache — or only at the extremes of suffering.
5. Whether the verdict changes when the suffering and the pleasure fall on different people rather than on the same person.

Then combine the terms into a single number, tell me which term that number is most sensitive to, and tell me what argument or discovery would move it most. My own rough credence is about 0.35, mostly because I cannot see how a brain could encode an infinite exchange rate between two signals; tell me whether that intuition is doing work it should not be doing, and where you would expect the two of us to disagree.

As a secondary question, independently of [T]: what is your credence that pleasure has no positive value at all — that only suffering counts?

If your decomposition needs a term I have not listed, add it and say why.
