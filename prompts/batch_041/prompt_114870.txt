I sit on the advisory board of our state's history curriculum review panel, and next month we vote on a middle-school unit called "Founding Narratives," which covers the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. A parents' coalition has filed a complaint: the unit's teacher guide labels all three as legend, and they argue that wherever archaeology has turned up a real city, a real siege layer, or a plausible migration, calling the story a legend is a thumb on the scale against traditions their families hold. Two of our historians counter that the guide is still too credulous, because "there was a real Troy" and "there was a Trojan War of the kind the epic describes" are different claims, and students will blur them.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt, reject, or adopt with specified amendments, with a deadline for the revised guide — and give us a usable standard: for a narrative of this kind, what has to be true before a curriculum may present it as history rather than legend, and what does the standard do with the awkward middle case where something happened but not the thing the story says happened? Take the parents' position seriously rather than treating it as motivated reasoning, and be explicit about the strongest thing the historians' side gets right.

I've read enough rationalist blogs to be wary of "kernel of truth" arguments that quietly slide from a city existed to the story is basically accurate, so I don't want the standard doing that unexamined; but I also don't want a standard so strict that no ancient narrative survives it. In the memo, separate what is actually settled among specialists on these three cases from what is genuinely contested, and don't hand us a contested position as though it were the consensus.
