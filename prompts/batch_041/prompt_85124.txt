I am writing an essay for my school's philosophy club, with my maths teacher supervising, on whether the principle of indifference can be used responsibly, and we agreed that the interesting question is not which answer to the circle-and-chord problem is correct but what an adequate theory of "equally likely" would have to deliver. Please write the desiderata for me: the cases the theory must get right (the chord question, which comes out 1/3, 1/2 or 1/4 depending on how the chord is randomised, is the obvious one), the puzzles it must dissolve (why an indifference argument can feel compelling and still change its answer when the same problem is described differently), and the distinctions it must not collapse (between ignorance and a genuine symmetry, between a partition that is arbitrary and one the problem itself dictates, and between having no information and having partial information). Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of particular approaches, such as the view that indifference is licensed only by invariance under a transformation of the problem. What I most want to know is whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if a unique answer, applicability to any state of ignorance, and consistency with the axioms of probability cannot all be had at once, say so plainly, because that would be the actual result of my essay. I do not need a scorecard of which approaches get things right; keep the weight on the list itself.
