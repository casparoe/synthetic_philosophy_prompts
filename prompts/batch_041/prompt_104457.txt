Write a dialogue between two people who disagree about the precautionary principle, and set it at a concrete decision point rather than in the abstract. The first is a regulator who must decide whether to authorize a proposed intervention. Its intended benefit is large and well characterized; its known risks are small and well characterized; its unknown risks are not merely improbable but undescribed, in the sense that the class of possible harms is itself contested and there is no agreement about what would count as a harm. The second is a decision theorist who suspects that the precautionary principle, once stated precisely enough to guide this decision, will turn out either to be redundant or to be incoherent.

Both should be first-rate at what they do. Neither should be a strawman, and each should engage the other's strongest formulation rather than a convenient caricature. The regulator must not simply assert that uncertainty counsels caution; the decision theorist must not simply recite that there are risks on both sides, since that objection is familiar and the regulator will have heard it many times.

Let the argument move through the distinctions that actually do the work here: the difference between a weak reading of the principle, on which uncertainty is not by itself a reason to postpone protective action, and a strong reading, on which uncertainty is a reason to act and shifts the burden of proof onto the proponent; the difference between decisions under risk, where probabilities are known, and decisions under ambiguity or radical ignorance, where the state space itself is unsettled; whether the principle is a decision rule or instead a device for allocating the burden of argument; whether it can be reconstructed from the value of preserving the option to learn before committing irreversibly, and whether that reconstruction presupposes exactly the probabilities the regulator claims not to have; the objection that a maximin-style rule is well defined only once one fixes how the possibilities are carved up, so that precaution under one description of the worst case is recklessness under another; and the thought that some harms, such as the loss of a species or of a way of life, cannot be traded against benefits at any rate, with the reply that this is a lexicographic preference ordering rather than a distinct principle of precaution.

Have them arrive at a precise point of remaining disagreement, ideally a disagreement about which of these questions is the fundamental one, rather than at a synthesis. Where one of them is right about something, let the other register it; do not distribute the credit evenly out of politeness. By the end it should be clear exactly what they still disagree about and why neither can concede it.
