Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on the "what is life" question and I keep catching myself being inconsistent, and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind my gut calls or if I'm just making it up as I go. Here's what I think, case by case.

The cold virus that laid me out last winter: not alive. Just a bit of code in a protein shell that hijacks my cells. Same for prions, the misfolded proteins behind mad cow and that kind of thing -- one of them makes other proteins misfold, so it spreads and kind of "reproduces," but it's not alive, obviously. But a mule: alive, no question, even though it can never have offspring. A worker ant in a hive: alive, also sterile. So being able to reproduce can't be required.

Now the seed they dug out of a vault after a thousand years that still sprouted: alive the whole time, right? Even though it was doing absolutely nothing. Same for an embryo frozen at a clinic: alive. So being active can't be required either.

Here's where I get tangled. A mitochondria inside one of my cells has it's own DNA, divides on its own, and was apparently once a free-living bacteria -- I want to say it's alive, but I don't want to say it's a living thing, like it's a part of me and not a creature. A heart pumping in a box in a lab: the tissue is alive but the heart isn't an organism. Same with a bit of tissue in a dish. And then there's a cancer that spreads from animal to animal like a cold, host to host, and it's been doing that for ages, evolving the whole time. It's made of living cells, so it's alive, but is "the cancer" itself a living thing? Feels like no. Or is that just me not wanting to say it.

A fire copies itself across a field, eats fuel, spreads -- nobody says fire is alive. A computer virus: no. And a bacterium I've boiled is dead even if it looks identical under a microscope, so it's not about the shape. Meanwhile scientists built a bacterium with a stripped-down synthetic genome a while back and I'd say that one is alive; the fact that we wrote the instructions doesn't seem to matter.

My best attempt at a rule: something is alive if it has a working factory -- the machinery to build itself out of raw materials -- even if the factory is switched off. A seed is a factory that's off. A virus is a blueprint with no factory, which is why it isn't alive; it has to borrow mine. A mule's factory works fine, it just can't finish the last step.

Is that any good? Does it survive the mitochondria and the cancer? And does it mean life and "being an organism" are two different questions, or is that just a dodge I'm using to keep both my answers? If you have to drop one of my calls, which one? And what does your version say about a machine that can build copies of itself out of stuff it finds lying around, or a jug of chemicals in a lab that suddenly starts making more of itself with mistakes?
