I write about philosophy and the sciences of human nature for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on an essay about the oldest and most durable argument in moral philosophy — the one over human nature. I'd like you to draft the centerpiece for me: a dialogue between two contemporary interlocutors, one speaking for Mencius and one for Xunzi, each as formidable as the other, each arguing in good faith and answering the other's actual points rather than talking past them.

What I don't want is the textbook recital — Mencius says we're born good, Xunzi says we're born bad, and both agree we can improve. I want the exchange to earn its conclusion by working through the details, so that at the end I can tell my readers exactly where these two part company and whether the parting is a genuine disagreement or two people using one word for different things.

The puzzle I'd like the dialogue to start from is this. Both men are optimists about the destination: both hold that almost anyone can become a sage, and both locate the obstacle in something that has to be worked on or drawn out. So the quarrel cannot be a simple disagreement about whether people can be good. It has to be about what the raw material is and what has to be done to it.

Let the Mencian make the case from the inside: the immediate, unreflective recoil anyone feels at the sight of a child about to fall into a well; the idea that compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong are present in everyone as beginnings rather than finished virtues; the claim that these beginnings are like seedlings to be tended and extended, not manufactured. Let the Xunzian grant the recoil but deny that it carries any weight — a passing sentiment is not a motive you can build a life on — and argue that what we are born with is appetite for gain and comfort, so that goodness has to be made by deliberate effort and the reshaping power of ritual, the way a press straightens a warped board.

Then have them press each other on the joints: what each of them means by nature, and whether the two are simply using the word for different things, so that part of the dispute is verbal; whether the well-side recoil is a foundation or a distraction; whether tending a seedling and straightening a board name two genuinely different processes or one process under two metaphors; whether a person who reliably does good only because ritual and habit have reshaped her is, on the Mencian's own account, actually good; and, hardest of all, where the first rituals came from if the nature they were meant to correct is the only material available.

I want the two of them to get somewhere — not to a knock-down victory, but to a precise statement of where the disagreement lives and a clear-eyed verdict on whether it could ever be settled, or whether it turns on a starting intuition neither side can argue for.
