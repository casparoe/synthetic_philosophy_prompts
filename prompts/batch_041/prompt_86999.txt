I'm a novelist, and I've been having the same argument with a friend of mine who used to be a statistician for about six years now, and I've finally written down my actual verdicts instead of just scowling at her. Her line is that everything has a number, you just have to be honest about your credences. I don't think thats true, but I can't say what I do think, so — here are my gut verdicts. Tell me what rule they add up to, and which ones I have to throw out to get a rule at all.

1. Urn with 70 red and 30 black, one draw. It's 0.7. If you say 0.6 you're wrong about the urn — not wrong about your feelings, wrong, the way you'd be wrong about the number of balls in it.

2. Drug trial: one clean study, 70 of 100 patients improve. Another drug: twelve clean studies, same 70%. I say the probability is the same in both cases. But I'd stake a year of my life on the second and I'd hesitate on the first, and I don't think I'm being irrational about that — I think there's a second quantity in play that isn't probability. (Keynes had a name for it, I'm almost sure — weight of evidence, weight of argument, something like that. I'm not asking you for the citation, I want to know whether the second quantity is real or whether I'm fooling myself.)

3. My uncle smoked till he was 91 and dropped dead of nothing. Technically that's evidence about smoking. It moves me by zero. My doctor says 'anecdotes aren't evidence' and I think that sentence is false as written and true as meant, and I can't hold both of those thoughts at the same time.

4. Is there life anywhere else in the galaxy? I don't have a number and I don't think there is one. 0.5 is what you get from 'either there is or there isn't,' and it's an insult. Not 0.5, not 0.3, not 'roughly a third' — the question isn't wearing that shape.

5. Butler or gardener, same evidence. It's definately more likely the butler did it, and I think that's a fact about the evidence rather than about me — the other detective with the same file who says gardener is making a mistake. Then he finds a glove, and now his number is different and also right. So the probability seems to live in the evidence, and different evidence gives different answers that are both correct.

6. I can't rank 'the butler did it' against 'it rains in London on my birthday next year.' Not 'I'm unsure which is higher' — I don't think there's a fact of the matter about which is higher. That one bothers me more than the rest.

My half-theory, take it apart: probability is a relation between a body of evidence and a claim, not a property of the world and not a feeling in my head. Some claims can be ranked on given evidence and some genuinely can't. And separately there's the weight thing — how much evidence you're standing on, which can pile up while the probability sits still, and which is what actually decides whether I'd act. Does that hold all six? Which verdict do I have to give up? And what does your version predict for a case I haven't listed: I have a hundred pages of a real seventeenth-century merchant's letters and I want to say how likely it is that he cheated his business partner. Is there an answer to that, or is it one of the ones with no fact of the matter?
