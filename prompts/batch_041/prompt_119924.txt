I retired last year after thirty-five years as a field geologist, and I have been spending my mornings reading moral philosophy with a small group of friends. For the past year, two of them have been arguing about the familiar rescue case: you can save one person or five, but not both. One holds the anti-aggregationist view that the numbers do not count — each person has an equal claim, and if you can save either, you may save either, perhaps by a coin flip. The other holds the aggregative view that of course the numbers count, and that saving five is at least morally preferable to saving one. They have been talking past each other, and I want to see whether they can produce a document they could both sign.

Please draft the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can accept, about the structure of the case, what it means to have a claim on being saved, the separateness of persons, the relevance of numbers, the difference between saving and not saving, and whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal; (2) a disagreements table that states each remaining disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own position; (3) a list of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — including, but not limited to, whether the value of a person’s life is aggregative, whether a coin flip is a procedure of respect or an evasion, whether probabilities and expected numbers of lives saved are morally relevant, and whether the distinction between cases where the numbers differ and cases where they do not is principled; and (4) for each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. The document should be something both parties could put their names to, not a neutral summary of their views.

Please also incorporate the following case, which has been nagging me. A rescue team can certainly save one trapped miner, or it can attempt a risky operation to save five miners trapped elsewhere. The risky operation has a 40% chance of saving all five and a 60% chance of saving none; the certain operation saves the one. All six are strangers, and there are no other differences. Does the anti-aggregationist’s coin flip apply between the one and the risky five, and if so, what exactly is being flipped? Does the aggregator recommend the risky operation because it maximizes expected lives saved, and does that recommendation depend on treating risk as a moral quantity? I want the joint statement to say what each side predicts about this case, whether the disagreement here is a new crux or a special case of one already listed, and what evidence or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it. Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, and citations; I am after the argument, not the literature.
