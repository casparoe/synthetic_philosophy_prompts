In my European history class we have been reading Machiavelli's The Prince, and the passage everyone remembers is the simile in the chapter on fortune: fortune, he says, is like one of those rivers that swell into a flood and sweep away buildings and trees so that nothing can stand against them, except that in the calm season men can build dikes and dams, and then when the water rises it is channeled off and does no harm. My teacher used this to make a point that felt almost comforting: that fortune is not all-powerful, and that virtù consists largely in the foresight to raise the defenses before the flood arrives — in preparing institutions and plans in advance so that bad luck cannot simply destroy you.

I want to know how far the simile actually holds, because the more I think about it the more work it seems to be doing that Machiavelli has not paid for. Could you set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the river, the flood, the dikes, the calm season, the engineer — and then the places where the mapping breaks down? I am suspicious of several things in particular. A river is an impersonal natural process with no intentions, whereas Machiavelli's fortune elsewhere reads almost like a person with a will of her own. A flood is seasonal and its course is known in advance, whereas political misfortune may arrive with no warning and no precedent. And building dikes presupposes that you already know which river and which bank you are dealing with, which is precisely what a statesman facing an unprecedented situation may not know. I would also like to know which features of the river picture get quietly imported — for instance, the idea that the most you can do against fortune is defend yourself, never exploit her. Then tell me whether Machiavelli's own conclusion, that virtù can hold fortune back and that fortune governs only about half of our actions, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the simile was carrying that conclusion all along. If the river turns out to be a poor model, propose a better one.

Finally, since I am trying to argue this in an essay and do not want to overstate my case, tell me what would change your verdict — which reading of Machiavelli, or which general point about how analogies function in arguments, would push you toward the opposite conclusion — and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
