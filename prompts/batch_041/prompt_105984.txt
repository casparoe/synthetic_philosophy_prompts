I have been reading a popular book about the prospect of uploading minds, and I have reached a chapter whose argument strikes me as either very good or quietly cheating, and I cannot tell which. Reconstructed, it runs as follows. Functionalism tells us that a mental state is nothing over and above the job it does in the system, so what a mind is made of does not matter: the brain is hardware, the mind is software. Now suppose we replace the neurons of a living brain one at a time with silicon chips, each engineered to do exactly what the cell it displaces did — to take in the same signals from its neighbours and to send back the same signals in turn — so that nothing further downstream can tell the difference. The subject notices nothing at any stage; his behaviour, his reports, his memories are untouched. If he dies during the procedure, he must die at some particular chip. But no chip is special; each does what its predecessor did. So he dies at no chip — or else there is a fact about which chip kills him that nothing in the world could ever disclose. The author says the burden is on anyone who denies this to name the step. What comes out the other end is conscious, remembers his childhood and wants to finish his book, so the man who walks out is the man who went in. The only way to resist, he adds, is to insist that consciousness requires some non-physical ingredient, which is to say a soul. He grants that running the procedure twice would leave two people with equal claim to be the original, but treats this as odd rather than fatal, since identity is not the same thing as uniqueness.

I would like an audit rather than a verdict. Take it in order and tell me which sentences someone working on personal identity or on the metaphysics of mind would simply accept, which would make them wince, and which are the author's own gloss rather than anything the views he gestures towards actually deliver. I am inclined to think the no-sudden-death step is strong, and I am fairly sure the soul remark is cheap; I would rather be told where I am wrong than have my suspicions confirmed. I am especially unsure whether the conclusion follows from the premises he has actually established, whether the burden of proof has been placed where it belongs, and whether the chips' doing the same job as the cells they replace is really the same claim as those cells' playing the same role in the system.

One practical request: write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And do not trouble yourself with citations — I am not asking for a reading list, I want the reasoning.
