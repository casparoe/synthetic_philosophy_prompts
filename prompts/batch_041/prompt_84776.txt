Ok so I'm a junior in political science and I'm taking this constitutional theory seminar this term — we spent two weeks on precommitment, the Ulysses and the Sirens thing Elster made the standard reference for, and then the Waldron objection that a person can tie himself to a mast but a legislature isn't a person and the people it binds never agreed to anything. Honestly I left that class thinking the objection is devestating and also that I have no idea what to do with it. Meanwhile I have a part-time gig at the state legislature's nonpartisan research office, the one that writes the little background memos for members, and my supervisor found out I'm in this seminar and now she wants me to build her something. What she wants: a reusable screening procedure our office can run on any proposal that would bind future officials. She gets these constantly, maybe three or four a month, we have to turn a memo around in about a day, and the members who read it will not read philosophy. The kind of thing that crosses her desk: a charter amendment creating an independent redistricting commission with staggered twelve-year terms; a constitutional amendment requiring a two-thirds vote for any tax increase and another two-thirds vote to repeal it; an automatic trigger that cuts every department's budget by 2% with no vote whenever the rainy-day fund falls under 5% of general revenue; and a rule requiring an agency to get sign-off from a standing advisory panel, members appointed to fixed long terms, before it can revise any of its rules. She signs off on whatever I produce and she's told me it has to be usable by an intern who's been here three weeks.

So: give me the procedure. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and — this is the part I care about most — where it has to stop and hand the thing to a human because it's a values question rather than a fact question, since I think that's actually where the seminar left us. My worry is that I'll just build a machine that launders my own politics: I'm pretty sympathetic to the redistricting commission and to anything that locks in transit or climate spending, and I suspect a good procedure would make at least one of those look bad, so please don't let me off the hook. It should also survive the cases I can't tell apart — a rule that binds for five years versus forever, a rule that only requires a body to consult versus one that requires it to reach a particular outcome, and an emergency where the entire point is that the commitment gets overridden. Then run it on three of the four examples above, showing what each step returns and where it gets stuck, and add a seperate short section on what the procedure can't do at all. One page for the checklist itself; the worked examples can run long.
