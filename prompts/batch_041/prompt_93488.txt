I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and science, and I'm putting together a sidebar for a piece on why anyone outside the seminar room should care about G.E. Moore. The angle I keep circling is the odd status of beauty and friendship in his ethics: he obviously thinks they're among the best things there are, but the machinery he builds to defend that — the isolation test, organic unities, the idea that the value of a whole isn't just the sum of the values of its parts — seems to either over- or under-generate. I want a short dialogue I can actually read and maybe pull a line from, between two people who both know Moore's work well. One thinks his account of friendship as an intrinsic good basically works and is one of the real insights of the book. The other thinks the account quietly collapses — that once you take his own test for intrinsic value seriously, the value of a friendship turns out to be the value of the experiences of the parties, or of some whole state of affairs, and the relationship, the other person, the particular history, all drop out or become replaceable. Make them equally sharp, no strawman, and make them actually respond to each other: the defender should get to press the critic on what alternative they've got, the critic should get to press on what exactly the unity is supposed to add.

After the dialogue, give me a short note separating what's actually settled among people who know Moore from what's genuinely contested. I don't want a confident verdict dressed up as the consensus — if the reading of the isolation test or of the organic unity doctrine is disputed, say so, and say which parts are the live disagreements. And if the dialogue takes any liberties with the ideas, or leans on a contested reading, flag that too.
