Here's the thing that nags me. We treat mental illness like a real illness in most of the ways that matter: doctors diagnose it, the health service pays for treatment, you can get signed off work for it, and when someone does something terrible mid-psychosis we say they weren't themselves and we don't blame them the way we'd blame a cold-blooded killer. Fine. But then, unlike literally any other illness, you can be locked up against your will for it — sectioned, held in a secure unit, not for a crime but because someone judges you a risk — and that can drag on for longer than a prison sentence for the same behaviour would. And the insanity defence makes it stranger still: being ill enough to be excused can land you in a secure hospital indefinitely, while a well person who did the very same thing walks free after a fixed term. So we excuse on one side and detain on the other.

What I want to know is what this whole mess presupposes about what mental illness actually is. Does the practice need the disease model — that it's an illness in the same sense diabetes is, something that befalls you and can therefore excuse you — or could you run exactly the same practices on Thomas Szasz's view that mental illness is a myth, that we're really just making moral judgements about people's conduct and dressing them up in medical language? Which bits strictly presuppose the disease model, and which bits would be perfectly happy with the myth view? Is the excuse-here-detain-there combo internally consistent, or is there a real contradiction hiding in it? And if we swapped the disease model out for Szasz's, what would actually have to change on the ground? Where is the practice underdetermined — i.e. several theories would license it and it doesn't really commit you to one?
