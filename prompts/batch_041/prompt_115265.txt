Write a poem to close a graduate seminar on the axiom of choice — a poem about Zorn's lemma in which the form, not the vocabulary, carries the mathematics, and which still works as a poem for someone who has never opened a set theory book.

Treat the poem as a partial order, in this concrete sense: a stanza stands above another when it opens with the other's closing word or phrase, and two stanzas count as comparable when one can be reached from the other by a run of such links. The poem must then honor, in its own construction, the shape of the lemma:

- Build at least one visible run of three or more linked stanzas, so the poem is seen to ascend, and let every run close inside the poem rather than trailing off.
- End at a fork, not a summit. The last two stanzas must be incomparable — neither opens with the other's ending, and neither is reachable from the other — while each is reachable from earlier stanzas, so that each caps a chain. Nothing in the poem may stand above either of them. Each is therefore maximal; neither is greatest; and a reader should feel that the ending was guaranteed before it was found.
- Make the non-constructiveness matter in a line or an image rather than a lecture: the poem should hold apart there being such an ending and being able to exhibit one, and it should not pretend to have built what it ends on.
- Do not name the lemma, do not restate it in prose, and keep the technical vocabulary out of the verse itself — no chain, no bound, no maximal element. A reader who knows the mathematics should reconstruct it from the shape; a reader who does not should still be held by the poem.

Because any poem is finite, the hypothesis will be satisfied almost for free. Do not spend lines asserting it; put the weight on the conclusion, and on the thought that a fork is a result rather than a failure — the two endings stand equal precisely because neither can claim the other.

Form: free verse, eighteen to thirty lines, no fixed meter or rhyme. The linking rule must be followed exactly, and the last two stanzas should be the same length, so that the fork reads as a fork and not as a coda. If a scene helps, the poem may be a staircase that only climbs, a genealogy of footnotes, or a sequence of rooms each entered through the last word of the previous one; the scene must not turn the poem into a riddle. Let the two final stanzas be genuinely different answers to the same question, and let neither one be the top.
