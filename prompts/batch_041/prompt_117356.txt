I've been betting football (soccer) markets for about four years now -- it started as a hobby, it's currently something like a third of my income, which I still find slightly insane -- and last year I went down the Kelly criterion rabbit hole and came out a convert. I've read the original paper, the fractional-Kelly literature, the ergodicity crowd, all of it, though I'm definately not a mathematician by training (I teach secondary school), so I know I'll get eaten alive if I'm not prepared. In three weeks I'm giving a 25-minute talk at my old university, in a joint session between the stats department and the finance group, defending this: for someone compounding a bankroll they can't top up, maximizing expected log wealth isn't a risk preference or a heuristic, it's the only thing that survives; expected-value betting looks better and is strictly worse; and the practice of betting a fraction of Kelly is a patch for estimation error, not a patch for the criterion itself. My talk covers why E[log W] rather than E[W], the asymmetry where overbetting costs more growth than underbetting, why ruin arguments only bite under my compounding assumption, and a slide on the "fallacy of large numbers" objection that I am honestly not confident in.

The room, as best I can work it out: a decision theorist who thinks I've smuggled in a utility function and then called it rationality; my old supervisor, who does stochastic processes and will go straight at ruin and at parameter uncertainty; two guys from a prop firm who size at half Kelly and will tell me that's the real answer and that my full-Kelly talk is irresponsible; someone from the philosophy department who's read the ergodicity people and thinks the whole log-utility argument is a confused way of talking about time averages versus ensemble averages; and one guy near the back who just thinks gambling is a tax on people who can't do arithmetic. Write me the Q&A prep sheet for this audience, not a defense of my position: the eight or so questions I'll actually get, phrased in each person's own vocabulary and roughly in the order they'll come, ranked by how much damage each one does, with a two-minute reply for each, a note on where the reply is soft or where I should just concede the point outright, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. If some objection only has force because of something in my framing -- say, my "can't top up the bankroll" caveat quietly doing more work than I think it is -- flag that too.

And be straight with me about what's actually settled here versus what's a live fight among people who know the math. I don't want a contested objection presented as if everyone agrees it's fatal, and I don't want an objection declared dead when it isn't. If it turns out that a chunk of this is just people preferring different utility functions and dressing the preference up as a theorem, say that plainly instead of handing me a tidy resolution.
