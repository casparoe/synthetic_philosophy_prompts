I'm a second-year PhD student, and in three weeks I have to run a seventy-five-minute session on material constitution — the statue-and-the-clay business — for an odd mixed group: a dozen first-year undergraduates from a general 'problems of philosophy' course, plus two visiting cognitive scientists who signed up out of curiosity. The room has a whiteboard but no projector, the slot falls straight after lunch, and I will lose them the moment I front-load terminology. My problem is not that I don't understand the material; it is that several of the first-years met the statue and the clay in an introductory lecture, where it served as a throwaway illustration, and they left certain they already know the answer — that the statue simply is the clay, one object picked out by two descriptions, and that the whole affair is a quarrel about words. I want a plan for teaching it, not an explanation of it. Specifically, I want the order in which the components have to arrive so that the puzzle feels like a discovery rather than a piece of professional jargon. My own guess at the sequence is: identity first, then the divergence in persistence and in what could have been true of each, then the question of how two things can occupy the same place at the same time — but I am not confident, and I suspect the phrase 'two things in the same place' is itself a framing I will later have to dismantle, so tell me what genuinely depends on what, and where I would be creating a debt I must pay off before the session ends.

For the case that carries the whole thing I have been tempted to abandon the statue and the clay, since they have half-memorised it, in favour of a sandcastle: the sand is older than the castle, the castle exists only while the sand is arranged, the tide destroys the castle while leaving the sand, and the very same grains could later make 'the same' castle again. What I cannot judge is whether the sandcastle is doing the same work as the statue or importing confusions of its own — and note that it has no pre-existing lump the way the clay does, which I take to be either the entire point or a fatal flaw. I also cannot decide whether the honest formulation of the puzzle is 'two objects, one place' or instead something about one thing depending on another, or whether that distinction is worth drawing for this audience at all. So I need to know which case should carry the session and what that case must have and must lack for the puzzle to come out cleanly, including whether to keep or discard my sandcastle and why the stock example fails or succeeds where it does. Then: the misconceptions they will form, in the order they will form them — I am expecting 'it is just two descriptions of one thing', 'the castle simply is the arrangement', 'this is a verbal dispute', and something like 'a thing is its matter' — together with the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than merely denied. Finish with one question I can put to them at the close that would show whether the idea has landed rather than been politely nodded at, and tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out. I do not want a reading list; I want the session. Please write it up under these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths: 'Order of ideas' (about 250 words); 'The carrying case' (about 200 words); 'Misconception map' (about 300 words, as a list with the sequence position attached to each item); 'The check for understanding' (about 100 words); and 'What to leave out, and why' (about 100 words).
