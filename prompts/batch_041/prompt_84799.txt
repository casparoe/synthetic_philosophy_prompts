I'm a TA for a grad-level Bayesian epistemology seminar, and next week I have to run a 75-minute session on the Judy Benjamin problem. I know the problem well enough to explain it, but I always end up either getting bogged down in the formalism or turning it into a debate about which update rule is right before the students even see why there's a problem. I need you to plan the session for me, not explain the problem to me.

The setup, as I'll present it: Judy is dropped into a territory divided between the Blue Army (hers) and the Red Army. She has some initial credences about where she is—say, some probability she's in Blue territory, and within Blue there are two sub-areas, second company and third company. She then gets a radio message that doesn't tell her where she is outright; it tells her something conditional, like "if you're in Blue territory, the chance you're in the second company's area is 1/2." The puzzle is that this doesn't fit the standard Bayesian updating story: she hasn't learned a proposition that rules out possibilities, so she can't just conditionalize. And when you try to extend the rules—Jeffrey conditionalization, minimizing information, etc.—they give different answers, or no unique answer.

What I want from you is a teaching plan. Specifically:

- The order in which the pieces have to come in so that the problem feels like a problem rather than a magic trick. What does she need to already believe, and what does she need to learn, for the conditional to bite? I want the students to feel the tension between "she learned something" and "nothing in her probability space got ruled out."
- The one example that should carry the whole session. Is the Judy Benjamin story itself the best carrying example, or is there a simpler one that does the same work? If Judy Benjamin, how do I set it up so the numbers are easy to follow and the conditional isn't trivially already true? If there's a better example, what is it and why does it work?
- The misconceptions I should expect. I'm guessing: (1) they'll think she just conditionalized on the conditional, (2) they'll think the problem is just about ambiguity in the message, (3) they'll think any update rule is as good as any other, (4) they'll think the problem shows Bayesianism is refuted. I've seen alot of these in past years. Where in the sequence should I head each one off, and how?
- A check for understanding at the end that would show they've got the problem rather than just memorized the setup.
- What to leave out at this level. I don't want to get into the full formal machinery of infomin or the various distance measures unless it's necessary. Tell me what I can safely skip and say so explicitly.

One more thing: I need you to seperate what's settled from what's contested. Some parts of this are not in dispute—the setup, the fact that ordinary conditionalization doesn't apply, the fact that different update rules give different answers. But other parts are genuinely contested among people who work on this: which update rule is correct, whether the problem shows a flaw in Bayesian epistemology or just a limitation of certain formal tools, whether the "right" answer is even well-defined. I definately don't want to present a contested view as if it's the consensus. So in your plan, flag which claims are settled and which are live disputes, and if you recommend a particular line, say that it's a choice and not the only defensible one.

Also, I have students who are comfortable with probability but not with measure theory, so keep the plan at the level of finite probability spaces and simple conditional probabilities.
