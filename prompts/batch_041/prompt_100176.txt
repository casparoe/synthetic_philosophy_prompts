I spent 30 years as a clinical pathologist — my whole job was looking at tissue and telling people whether it was normal or not — and I retired about six years ago. Since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy seminar at the local university, and I've gotten hung up on one thing the group keeps dancing around alot: when you call something a disease, are you reporting a fact about the body, or making a value call about a life? I don't want another discussion where everybody agrees it's "both." I want to run an activity that makes them feel both pulls in their hands.

Setting: the seminar is 16 people, a mix of grad students and retirees like me, and I get 90 minutes to run the whole session myself. No lecture from me up front.

Here's my sketch — tell me if it works, and fix what doesn't. I want the full runnable version: rules, materials, a timeline, the debrief, and where it'll blow up.

Split the room into four tables of four. Each table gets a deck of about a dozen "intake" cards. Every card describes a made-up condition (invent the conditions, please — I don't want anybody's real diagnosis on the table) in plain physiology, and each card comes with two printed lines. The first line is from a made-up device I'm calling the Function Panel: for the mechanism named on the card, it reports whether that mechanism is pulling its statistically typical weight for survival and reproduction — typical, below typical, or above typical. The second line is the person's own attitude: wants it gone, wants to keep it, indifferent.

Before they sort anything, each table has to write and sign one sentence: a condition counts as a disease exactly when ____. Then they sort every card into DISEASE or NOT A DISEASE. Here's the part I care about: I'm the referee, and the signed sentence is binding. If they call a card a disease, their sentence has to cover it. If they want an exception, they have to amend the sentence in writing, out loud, and the amendment binds them for every card after. Each table starts with three chips; every amendment costs a chip; a table that runs out is told it's "rule" is now just a list and sits out the last round.

Then I hand out a second small deck built to break the rules I expect them to write. I'm thinking: a mechanism that's clearly below typical but the person is glad to have it and lives a long happy life; a below-typical mechanism that's statistically universal past a certain age; a condition with nothing wrong on the Panel at all that everybody in the room still flinches at; a below-typical mechanism that turns out to be protective in one environment and a liability in another; a condition only one culture files under disease; and a below-typical mechanism the person insists is just who they are. Add or swap cases as you see fit, but keep them invented.

The payoff moment should be when I lay all the signed sentences and amendments on the table and read them back in order, and they hear the value-words creeping in — harmful, unwanted, gets in the way of a good life — because the pure-biology versions kept forcing them to call things diseases they wouldn't. That's the thing I want them to feel before I say a word about it.

So: give me the runnable design, the card list with the two printed lines for each, the referee script, the debrief that maps what happened onto the two camps (I think there are standard names for the two poles, I can never keep them seperate — put the labels on at the end), what predictably goes wrong the first time and how to handle it, and an honest note on what this setup can't show them and I'll have to supply myself.
