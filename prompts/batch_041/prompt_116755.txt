I'm a second-year PhD student splitting my time between a reinforcement learning lab and a philosophy of mind reading group, and a labmate and I have now spent three weeks failing to settle whether an RL agent could ever literally suffer, as opposed to merely behaving in ways we are disposed to describe as suffering. Every attempt turns into an argument about something else — about whether "suffering" is a natural kind, about whether behavior is evidence or constitutive, about whether the question is even well formed — so I would like a case built to order rather than another round of dueling intuitions. Please construct a thought experiment to the specifications below.

The specifications are these. First, both systems in the case must be things a competent engineer could build today with ordinary reinforcement learning methods: no exotic hardware, no appeal to a hidden inner light, and no assumption that we possess, or could possess, an instrument that simply reads off experience. Second, the case must isolate a single variable. The two arms must be identical in every respect the agent could in principle detect from the inside — same architecture, same weights, same policy, same value estimates, and the same sequence of observations and reward signals at every timestep — and they must differ only in a fact about the world that lies outside the agent's observation. Specifically, in one arm the states the agent is trained to avoid are states that would degrade the agent's own future capacities, while in the other they are states that would harm only the person who wrote the reward function. Third, the verdict must not be stipulated anywhere in the setup; it has to fall out of a view about what valence is, not out of a sentence that says one agent hurts.

Then, for the case you build: give the scenario itself in a single paragraph, and afterwards lay out what the view that identifies the sign of the reward signal with the sign of the felt state says about each arm and why, and what a view that treats reward as a purely formal quantity — defined only relative to someone's choice of reward function, with no intrinsic connection to anything's good or ill — says about each arm and why. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the separating work, and run the removal test: state exactly what would have to change for the two arms to collapse into one, and whether the case becomes trivial once it does. Finish by arguing the other side: put on the hat of someone convinced the case is a cheat, give their strongest objection, and say whether any repair leaves the case standing. I would genuinely rather be told the case cannot be built to spec than be given one that only appears to meet it; if it cannot, say precisely which specification fails and describe the nearest satisfiable version. Keep the whole reply under 800 words, with the scenario itself confined to one paragraph.
