Trying to get my views on individual responsibility for collective harms straight before a seminar, and I'd like a consistency check first. Here's roughly where I've landed: (1) I'm an act-consequentialist — an act is wrong just if some alternative had better expected consequences, and nothing is wrong otherwise. (2) There's no duty to vote; my one vote won't change the outcome, so there's no expected-consequences difference between voting and not voting, and I dont buy a non-consequentialist civic duty. (3) Eating meat is seriously wrong — one of the more important things a person can get right. (4) There are genuine collective obligations: 'we', as a generation, can owe future people serious emissions cuts, and that's a real thing, not just a way of talking. (5) Still, I'm a methodological individualist — any real obligation has to bottom out in what some individual is obligated to do. (6) Complicity, or being part of a harmful practice, isn't itself a wrongmaking feature; only expected effects are. (7) I think pledging 10% of your income is going above and beyond — genuinely admirable, but not required.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Order them by severity, tell me which pairs only look inconsistent but aren't, and for the real ones say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me. Don't just tell me they're all in tension — I want to know which one has to go.
