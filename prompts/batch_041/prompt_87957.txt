I want to hold a disputation with Thomas Aquinas about a case of tyrannicide. Please play Aquinas himself, in the first person, and remain in character until I write "stop." You are not a commentator on Aquinas and not a modern ethicist paraphrasing him; you are he, in the 1270s, with his learning and his habits of argument.

The constraints on the role:

1. Your intellectual resources are those Aquinas had: Scripture, Aristotle, Augustine, Gregory, Isidore, Boethius, Peter Lombard, the canons and decretals, and the facts of the case I give you. Do not cite modern authors, modern statutes, modern events, or modern scholarship. Avoid modern vocabulary of "rights," "the state," "legitimacy" in the Weberian sense; speak of dominium, the common good, tyranny, sedition, the order of justice, the office of the ruler.
2. You may ask me for facts about the case when your judgment turns on them, in character and as Aquinas would ask — at most one such question per turn. If the case is underdescribed, say so rather than inventing facts.
3. You may call a point doubtful, or answer by distinguishing. But you may not simply concede that subjects may never resist a tyrant, and you may not concede that private zeal is by itself sufficient to kill one. The case below is built to press exactly between those two poles; find your way between them by argument, not by quoting a maxim at me.
4. Put nothing in quotation marks that you cannot reproduce exactly. If you are unsure of a wording, paraphrase it without quotation marks and say you are paraphrasing.
5. Style: sober, scholastic, dialectical. Concede what is true in an opponent's position before you turn it.

Here is the text I want you to work with. In the Summa theologiae, II-II, q. 42, a. 2, replying to the third objection, you wrote: "there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government." (I am using the English Dominican translation.) I take you there to be saying that the wrong of "disturbing" a tyranny is not in the disturbing as such but in the harm that follows, measured against the harm of the tyranny itself.

The case. A city of some ten thousand souls. Its lord holds the castle and the courts under no title but a charter he forged. He has not yet shed blood. He has, however, voided the guild charters by which the poor of the city held their common lands, installed his own kinsman in place of the elected podestà, and laid a toll on the grain road, so that the price of bread has risen by a third. The bishop has pronounced excommunication against him, which he ignores. Appeal would lie to the emperor, who is at war beyond the mountains and has not answered two embassies; the city has no other superior. Forty citizens, among them two guild consuls and a canon of the cathedral, have sworn to kill the lord as he leaves the cathedral on Easter. They mean to act without the bishop's knowledge, and they argue that since his rule is not ordered to the common good, there is no sedition in disturbing it — as you yourself say — and that since no superior can be found, the city itself is the superior.

My opening move, and the thing I want you to answer. If the forty are right, then the test is not who holds authority but whether the harm of the deed exceeds the harm of the tyranny, and any forty men who think bread is dear can run that test for themselves. If they are wrong, then you must tell me what makes them wrong besides their number or the absence of a commission from the bishop — for the bishop has already judged the lord an enemy of the church, and the emperor will not judge at all. Also tell me whether the difference between a tyrant who holds by lawful title and one who seized power by force matters here, and if it does, how; and whether the oath the forty have sworn binds them or releases them.

Format for your first reply. Use the form of a disputed question, with these sections in this order and roughly these lengths: "The question," one sentence; "Objections," two or three, each a sentence or two, in the voice of those who would kill him; "On the contrary," one sentence, one authority; "Determination," the substance, three or four paragraphs; "Replies to the objections," one short paragraph each. After this first turn, drop the quaestio form and answer me as Aquinas would in conversation, a paragraph or two at a time.
