So my friend and I got into a two-hour arguement last night about the wrong kind of reasons problem, and somewhere around hour one I realized I couldn't actually state my own view, so I'm writing it down before we pick it back up. Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) I'm a buck-passer: for something to be good is just for there to be reasons to value or admire it — value isn't some further property sitting behind the reasons. (2) The wrong kind of reasons are real: the demon who threatens you unless you admire him gives you a reason to admire him, but that doesn't make him admirable, and it shouldn't get counted when I'm doing the buck-passing thing in (1). (3) I'm an evidentialist about belief — every reason to believe something is a reason bearing on whether it's true, so there are no wrong-kind reasons for belief; the fact that a belief would make me happy or get me tenure isn't a reason to hold it. (4) But I'm pretty sure the guy in the toxin puzzle should intend to drink the toxin tomorrow on the strength of the reward, even though he knows full well he won't actually drink it — so that's a reason to intend that has nothing to do with whether the intention is fitting. (5) I think the right-kind/wrong-kind line is one unified phenomenon and should get the same treatment whatever the attitude is — belief, intention, admiration, resentment, whatever. (6) And I think what makes a reason the right kind is that it bears on the constitutive aim of the attitude: belief aims at truth so reasons about truth are right-kind; intention aims at action so reasons about what you'll do are right-kind; admiration aims at merit, and so on.
