For some years now two groups working on the problem of recovering an agent's objectives from its behavior have been arguing past each other, and I would like the document they should have produced together. On one side are the recovery optimists. They hold that an agent's preferences are, in the sense that matters for this problem, its choice dispositions; that inverse reinforcement learning is the correct formal setting; and that given sufficiently rich and varied behavioral data the objective is pinned down, with the familiar non-uniqueness results being nuisances about how the objective is represented rather than genuine obstacles to recovering it. On the other side are the underdetermination skeptics. They hold that behavior is a thin and theory-laden signal; that the demonstrations we actually obtain come from bounded, biased, and only approximately rational agents rather than from optimizers; that distinct preferences routinely generate identical behavior; and that the notion of preference at issue in the wider project carries content — what an agent would want under circumstances it never faces — that no quantity of observed choice can disclose.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign. I want, first, a numbered section of claims they can agree on: about what the formal setup actually is; about what the existing impossibility and non-identifiability results do and do not establish; about which of their disagreements concern the mathematical object being inferred and which concern the word preference; and about what would count as genuine progress. Then a disagreements table in which each surviving disagreement is stated in a formulation its own side would accept, followed by the crux or cruxes that divide them.

Two technical results should be made to bear on the statement, because I think they cut in opposite directions. The first: for any potential function over states and any discount factor, adding to the reward the term equal to the discounted value of that potential at the successor state minus its value at the current state leaves the agent's set of optimal policies unchanged — so no behavioral evidence can distinguish a reward from any of its potential-shaping variants, even though such variants may rank non-optimal policies differently from one another. The second: when the demonstrator is modeled as only noisily optimal, the scale of the reward and the degree of the demonstrator's rationality trade off against each other, so the data fix at most their combination rather than either one alone. For each crux, identify a case, construction, or comparison that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case — including the possibility that a side's prediction is that the case is irrelevant, together with its reason.

Where you judge part of the dispute to be verbal — for instance, that the two camps mean different things by preference, one behavioral and one dispositional or counterfactual — say so explicitly, name the two readings, and state which of the agreed claims and which disagreements survive once the ambiguity is removed. Where the dispute is substantive, do not smooth it over; the document is worth producing only if both signatures would be honest.
