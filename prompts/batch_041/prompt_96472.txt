I've been trying to think through the orbital-debris problem as a commons case and I keep tripping over the label, so I want you to do the mapping for me. The setup, and just work with it even if I've got a detail or two off: orbit is a resource where every operator's use adds a little collision risk to everyone else, you can't really keep anyone out of the useful orbits, and a handful of events -- an anti-satellite test, a couple of accidental collisions -- each scattered thousands of fragments, plus alot more that are too small to track. Debris makes debris: a fragment hits something and makes more fragments, and past some density the collisions feed themselves regardless of what anyone launches next. No operator internalizes the risk it dumps on the others, there's no binding international regime that makes it do so, and the nearest thing to a rule is a set of voluntary guidelines that everyone signs and nobody is bound by.

Now the part I actually want to test. I think there are two structures floating around under the one name 'tragedy of the commons' and people never keep them apart. One is subtraction: many appropriators drawing down a rival stock -- Hardin's pasture, a fishery, an aquifer. The other is deposition: many actors adding a shared bad to a medium everybody depends on -- a pollution or shared-sink problem, which I think is what debris really is. And my suspicion is that the whole toolkit -- Ostrom's design principles, the commons-versus-open-access distinction, the 'commons is a bundle of rights' line -- got built mostly on subtraction cases and doesnt carry over cleanly to deposition ones.

So what I want:

1. Map debris onto each structure -- who the players are, what's being drawn down or added, and what game each version actually is. Prisoner's dilemma, assurance game, chicken, a threshold/tipping game? And be honest: are subtraction and deposition genuinely different structures, or the same thing in different clothes?

2. Then run it the other way. What does the subtraction story get right about debris, and where does it misdescribe it? Especially the governance failure -- the stock story usually blames missing institutions, but here an institution exists and is toothless, so is that even the same failure?

3. Press on the 'non-excludable' diagnosis. You can partly exclude people from orbits, and you can often tell whose fragment is whose, so is 'commons' even the right word, or is the real problem something else -- a coordination failure, or an anticommons (too many overlapping claims and jurisdictions making the thing ungovernable)?

4. And the bit I care about most: what does the case show about the theory, not the theory about the case? If deposition really is its own structure, does 'the tragedy of the commons' need to split into two theories with different remedies -- and would the deposition half end up looking more like pollution regulation than like anything in the commons literature?

Don't give me a survey of the literature. Do the mapping, and say plainly where the theory is silent or just wrong.
