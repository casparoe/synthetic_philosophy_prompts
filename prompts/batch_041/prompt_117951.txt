I have been carrying an argument around since an old forum thread and I can no longer tell whether it is a proof or a trick. It is meant to show that objective Bayesianism is dead — not dead as a slogan, but dead as the claim that some priors are required and others forbidden. Here it is in the form I usually give it.

(1) Principle of indifference: if your evidence does not favour any one of a set of alternatives over the others, rationality requires equal credence in them.

(2) A chord is drawn at random in a circle. What is the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? As stated, the evidence favours no chord over any other: not chords with one midpoint over chords with another, not chords at one distance from the centre over chords at another, not chords with one pair of endpoints over another.

(3) So indifference requires 1/4, if the chords are grouped by midpoint; 1/2, if they are grouped by distance from the centre; 1/3, if they are grouped by endpoints.

(4) One proposition, three required credences. A rule that requires contradictory credences is no rule at all, since rationality cannot require p and not-p.

(5) The same collapse happens without needing three groupings of a single object. A factory turns out cubes whose edge length is somewhere between 0 and 2 centimetres and nothing else is known about them. Indifference over edge length puts the probability that the edge is under 1 centimetre at 1/2; indifference over face area puts it at 1/4; indifference over volume at 1/8. Nothing in the evidence marks out edge length as the parameter over which we are supposed to be indifferent.

(6) Two repairs get offered here. Either the problem is under-specified and has no unique answer, or indifference is legitimate only where an invariance group fixes the measure. The first makes indifference idle: where the chance mechanism is fixed we already know the chances, and where it is not fixed there is no question to be indifferent about. The second looks better but only relocates the difficulty, since it has to import a symmetry group the statement of the problem never mentions — the rotations of the circle leave a chord's distance from the centre alone, and the usual fix appeals instead to invariances of size and position that the problem does not contain.

(7) Nor is the residue confined to geometric puzzles. Take an urn of unknown composition, with no data. The only symmetry available, swapping black for white, fixes the first draw at one half and says nothing about the second. The uniform prior over the fraction of black balls and the Jeffreys prior, the Beta(1/2, 1/2) one, then disagree about the second draw once a black ball comes out: two thirds against three quarters. The evidence does not decide between them.

(8) And every credence not fixed by observed frequencies or by a stipulated physical chance is indifference or indifference renamed. Maximum entropy is indifference over a basis; a non-informative prior is indifference with a different label.

(9) Therefore in every case where a prior is actually needed, no credence is required. Every prior is permissible. Two agents who disagree about a frequency-less question are on an epistemic par. Calibration is not a norm, and objective Bayesianism is dead.

I do not want (9). Each step above looks plausible to me on its own, and I am not asking for pointers into the literature — I want the diagnosis. Which step do I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? If I keep whatever I have to keep in order to avoid (9), what is left of the claim that there are right and wrong priors, and what residual arbitrariness have I agreed to live with?
