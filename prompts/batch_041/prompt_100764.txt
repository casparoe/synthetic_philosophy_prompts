I run a small aesthetics reading group in the back room of a pub — 12 people, 90 minutes, mix of grad students, two engineers, a retired teacher, and one guy who has read every rationalist blog since 2009 and will let you know. Next week is the one about why we pay good money to be made sad by things that didn't happen. What I want is for them to walk into the puzzle themselves, with no intro talk from me, and not hear the usual name for it until the debrief. Here's a shape I sketched; please rework the mechanics wherever I've made it clunky, and don't put the standard name of the puzzle anywhere in the rules or the participant sheets.

Everyone gets a budget of 8 tokens and a menu of eight evenings, priced 1 to 4, and they can't afford everything so they have to trade off. The items are meant to vary along three seperate axes, not just how sad they are: how sad they expect to feel (0 to 10), whether there's a real person in it or it's made up, and whether it asks anything of them — a friend sitting you down to talk about a death asks something of you, a novel doesn't. Make sure a couple of items are high on sadness but low on status, so that enjoying them is faintly embarrassing, and a couple are high on status and high on sadness but low on enjoyment. That's on purpose.

For each item they fill in three columns: expected sadness, expected enjoyment, and how much they'd be willing to be seen having chosen it. Three columns, three different ledgers, and I want the activity built so they can come apart — that's most of the point. Then the bit I care most about: after everyone has committed, I relabel two items on the sheet. The novel turns out to be a barely-disguised memoir, so the sadness is now attached to a real person; the memoir turns out to have been made up, so it now isn't. Content unchanged. Everyone re-prices, and before they see the room's numbers they each write down a prediction of how much the group's enjoyment ratings for those two items moved. Closest two predictions split a bar of chocolate. Real stakes, tiny, no money.

Then we put the whole thing on the board — sadness on one axis, enjoyment on the other — and they have to explain the pattern, and then defend it against a debunker I assign at random who has to argue the whole effect is about safety and control rather than about sadness itself. I want them to notice that the puzzle isn't why we like sadness, it's why the same feeling gets priced completely differently depending on what kind of object it's stuck to, and that a lot of the answer lives in the second ledger rather than the first.

So what I need from you: the rules and the actual menu (write the eight items), the sheets, the relabelling script, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the candidate explanations of the puzzle — the safety-and-control story, the form-and-craft story, the significance story where the sadness is a signal that something mattered, and whatever else you think is live — and says which of them our own data can actually distinguish and which it can't. Also please be honest about the parts a 90-minute pub game can't reproduce and that I'll have to just tell them: sustained absorption, people returning to the same devastating thing for thirty years, the way the sadness in real art comes mixed with beauty and relief rather than neat. And tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time — I'm expecting at least one person to insist they simply like being sad, one person to refuse to price their feelings at all, and the whole thing to drift into everyone listing sad films they like. What do I do about those, and what am I not anticipating?
