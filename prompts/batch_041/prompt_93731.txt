Write me a short story about the indeterminacy of translation, but I don't want the usual rabbit thing just rehashed as fiction — I want the indeterminacy to actually cost someone something.

Quick setup so we're on the same page: Quine's gavagai bit, the linguist can't tell whether the word means rabbit, undetached rabbit parts, or a rabbit-stage, and no amount of pointing seperates the options, because the rival translation manuals agree on every observable bit of behavior. Fine, we all know that one. Now my twist: the word in question isn't a rabbit word.

My linguist has been flown out somewhere not because anyone there cares about metaphysics but because there's a land dispute, and she's the only person around who can translate for the negotiation. The contested term is the one the locals use when they say a family "holds" a piece of land. She's built two translation manuals that fit everything she's recorded so far. On one, the term means something like owns — a standing relation that survives the family walking away, that can be inherited, that can be violated without anyone noticing. On the other, it means something like is currently working or using — a relation that lapses the moment they stop. Every utterance she's written down is compatible with both manuals, and the two manuals disagree about cases that haven't come up yet: abandoned fields, inherited plots, land nobody's touched in a decade.

Rules for the story. Don't let her crack it with one clever experiment or a decisive observation — that would be cheating, the whole point is that the evidence underdetermines the choice. Let her be smart and definately still stuck. Keep the stakes concrete: the negotiation is tomorrow, she has to translate something, and whichever manual she's running changes what she ends up telling the other side. Make the reader feel why the choice is underdetermined instead of just being told it is. Show her trying a couple of the standard moves — asking "is this the same rabbit" style questions, checking whether the term takes the same modifiers her own word for ownership does — and show those moves either failing outright or getting quietly absorbed by both manuals.

One more wrinkle I'd like in there: she notices she could read her own language's ownership word the same two ways, so she can't even use her own "owns" as a fixed point. Keep that light, don't turn it into a lecture.

Aim for maybe 1000–1500 words, third person is fine. I don't need a tidy resolution — if she just has to pick one and live with it, that's good, but make the picking feel like a real loss either way.
