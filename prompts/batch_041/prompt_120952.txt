I am writing a scene in which a man in Damascus is visited by Death, who is never wrong about where a person will be. The man can stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo. Death's location is perfectly correlated with his choice: if he stays, Death is in Damascus; if he flees, Death is in Aleppo. The man, who prides himself on his reasoning, lays out this argument:

(1) If I stay, Death is in Damascus.
(2) If I flee, Death is in Aleppo.
(3) In either case, Death is where I am.
(4) So my choice cannot separate me from Death.
(5) So my choice makes no difference to whether I live or die.
(6) So I have no reason to prefer one action to the other.
(7) So any action I take is as rational as any other.
(8) So reason gives me no guidance.

He concludes that reason has abandoned him. I want the scene to turn on a flaw in this argument, but I cannot find it. Where does it go wrong? Which step would you reject, and what would the man have to give up if he rejects it? I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, since they are the engine of the scene, but tell me the cost.
