Question: Explain Bradley's regress and what it is supposed to show about the unity of the proposition. Then assess whether Frege's distinction between saturated and unsaturated entities offers a way to avoid the regress.

Answer: Bradley's regress is about what holds a proposition together. Take 'a is F'. You've got a and F, but that's just two things. For it to be a proposition, they have to be unified. If you say a relation R unifies them, then R is a third thing. But now you need a relation to connect R to a, and another to connect R to F, and then more relations to connect those relations, and so on forever. So if unity is a relation, you never actually reach unity. Bradley uses this to argue that relations can't be ultimately real; they're appearances. The proposition's unity can't be explained by adding a relational glue, because the glue itself needs gluing.

This is basically the same as the Third Man argument. In the Third Man, the Form of Largeness is supposed to explain why large things are large, but then you need another Form to explain why the first Form is large, and so on. Same structure: you try to explain something by positing another item of the same kind, and you get a regress. So Bradley's regress is really just the Third Man pointed at relations instead of Forms.

Frege tries to get out of this by saying that concepts are unsaturated and objects are saturated. In 'a is F', F is unsaturated; it has a gap that a fills. So the unity comes from the incompleteness of the concept, not from a separate relation. That does avoid the regress for simple subject-predicate propositions. But for relational propositions like 'a is to the left of b', the relation is a two-place thing that sits alongside a and b, so you'd still need something to tie it to both of them. Frege's trick only works when there's one object and one concept. So I don't think he fully solves the unity of the proposition.
