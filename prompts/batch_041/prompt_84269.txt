I'm a second-year PhD student in the philosophy of science, working on the epistemology of long-run prediction — in particular the "right but early" defense, where a theory whose forecast failed is said to have been vindicated by later events or excused because its time horizon had not yet arrived. In a lecture I attended, a historian of economic thought summarized Malthus roughly as follows.

Malthus's law was that population grows geometrically while the food supply grows arithmetically, so every improvement in living standards is self-defeating and humanity is condemned to subsistence-level existence. He predicted mass famine in nineteenth-century England, and the Industrial Revolution refuted him. But the law was right, only early: it is a claim about what happens when population is left unchecked, so the absence of famine shows only that the check had not yet arrived, and no conceivable evidence could count against it. Malthus is therefore the classic case of a theory that can be neither falsified nor dismissed, and the real lesson is that "right but early" is just the name we give to a claim that has been immunized against refutation.

I suspect this is too quick in both directions — too quick to treat Malthus as refuted, and too quick to conclude that the rescue is illegitimate — but I don't know where the errors lie. Please audit the summary: which parts a demographer or a historian of economic thought would accept, which are the standard simplifications, and which are misrepresentations that a careful defender of Malthus would reject. I would especially like to know whether the closing moral about unfalsifiability follows from anything true in the summary, and what would count as evidence for or against the Malthusian claim in its best formulation.
