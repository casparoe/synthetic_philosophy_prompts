I have just moved from the interpretability team at a mid-sized AI lab to its deployment-safety team, and my first deliverable is a safety case for a model we intend to give write access to an internal code repository: it can open pull requests, run jobs and post on our internal chat. My case is a control argument rather than an alignment one. I do not assume the model is aligned; I assume it may be deliberately working against us, and I locate the safety in the protocol wrapped around it rather than in its training. The protocol I am defending runs as follows. The untrusted model does the work; a deliberately weaker trusted model monitors every action and output; flagged cases are either rewritten by the trusted model or escalated to a human reviewer; a fixed fraction of the workload consists of tripwire tasks whose correct handling we know in advance, so that a monitor which quietly stops flagging them is itself evidence of trouble; and the whole arrangement is validated by an adversarial evaluation in which a red team plays the untrusted model, attempting to cause a specified harm — say, degrading the monitoring system itself — without being flagged, under an audit budget expressed as the number of escalations per thousand actions we can afford before the product team revolts. We can log everything the model says, including its stated reasons, but we may not treat those reasons as faithful. The claim is that even if the untrusted model knows the protocol in full, we can hold the probability of at least one successful act of sabotage over a month below the threshold the board has set.

The board is not the audience I am used to. It has three parts. Three alignment researchers who came up through RLHF and scalable-oversight work, who think of misalignment as something you fix during training, and who regard the suggestion that the model is scheming as science fiction at this scale. The product lead whose roadmap my protocol delays, who cares about latency, cost, and whether any of this survives contact with a real workflow. And an external member from a government AI safety body — a policy person, not a technical one — who will want to know what would count as evidence that the protocol works, and whether an evaluation designed and run by my own team counts as evidence at all.

Prepare my question-and-answer sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this board, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that member would phrase it and labelled with which of the three camps it comes from. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede at the outset and what I must refuse to concede. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises, and say which single objection, if I lose it, takes the case down with it.
