I spent thirty-odd years as a condensed-matter physicist before I retired, and I've been reading philosophy ever since, so go easy on me if I get the labels wrong.

The thing I keep bumping into is the fight about laws of nature. One side says a law isn't a thing that pushes anything around, it's just one of the axioms in the most economical true description of the whole history of the world, and the necessity we feel when we talk about laws is us mistaking a compact summary for a cause. The other side says no, laws genuinely govern, they're what makes things happen the way they do, and you've lost something if you reduce them to patterns. I understand every word of that and I still can't feel the disagreement.

So do this for me: restate both sides in the language I actually think in. Model selection, description length, overfitting, regularizers and priors, bias-variance, symmetries, initial conditions versus dynamics, ensembles versus a single run, effective descriptions that only hold above some scale. I'm no statistician by training but I've fit a great many models in my time and I've sat through enough seminars to know the MDL story.

Then give me the residue report. What translates cleanly, what had to be bent to fit, and what simply doesn't exist on my side of the fence. I have a suspicion I'd like you to test: that the best system is just whichever model wins the description-length-versus-fit tradeoff, that simplicity is doing the work of a regularizer, and that the whole exercise is being run on one sample with no ensemble, which any statistician would call hopeless. If that's roughly right, does the leftover -- all this governing and necessity talk -- tell me my vocabulary is blind to something real, or that the two sides are seperate arguments that never actually touch?
