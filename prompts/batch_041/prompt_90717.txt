I chair the safety-review board of a mid-sized hospital network — six of us: two clinicians, a clinical engineer, a data scientist, our general counsel, and me — and roughly four times a year an incident reaches us with an automated system somewhere in the causal chain: a sepsis-prediction model that never fired, an infusion pump working from a centrally updated dose library, a scheduling tool that spread a night shift too thin, and last month a radiology triage queue that ranked a tension pneumothorax below a routine follow-up. Our remit is narrow and dated. Within ninety days we must publish a finding naming the party or parties answerable for the harm, together with a proportionate response — retraining, a change to deployment terms, a referral to the professional regulator, a vendor claim, or, if nothing else fits, a no-fault remedy for the patient — and the finding is published in full, with any member free to attach a dissent. What defeats us is that the causal story is usually clear enough and yet nobody seems to have done anything wrong: the clinician followed the tool in a way the validation data made reasonable, the engineer built to a specification that was met, the vendor shipped an update that was announced, and the procurement decision that put the tool on the ward was made three years ago by people who have since left. I have stopped believing the question is who is to blame; I think it is who must answer, and that these two can come apart, and I want a procedure that keeps them apart rather than collapsing one into the other. Our counsel has taken to sorting the cases where a harm fails to land on anyone into four: the opacity case, where we cannot say which component or which human decision produced the output; the fault case, where we can say, and every contribution was reasonable given what that party knew at the time; the authority case, where someone saw the risk coming but had no standing to stop it, as when a ward nurse cannot disable an override the vendor's contract makes mandatory; and the remedy case, where a party can be named but the party who could prevent a recurrence sits outside our jurisdiction. I am not sure those four carve the space cleanly, and I suspect they are not mutually exclusive, so part of what I am asking for is a verdict on the taxonomy itself.

Please write the procedure the board can actually run in a ninety-minute session, with no one at the table who has read the academic literature and one member who keeps invoking meaningful human control without being able to say what it asks of us on a Tuesday. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a finding of no fault, the points at which it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and an honest marking of which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It must treat a finding of no fault and a finding of no answerable party as different outputs and tell us what to do when they come apart, and it must say whether answerability may be assigned on grounds other than fault — role, benefit from the risk imposed, or bare capacity to prevent the next one — without our dressing that up as a finding of wrongdoing. It also has to survive two of us pulling in opposite directions: the clinicians want a rule that always names a human, counsel wants a rule that names no one absent a breach of a written duty, and I need something I can defend to both. Then run it on three cases: the triage queue above, where the tool was performing within its validated range on a population the validation set did not represent and the radiologist deferred to it as she had been trained to; a case in which an override was locked by contract and the harm fell on a patient; and a third of your choosing in which the answerable party turns out to be this board or the procurement process we ourselves designed years ago — because if none of the three comes out in a way we will not like, the procedure is not doing its job. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do, and where it will misfire. I am not after a survey of the field; I need the artifact.
