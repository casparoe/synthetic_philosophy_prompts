Here is a puzzle about how we actually govern autonomous systems, and I would like the implicit philosophy dug out of the practices rather than the usual policy platitudes.

Two practices now sit side by side in law and regulation. The first is the requirement of meaningful human control. For an autonomous weapon to engage a target, or for a highly automated vehicle to make an irreversible maneuver, a designated human must be positioned to authorize or veto the action, and when something goes wrong it is that human — not the model, not its trainers, not the deploying organization — who is treated as the responsible party. In practice the human is given seconds, partial information, and a system engineered so that agreement is the path of least resistance; the veto is rarely exercised and, in exactly the cases that matter, may be unusable. The second practice is the no-fault compensation scheme. For a growing class of harms — vaccine injuries, certain medical accidents, and now several proposals for automated-driving injuries — we drop the question of fault entirely, spread the cost across a fund, and pay out on proof of injury alone.

What conception of responsibility does the first practice presuppose? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory and then stress-test it. Specifically: does the human-control requirement presuppose a control condition on responsibility — that a person is answerable only for what they could have foreseen and prevented — or does it presuppose instead that responsibility attaches to a role rather than to causal efficacy, so that whoever occupies the chair is answerable whether or not they could have done otherwise? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Given that the human is chosen for the role, trained to defer, and placed behind a veto that the system's own design makes costly to use, is the practice internally coherent, or does it presuppose a kind of agency that the surrounding engineering is designed to erode? Is the coexistence of the two practices a genuine contradiction, or evidence that the human-control requirement is underdetermined among several theories that would each license it?

Finally, assess a concrete reform: replace the veto with a responsibility ledger, in which each deployment records the identity of the last human who had a genuine opportunity to intervene, and liability attaches to that person. Does this close the responsibility gap or merely relocate it? Does it commit us to the view that an opportunity to intervene, rather than actual causal contribution, is the ground of answerability? And say what would have to change in the practice if we adopted, instead, a strict control condition or a purely forward-looking risk-spreading rationale. Distinguish throughout what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, and where it leaves the underlying question open. No citations needed.
