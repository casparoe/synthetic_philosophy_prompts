Our local historical society has given me a room to mount a small exhibition on the archaeology of Troy, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks. The audience will be awkwardly split: a few university archaeologists and classicists, who will wince at every overstatement, and a much larger crowd of members and donors who grew up on the Iliad and have come hoping to be told that the war really happened. After a year of reading I find I can say less than I could when I started. Schliemann's trench cut straight through the mound and destroyed much of what he was looking for; the gold he called Priam's Treasure comes from a layer centuries older than the city the Greeks would have burned; the Hittite records that mention a kingdom in the right place, under a name that may be the same as Ilios, never mention a siege or a horse. Please write the remarks — about five minutes, delivered as a speech rather than a lecture — in which I try to say something defensible about when a story is entitled to move from the legend shelf to the history shelf, a question that recurs for King Arthur and the Exodus as much as for Troy, and I want to end on a verdict of my own rather than a shrug. The Iliad-lovers should go home feeling that the poem has been honoured, not dissected.
