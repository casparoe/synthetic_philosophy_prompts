I am one of four people in a small reading group — mostly philosophy of mind, with one cognitive scientist — that has spent the term on what we have been calling sphexishness: behavior that looks goal-directed and adaptive until the environment is perturbed slightly, whereupon the agent loops back into a fixed routine as if nothing had happened. The stock illustration is a digger wasp that provisions a burrow by dragging a paralyzed cricket to the entrance, leaving it there while she inspects the burrow, and then hauling the cricket inside; move the cricket a few inches while she is underground, and she does not simply carry it in, she restarts the inspection step, and will restart it indefinitely as long as the cricket keeps being moved. The philosophical interest is obviously not the wasp. It is the suggestion that the difference between the wasp and us is one of nesting depth rather than kind — that human flexibility is a stack of heuristics, each perturbable in the right circumstances, so that any of us can be made to look sphexish by the right intervention — and the further suggestion that this bears on free will, on moral responsibility and the excusing force of not being able to do otherwise, and on how we should regard artificial agents that pass for flexible until someone pushes them. My own suspicion is that the notion is currently doing more rhetorical work than theoretical work, but I would rather be argued out of that than confirmed in it.

What I need from you is a working agenda for one philosopher, essentially alone for a semester, with a background in philosophy of mind and enough cognitive science to read empirical papers but no lab, no subjects, and no collaborators. Which of the open questions in this area actually matter most, and in what order should they be taken up? Rank them, and defend the ranking against the obvious criteria: how much other work depends on the answer, how tractable the question is for one person in a semester, whether anyone is already on it, and whether settling it would change anything downstream — in the free will debate, in animal cognition, in how we assess artificial agents, or in ordinary practices of blaming and excusing. Among the candidates I can see are these: whether the wasp anecdote is empirically sound at all, and what becomes of the philosophical uses of the notion if it is not; whether sphexishness names a distinctive phenomenon or is a rebranding of mechanism, compulsion, or stimulus-bound behavior, and whether there is any principled threshold between rigid and flexible rather than a continuum we draw lines on for convenience; whether the excusing force of rigidity survives the observation that humans are never rigid in the wasp's way but are rigid in countless local ways, which would make excuses either ubiquitous or unavailable; whether the concept earns its keep in the animal cognition literature or in debates about artificial agents; and whatever I have failed to think of. I am not asking for a survey of these, and I do not want the answer that everything depends on how the terms are defined.

Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose among: name the one question you would start with, say what you would actually do in the first six weeks, and say what a semester's work would deliver — a distinction, a counterexample, an argument that the notion should be dropped, a criterion, something else — and what would count as the project having failed. Tell me which of the candidates you would deprioritize, including any that look tractable but are really terminological disputes dressed up as substantive ones, and say what evidence or argument over the next year would move a question up or down the list. I would also like your honest self-assessment: where is your recommendation most likely to be wrong, and what would make you abandon it? Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not guess at specific papers or authors — refer to positions and arguments in your own words and I will fill in the references myself.
