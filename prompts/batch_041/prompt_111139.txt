Reading group thing I can't get out of my head. We're doing Politics I, the natural slavery chapters, and Dan (classics guy, very literal about arguments) came in with a reconstruction that he thinks is valid and now can't decide whether to accept. He put it on the board like this:

1. In any composite, the element that can deliberate should govern the element that can't — that's what the good of the composite consists in (soul over body, reason over appetite).
2. There are human beings related to other human beings the way the body is related to the soul: they can take in a plan and carry it out, but can't form one.
3. For any two such parties, it's better for the one who can only follow to be governed by the one who can deliberate than to be left to himself; that's what better means for a composite.
4. Governance exercised for the good of the governed is just, consent or no consent (parents, doctors).
5. So it's just for the first sort of person to govern the second sort.
6. And since a being that can only follow a plan has no ends of its own to protect, governing him just is owning him — so it's just for the first sort of person to own the second sort.

Dan's line is that 1–5 are things anyone would sign up to, 6 follows, and if you want to reject natural slavery you have to give one of them up, and he can't see which. Two of us said 6 is the slide: being governed and being owned are different, and Aristotle himself keeps kinds of rule apart — a master's rule over a slave is one kind among others, and whatever else you say about him, he doesn't think a father owns his children. Dan said that's just relabelling: if the incapacity in 2 is deep enough, ownership is exactly what governance of such a being amounts to, and the multiplicity of kinds of rule is a distinction without a difference. Someone else said 2 is the weak link — it's an empirical claim, and it's definately false of everyone Aristotle would have applied it to. Dan said you can't refute a conceptual argument with an empirical premise, and anyway he's offering a conditional: if there are such people, then slavery is just. Then someone asked why children aren't ownable on this argument, since they can't deliberate either, and Dan said their incapacity is temporary — which felt like a premise he didn't actually have.

So the group stalled and I said I'd try to sort it out, and I'm stuck. What I want is the step to give up, and a price tag for giving it up. I'm not interested in "Aristotle was just rationalising Athenian slavery" — I already believe that and it doesn't tell me whether the argument works. If it's 6, tell me what else goes with it: does the household survive, does the distinction between kinds of rule survive? If it's 2, tell me whether the argument can be restated without it and what the restatement costs. And if Dan is right that it's valid, just say so, and tell me which premise is cheapest to drop.
