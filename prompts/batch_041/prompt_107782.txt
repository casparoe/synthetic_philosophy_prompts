I'm putting together a handout for a comparative-policy reading group — civil servants, a couple of criminologists, one very patient police liaison officer — on how states regulate sex work. Every time we do this it degenerates into a country-by-country recital (Sweden does this, Germany does that, New Zealand does the other) and nobody comes away with anything they can use. So I want it as a decision tree instead: a taxonomy of the positions on 'what should the law do about commercial sex?', built up from the questions that split the positions, not from a list of countries.

Rules I'd like you to follow:

- Every branch node is a question that genuinely separates positions, phrased so a reader could answer it without knowing which country they're being sorted towards.
- Every leaf is a position: give it its usual name where it has one, 'unnamed' where it doesn't, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders have to swallow.
- Some leaves will be countries or regimes, but I don't want the tree to be a map of the world. If two countries land on the same leaf, say so on the leaf rather than inventing a branch to separate them.

The distinctions I want on the tree, because they're the ones that do the work in our arguments and they never survive the country-by-country version:

1. what exactly is criminalised — the sale, the purchase, organising or pimping, advertising, or the premises (the landlord who lets the flat, the hotel that takes the booking);
2. what the offence is meant to protect — the seller, the buyer's character, the neighbours' peace, or the mere existence of the market;
3. whether the regime treats the transaction as work (labour law, tax, health and safety, the right to organise) or as a harm to be shrunk regardless of consent;
4. whether legality varies by venue — street vs indoor vs online — which I think cuts right across the named models, and is where alot of the real policy actually lives;
5. whether the state pays for exit routes (housing, drug treatment, benefits) or only for enforcement, on the theory that a model with no funded exit is just a model with a different arrest target;
6. de jure vs de facto: whether the leaf is a rule on paper or a policing practice, and whether those come apart. Don't fudge this one — if it can't be a branch, say why.

Leaves that have to be in there: the Nordic/Swedish arrangement (buying criminalised, selling not); legalisation with licensing and inspection (Germany/Netherlands territory, but don't lump them together if they don't actually land in the same place); full decriminalisation with regulated third parties (New Zealand); the both-sides-criminalised status quo; and the UK muddle as I understand it — selling itself isn't an offence but soliciting, brothel-keeping and organising are, so correct me if I've got that wrong. Plus the boring option everyone forgets: no special legislation at all, sex work is just work under ordinary contract and employment law. Plus the interesting one — buyer liability that turns on whether the particular transaction was coerced or deceived, which I suspect collapses into one of the others, so tell me where it lands and why. And a leaf for the 'we don't mind what you do, we mind that we can see it' position, where the offence is about visibility and nuisance rather than the transaction. I think that one is genuinely distinct and it always gets lost.

Size: 6–8 branch questions, 8–12 leaves. Also list the answer-combinations that no position occupies, and for each say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's coherent but nobody wants it, or because it's merely unexplored. And flag any pairs of leaves the tree can't separate — I'd rather know than have it papered over.

Give it to me as a JSON object, structured like this:

Where 'path' is the sequence of branch answers that gets you to that leaf, written out as q1: answer, q2: answer, and so on, and 'where_it_actually_runs' is jurisdictions or 'nowhere' if nobody has it. After the JSON, two or three sentences of plain prose on anything you had to flatten to make the tree work.
