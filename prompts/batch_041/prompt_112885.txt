A friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about whether thought experiments in science can yield knowledge, and we got no further than usual: she holds that a thought experiment is an argument in disguise, so it can only tell you what was already implicit in the premises you brought to it, whereas I think the reconstructions are written after the fact with the conclusion already in hand, and that some cases do work no argument could do. On Thursday I am interviewing a philosopher who takes her side of that dispute, and I want questions that press him rather than let him restate the position.

Please draft eight questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I want at least one question on what he does with cases whose reconstruction is only available retrospectively; one on why the vividness of a thought experiment should matter at all if a dry argument would do the same work; one on how he distinguishes a good thought experiment from a merely persuasive one without circularity, that is, without appealing to whether the conclusion is true; and one on what would count against his view.

The last two should turn on a case I invented during the argument. Take a universe containing exactly one particle and nothing else, and a second universe identical to it except that everything in it is displaced one metre to the left. My friend says no imagining is needed: it is a one-line argument from a symmetry premise. My question is where she got that premise, since nothing in the scenario as described supplies it. Ask him, first, to reconstruct the case as an argument, and then to say what becomes of his view if the reconstruction requires a premise that is neither analytic nor contained in the scenario but imported from our best theories, or from a methodological norm about what a theory may distinguish — and whether that import is a cost of his view in particular or of every thought experiment.

Give the result as a table with the columns Question, What it presses on, His most likely answer, Follow-up. Keep each question to three sentences at most. British English throughout.
