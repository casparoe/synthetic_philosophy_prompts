Write a dialogue between two philosophers of causation who are equally competent and neither of whom is a strawman. One holds that omissions can be genuine causes; the other holds that omissions are not causes in the same sense as positive events, but are instead explanatory or normative constructs. Set the dialogue around a hypothetical case, which you should introduce and keep in view. In the case, an automated hospital medication system has two independent safety checks. Either check, if it fires, blocks a dangerous order. The rule engine does not fire because its contraindication rule contains an off-by-one dosage threshold. The anomaly detector does not fire because its training data underrepresented the patient's comorbidity. The order proceeds and the patient is harmed. A later review says the system failed to block the order. The system's logs record only positive events; the omissions are inferred by the reviewers. The omission-causalist argues that each silent check is a cause of the harm: the harm counterfactually depends on each omission, and each omission made a difference. The omission-skeptic argues that there is no event of not-blocking, only the absence of an event, and that calling the silence a cause is either a loose way of speaking or a disguised claim about what the system ought to have done. The dialogue should explore whether the two silent checks are causes, whether they are causes in the same way as the positive events (the order being sent, the drug being administered), and whether the distinction between causing and allowing survives the case. The philosophers should respond to each other's actual arguments. They should discuss counterfactual dependence, causal selection, preemption, double prevention, overdetermination, the role of norms and expectations in identifying omissions, and whether representing omissions as nodes in a causal graph is a modeling convenience or a metaphysical commitment. They should also consider whether the two omissions are symmetrical: one is a failure to apply a rule, the other a failure to represent a feature. They should examine whether tracing the causal chain upstream, to whoever failed to update the rule or curate the training data, makes the proximate omissions causes or merely conduits, and whether that regress trivializes the original causal claim. They should also consider whether an omission is best modeled as a negative event, a non-event, or a relation between a causal model and a normative baseline. The dialogue should be self-contained: do not refer to authors, titles, dates, or reading lists, and do not use quotations. Use the hypothetical case to anchor abstract points. Aim for a polished, rigorous exchange of roughly 1000 to 1500 words.
