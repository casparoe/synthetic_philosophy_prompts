I cover international law and human rights, and I'm working on a piece about "crimes against humanity" -- not the general history, the specific wrinkle in how the category was originally built. Here's what's nagging at me.

The Nuremberg charter gave the tribunal jurisdiction over crimes against humanity, and the way its written, the acts have to be done "in execution of or in connection with any crime within the jurisdiction of the Tribunal." Which means: atrocities a government commits against its own people only count if they're tied to the war. Mass murder inside a country that isn't at war, or isn't tied to the aggression, is apparently not the tribunal's business. The judges seem to have read it that way too -- they said the pre-war persecution of German Jews was outside their reach for exactly that reason, however horrible. (Though I've also seen people argue the punctuation means that clause was only meant to modify the last item in the list, not the whole thing.) Then the same charter turns around and says a defendant's official position, even head of state, is no excuse, and following orders is no excuse. Fine. But nobody ever charged the Japanese emperor, who sat at the top of the same chain of command, and most of the people who did get convicted in both cities were back home within about a decade. Meanwhile the defense's standing complaint at both trials was that waging aggressive war wasn't a crime on the books when they did it.

So I've got things that don't obviously sit together. A tribunal that says the individual has duties outranking the state, yet says what a state does to its own citizens in peacetime is off-limits. A charter that says rank shields nobody, applied in a way that shielded the highest rank and then let the rest out early. And a crime that supposedly always existed but needed a statute to exist. Later instruments dropped the war link entirely -- the modern definition of crimes against humanity doesn't require any connection to a war at all.

What I want from you: reconstruct the view of law this practice presupposes. Where does international criminal law get its authority over an individual -- a natural-law-ish claim that these were always crimes and the charter just recognized them, or a positivist claim that states create these crimes and can only do so where they've actually handed over sovereignty? Was the war link a conceptual limit or only a prudential one, and does the charter's own text settle that or is it genuinely underdetermined? Same question for the immunity of the emperor and the early releases: do those presuppose a retributive account of what the trials were for, or a political one about rebuilding states and fighting the Cold War? Tell me which rival theories the practice quietly rules out, which it merely fits well with, and what would actually have to change in the doctrine if you swapped in the rival view.

One more thing: seperate out what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this material, and don't hand me the victors'-justice reading as if it's the consensus -- I've been burned before by writing up one camp's position as the standard view.
