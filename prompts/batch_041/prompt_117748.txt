Bit of a weird one, but this has been eating at me for a week. I'm two years into a cog sci PhD and my thesis is basically all active inference / free energy stuff, so I live inside that framework now and I've stopped being able to see it from outside. Over the break I went and read a chunk of Helmholtz — the physiological optics book, plus some of the shorter things on perception — partly out of guilt and partly because someone at our reading group said, offhandedly, that the free energy principle is just Helmholtz with more maths, and everyone laughed and moved on, and I couldn't. Half the time I was reading him thinking, yeah, this is the same guy, and half the time thinking, no, he'd have hated all of this. So I want you to do a serious job of predicting what Helmholtz would have thought about the modern predictive processing / free energy program, given a decent explanation of it. Not a vibe, actual positions on the things that are genuinely contested now. Would he see the core claim — perception as inference to the best explanation of the sensory signal, driven by a learned generative model, with only prediction errors propagating up the hierarchy — as his unconscious inference finally made precise, or would he say the formalisation quietly changes the claim? His own line was that sensations are signs of external objects that don't resemble them, so where does he come down in the current fight over whether this framework is the most representationalist theory ever built or the one that finally kills off representation? Would he accept reading attention as precision weighting as a decent reconstruction of the active, selective side of perception he kept insisting on?

Then the harder stuff. The free energy principle comes with a very strong general claim — that any system that goes on existing must be minimising something like surprise, and that you can carve such a system off from its environment with a statistical boundary — and that's meant to cover everything from a bacterium to me typing this. Helmholtz was a hardline anti-vitalist who wanted life explained by physics and chemistry, so does that continuity strike him as the vindication of everything he fought for, or as a category error, or (worse) as a tautology dressed up as a discovery? People do say the whole thing can't be falsified, that it either says nothing or is false. And he'd have had the dark room point put to him: if the only imperative is to minimise surprise, why doesn't the ideal agent just find a dark quiet room and lie there forever? Is his reaction that that's a reductio of the framework, or that we've misdescribed what he was after?

What I really want out of this is the arguments he'd make that I'd find hardest to answer, given his particular commitments — his empiricism about space perception, the long fight he had with the nativists, his insistence that inference has to be learned and grounded in what the body actually does — not generic science-has-moved-on stuff. And be explicit about which parts of this are documented Helmholtz and which are you extrapolating, and where the extrapolation is shakiest. I'd much rather be told he's genuinely underdetermined on something than get a confident answer I can't use.
