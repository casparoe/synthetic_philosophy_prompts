I spent most of last night arguing with a friend of mine — a very sharp software engineer with a good mathematics degree — about the Church–Turing thesis, and I have come away genuinely unsure which of us, if either, was making sense. He has read more on the subject than I have, so what I want from you is not a general tutorial but a careful audit of what he told me.

His case, as best I can reconstruct it, went like this. Turing proved in the 1930s that a machine consisting of an endless tape, a read–write head and a finite table of rules can compute any function that any other computing device whatsoever could compute. This, he said, is a mathematical theorem, established by proof, and it is what people mean by the Church–Turing thesis. Because the proof covers any effective procedure — anything a human being could carry out mechanically by following rules — it follows that the human mind, at least in its rational operations, is a kind of Turing machine, and so thought is computation. Moreover, since the proof establishes that nothing can compute more than a Turing machine can, the thesis rules out the possibility that the brain exploits quantum effects, or anything else, to do work that is not Turing-computable; talk of hypercomputation is therefore confused. He added that Church and Turing arrived at the same result independently in 1936; that the thesis is really a definition rather than a claim about the world, which he thought made it immune to empirical refutation but also, in the end, trivially true; and that the thesis has nothing to say about how long a computation takes, which he treated as a footnote.

Several of those steps seem to me unable to stand together, but I cannot say which, and I do not trust my instincts here. I suspect the definitional point is doing more work than he realises.

Please take the role of a philosopher of mathematics with a logician's training, someone who works on the foundations of computability, and give me an accuracy audit I can act on. Before you write, spend a little time working out for yourself how many genuinely distinct claims are bundled together in what I have reported, since I think the main trouble lies there. Then, in continuous prose, tell me what the thesis actually asserts and what its epistemic status is; go through my friend's claims one by one, marking what a specialist would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong; explain what a defender of the thesis would say in reply to the parts that are wrong; and tell me whether the conclusions he drew — about the mind, about physics, and about the triviality of the thesis — survive once the errors are corrected.

Write in British English, and please use no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables: continuous prose only, as you would in a long email to someone who is interested but not a specialist.
