I have been reading a trade book on the infinite that a friend lent me, and I have come across its chapter on Skolem's paradox, which I am not equipped to judge. The chapter's summary runs roughly like this:

'Skolem's paradox is one of the great scandals of modern logic. Cantor proved that the real numbers are uncountable: there are strictly more of them than there are natural numbers. But the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem tells us that any consistent theory set out in a countable language has a countable model. Set theory is such a theory, so it has a countable model — a structure containing only countably many things, in which every axiom of set theory, and therefore every theorem, comes out true, including the theorem that there are uncountable sets. It follows that one and the same set of real numbers is countable when viewed from one standpoint and uncountable when viewed from another, and that Cantor's result is at bottom an artefact of the language in which we state it rather than a discovery about mathematical reality. Skolem himself drew the moral that the distinction between the countable and the uncountable belongs to our descriptions of the world rather than to the world itself, so that set theory tells us nothing about an actual infinite. The standard resolution of the paradox is simply to observe that countability is not an absolute notion: to say that a set is countable is to say that there is a bijection between it and the natural numbers, and whether such a bijection exists depends on which sets the model contains. There is thus no contradiction after all, only a salutary reminder that a first-order theory can never pin down the size of its own universe.'
