Alright, so I've been going back and forth on David Lewis's essay about penal substitution -- the one where he says we already believe in something like it, because of how we handle fines and sureties and people volunteering to take on someone else's penalty. I want you to argue for a position, and the position is this: that Lewis's secular analogues only support a *deflationary* version of penal substitution, where what gets transferred is a specific penal debt or liability, not guilt or desert, and that once you separate those two things, the doctrine is defensible against the usual objection that guilt can't be transferred -- but it's also much weaker than its traditional defenders need, because the strong version requires that the substitute actually deserve the punishment, or that the punishment fit the substitute's own moral standing, and nothing in Lewis's examples gives you that. So make the case for the deflationary reading, show why it survives the standard objection, and then be honest about whether it's still recognizably the doctrine anyone cares about. At some point, say what the deflationary reading commits you to about what punishment is for -- because I suspect it forces a broadly deterrent or debt-based view of punishment, and thats going to annoy retributivists. I'd also like you to say which part of the deflationary reading is doing the real work, and whether a defender of the strong doctrine could just help themselves to that part without the rest.
