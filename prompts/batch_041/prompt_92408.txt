I run a 75-minute session for sixteen upper-level undergraduates in a course on practical reason, and I want an activity that makes the money-pump argument land in the body rather than the ear. The trigger: over the past month I watched a friend lose about forty pounds in a chain of swaps she accepted one at a time and would have refused as a package — a bike for a kayak, the kayak for a guitar, the guitar for a bike, each step sweetened with a small cash adjustment — and I have since caught the same shape in my own tariff-switching and subscription-hopping. My students can recite that cyclic preferences are exploitable; none of them feels it.

Design an activity, with no real money, that drives participants through a cycle of pairwise trades while a trader collects a small fee at each step, and then has them run it a second time — once with the trader's schedule known in advance, once with a chance to commit to a plan before trading begins. I want them to discover for themselves that the pump bites only the myopic agent, and to leave arguing over whether that shows cyclic preference to be irrational or only shows myopia to be. Give me the materials and the stakes, the timeline, the debrief that maps each moment of the run onto a piece of the argument (including why foreseeing the cycle and committing in advance are not the same fix), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. Participants should not hear the term money pump until the debrief.
