I am a high-school student writing a paper on whether a decision rule can give weight to tiny probabilities of enormous outcomes without being taken over by them. Before I commit to a position, I need the standard of adequacy: what would a rule have to accomplish to count as a satisfactory answer to this problem? Set out the requirements — the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it must preserve, and the theoretical results it must not contradict — and give the consideration behind each one. I want the list to register a distinction I have been worrying about: between an outcome that is improbable because some real chance mechanism makes it so, and an outcome that is improbable only because I have assigned a low credence to a hypothesis about an agent's intentions or about hidden features of the world; a rule that simply discounts small probabilities may treat those two alike, and I would rather have that examined than granted. Tell me which requirements are indispensable and which merely reflect the commitments of one camp, and if the list cannot be satisfied as a whole, say which requirements pull against each other and which single one rules out the most otherwise attractive rules — that would be the most useful part of the answer. Close with a short assessment of how standard expected-value maximization, capping or bounding the value of outcomes, and ignoring probabilities below a fixed threshold each fare against the list.
