Over the years I have kept a running list of my verdicts about bringing new minds into existence, mostly to keep myself honest in arguments, and I would now like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Creating a person who will live a life of unremitting suffering, when not creating them was available: wrong, and not merely worse. Creating a person who will live a very good life: permitted, and I do not think the world is the better for it; if two possible worlds differ only in that one of them contains an extra happy person, I do not think the one with the extra person is better. Declining to create a happy person in order to spend the resources elsewhere: no one is wronged and nothing is lost, and this one I hold firmly. The same three verdicts transposed to minds that are not biological, a conscious program that will be tormented and a conscious program that will be content, come out the same; the substrate does not move me, and duration only scales the magnitude.

Two cases I cannot square with that list.

The forced choice: suppose I have already decided to bring one new person into existence, and the only remaining variable is which one. Depending on the timing, the child will either be healthy and do well or be born with a condition that makes their life go badly. I think I am required to choose the healthy version, and that the world in which I do is better than the world in which I do not. But if creating a happy person is not better than not creating one, and the only bad thing in view is the suffering, why is one of those worlds better? The happiness seems to be doing work I have not licensed it to do.

The mixed life: a person whose life will hold forty good years and five bad ones, net clearly positive. I think it is fine to create them. But on the asymmetry as I have stated it, the good years are not a reason for anything and the bad years are a reason against, so a mixed life ought to be judged exactly as a bad life is, and to me it plainly is not. Either the good parts of a life count for something when it comes to starting it, or they do not, and I keep changing my mind about which verdict to surrender.

There is also a case on which I have no stable verdict at all: the coin flip. I can create a mind that will, by a fair coin, receive either a very good life or a very bad one. I think I should not. I cannot tell whether that is because the bad outcome is bad, which the asymmetry already covers, or because exposing a not-yet-existing someone to risk is itself a harm, which sounds as though it requires the someone to exist already.

My tentative principle is this. The value of a life is a value for the one living it; you cannot confer a benefit on someone who does not yet exist, but you can put someone into a bad state by making them exist in it. Hence there are reasons of the created person's own good against creating bad lives, and no such reasons for creating good ones. Please test it against the list: say which verdicts it delivers, whether it can be repaired to reach the forced-choice case without quietly becoming the ordinary view that simply adds up total welfare, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. If no single principle gets them all, I would rather have two clearly labeled candidates than one blurred one. Then say what the surviving principle predicts for two cases I have not given: one in which creating a single miserable person would prevent ten already-existing people from suffering, and one in which I can create one happy person now and one miserable person later, and the two have to be decided together.
