I've been slowly working through Lucretius's On the Nature of Things in an old verse translation I pulled off one of those public-domain sites (it reads like it was done a century ago — lots of "thou" and "may'st" and lines broken in odd places), and I've gotten stuck at the very beginning of Book 2. Here's the stretch, copied exactly as it sits in my copy:

'Tis sweet, when, down the mighty main, the winds
Roll up its waste of waters, from the land
To watch another's labouring anguish far,
Not that we joyously delight that man
Should thus be smitten, but because 'tis sweet
To mark what evils we ourselves be spared;
'Tis sweet, again, to view the mighty strife
Of armies embattled yonder o'er the plains,
Ourselves no sharers in the peril; but naught
There is more goodly than to hold the high
Serene plateaus, well fortressed by the wise,
Whence thou may'st look below on other men
And see them ev'rywhere wand'ring, all dispersed
In their lone seeking for the road of life;
Rivals in genius, or emulous in rank,
Pressing through days and nights with hugest toil
For summits of power and mastery of the world.
O wretched minds of men! O blinded hearts!
In how great perils, in what darks of life
Are spent the human years, however brief!-
O not to see that nature for herself
Barks after nothing, save that pain keep off,
Disjoined from the body, and that mind enjoy
Delightsome feeling, far from care and fear!
Therefore we see that our corporeal life
Needs little, altogether, and only such
As takes the pain away, and can besides
Strew underneath some number of delights.
More grateful 'tis at times (for nature craves
No artifice nor luxury), if forsooth
There be no golden images of boys
Along the halls, with right hands holding out
The lamps ablaze, the lights for evening feasts,
And if the house doth glitter not with gold
Nor gleam with silver, and to the lyre resound
No fretted and gilded ceilings overhead,
Yet still to lounge with friends in the soft grass
Beside a river of water, underneath
A big tree's boughs, and merrily to refresh
Our frames, with no vast outlay- most of all
If the weather is laughing and the times of the year
Besprinkle the green of the grass around with flowers.

Here's what's tangling me up. I always took Epicurus and Lucretius to be hedonists — pleasure is the highest good, that's the headline. But the line I keep circling is the "barks after nothing" one. Read flatly, nature wants two things: pain kept off the body, and a mind enjoying "delightsome feeling, far from care and fear." The first is purely negative, an absence. The second might be an actual positive feeling with absences stuck onto it, or it might just be the same absence again in a fancier coat. And a friend of mine has a word for this whole family of views — she calls it tranquilism: only the absence of craving and suffering matters, nothing positive has to matter at all, and every apparent good is either the relief of a pain stopping or beside the point. So is this passage signing Lucretius up for tranquilism in her sense, or is "delightsome feeling" doing real work and making it a positive hedonism after all? The two halves of that sentence don't look symetrical to me and I suspect that's not an accident — but I'd rather know whether the translation is flattening something that's clearly distinguished underneath it.

Two smaller things, and then the thing I actually care about. First, "barks" — what's going on with that metaphor? Nature personified as a dog, barking, and barking for *nothing but* those two things. It's such a strange verb to land on, and I can't tell if it's a quirk of this translator or an image I'm meant to see (a dog's wanting being wordless and insistent and small? a dog barking at nothing at all?). Second, the passage sits immediately after the famous shore-and-shipwreck opening, where he's careful to insist it isn't that he enjoys watching people drown, it's that it's sweet to see what you yourself have been spared. I have a nagging feeling the barking line is meant to answer the worry that opening raises — the wise man on the cliff isn't a monster because the only thing he's actually after is the absence of pain — but I cant make the argument close. And the real question, the one that sent me here: if nature barks for nothing but the absence of pain, why are we the kind of creatures who never know what we want? Later in the poem, Book 3 I'm fairly sure, there's the man who storms out of his own house and then turns right around and comes back, and the line about how each of us "flees himself" and can't get away, and how we go on "knowing not what 'tis they want" and just keep changing places. If desire has that natural stopping point — two things, no more — where does all the bottomless wanting come from, and what is the passage at the start of Book 2 actually claiming about it? Walk me through the argument and the metaphor together, please, and tell me what the tranquilist reading gets right and what it misses.
