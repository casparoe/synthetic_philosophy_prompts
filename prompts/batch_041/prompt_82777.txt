I'm on the board of a small heritage trust — we look after a ruined abbey and a hill fort just outside town — and I've got to write the board's decision memo before we meet on the 14th, and I'm stuck. Bit of background: our bid to the National Lottery Heritage Fund for a new visitor centre hinges on us finally sorting out how we describe the founding legend. Short version: a holy man is said to have founded the abbey after a vision, and the town next door says the same bloke founded their church first, and there's been low-level sniping about it for longer than anyone can remember. Our current caption says "according to legend" and everybody hates it. The tourism lot think it's weak and puts people off. Our schools officer says if we drop it, teachers will stop coming because they can't defend it to parents. Two trustees are convinced it's basically true and that we're being cowardly. And the vicar, who is also a trustee, awkwardly, wants us to say the vision "is remembered", which nobody can parse. My lad did Troy and King Arthur at school and apparently it's the exact same argument, so I know I'm not the first person to trip over this, but I don't want a lecture on the Romans or the Ancient World, I want the rule tested.

So what I need is the memo. It should land on a concrete recommendation: what the new caption actually says, what our line is when the other town kicks off in the local paper, who signs it off and by when.

Now here's the half-baked idea I'd like you to take seriously rather than just wave through, because I genuinely don't know if it's clever or rubbish. I keep thinking the whole row is stuck because we're asking one question — did it happen — when there are really three. Did it happen. Did people believe it happened. And did the believing do anything: did it get the abbey built, did it fix the boundary between the two towns, did it win us the old market charter. My rule of thumb is that we're allowed to call something history if we can point to the third one, whatever the answer to the first. I'd rather have a display about what a story did then about whether it's true.

So: tell me if that rule is any good, what it gets wrong, and what the strongest case against it is — I suspect it might let in absolutely anything, since a made-up story can definately have real effects, which is sort of what makes it dangerous. Then turn it into something our staff can apply to a caption, a leaflet and a school worksheet without needing a PhD, with the three seperate cases set out so a volunteer can follow them. About two pages. No references, I'm not academic and neither are the trustees.
