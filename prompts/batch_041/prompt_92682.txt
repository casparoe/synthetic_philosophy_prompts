For three evenings now my friend and I have been arguing about the patients whose hemispheres have been surgically disconnected, and the argument has fallen into a pattern: we agree on every experimental result either of us can state accurately, and then disagree about what those results are results about. I would like a decision tree that maps the positions on the unity of consciousness in these cases, so that we can find the first node at which we actually part company instead of relitigating the experiments for a fourth time.

Build the tree from the space of possible answers first, and only then locate the positions in it, rather than starting from a list of isms and hunting for questions that separate them. Every internal node should be a question about a claim or a case that someone can answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to, and every leaf should carry a descriptive label for the position plus one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. I want the tree to be able to distinguish at least the following, though you should tell me if some of them collapse into each other: that there are two centres of consciousness; that there is one subject whose experiences are simply not jointly available to it; that there is one subject with one stream that switches; that unity comes in degrees and the patient is a partly unified subject; that there is one subject which is neither hemisphere nor the whole brain; that there are two subjects of experience but only one person; that the vocabulary of subjects is a façon de parler the data merely expose; that unity is not a fact about minds at all but something the narrating hemisphere manufactures after the fact; and that what makes a being one is not any fact about experience but its being answerable to a single set of commitments, i.e. a single practical deliberator.

The nodes should turn on questions of this sort, and you should add whatever others the logical space requires: whether unity is a property of experiences, of subjects, or of the relation between them; whether the unity at issue is phenomenal, in the sense that there is something it is like to have two experiences together, or functional, in the sense that information is available to a single system for report and control, or practical, in the sense that it is available to a single reasoner; whether disconnection evidence bears on consciousness at all or only on access and report; and whether unity is all-or-nothing or admits of degrees.

Certain cases should appear as nodes rather than as decoration, and where I describe them loosely, treat the description as the intended case: the standard findings in which information delivered to one hemisphere cannot be reported by the speaking one; the possibility that patients achieve apparent unity of behaviour by using external cues rather than by any inner integration, which would make the two-streams reading an artefact of testing; the disconnection phenomena in which one hand acts against what the speaking hemisphere says it intends; the tendency of the speaking hemisphere to invent a reason for an action it did not initiate; the intact or non-surgically-divided people whose two hands come into conflict; the people who lost a hemisphere early and kept most function; and the ordinary divided-attention case, in which two streams of processing run at once in an undisputed single subject.

Three further requirements. First, note the combinations of answers that no position occupies, and diagnose each: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored? Second, if the tree cannot separate two positions, flag them as a pair and say why no further node does the work, rather than inserting a question that pretends to. Third, locate us: I hold that there are two subjects, and that the appearance of a single one is produced after the fact by the speaking hemisphere's habit of narration; my friend holds that there is one subject all along, and that what the surgery reveals is the degree to which a subject's experiences can fail to be jointly available to it. Put both of us on the tree and tell me the first node at which we split. If we turn out to occupy the same leaf under two descriptions, say so plainly; that would be the most useful thing you could tell me.

Do not give me citations, a reading list, or names and dates you are not sure of. Descriptive labels are better than guessed attributions. I am aiming this at an upper-level undergraduate seminar whose students know the basic findings but not the literature, so keep it to roughly six to nine internal nodes and eight to twelve leaves, and write the node questions in ordinary language.

Finally, invite my disagreement: if the task as I have posed it cannot be done well, if some of the distinctions I am asking the tree to draw are not distinctions at all, if the cases I have named do not actually discriminate between the positions, or if the whole exercise presupposes a notion of unity that is doing illicit work, say so directly instead of producing a tidy diagram. I would rather have a smaller tree that holds than a symmetric one that does not. Tell me which nodes you take to be load-bearing and which you suspect I included mainly to keep the branches balanced.
