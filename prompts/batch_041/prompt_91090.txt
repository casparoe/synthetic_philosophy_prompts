My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the best world is the one with the most people whose lives are barely worth living. He defended it: adding a person whose life contains more good than bad makes the world better, so the ideal world simply has the most such lives in it; when I said no one would choose to live there, he said that was squeamishness about scale, and that every alternative either declares a world worse for adding happy people or ranks a few blissful lives above billions of tolerable ones. I could not say what was wrong with it, so I would rather work it out on paper than argue it again.

Write the FAQ page for the Bureau of Optimal Population: the fictional agency of a world that has fully implemented his position and is entirely serene about it. Give it a masthead with a one-line mission statement, then seven to nine questions with answers, around 700 words, in the voice of a patient spokesperson who has answered each of these a hundred times.

- Deadpan throughout. No narrator, no winking, no irony-signalling punctuation. The document should read as if the agency really wrote it and is proud of it.
- Internal consistency is the whole game: every answer must follow from a single axiology, and the spokesperson must never concede that any person is worse off or that anything of value was given up. Where an answer sounds like a concession, it should turn out to be a re-description. If the agency's official position includes a floor below which a life counts as a cost rather than a benefit, keep that floor barely above zero: the refinement should be visible and immaterial.
- Not a dystopia. There is no coercion and no secret police; the world arrived here through subsidies, incentives, and persuasion, which is precisely what makes it hard to wave away. Set the numbers yourself but keep them concrete and consistent — a population in the tens of billions, an average welfare a short step above the threshold of a life worth living.
- The comedy must come from the content, not from mockery of bureaucracy. The funniest lines should be the ones a population ethicist recognizes as correct consequences of the view. Still, let the world feel inhabited: include at least one practical item, such as how to claim the credit for an additional child.
- Required exchanges: whether anyone is worse off; why we should not prefer fewer, better lives, given the objection that this trades quality for quantity while benefiting no one; the step-by-step thought experiment that leads here — adding people who affect no one else, then evening things out — and why the agency insists every step is harmless; why the agency did not adopt some other axiology, with a correct nod to the impossibility results, on which no view can satisfy all the apparently obvious conditions at once; and at least one question from a hostile visitor.
- Give the objections at their strongest. Quote two or three sentences from a fictional critic — a demographer, a novelist, a former commissioner — in the critic's own words, and answer them as well as the axiology permits. A reader should be able to see where the answer is weakest without the text pointing at it.
- Never use the phrase "repugnant conclusion," never name or quote a real philosopher, and never cite any literature. A specialist should identify the target within the first paragraph; a reader who has never heard of any of this should finish the page able to reconstruct the argument and to see why someone might refuse it.
