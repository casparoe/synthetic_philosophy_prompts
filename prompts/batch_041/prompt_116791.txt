I'm a graduate student in a seminar on the ethics and governance of automated decision-making, and I have spent two weeks arguing with a classmate about an argument she wrote for a short response paper. Her claim is that the responsibility gap is not a gap at all but a verdict: if no one can answer for a machine's particular choices, then the machine may not be deployed. I can follow every step and I cannot accept where it lands. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it, with her numbering.

(1) A person is morally responsible for an outcome only if the outcome is within her control—only if she could have foreseen it and could have prevented it. (2) When a hospital turns on a sepsis-prediction model, or a city turns on an algorithm that decides which buildings get inspected, no one involved can foresee the particular judgments the system will make. Its behavior emerges from training data no one has fully audited and from inputs no one anticipated, and the clinician or inspector who acts on the output cannot reconstruct why it said what it said. (3) So no person is morally responsible for the particular harms such a system causes. (4) We may not impose serious risks on people that no one answers for. (5) So deploying such a system is impermissible. (6) Nothing in steps (1) through (3) turns on the decision-maker being a machine. A physician who prescribes a drug whose rare interaction no one had documented, a captain who delegates a maneuver to a subordinate who then improvises, and a parent who hands a teenager the car keys all fail the same foresee-and-prevent test with respect to the particular outcome—not merely in practice but in principle, since the specific downstream act is not theirs to determine. (7) So those are impermissible too. (8) But (7) is absurd, and its absurdity cannot be quarantined, because the premise doing the work, (1), is the premise we ordinarily rely on to block blame in the first place.

I would rather not land on (7), and I would rather not surrender (1), but I do not see how to keep both. So tell me which step to reject: name it, explain why it looked innocent, and then price the repair. If we keep (1) in the strong form stated, what else has to go? If we keep the verdict about machines but refuse the generalization to physicians, captains and parents, what distinguishes the cases without special pleading? And if the real fix is to stop hunting for a culpable individual and let the harm be answered for by something like a no-fault compensation scheme, tell me whether that satisfies the argument or merely changes the subject. I am less interested in a verdict on autonomous systems than in knowing what I would have to give up.
