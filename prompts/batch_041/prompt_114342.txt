As a professor of philosophy, I would like your help thinking through a situation that has unsettled me, both personally and pedagogically. Please take on the role of an expert in the theory of knowledge — someone at home in the classic puzzles about evidence, certainty, and rational belief — and work through what follows carefully and without rushing to the first tidy answer. Begin by brainstorming: sketch the several ways the reasoning I describe might be reconstructed, and mark where those reconstructions come apart, before you commit to a recommendation.

The situation. A close friend of mine, a historian whose judgment I deeply respect, has spent more than a decade establishing that a particular medieval charter is authentic; her book rests on it. Last month a younger scholar showed her a laboratory report indicating that the parchment and ink date several centuries later than the document purports to be. I asked her how she intended to respond. She said: "I know the charter is genuine. I didn't guess and I didn't take anyone's word for it — I know it. And if I know it, then anything that seems to count against it is, by that very fact, misleading. That isn't arrogance; it's just what knowing comes to. So the report must be flawed, and I am under no obligation to drop everything and hunt for the flaw. In fact, the same point tells against going looking for counter-evidence at all: if I know it, then whatever I turn up will be something I am entitled to set aside."

Part of me wants to reply that she is confusing confidence with knowledge, or letting devotion to her own thesis do her arguing for her. But another part cannot locate the error in what she actually said — and that troubles me, because the principle appears to license a kind of willful deafness I would never tolerate in my own students.

What I want from you is not a verdict on her character but an analysis of the move she is making, followed by advice I can act on. Specifically: Is the inference from "I know it" to "whatever counts against it is misleading" valid, and if so, in what sense? Can it be valid as a claim about the evidence while being useless — or worse, self-undermining — as a guide to what she should now do? What does she stand to lose by insisting on the principle, and does anything about her situation, as against a student's, change the answer? Then give me something I can actually say to her: a concrete recommendation about how she should treat the report, and an honest description of her position, allowing that she may be right about the charter and the report may be worthless. Close by naming the further piece of information that, if I could obtain it, would most cleanly settle which of your competing diagnoses is correct.
