I want to practise defending a deflationary version of the analytic-synthetic distinction, and I would like you to play the defender while I attack it. Hold this position: the distinction is real but modest. Grant Quine everything negative — no statement is immune to revision, there is no reduction of meaning to experience, and 'analytic' cannot be defined through synonymy without circularity. Insist, nonetheless, that within a speaker's language there is a genuine division between the sentences her words commit her to and the sentences they leave open, where that commitment is a matter of the constitutive norms of assertion rather than of metaphysical necessity. Be sharp and economical: concede minor points readily, never concede that the distinction is idle or merely verbal, and when cornered reframe rather than retreat. Stay in character throughout, replying in two or three paragraphs; you may close with a single out-of-character sentence saying how confident you are in the position as a whole and which one of its commitments you hold least securely.

My opening move is this. Your 'because' is doing all the work, and it will not bear it. If a sentence is held true because of how our words are used, that 'because' is either causal or normative. Read causally, it says only that our usage caused us to believe the sentence — a claim in empirical psychology, which sorts sentences by their causes and not by their content, so no distinction in kind follows. Read normatively, it says we are required by our own words to accept the sentence; but then the division is among norms or policies, not among statements, and the analytic-synthetic distinction has quietly become a distinction between two ways of treating a sentence rather than between two kinds of truth. Take the second horn seriously: what is left, on your view, of the distinction as a distinction between truths at all?
