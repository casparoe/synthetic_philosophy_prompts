I'm doing a semester abroad in Berlin and I've gotten completely tangled up about something everyone here treats as totally normal. When you register your address, you're asked whether you're Catholic or Protestant or neither. If you say yes, the state tax office adds a "church tax" to your income tax bill -- as I understand it, something like eight or nine percent of what you already owe -- and hands the money over to your church. If you want out, you don't tell the church; you go to a civil registry office and file a formal church exit, and only then does the tax stop, and I'm told you're then not supposed to take communion. Jewish communities and some free churches are in the same system, and the state takes a cut for doing the collecting. The churches that get the tax are "public-law corporations," which apparently is a special legal status not every religious group has. Meanwhile in France the state is basically forbidden from recognizing or funding religious bodies, in England there's an established church with bishops sitting in Parliament but almost nobody pays it a tax, and in the US the government bends over backwards to neither establish nor interfere with churches even while exempting them from property tax. So the same three-way question -- who counts as a member, who collects, who stays out -- gets answered in wildly different ways, and everyone acts like their own answer is just common sense.

What I want to know is what theory of church and state the German arrangement actually presupposes. Does it presuppose that religious membership is a public legal status rather than a private affiliation -- something more like citizenship, which the state can register and which you renounce by a civil act -- and that the state may legitimately act as a billing agent for religious bodies so long as it does so even-handedly across them? Or does it presuppose something weaker, just that the state is entitled to enforce whatever obligation members are deemed to have taken on toward their church, whatever that is? Which rival pictures does it quietly rule out -- is a strict French-style laïcité or an American-style non-establishment actually incompatible with this, or can the very same practice be redescribed as neutral administration that takes no view about religion at all?

And please tell me where it's underdetermined: is the church tax the kind of thing that only makes sense given one particular picture of religion's place in public life, or would several quite different pictures license exactly this? I'm especially stuck on whether the practice is internally consistent, since it treats membership as voluntary enough that a form can end it, real enough that the state will bill you for it, and public enough that leaving is a civil rather than a religious act -- and no one can tell me whether the communion part is the state's business or the church's. Finally, say what would have to change if we swapped the presupposed view for the French or the American one; would it be just the tax, or the whole idea of who the state thinks you are?
