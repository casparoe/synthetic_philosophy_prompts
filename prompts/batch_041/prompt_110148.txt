I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on free will and moral responsibility — twelve students, one ninety-minute session a week — and I've been given the session on Frankfurt's 1969 counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities. I want to run it as an activity rather than as a discussion, so I'd like you to design that activity in full: rules, roles, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief. The thing I want people to feel in their hands is the specific shape of the discomfort: that we seem to keep holding an agent responsible for what he did even after we have stipulated that he could not have done otherwise, and that this verdict holds up only for as long as nobody presses on what exactly made the alternative unavailable. Nobody should hear the labels or the names until the debrief; they should build the problem themselves and only then find out it has a standard anatomy. Also, I don't want a lecture disguised as a game — the rules themselves should do the work, so please tell me explicitly which rule produces which piece of the experience.

My rough idea, which you should feel free to improve or replace, is a responsibility review panel. Panels of three or four judge a series of cases that are all, underneath, the same fact pattern, differing only in how the agent's alternative was closed off. Before anything is revealed, each panellist writes a verdict and a confidence number and a one-line reason on a sheet; then the facilitator reveals the hidden fact and they write again; the sheets are compared across rounds, and the individual-then-collective ordering is important because I want them to watch their own confidence move rather than settle into a group position. I'd like roughly five rounds. First, a plain case where the alternative really was open, so the panel establishes a baseline. Second, the same case with a hidden intervener who would have forced the agent's hand but never had to, because the agent did it on his own — the removal of the alternative is purely counterfactual, and I want them to notice how little their blame depended on it. Third, a variant where the intervener's trigger is a reliable sign of what the agent is about to decide, so the fact that nothing had to be forced is itself somehow prefigured; this is the round I care most about, because I want the room to feel the two-horned squeeze for themselves — if the sign is merely a correlation, the agent's alternative looks untouched and the case proves nothing, and if the sign is more than a correlation, then the agent's decision was already fixed, which is precisely what a critic will say the case has to assume in order to work at all. Fourth, a case where the agent himself arranged the inevitability in advance, so the panel has to decide whether it changes anything that the obstacle was his own doing. Fifth, an omission case, where what the agent is blamed for is something he failed to do and the backup would have made him fail at it regardless. Whatever the panel reaches for to save its verdicts — that he could at least have deliberated, or tried, or wanted otherwise — let it happen and let the debrief name it, because that residual-alternative move is one of the things I want them to catch themselves making. For stakes, I'd rather not use real money; something small and real will do, e.g. the panel's verdict in each round decides which of two sealed envelopes the group opens, one of which contains something the group wants, so the decision has a visible consequence without anyone being punished.

Finally, the debrief has to do four things, and please write it out. It should map each moment of the activity onto its counterpart: the principle of alternative possibilities, the counterfactual intervener, the prior-sign version and the dilemma about whether the connection between the agent's inclination and his decision is deterministic or not, the residual-alternative or "flicker" response, self-arranged inevitability, and omission cases. It should tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — I'm expecting at least one person to argue that the intervener's presence in the background taints the agent's freedom even though he never acted, one person to litigate the mechanics of the device instead of the principle, one person to ask whether determinism is actually true and try to take the session there, and general suspicion once the pattern across rounds becomes obvious. It should be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I therefore have to supply in my own voice: in the activity the absence of alternatives is a stipulated fiction rather than a feature of the actual causal order, so nothing the panel feels licenses any conclusion about determinism, and the position of someone who denies that these cases are even possible as described — who insists that if the connection between inclination and decision is left open then the agent did have an alternative, and if it is closed then the case simply assumes what it was supposed to prove — cannot be lived through in the room and has to be stated. And it should tell me how to close, in a way that leaves the disagreement open rather than settling it, since the session is meant to make the problem vivid, not to hand them a verdict.
