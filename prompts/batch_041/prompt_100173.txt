You're a philosopher who works on the nature/nurture debate and on what social policies quietly assume about human nature — so please think carefully here, and maybe start by brainstorming what a programme like the one below would have to believe about where career preferences come from before you commit to an answer.

Here's the practice I want you to look at. My old department sits in the science faculty of a big public university and runs an equality-in-science programme. It does outreach aimed at schoolgirls, it has a target of getting the share of women in its physics and engineering intakes up to 40%, and the annual report scores each department on that number. It never tracks the share of men in nursing or primary teaching — those are in other faculties, fine, but the same office also never mentions them as a comparison point. Meanwhile the numbers are a bit odd. Our share of women in physics crept up, but the countries that top the international gender-equality rankings often have *smaller* shares of women among physics and engineering graduates than countries with much worse records on women's rights and labour-force participation. (I think this is called the gender-equality paradox?)

What I'd like from you: what does a programme like ours presuppose about the causes of gendered career choice? Does it have to assume those choices are basically produced by culture and constraints, so that once culture and constraints are sorted the gap should shrink to nothing? Or could the exact same programme be run by someone who thinks a decent chunk of the difference is innate, or at least not culture-driven? Which views does the practice actually rule out, and which ones does it merely sit comfortably with? And the part that puzzles me: if more equality comes with bigger gaps in some fields, is the programme's own logic undermined, or can it be re-described — equality of opportunity simply producing a wider spread of expressed preferences — without changing anything it does? Last thing: tell me what would really have to change in the practice if you dropped the assumption that the remaining gaps are just residual discrimination.
