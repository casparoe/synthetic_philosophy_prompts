My friend Dana and I spent most of last night arguing about whether it can ever be rational to choose something when you can't possibly know what it will be like to have chosen it, and I think I lost. She'd just read L.A. Paul's book on transformative experience and came away with the whole apparatus: some choices are epistemically transformative, meaning you can't know what the experience is like until you're in it, so you can't even rank the outcomes against each other; some are personally transformative, meaning they change what you care about, which raises the nasty question of whose preferences you're supposed to be maximising, the ones you have now or the ones you'll have after; and her own resolution, which as far as I can tell is that when the whole value of a thing just is in finding out what it's like, the sensible question stops being is this good for me and becomes do I want to be the kind of person who finds out. Dana thinks that's the end of the story, that every big choice is a leap and the leap is all there is. I think that's a cop-out, because people obviously leap better and worse, and anyway I'm the one who has to sit in the room with them while they do it.

Here's my situation. I'm on a small decision-support team attached to a hospital ethics center; we started out doing consults on irreversible-ish elective stuff (an adult weighing a cochlear implant after thirty years of progressive hearing loss, someone with treatment-resistant OCD weighing deep brain stimulation and terrified that the person who wakes up won't be them) but we get referrals from all over now, including things that aren't medical at all — a woman deciding whether to enter a monastery, a guy deciding whether to have a first kid at 44. I get maybe eight of these a week, forty-five minutes each, one sitting plus sometimes a follow-up, and the people doing most of them are volunteers with no philosophy background. We have to be non-directive, we have to write a note afterwards that would survive a complaint, and the person is often either in distress or has already decided and wants me to bless it. What I need is an actual procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops and the answer is no (or where it stops and the honest answer is that this isn't the right consult), where it hands the decision back because the remaining question is a values question and not mine to answer, and who has to sign off before we're done. And it has to be specific enough that two of us running it on the same case land in the same place — I don't want another worksheet that says explore your values.

The part I can't solve on my own, and the reason I'm asking rather than just making a checklist, is testimony. Everyone's actual method is to go find people who've been through it and ask them, and I've watched that method fall apart in every direction: the ones who regret it go quiet, the ones who don't need to believe they made the right call, the community you'd be leaving is hostile to the people who left and the community you'd be joining is starry-eyed about it, and the person sitting in front of me is talking to a future self who doesn't exist yet. Also, honestly, some of these people can't tell me anything useful about their current preferences at all, because the thing they're deciding about is whether those preferences should be replaced. So the procedure needs to say what to do with testimony given all that, and it needs to handle the four cases that keep eating my afternoons: the one who says I just know, the one who wants permission, the one whose depression is doing the talking, and the one for whom there is no one to ask. Then run it on two or three of the cases above, including at least one where it gives an answer I won't like, and tell me straight what the procedure can't do — including which of its steps are just judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.
