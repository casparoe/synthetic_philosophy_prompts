I am a novelist, and next month I am on a panel at a literary festival defending the thesis that qualia—understood as intrinsic, ineffable, private, non-relational properties of experience—are an incoherent posit, and that first-person writing can be explained without them. The audience will be mostly literary novelists and memoirists with strong phenomenological sympathies, plus a few cognitive scientists and one Buddhist contemplative who will press on whether my critique is just the no-self doctrine in analytic dress. They will not care about technical literature; they will care about authenticity, first-person authority, the difference between pain and the description of pain, and whether my view insults lived experience. I want to center my talk on a new intuition pump I have written: a novelist revises a grief scene for years; she says the new version feels truer, her editor says only the punctuation changed, and she eventually cannot tell whether her grief changed, her sensitivity changed, or only her narrative framing changed. You are a philosopher of mind and an experienced panel respondent. Think carefully about this audience's commitments before writing; brainstorm the objections first, then give me a Q&A prep sheet: the six or so questions I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a novelist or memoirist would phrase it, with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should concede up front versus hold. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not give me citations or reading lists; keep it to what can be said aloud.
