I have recently taken on work that requires me to make explicit expected-value judgments about very low-probability, very high-consequence risks, and a colleague who runs a forecasting blog circulated a short post on Pascal's mugging to argue that our whole approach is unworkable. His post reads roughly as follows. "Pascal's mugging is a version of Pascal's wager. A stranger threatens you with an unimaginably long torture unless you hand over your wallet, but he can only offer a tiny probability that he is able to carry the threat out. Because the expected disvalue of the torture is enormous, even a minuscule probability makes compliance the rational choice, so you must hand over the wallet — or accept the wager. The lesson is that Bayesian decision theory is self-undermining: any theory that tells you to maximize expected value can be held hostage by anyone who can name a big enough number. The standard fix, and the one I recommend, is a probability floor — a rule that assigns zero weight to any probability below some fixed threshold, so that far-fetched threats can be dismissed outright. Note also that the mugging is just the wager with the theology stripped out; the two stand or fall together, so anyone who thinks the wager is a bad argument has already given up the mugging."

I would like to know how much of this I can trust before I repeat any of it in a meeting. Please go through it essentially sentence by sentence: tell me what someone who works on decision-making under Pascalian probabilities would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. In particular, I want to know whether his concluding claims — that the mugging and the wager stand or fall together, and that a probability floor is the standard fix — follow from anything true in the rest of the post, and what a defensible version of his conclusion, if any, would look like. Please also say how confident you are in each of your claims and single out the one point you are least sure about.
