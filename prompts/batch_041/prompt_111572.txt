I've been chewing over that Bernard Williams complaint — the one about the man who pulls his wife out of the water and then, because he's a good utilitarian, adds that he'd have saved whoever was drowning. Williams says he's had a thought he didn't need to have, and that a morality which makes him have it is eating into him, or words to that effect. (I'm not academic, I just read about it somewhere and it's stuck with me.)

Anyway, I want a satire on the whole moral self-improvement racket that grows out of that demand — the idea that you should be able to give an impartial reason for every partial thing you do. Write it as a deadpan newspaper advice column, call it something like Ask the Impartial Adviser, about 550 words. Include a reader's letter from a woman whose husband hauled her out of a canal and then told her he'd have done it for anyone, and make the adviser's reply take his side in the worst possible way: she agrees the rescue was right and then treats the real failure as bookkeeping — he should have had his reasons ready, in writing, and been able to show he'd have saved a stranger too, not merely felt it. End with her prescribing something concrete for the couple to do, a bit like a modest proposal, following the logic somewhere the reader will wince at. Throw in two or three short quick-fire questions from other readers and a sidebar called The Three Thoughts You're Allowed To Have. The adviser must never doubt impartiality itself, only the sloppiness with which people carry it out, and she mustn't wink at the reader or say anything like and of course that's absurd — the joke should be legible to someone who knows the objection, not just to me. Could you do it in British English please.
