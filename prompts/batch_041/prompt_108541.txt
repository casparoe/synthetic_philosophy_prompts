A friend and I spent most of Saturday arguing about a game she built to illustrate something about backward induction, and we got nowhere, so I am hoping you can adjudicate. Please answer in American English.

The game. Two players, A and B, alternate moves for at most N nodes, with A moving first. At each node, the player whose turn it is chooses TAKE or PASS. TAKE ends the game immediately: the taker receives three times the node number, and the other player receives one less than the node number, so a TAKE at node k pays the taker 3k and the other player k − 1. If both players PASS at every one of the N nodes, the game ends and each receives 2.5N. Each player cares only about her own final payoff.

Her argument:

(1) A player is instrumentally rational just in case, at every node where she moves, she chooses the action that maximizes her own eventual payoff given her beliefs about how play would continue after each available action.

(2) It is common knowledge between the players, and remains common knowledge at every node, that both are instrumentally rational.

(3) Rational expectations are sticky: a player who is rational, and who knows the other player is rational, keeps being rational and keeps expecting rational play even at nodes that could only have been reached if the other player had already done something irrational.

(4) Node N is B's move. TAKE pays her 3N and PASS pays her 2.5N, so she takes.

(5) Node N − 1 is A's move. By (2) and (3), A is certain that if she passes, B will take at node N, leaving A with N − 1; if A takes, she gets 3(N − 1). So A takes.

(6) The same comparison recurs at every node: by (2) and (3), the player to move knows the other will take at the next node, so taking now (3k) beats passing and being taken (k). By induction down to the first node, A takes at node 1.

(7) So the uniquely rational outcome is that A takes at node 1, for payoffs of 3 to A and 0 to B.

(8) Nothing in the induction used the number of nodes. Set N at a thousand and the mutual-pass payoff at 2,500: the same steps run, and rationality still takes three dollars on the first move, even though passing all the way would pay each player 2,500.

(9) She says the same backward-induction style of reasoning shows that rational players defect on the first move of any finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, and that no partnership with a known final period can sustain cooperation.

I can accept (1), and I can accept the game exactly as described; those are the two things I most want to keep. What I cannot stomach is (9) — that a rational player must grab three dollars on the first move of a game whose cooperative path pays each player 2,500, and that no increase in what is at stake can make the theory hesitate. Yet when I try to say where the argument goes wrong, every step seems to follow from the one before it, and I cannot find the loose joint. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up actually cost me? If you think no step should be given up, then tell me plainly what I am committing to by keeping all of them, and why the thing I find intolerable is not in fact a defect.
