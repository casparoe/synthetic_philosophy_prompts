My country votes next month on whether to end its administration of an island group several thousand miles away. The islanders have their own assembly, but we conduct their defence and foreign affairs, our citizens may trade and settle there on terms nobody else is offered, and for as long as anyone can remember the territory has been a net charge on the treasury — the garrison and the naval station cost more each year than the islands produce. My brother, who has gone through the accounts, says the arrangement has always been a bad bargain: a narrow circle of merchants and shipping families do very well out of the privileged access, everyone else pays for the fleet, and the islanders would be better off running their own affairs. He adds that nobody in the capital will say so out loud, because the people who administer the place get a great deal of consequence out of being masters of something so distant. I suspect he is right about the numbers and wrong about the rest: deserting people who have come to depend on us looks less like thrift than betrayal, and the islands sit beside a sea lane we would not want a rival to control. If there is a classic treatment of my brother's argument, tell me what it actually claims, where it breaks down, and what it would have me do at the referendum.
