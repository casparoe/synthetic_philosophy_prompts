I teach a fortnightly session in a philosophy of mind reading group whose members are a mixed bag — two mathematicians, three philosophers, and a linguist who has never seen a formal proof — and in a fortnight it falls to me to cover the Lucas–Penrose argument that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the mind cannot be a machine. I do not want an essay defending or attacking the argument; I want a teaching plan for the ninety minutes, because I have taught it once before and watched it go wrong: the mathematicians treated the Gödel sentence as a party trick, the philosophers came away convinced that "Gödel proved the mind isn't a computer", and the linguist stopped following somewhere in the middle and never said so.

So please plan the explanation for me. What has to be in place before the argument can even be stated — and in what order, so that each step is seen to depend on the one before — such that the moment the conclusion is drawn feels like the natural end of a chain rather than a leap? I have been toying with carrying the whole session on a single device that I have invented and would like you to assess and, if it is serviceable, to improve: two players, Mechanist and Anti-Mechanist, take turns. Mechanist offers a formal system and claims it captures everything Anti-Mechanist can know; Anti-Mechanist writes down the Gödel sentence of that system, which the system cannot prove but which she can see to be true; Mechanist absorbs the sentence as a new axiom and offers the enlarged system; Anti-Mechanist writes down its Gödel sentence; and so on up an endless staircase. Tell me what this example must have if it is to do the work, and what it must lack, where exactly it breaks, and whether a better carrying case exists — a Turing machine that prints its own unprovability, say, or the consistency statement itself — and if so, which and why. I would far rather the example fail instructively in front of them than be merely tidy.

Then the misconceptions, which I want mapped to positions in the sequence rather than merely listed: at which point in the plan should I head off "Gödel showed that mathematics is incomplete, or worse, inconsistent", "the theorem is a claim about what machines can do", "since I can see that the Gödel sentence is true, I am not a machine", "if I were a machine I could simply add the sentence as an axiom and carry on", and "the argument only fails because Penrose is a Platonist" — plus whatever else you predict this particular audience will form. For each, say what the misconception is a mishearing of and what the minimum repair is. Include one question I can put to the group at the end that would genuinely distinguish understanding the argument from being able to recite its conclusion, and tell me what I should explicitly refuse to settle in the session — whether the mind is a machine, whether Gödel's theorem bears on that question at all, whether Penrose's other arguments add anything — and how to refuse without leaving the group feeling cheated. Answer in British English, and keep the mathematics no heavier than it has to be for the linguist to stay aboard.
