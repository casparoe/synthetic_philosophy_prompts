Hi — I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm putting together a set of writing prompts for a side project. I need one from you.

Role: take the part of someone who knows decision theory and game theory inside out — the kind of person who could teach a grad seminar but also writes good literary fiction. Before you write a word of the story, think carefully and brainstorm out loud: give me a handful of different ways this could go (different settings, different central gimmicks, at least two possible endings), say which one has the most bite and why, and only then write that one. I want to see the thinking, not just the polished thing.

The idea I want the story to carry is the common prior assumption. Quick version from my amateur understanding so we're on the same page: in alot of econ and game theory you model two people who disagree as people who started from the exact same beliefs about how the world probably works and only diverged because they saw different private evidence. Same prior, different signals. What I find wild about that assumption is how much it rules out. If two people genuinely share a prior and it's out in the open that each has looked at the evidence and reached their own confidence level, they can't just keep disagreeing — somebody has to move. And the market version: if everyone shares a prior and only differs by private info, a purely speculative bet has no taker, because the mere fact that you're willing to take the other side of my bet is itself information to me.

So: write me a short story, roughly 1500–2500 words, that puts a reader inside that. My rough sketch, which you should throw away if you find something better: a small company runs an internal prediction market — people bet on whether the launch slips, whether the big client churns, that kind of thing — and the whole thing is built on a shared model where everyone's beliefs start from the same base rates. Then one engineer notices two colleagues making a bet that, given the assumptions baked into the system, shouldn't be able to happen at all. Figuring out which assumption is actually broken — is it the shared prior, or is it that each knows what the other concluded but not that the other knows they know, or is it something dumber and more human — is the story.

Requirements:
- The idea has to live in what happens, not in what anyone says. No character explaining the theorem to another character. No lectures, no jargon dumps.
- Dont name any economists or papers, no citations, no reading list, no bibliography. I don't want the story leaning on names.
- Ordinary prose, real people, stakes that feel small and local — not a parable with the moral stapled on.
- End on something the reader has to sit with, not a tidy reveal.

Then, after the story, a short note — 3 or 4 sentences, not an essay — on which part of the concept the story actually leans on, and which part it can't show and just has to assert.
