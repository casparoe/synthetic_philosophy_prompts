A few of us have wangled three years of funding for a small project on self-amending systems — four people, deliberately mixed: a legal philosopher, someone who actually drafts constitutions for a living (comparative constitutional law, mostly post-conflict charters), a logician who cares about self-reference and fixed points, and an engineer who builds systems that rewrite their own configuration at runtime. Our common object is the family of cases where a rule-set contains the rules for changing the rule-set. That includes Suber's Nomic from 1982, the game where changing the rules is itself a move, but also eternity clauses and basic-structure review in real constitutions, charters that specify how they may be rewritten, corporate bylaws, DAO governance, and now the design of a constitution for an AI system that's allowed to edit it's own rules. The reading group has already stalled in a predictable way, which is why I'm asking: half of us think the core issue is the logical one — a norm that governs its own revision, the legal cousin of the paradox of the stone, or of the question whether a sovereign can bind its future self or is simply sovereign afresh at every moment — while the other half thinks that's a pseudo-problem that dissolves once you're careful about levels (the amending rule and the amended rule aren't the same object, and the act of amendment is a different sort of thing from the rule amended), and that the real work is institutional: what actually makes a self-amending system stable, what entrenchment buys you and what it merely delays, who is entitled to rule on whether an amendment was validly made when the umpire's own authority is itself one of the things up for amendment, and whether the honest answer to "what if the amendment power is used to abolish the amendment power?" is a paradox or just a fact about power.

So if this were your three years, which open questions would you put first, and why? I don't want a survey or a reading list — I want a defended ordering, with the criteria made explicit: which questions are upstream of which, which are answerable with conceptual tools by four people rather than needing a research program, which look deep but wouldn't change anything downstream if they got settled, and where a mixed group has a comparative advantage over a single-discipline one. Say too what would move something up or down the list over the next year or two — a result, a case, a piece of engineering practice. Cap the whole reply at 800 words, and please lead with the ordering itself in three or four sentences before you defend it.
