I'm a journalist who covers ideas — science mostly, but I wander into politics and philosophy when something's good enough — and I'm working on a piece about the debt-ceiling standoffs, those recurring fights where Congress threatens to default unless it gets the spending cuts it wants. The frame I want to use is the Ulysses contract: Odysseus lashed to the mast so he can't act on the Sirens' song, which political theorists are forever holding up as the model for constitutional precommitment — a cool-headed you binding a future you so the hot-headed one can't do something dumb later. My problem is the debt ceiling doesn't look like that at all. Nobody tied himself to the mast; one faction ties a later majority, and the whole thing isnt about weakness of will, it's about extracting concessions. So take the standoffs as the episode and the Ulysses contract as the theory and run it both ways. First: what does the theory actually say is going on, and which bits of the standoffs does it really capture — the calm-moment-versus-crisis-moment logic, the entrenchment, the strange fact that the threat is never actually carried out? Then where does it break — in particular, is a contract you make that binds a *different* future you even a Ulysses contract, or is it just a hostage situation with better branding? And the other direction: does the debt ceiling expose something the theory quietly gets wrong, like that self-binding and binding-others have been conflated all along?
