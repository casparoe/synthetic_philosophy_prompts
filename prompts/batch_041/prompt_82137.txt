I have been trying to work out, on my own and without any training in the subject, whether it means anything to say that history bends toward justice, and I have reached a point where I am fairly sure the confusion is mine but cannot see where it lies. I would be grateful if you would tell me which of my assumptions to give up rather than answering the question as I have posed it.

I have been sorting processes into two kinds. Some drift: they go somewhere, and may even go reliably in one direction, without the destination playing any role in explaining the motion — a river silting up, a gambler's bankroll under a house edge. Others aim: here the end is doing real work in the explanation, so that you cannot say what the process is without saying what it is for. I take it that aim, understood properly, means the destination reaches back and governs the steps — the future pulling the present, or a designer arranging the present with the future in view — and that this is what providence has always meant.

The difficulty is this. I can sketch a drift story about moral progress: expanding circles of cooperation, literacy, cheap information, the declining usefulness of violence, with nothing at the top and nothing at the end. My instinct is that if that story is roughly right, the claim about history and justice is refuted. A friend tells me it is the reverse: a mechanism that reliably carries us toward justice is exactly what the claim asserts, and all I have refuted is the version that needs a person at the top. I cannot tell whether I have killed the thesis or proved it.

Two things make it worse. First, I do not see how any evidence about trends could distinguish a very strong drift from an aim, since the drift story, if it works, predicts everything the aim story predicts; that would make the aim hypothesis neither true nor false, which cannot be right, and I do not know what mistake I am making. Second, everyone I argue with treats the sentence as one claim with one truth value, while it seems to me to be several claims wearing one coat: a prediction, a description of a mechanism, an assertion about what is owed to the wronged, and a commitment about what the speaker will do. If that is right, then asking whether it is true may be like asking whether a promise is true.

So which is it — is my drift-and-aim division the wrong way to cut the cake, or is my account of what aiming amounts to the thing that has gone wrong, or am I wrong that a sentence can be doing several jobs at once? And after I have given up whatever needs giving up, which of my questions remain live, and which were only artifacts of the mistake?
