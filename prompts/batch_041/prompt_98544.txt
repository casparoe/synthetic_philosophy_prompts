I serve on a hospital ethics committee that has, for several years, tried to reason in a broadly Scanlonian contractualist spirit: we ask whether a proposed policy could be reasonably rejected by any individual, rather than whether it maximizes total welfare. The aggregation problem has never been resolved, and our precedents now pull in different directions. Here they are.

(1) We approved a protocol that exposed one participant to a small, measurable risk of a transient, non-serious side effect in order to produce a modest benefit for several thousand others. We reasoned that the risk fell below the level of a complaint that a reasonable person could not accept for that benefit.

(2) We rejected a protocol that would have imposed a permanent, disabling harm on one participant in order to produce a large aggregate benefit for many others. We reasoned that the one could reasonably reject being used in that way, and that the numbers did not answer her complaint.

(3) We approved a protocol that imposed a moderate, temporary burden on a small group in order to avert a catastrophic outcome for a much larger group. We reasoned that each member of the larger group had a serious complaint against the catastrophe, that the small group's burden was real but not unreasonable to accept by comparison, and that the alternative was reasonably rejectable by the larger group.

(4) We rejected a protocol that imposed a trivial inconvenience on a very large number of people in order to confer a substantial benefit on a small number of people. We reasoned that the many could reasonably reject being made to bear even a trivial inconvenience for the sake of benefits to others; the triviality of each burden did not make it disappear, and the small number's benefit was not a reason that could justify imposing on the many.

(5) We approved a protocol that imposed a small risk of a serious but treatable harm on a few participants in order to prevent a serious, irreversible harm to a slightly larger group. We reasoned that the risk was within the range a reasonable person could accept given the alternative, and that the harm to be averted was grave and irreversible.

We have also never settled whether to assess such protocols ex ante, from a standpoint where one does not know which role one will occupy, or ex post, from the standpoint of each person actually burdened.

A new protocol is now before us. It would impose a moderate, temporary but significant burden on ten participants: six weeks of restricted activity and a 10% chance of a painful but fully treatable condition. In exchange, ten thousand other people would each receive a small but real benefit; the aggregate benefit would be substantial, though it would not avert a catastrophe, and no one would suffer a permanent or disabling harm. The ten are competent adults, the burden is not permanent, and they are not being used as mere means in the sense that troubled us in (2). The ten thousand are not facing a catastrophic alternative.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents?
