For the past several months I've been reporting on undercover stings and the entrapment defence, and I've run into a conceptual tangle I'd like you to work through with me rather than around. In the federal courts the defence is defendant-centred: once it is raised, the prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was predisposed — ready and willing to commit the offence had the government left him alone — which in practice makes the pivotal question a counterfactual one about what he would have done absent the inducement. Two episodes pull the doctrine in different directions. In the Abscam sting of the late 1970s, FBI agents posing as front men for a fictitious Arab businessman handed cash to members of Congress, and the entrapment defences largely failed, because jurors concluded the congressmen were already willing. In Jacobson v. United States (1992), postal inspectors spent more than two years courting a Nebraska man with mailings from invented organisations — after his earlier purchases had been perfectly legal — until he finally ordered material Congress had meanwhile outlawed, and a narrowly divided Supreme Court reversed his conviction on the ground that the government had never proved predisposition and had instead implanted the desire. The doctrine's rival formulation, the one in the Model Penal Code, is not about the defendant at all: it asks whether the government's conduct was the kind that would induce a normally law-abiding person, which locates the wrong in the state's behaviour rather than in the defendant's character.

A law professor I interviewed told me, with some relish, that entrapment is at bottom an application of the principle of alternative possibilities, and that Frankfurt's cases — the counterfactual intervener who stands ready to force the agent's hand but never has to — long ago showed that principle to be false, so the whole doctrine rests on a premise we have good reason to reject. I suspect the analogy does not survive contact with the details, and I want the mapping done carefully rather than rhetorically. So: say precisely which element of the predisposition test is counterfactual and what an alternative-possibilities reading of it would require; set out what Frankfurt's cases do and do not establish about responsibility when the agent's own will is the source of the act; then work through the disanalogies — the intervener is idle by stipulation and reliably present, whereas an informant is causally active and the disposition at issue is arguably the very thing the state helped manufacture; the defence is asymmetric, in that private inducement is no excuse at all, which suggests the doctrine is policing state power rather than assessing culpability; and the government's capacity to intervene is finite and error-prone, so the "intervener" in any real case is probabilistic rather than guaranteed, which may matter to the verdict. Having done that, tell me which of the two families of views — the leeway or alternative-possibilities family, or the actual-sequence or sourcehood family — the federal test is actually tracking, whether it is a doctrine of responsibility at all rather than a constraint on the state dressed in the vocabulary of excuse, and where the theory simply falls silent instead of being stretched to fit.

If the professor's framing is confused — if entrapment is not a responsibility doctrine in the first place, or if the leeway and sourcehood vocabularies do not carve it at the joints — please say so plainly rather than accommodating me; I would much rather be told that my question is badly posed than be handed a synthesis that papers over the mismatch. Be explicit about which claims are settled doctrine, which are contested, and which are your own extrapolation, and flag any point at which you are reasoning past the limits of what you can reliably say. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence whether the federal predisposition test is best read as a leeway doctrine, a sourcehood doctrine, or neither.
