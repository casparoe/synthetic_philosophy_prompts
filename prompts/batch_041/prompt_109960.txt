I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I have been losing an argument to a colleague in the biology department. Her position is that the extended mind thesis, once the vivid examples are stripped away, is a redescription of something her field settled long ago: that phenotypes extend beyond the body, that organisms construct and inherit their niches, and that the boundary between organism and environment is not principled. I know the extended mind literature well enough to teach it — the parity principle, Otto's notebook and Inga's memory, the objection that reliable causal coupling is not constitution — and I know the philosophy of biology only well enough to be dangerous, mostly at second hand. What I would like from you is a systematic translation in that direction, done as faithfully as you can manage, followed by an honest residue report.

Take the extended mind thesis at its strongest — the claim that the boundaries of a cognitive system are not the boundaries of the skull, that a reliably available, automatically endorsed and easily accessed external resource can be part of the realization of a standing belief, and that the difference between Otto and Inga is a difference of location rather than of kind — and restate it in the vocabulary of the extended phenotype, of niche construction, and of developmental systems theory. Use their terms of art rather than mine: phenotypic effect and the gene's-eye view, selection pressure and adaptation, ecological inheritance, scaffolding, the parity of developmental resources, the rejection of any privileged organism–environment boundary. Gloss each term as you deploy it, since I will be reading the translation as an outsider, and keep the translation honest: do not help yourself to a notion of realization, or of content, that the biological vocabulary does not contain and then present the result as a translation.

Then tell me what did not survive. I want the report sorted — claims that go through unchanged; claims that go through only under distortion, with the distortion named rather than gestured at; and claims that have no expression at all in the target vocabulary. I expect the normativity of belief (that a belief can be false, unjustified, warranted) and the first-personal character of occurrent thought to be the difficult cases, but I do not want to prejudge, and I would rather be told that I am wrong about where the difficulty lies. Three questions matter most. First, does the translation leave the constitutive claim intact, or does it quietly convert it into the causal claim that the coupling–constitution objection already grants? Second, the gene's-eye view appears to dissolve the individual agent altogether, whereas the extended mind thesis is at pains to preserve an agent and merely relocate its boundary — is that a genuine opposition, or are the two projects simply not addressed to one another? Third, does the biological framework have resources my debate lacks, and are there things the philosophy of mind can say that the biology cannot? Close with the verdict I actually need: is the residue a defect of the target vocabulary, or is it a sign that the thesis I have been defending is less determinate than its proponents, myself included, tend to suppose? Length is no constraint; precision is.
