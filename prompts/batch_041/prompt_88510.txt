Retired 8 years now from a career in wetland ecology — thirty field seasons measuring what happens downstream of a levee, a ditch, a dam. Since I hung up the waders I've been sitting in on the philosophy seminars at the university here, mostly the AI ethics reading group, and I've got a bee in my bonnet. I've written up a short piece for the group and I'd like you to tear into it: accuracy first, then whether there's anything actually interesting in it.

Here's the gist.

Everybody in this field says we want AI systems with "low impact," or systems that don't produce "side effects." I want to know whether that can even be defined without already having a full account of what's valuable. My claim is it can't, and here's why.

Take a river. I build a weir to stop the bank eroding on one side. Fine — but now the whole downstream regime shifts: sediment, fish passage, the lot. Was the fish passage a "side effect"? Only relative to a picture of the river in which stopping the erosion was the point and the fish weren't. That picture isn't in the physics. I chose it. Same story with an agent: calling something a side effect means comparing what happened against what was supposed to happen, and "supposed to" is doing all the work. The obvious fix is to say a side effect is a deviation from the default — from what would have happened if the agent had done nothing. But "nothing" isn't a well-defined act. An agent that does nothing still changes the world by omission (I don't reinforce the bank, so it washes away). And "what would have happened" depends on which counterfactual you pick: the agent absent entirely, the agent present but idle, the agent achieving the same task some other way. Different pick, different set of side effects.

So I've started calling this baseline smuggling — any impact measure that looks objective has a baseline baked in, and the baseline is a value judgement wearing a lab coat. My tentative conclusion: "avoid side effects" isn't a separate safety property at all. It's "do the right thing" with fewer words, and it only feels more tractable because we've hidden the hard part in the baseline.

What I want from you:

1. Is the argument right, or have I conflated "unintended" with "bad"? A side effect can be a good thing, and maybe that means I'm knocking down a strawman.
2. If I'm right that the baseline is normative, is there any way to fix it that isn't just "specify the entire value function"? I'd love a counterexample — a case where a side effect is definable with no reference to what anybody wants.
3. Is the river analogy doing real work, or is it just pretty?
4. The big one: is any of this new, or have I reinvented a wheel that's been rolling for years? If it's old, what's the standard objection to my version? Don't worry about references, I'll chase those down myself.
5. I've also got a nagging feeling I'm missing a distinction between "side effect" and "externality" from economics. Tell me if thats a red herring or if it's the whole thing.

Be blunt. I've got thick skin, and I'd rather be told the essay is confused then be humored.
