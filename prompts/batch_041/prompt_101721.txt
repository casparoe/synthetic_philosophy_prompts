My friend spent most of last night trying to convince me that Camus's opposition to the death penalty, followed honestly, condemns every punishment, and I could not find the exit. His reconstruction went like this. (1) A punishment is just only if it treats the offender as a person who remains the author of his own life — someone still able to respond, to amend, or at least to go on existing as a subject rather than being handled as a thing. (2) Execution is the refusal of exactly that: the condemned is disposed of in cold blood, by a procedure whose whole point is that it cannot be undone and after which nothing he does can matter. (3) So execution is unjust. (4) Imprisonment, judged by the same standard, is a man held in cold blood against his will, his years taken from him beyond recovery, in the service of ends — deterrence, public expression, security — that are not his own. (5) So by parity imprisonment is unjust too, and nothing in the argument stops there. (6) Camus did not accept (5); he treated imprisonment as the humane alternative to the scaffold. So either one of the steps is bad or he owes us a principled difference between killing a man and locking him up. I would rather keep (3) than (5). Which step should I give up, why did it look so plausible, and what does giving it up cost the rest of the position? And if there is a real difference between the two punishments, I want it named.
