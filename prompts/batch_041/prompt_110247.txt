I'm a novelist — three books in, all realist — and I'm trying to write something with a speculative premise for the first time, which is going about as well as you'd expect. So I've got a wierd ask for you, and I'd rather spell out what I'm circling than have you guess at it.

Here's what keeps eating my weekends. There's a philosopher, Derek Parfit, who spent decades on a question that sounds stupid until it doesn't: what actually matters in survival? Not "will I be the same person tomorrow" but the prior question — why do I care about tomorrow's me at all? His answer, roughly, is that identity isn't the thing that matters. What matters is something thinner: memories, intentions and character carried forward with the right kind of causal link. He calls that thinner thing a relation, and the two interesting features are that it comes in degrees and that it can branch. Scan a person, build two of them, and identity has failed — there's one of you and two of them — while the thing that actually mattered has doubled. People then bolt a no-branching clause onto identity to rescue it, which has always struck me as a fudge. He also has a spectrum case: swap one neuron at a time, or one memory at a time, and ask where along the line the person stops being them. There's no line. That's the whole point of it.

Then there's an older case, in a paper by Bernard Williams from 1970, where you're told you'll be tortured tomorrow — and then, in a second telling, that your memories will be wiped and someone else's put in their place, and that it's the other body that gets tortured. Same facts, two descriptions, and your gut flips between them. That flip is what I want inside a character.

So: teletransporter story. But not the version everybody writes, the one where the machine kills you and something else steps out and the protagonist has a crisis in the departure lounge. I want the boring, administrative afterlife of a technology like that.

Three wrinkles I want you to build on. They're the entire reason I'm asking you instead of writing it myself:

1. Non-destructive scanning. The machine stopped killing people a generation ago. A scan is just a scan, a rebuild is just a rebuild, and nothing prevents there being two of you. Then one of them gets scanned again. Now there are three, and "which one is me" stops being a riddle and becomes a filing problem — a marriage, a pension, a criminal charge, a mortgage, a funeral.

2. The rebuild is never clean. Say 99.4% overlap. The last eleven minutes before the scan don't come through, or one face is gone, or a language is. So does a threshold matter, and if the copy is missing the memory of one particular person, is it the same claimant for the purposes of the will? I want that percentage printed on a form somewhere.

3. Identity by convention. I want one character to argue that we already operate a working concept of identity through total replacement, and it's called a company: every employee can leave, the entire staff can turn over, and nobody thinks the company died — we simply agree to go on calling it the same entity. Why is a person different, other than that we're sentimental about it? Give that argument to someone unsympathetic, and make it annoyingly hard to answer.

Craft constraints, because I'd rather over-specify than get a story I can't use:

- Put the protagonist in a job where they have to apply a rule about who counts as the same person. An assessor, a registrar, a notary, a claims clerk. The metaphysics should arrive as paperwork.
- Mundane register. Domestic, slightly dull, office lighting. The strangeness should come from the tech being old news.
- No gotchas. Nobody turns out to be a soulless machine, nothing turns out to have been a simulation, no evil corporation. And no scene where two copies fight over which one is real — that scene has been written four thousand times.
- At least one scene where two of them meet and it's quiet. A shared bank account. One of them going to a parent's funeral and the other not.
- The Williams flip has to happen to a character, not in the narration: the same facts described two ways, and their fear moving when it has no business moving.
- End on an image or a gesture, not a thesis. I don't want the story to decide which of these views is right, and I especially don't want the narrator deciding.

Before you write, think it through and show me your working. Give me a short block stating the rules of the technology you're assuming — what a scan captures, what survives a rebuild, whether copies are legal persons, who can consent — then five seperate possible scenes with one line each on what each is actually about philosophically, then pick the two or three that carry the most and say why. Only after that, draft the story: 2,500 to 3,500 words, first person or close third, whichever the material wants. Finish with three lines for me and not for a reader, naming which character is arguing which position, so I can keep them straight while I revise.

You're going to be my philosopher and my co-writer on this one, so please don't dumb down the philosophy and don't let the philosophy eat the story. Think carefully about the seam between the two — that's the part I can't do alone.
