For the past couple of months a small reading group I belong to has been working through The Mind's I, and we keep circling back to the same thought experiment: suppose someone produced a book that specifies, neuron by neuron and synapse by synapse, the complete state of Einstein's brain at some given moment, together with a full set of rules for updating that state, so that a sufficiently diligent reader working through the pages by hand could in principle derive exactly what Einstein would think next. Our opening question was whether such a book — or the reader working through it — could be said to be conscious at all. One of our members, a committed physicalist who is nonetheless certain the answer is no, makes his case by analogy: a computer simulation of a hurricane is not a hurricane. It does not blow down houses and it does not get you wet, however elaborate the model. Likewise, a simulation of thought is not thought, however detailed the book. The moral he draws is that consciousness requires the right kind of physical stuff doing the right kind of causal work, and that no arrangement of marks on paper, or of voltages in a machine, will ever amount to more than a description of a mind rather than a mind. I cannot tell whether I find this convincing or merely persuasive, and I suspect the difference matters a great deal.

What I would like from you is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on consciousness as such. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the hurricane, of the simulated hurricane, of the water molecules, of the computer running the model, of the wetness — and then what plays the role of the book, the reader, the symbols, and the thinking. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, taking care to separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. Four things interest me in particular. First, the storm example trades on a simulation that is coarse-grained and incomplete, whereas the book is stipulated to be exhaustive; does the intuition that it does not get you wet survive being scaled up to molecule-by-molecule fidelity, and if it does not, how much of his argument was resting on the low fidelity rather than on simulation as such? Second, in the hurricane case we seem to have an independent, non-question-begging account of what a storm is made of, and that is what licenses the verdict that the simulation falls short; do we have anything corresponding for thought, or does the analogy simply assume the conclusion that thinking is substrate-dependent? Third, the book is a static object unless somebody reads it, whereas the storm simulation is running; does the argument need to slide between 'simulation' as a process and 'description' as an object, and what difference does that slide make to whether the book, the reader, or the pair of them is the candidate thinker? Fourth, what does the analogy quietly import — a simulation is something built by someone, for someone, in order to predict something, and I wonder whether the case against the book inherits an assumption that the whole point of the thing is to serve an outside user's predictive purposes, and whether that assumption does any work in an argument about experience.

Finally, tell me whether his conclusion — that no simulation could be a mind and that the right physical stuff is required — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along. If the example is really neutral, or cuts both ways (I notice that one could run it in the opposite direction and conclude that a sufficiently faithful simulation just is the thing simulated), say so plainly and explain what a reader should conclude instead. And if there is a better analogy for the point he wants to make, or a better way to state the point with no analogy at all, I would like to see it.
