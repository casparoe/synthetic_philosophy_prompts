I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar that sits between decision theory and the philosophy of economics, and I want a single constructed case to anchor the discussion of iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies. Please design a two-player, finite normal-form game—three pure strategies per player is sufficient, but you may use four if it makes the iteration cleaner—presented as a payoff matrix, that meets the following specifications. First, no pure strategy of either player is strictly dominated by another pure strategy, either initially or at any stage of the pure-strategy elimination procedure; the pure-strategy version of iterated elimination must therefore leave the entire game untouched. Second, at least one pure strategy is strictly dominated by an explicit mixed strategy, and the elimination of that strategy must make at least one pure strategy of the other player strictly dominated by an explicit mixed strategy (again, not by any pure strategy), so that the mixed-strategy procedure genuinely iterates. Third, the game must illustrate the order-independence of strict dominance: show that any available sequence of eliminations yields the same surviving set, and if the order is forced at some stage, explain why that is still an instance of the property. Fourth, the strategies eliminated by the mixed-strategy procedure must be exactly those that are never best responses to any belief about the opponent's play, and the set of Nash equilibria of the original game must be unchanged after the eliminations; make both claims explicit and verify them.

After presenting the matrix, walk through the elimination step by step. Write out the mixed strategy that does the dominating at each stage, verify the strict inequalities for every pure strategy of the opponent, and identify the feature of the payoff structure that makes pure-strategy elimination powerless while mixed-strategy elimination succeeds. Then, for the philosophical payoff, explain how this case bears on the epistemic reading of iterated elimination: what exactly must be common knowledge for the mixed-strategy eliminations to be licensed, and why the fact that the eliminated strategies are never best responses does not by itself deliver a unique prediction. Finally, perturb the payoffs so that the mixed strategy only weakly dominates its target at one stage. Say whether the order-independence survives, whether the surviving set can change, and what that difference between strict and weak dominance reveals about the solution concept.
