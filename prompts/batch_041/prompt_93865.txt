I am a novelist, and for some years now I have been reading moral philosophy in the hope of sharpening how I render motive on the page — not so that my characters become illustrations of a thesis, but to keep myself honest about what I am asking a reader to see when I show a person doing the right thing. The literature I keep returning to has been stalled for a long time, and what I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The shape of the impasse, as I understand it. On one side: an action has moral worth only when it is done from the motive of duty — when the agent does it because it is right, and whatever warmth of inclination accompanies the deed is not what carries the day; a kind act done because one is kind is, on this view, at best a happy accident of temperament. On the other side: an action can have full moral worth when it flows from a good motive of any kind — love, loyalty, a lively sense of another's need — and the demand that it be done from duty is either a philosopher's fetish or a covertly different question about what makes an agent admirable rather than what makes an act worthy. A third position, which I find hardest to place, says that what matters is only that the agent be moved by the very features that in fact make the act right, so that the source of the motive — duty, affection, even a certain self-respect — is irrelevant.

Both camps read the same cases, and I read them the way a novelist would. The reluctant man who pays a debt he would rather dodge. The joyful helper whose kindness costs her nothing. The overdetermined case, where duty and desire both point the same way and the agent cannot say which moved him. The woman who keeps a promise because breaking it would embarrass her. And my own two hard cases, which I invented for a novel and cannot settle: the wife who stays with a dying husband out of duty and only afterwards discovers love, and her mirror, the wife who stays out of love and describes it to herself as duty. I would let the first off the hook and convict the second, and I cannot defend the asymmetry.

My hypothesis is that this is not one dispute but several conducted under one heading — that moral worth is being asked to mean the act's praiseworthiness, the agent's admirability, the act's being done for the reasons that make it right, and the act's expressing a good will, and that the camps have simply divided over which of these they are answering. But I hold that loosely, and I would accept a different diagnosis: that the sides differ about whether the motive of duty is a motive like any other, or that a hidden formal question — whether acting for the right reasons must be factive, whether the agent's operative reason must match the reason that justifies the act — is doing the real work.

Tell me which kind of disagreement this is, argued from what each side actually claims rather than from a survey of the field; and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a change of question, a formal result — or why none should be expected. I would also like to know which of my two hard cases your diagnosis forces me to give up.
