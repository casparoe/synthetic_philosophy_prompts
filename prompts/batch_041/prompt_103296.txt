I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I mangle the terms, but I've been sitting in on a Thursday-night reading group in the back of a pub -- two moral philosophers, a psychoanalyst who keeps bringing up Winnicott, and me -- and for about a year now we've been stuck on Williams's integrity objection. You know the one: a man saves his wife rather than a stranger in some emergency, and the question is whether he's supposed to have the extra thought that saving her is also what impartial morality requires, or whether that thought is one too many. The two philosophers split cleanly and haven't moved an inch. One says the objection is about moral psychology: the action can be right even if the agent doesn't have the justifying thought, so the point is just that certain theories give a bad picture of how a good person's mind works. The other says no, it's about the structure of practical reason: if the relationship isn't itself the reason, the agent is alienated from the ground of his own action, and that's a defect in the theory, not just in his inner monologue. Every move one makes, the other says begs the question, and I've started to think they're not even answering the same question -- one is asking what makes an action right, the other is asking what it is to be the person doing it -- but I can't tell if that's a real diagnosis or just a way of changing the subject.

I've also been trying to get them to seperate three things: the thought that actually moves the agent, the thought the theory says would justify the action, and the thought the agent could give if you asked him afterwards. It seems to me the whole fight might be about which of those the objection is aiming at. And I keep wondering if the real complaint is the opposite of the famous one -- not one thought too many, but one thought too few: whether a morally serious person should have some awareness of the impartial stakes even if it never enters her deliberation. Is that a genuine distinction or just a reframing? What I want from you is a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on who's right: is this a substantive disagreement about reasons, a verbal one about words like deliberation and justification, or two different questions wearing a single name? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress -- a distinction, a thought experiment, a change in the question -- or is this one of those disputes that should just be left to novelists?
