You are a political philosopher whose work sits at the intersection of democratic theory and social choice. Take this question slowly. Before you commit to any interpretation, brainstorm on the page: list the different things a referendum might be taken to express, the different things it might be taken to authorize, and the different agents it might be taken to bind. Only then write the analysis.

Consider two real arrangements for direct legislation that sit oddly together.

In Switzerland, a proposal to amend the federal constitution — whether it originates with the parliament or with a popular initiative that has gathered 100,000 signatures — must be approved both by a majority of all voters and by a majority of the cantons, with the cantons counting equally, so that a voter in a small canton carries far more weight in the second count than a voter in Zurich. Ordinary legislation, by contrast, can be challenged by an optional referendum and needs only a majority of those voting. So changing the fundamental law is deliberately made harder than passing a statute, and a majority of persons can be blocked by a minority of territorial units.

In California, an initiative that gathers enough signatures can amend the state constitution by a simple majority of those voting, with no territorial weighting and no supermajority; a modest turnout in an off-year primary would do. Yet the same constitution requires a two-thirds vote of both houses of the legislature to raise a state tax — a requirement that was itself enacted by a bare-majority initiative. So here changing the fundamental law is easier than raising a tax, and a simple majority of those who happen to show up can entrench a rule that two-thirds of the elected legislature cannot undo.

Reconstruct the implicit political philosophy of each arrangement, and of the pair taken together. Address at least the following.

First, legitimacy. Does the arrangement rest on authorization — the outcome binds because it is willed by a majority — or on something epistemic, as when a supermajority is treated as evidence of considered judgment or of a track record of being right? Which does each institution actually presuppose, and does it need that presupposition or merely fit it?

Second, the demos. Does the double majority treat the people as an aggregate of persons, or also as a body of constituent units whose votes are counted as such? What does that presuppose about whether the people is one thing or many, and about whether territorial minorities have a standing that head-counting does not capture?

Third, the status quo. What does a supermajority requirement presuppose about the default — that the existing arrangement enjoys a presumption, that coercion bears a higher burden of proof, that change should be slow? Is this a substantive claim about value or a procedural claim about risk and reversibility? Note that California attaches the higher burden to taxation while Switzerland attaches it to constitutional change: are these the same presupposition or two different ones?

Fourth, the ballot question. What does the practice presuppose about the determinacy of what is voted on — that the question has a single content, that a yes means the same thing for everyone who casts it, that the voters share one interpretation of the proposition? What happens to the legitimacy of the result if the question bundles several distinct issues?

Fifth, the vote itself. Does the practice treat a vote as the expression of a preference or of a judgment? Does it presuppose that all votes count equally regardless of how much the voter cares, and is that presupposition of political equality or merely a concession to tractability?

Then assess consistency. Which presuppositions are strictly required by the practices and which merely fit them? Where is each practice underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license it equally well? Finally, say what would have to change in each system if we replaced the presupposed view with a purely procedural authorization view, with a purely epistemic view, with an aggregation view that weighs intensity of preference, and with a view on which referendums are only advisory.

Write a structured essay of roughly 1,200 words, with headings, addressed to a reader who is intelligent but knows no philosophy and is genuinely puzzled by the fact that the two jurisdictions seem to disagree about what a majority is worth. Do not merely catalogue the standard theories of democracy; read the commitments off the practices themselves.
