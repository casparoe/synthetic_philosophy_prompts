For a seminar on the limits of formalisation, I have been trying to prise apart two very different uses to which Gödel's incompleteness theorems are put once they leave mathematics, and I keep finding that my fellow students slide between them without noticing. The first is the use associated with J. R. Lucas and, more recently, Roger Penrose: because any consistent formal system strong enough to encode arithmetic contains truths it cannot prove, a human mathematician who can see the truth of the system's own undecidable sentence is supposedly doing something no machine could do, so minds outrun machines. The second use is far less discussed: that a completed physics, or a "theory of everything" written as an axiomatic system, would inherit analogous limits, so that any final theory must be either incomplete or unable to certify its own consistency, and physics can never be closed. I should like you to compare these two moves head-on.

To focus the comparison, consider a distinction I have been calling the difference between a "transcendence" reading and a "boundedness" reading. On the transcendence reading, Gödel is invoked to show that something genuinely lies beyond the system; on the boundedness reading, he is invoked only to show that the system cannot fully vouch for itself. Do the two uses above rest on the same reading of the theorems, or do they pull them in incompatible directions? I am especially keen for you to press on whether the physics version is even well-formed: physics is not normally an axiomatic system equipped with a proof predicate, so what exactly would a "Gödel sentence for physics" be, and does the analogy survive once we remember that a physical theory is in the business of predicting rather than proving?

Finally, tell me which of the two uses you find the more defensible, and whether there is a way of stating the weaker, boundedness point that a working physicist could accept without smuggling in the stronger, transcendence claim.
