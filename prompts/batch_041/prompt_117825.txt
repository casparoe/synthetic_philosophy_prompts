I'm the TA for a graduate seminar on the technical side of AI safety — it's cross-listed with the CS department and half the students come in from the policy school — and I'm writing the take-home final. The course spent most of its time on the two framings everyone recites: average-case guarantees, where you bound expected loss under a distribution over environments, and worst-case guarantees, where you bound loss over an adversarially chosen environment. Somewhere around the eighth week I stopped believing that this distinction was the load-bearing one. What I think is actually doing the work — and what I've been teaching under a vocabulary I invented precisely so it wouldn't collide with the standard terms and let students pattern-match to the reading — is a different axis. Call a guarantee environment-worst-case if it bounds performance for every environment in some class while the policy or decision rule is held fixed: the system being evaluated is the one we wrote down. Call it trainer-worst-case if it bounds performance across every policy the training process could have produced, so that the adversary is the optimizer and the environment is incidental. My claim, which I flag to the students as a claim and not a theorem, is that nearly every formal worst-case guarantee in circulation is environment-worst-case and is perfectly compatible with arbitrarily bad trainer-worst-case behavior, and that when people argue worst-case versus average-case they are usually, without quite saying so, arguing about which of these two objects they care about. A friend who does formal verification for a robotics company pushed back hard on this over dinner; his line was that trainer-worst-case guarantees are just environment-worst-case guarantees over a larger space, with the training procedure folded into the environment, and I couldn't immediately articulate why that reduction should fail. That failure of articulation is the reason I want the exam to force the issue.

So: one essay question for a ninety-minute, closed-book final, pitched at students who have absorbed the standard material and will be tempted to recite it back. The question should turn on the crux above — in particular it should require them to say whether my friend's reduction succeeds, and if it fails, exactly where the argument breaks and what a non-vacuous trainer-worst-case guarantee would have to be a guarantee *about*. I want them forced to keep the two axes apart rather than collapsing one into the other, and to say what follows for how the field should state its claims. Give me the question itself, a model answer of roughly six hundred words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, with the marks for each out of twenty and a one-line note on how students typically get that particular point wrong.

Two constraints. The exam is closed-book and I don't want anything resting on a specific paper, theorem, or result — the students should be reasoning from the distinction and from arguments they can reconstruct on the spot, so please don't build the question or the model answer around named authors or cited findings. And the marking scheme has to separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain — I want to be able to put a finger on a script and say "you made this move" or "you didn't" — rather than by generic quality language about depth or insight.
