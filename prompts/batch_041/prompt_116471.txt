I spent thirty-five years as a biologist before turning to philosophy, and I keep returning to the American Psychiatric Association's removal of homosexuality from its diagnostic manual in 1973: the Board of Trustees voted to remove it, the decision was put to a referendum of the membership and upheld, and a residual category was left for people distressed by their own orientation or wishing to change it. That residual category reappeared in a different form in 1980, when the third edition introduced ego-dystonic homosexuality, which was itself removed in 1987. Analyse this episode through the two main accounts of disorder, together with the hybrid that combines them. On the naturalist account, disorder is a value-free matter of biological dysfunction, a failure of some internal mechanism to perform its species-typical function; on the normativist account, it is ineliminably evaluative, a matter of harm, disvalue, or social judgement; on the hybrid, often associated with Wakefield, it is a harmful dysfunction, requiring both a biological failure and a harm.

Map the episode onto each. For the naturalist: does the removal look like an empirical correction, and if so, what finding of dysfunction was rejected? My instinct, as a biologist, is that the naturalist must either deny that same-sex attraction is a dysfunction or deny that there is a species-typical function of sexual arousal that it violates; say which route is more defensible, and how much the answer depends on how the reference class is fixed or on an unstated teleology of sexual arousal. For the normativist: was the removal a revaluation, and what exactly was revalued — the orientation, or the distress caused by social stigma? For the hybrid: the two residual categories seem to do different things. The 1973 category appears to locate the disorder in the individual's distress or conflict; the 1980 category appears to require, in addition, a failure of heterosexual functioning. If that is right, the episode is a natural experiment on whether the dysfunction and harm components can be separated, and I want you to say which account handles each category better.

Then run the analysis the other way. What does the episode show about the accounts themselves? The decision was, at least formally, a vote, and later a task-force judgement: if disorder were a value-free biological fact, that procedure would be beside the point, whereas if it were normative, it would be a sensible way to settle it. Does the episode therefore favour normativism, or can the naturalist accommodate the vote by saying it merely ratified an empirical finding? And does the episode expose a weakness in the naturalist's notion of species-typical function, or in the normativist's ability to distinguish the disorder from the social reaction to it?

I want a disciplined mapping rather than a survey, and an honest account of where each account is silent or strains. I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I will supply those myself. Where a fact is contested or uncertain, say so rather than inventing detail.
