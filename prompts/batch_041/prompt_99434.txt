I'm a novelist, mostly historical fiction, and I'm about a year into a draft set in the last decades of the Roman Republic. I've been reading Sallust's account of the Catilinarian conspiracy as background and there's one passage I keep circling back to that I can't get the tone of — it's where he introduces Sempronia, one of the women in the circle around Catiline. Here's the translation I've got:

"Now among these women was Sempronia, who had often committed many crimes of masculine daring. In birth and beauty, in her husband also and children, she was abundantly favoured by fortune; well read in the literature of Greece and Rome, able to play the lyre and dance more skilfully than an honest woman need, and having many other accomplishments which minister to voluptuousness. But there was nothing which she held so cheap as modesty and chastity; you could not easily say whether she was less sparing of her money or her honour; her desires were so ardent that she sought men more often than she was sought by them. Even before the time of the conspiracy she had often broken her word, repudiated her debts, been privy to murder; poverty and extravagance combined had driven her headlong. Nevertheless, she was a woman of no mean endowments; she could write verses, bandy jests, and use language which was modest, or tender, or wanton; in fine, she possessed a high degree of wit and of charm."

My problem is I honestly can't tell if he's damning her or half admiring her, and I need to know, because I'm building a character on her and the whole voice of the book depends on whether she reads as a villain or something stranger. He spends most of the paragraph stacking up the vices — perjury, debt, even being "privy to murder" — and then the last sentence does this swerve into what sounds almost like a compliment. Whats going on there? Is that closing line meant to make her worse rather than better, like a woman of real gifts who's thrown them away, or is he actually a bit taken with her? And what do I do with "masculine daring" — in a moralist of that period, is attributing daring to a woman plainly an accusation, or is there something more complicated in it? Same with the bit about her being able to talk in a way that was "modest, or tender, or wanton" — is that a charge or a kind of admiration?

The other thing that nags at me: he's writing a moralizing history about how Rome rotted from the inside, and Sempronia isn't really part of the plot — she barely appears again. So why is she here at all? Is she a real woman he's reporting on, or a kind of emblem, and if an emblem then an emblem of what, exactly? How much of this sketch is shaped by his need to show that even the women of the elite had gone to seed? I don't need the scholarship, I just need someone to walk me through what the passage is actually doing — where the irony sits, which words are carrying the judgment, and whether that ending is a genuine softening or a rhetorical trap I'm walking into.
