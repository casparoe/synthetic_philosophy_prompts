I am a retired experimental physicist — a career in the physical sciences, much of it at a national laboratory — and since retiring I have been working my way through philosophy, chiefly decision theory and the philosophy of science. At a graduate reading group I have been sitting in on, a member introduced a view I had never encountered, risk-weighted expected utility, and his presentation has left me unable to tell whether I met a genuine rival to expected utility or a relabelling of it. What I would like is an audit of his account rather than a tutorial in the subject.

He spoke more or less as follows. 'Risk-weighted expected utility is the proposal that a rational agent does not maximize expected utility. Instead, she ranks the outcomes of each option from worst to best and, for each outcome, takes the probability of doing at least that well rather than the probability of that outcome exactly; she passes those cumulative probabilities through a risk function — a fixed, increasing transformation of chances that carries 0 to 0 and 1 to 1 — and weights each outcome's utility by how much the risk function rises between the chance of doing better than that outcome and the chance of doing at least as well as it, summing the results. An agent whose risk function is convex — one that makes the better outcomes count for less than their probability — is risk averse, and a concave risk function makes her risk seeking. The point of the exercise is that risk aversion is not a mistake: an agent may weight chances in this way and still be instrumentally rational, and that is what sets the framework apart from expected utility. In substance, however, it is expected utility with a curved utility function, since any risk-weighted maximizer can be redescribed as an ordinary expected-utility maximizer whose utility function is concave; the machinery is a notational variant of what it replaces. Its motivating case is the Allais paradox, which shows that people systematically overweight small probabilities, and its natural home is the situation in which no probabilities can be assigned at all — the Knightian case — which makes it the decision-theoretic cousin of ambiguity aversion. The lesson for economics is that expected utility was never in trouble; only its utility function needed curving.'

I have no stake in expected utility, but a career spent with transformations of variables has taught me to distrust the claim that two formalisms differ only in notation, so I would rather be told where the load-bearing claims are than guess. Please take his account sentence by sentence and sort it into three piles: what the framework's own defenders would endorse; what is a competent popularizer's simplification that a specialist would tolerate; and what is a misrepresentation of the view. Where he uses a term in a sense other than the one the framework gives it, say so explicitly. Then tell me what a defender would put in the place of anything you strike out — the accurate statement he should have made instead — and finally whether his closing lesson, that expected utility was never in trouble and needed only a curved utility function, survives the corrections you have made.
