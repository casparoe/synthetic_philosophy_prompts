I belong to a reading group working through the ethics of price regulation — rent ceilings, minimum wages, anti-gouging statutes — and I have built an argument that I now suspect proves more than I can accept. It runs as follows. (1) When two parties exchange at a price that both accept, each is better off by their own lights, and this is the whole ground of the presumption that such exchanges are permissible and frequently admirable. (2) A rent ceiling forbids exchanges that both parties would willingly have made at a higher price: a landlord willing to let at a figure above the ceiling and a tenant willing to pay it are both willing, and the law says no. (3) What the law forbids does not happen, so the surplus those exchanges would have produced is destroyed. (4) A rule that destroys surplus and leaves people worse off by their own lights, when a feasible alternative would not, stands in need of justification; and the standard justification — that the rule protects tenants — is unavailable here, because the parties whose exchange is blocked are themselves tenants. (5) Therefore rent ceilings, and by identical steps wage floors, are unjust and not merely inefficient.

My difficulty is not the conclusion about rent ceilings, which I accept, but that I can find no principled place to stop. Every rule that governs letting — habitability standards, occupancy limits, restrictions on refusing tenants — forbids exchanges that both parties would have made; so do tariffs, immigration restrictions, and prohibitions on selling a kidney; and in the end so does the enforcement of the property rights the argument silently relies on, since a landlord has something to let only because the law says so. If the argument condemns the ceiling, it condemns the floor it stands on, and I am left unable to say that any blocked exchange is unjust rather than merely unprofitable.

I would rather keep (5) than abandon it. Tell me which premise or step to give up, why it looked compelling until I pressed it, and what giving it up costs. I want to know, in particular, whether anything survives as a claim about the injustice of price controls as opposed to their inefficiency; whether the argument can be repaired so that it reaches rent ceilings and wage floors without reaching habitability codes and immigration law; and whether the repair has to differ between the two cases, since the labour market may not be the housing market — a wage floor could in some circumstances increase the quantity of employment rather than reduce it, and I do not know what that does to step (3).
