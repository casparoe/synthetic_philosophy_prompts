Some freinds of mine have been arguing about the moral equality of combatants for about two years now and I want to force them to actually write something together instead of rehashing the same three objections at each other. One of them holds the traditional view: the soldier fighting for an unjust cause isn't a murderer just for fighting — once the war is on, the same permissions and prohibitions apply to everybody, and what's permissible in fighting doesn't depend on whether your side was right to fight. The other is a revisionist: liability to be killed tracks responsibility for an unjust threat, so a soldier who knowingly prosecutes an unjust war is morally on a par with a guy attacking you in the street, and a uniform and a set of orders don't launder that.

Draft the joint statement they could both sign. I want the agreed claims, stated so that neither of them is quietly giving something up; the residual disagreements, each written in the strongest form its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view; the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling things would actually move somebody; and for each crux, the case, argument or finding both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Number the agreed claims, then put the disagreements in a table, then the cruxes. And tell me which chunk of this fight, if any, is really verbal or is two different questions wearing one coat (what's morally true vs. what the law of war should say; what a soldier can be expected to know; what a code of conduct is even for) and which chunk is a genuine moral disagreement that no amount of clarification will dissolve.

Things I want them to actually deal with instead of talking around:

- Whether "liable to be killed" and "permitted to kill" can come apart. Is there such a thing as a killing that's justified even though the person killed wasn't liable to it? If so, what does that do to the revisionist's story, and can the traditionalist get any mileage out of it, or does it just hand the revisionist a patch?

- The case where neither side's cause is just — a straight imperial scramble, no just side anywhere. Does the traditionalist's symmetry claim get vindicated here? And does revisionism have to say that nobody on either side is permitted to do anything, which sounds nuts? I want each of them to bite whatever bullet is actually there.

- Innocent threats: someone shoved off a bridge who's going to land on you, a kid with a rifle who's been told you're the enemy, a civilian used as a shield. What do these do to each side? My guess is the traditionalist will say they show liability is the wrong frame entirely and the revisionist will say they show it's the right frame applied carefully, and I want to know which of them is right about that rather than just both being asserted.

- Criminal liability after the fact. If the unjust side loses, should its ordinary soldiers be prosecutable? If the answer is "no, but not because they did nothing wrong", say exactly what that "but" is doing — is it a moral consideration or just a practical one dressed up?

- Whether the traditionalist view is really a claim about who deserves what, or a claim about what rule we should all adopt given that soldiers can't reasonably be expected to adjudicate their own cause. If it's the second, does it survive if the convention turns out not to buy us restraint or reciprocity? That seems like something they could actually agree counts against it, so make them say what they'd do if it failed.

Don't let them settle for "we agree there's a tension here" — the agreed section should contain things that somebody outside the room would actually deny. And flag any spot where one side has to admit that its view as stated implies something it doesn't want to own.
