I am an economist who works on information and consumer search. My working vocabulary is the theory of search and experience goods (goods whose quality a buyer can only learn by consuming them), credence goods, asymmetric information and market breakdown, Bayesian updating from noisy signals, signaling and screening, and welfare analysis over heterogeneous preferences. I know that literature well and I am comfortable being pushed on it. In aesthetics I know almost nothing beyond a vague sense that there is a puzzle about whether one may take another person's word for it that something is beautiful.

I would like the philosophical debate about aesthetic testimony restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage, strand by strand. Please treat the following as the strands to be translated, and add any you think I have missed:

1. The claim that testimony about a work's beauty cannot do for the hearer what testimony about an ordinary matter of fact can do — with the distinction kept sharp between three things: forming the belief, coming to know, and being entitled to rely on the belief in one's own subsequent aesthetic reasoning.
2. The norm, not merely the fact, that one ought to judge for oneself — the sense in which deferring to a critic is not merely uninformative but somehow improper.
3. The thought, usually traced to Kant, that a judgment of taste demands the agreement of others — that in calling something beautiful I am not merely reporting a preference of mine.
4. The restriction that appears to hold for some predicates and not others: testimony that a poem is in iambic pentameter is plainly acceptable, testimony that it is beautiful apparently is not.
5. The apparent fact that testimony is useful when it tells the hearer where to look or what to compare — so the puzzle is not about information transmission in general, and the translation has to explain why this case behaves differently from the others.

For each strand, give me the best translation you can into the economics of information: the good, the signal, the prior and posterior, the search friction, the externality, the welfare object. A compact ledger of claim against translation would be welcome for this part.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which claims survive with their content intact? Which survive only after I have quietly accepted a substantive assumption I would not otherwise have accepted — for instance, that a taste is a preference, or that the value of an experience is exhausted by its consumption value? And which have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary?

I am especially interested in three suspected residues, and I would like your verdict on each. First, whether the requirement to judge for oneself is anything more, in my terms, than a preference for first-hand information or a constraint on the feasible set — and if it is more, what kind of object it is. Second, whether the universal-assent claim is doing anything beyond predicting that preferences over aesthetic qualities converge — and if so, what. Third, whether the belief/knowledge/entitlement distinction survives at all, or whether my framework has only one slot where the philosophers have three.

Be honest about the direction of the distortion. Do not let the translation import my framework's assumptions and then present them as though they were the philosophers' claims; where the translation forces a choice, say so, and say what is lost on either side of it. Where a strand cannot be carried across, do not fudge it into something that can — say plainly that it cannot. And at the end, tell me whether the residue is better read as a defect in my framework, something it cannot represent but should, or as a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked.

End with a single final line of the form

Residue: <a short phrase naming the one thing that most decisively fails to survive the translation>
