Two friends of mine, Sam and Alex, got into it at a party about the hole argument in general relativity, and I ended up refereeing. I can follow the moves but I can't call them, so I need your help. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what — and who's more right overall? It got alot more technical than I expected.

Sam's opening: GR plus substantivalism about spacetime points gives you indeterminism. Fix everything outside some region — the hole — then apply a diffeomorphism that's the identity outside the hole but not inside it. You get two models that agree on all observable and predictable content, yet assign field values to different points. If points are real individuals, those are two genuinely different worlds, and the theory can't tell you which one is actual. So either GR is indeterministic or substantivalism is dead.

Alex's reply: that only kills a naive substantivalism where points carry identities over and above the metric. Make the metric field itself the substance, with no bare substratum of points sitting underneath it. Then the two models are just gauge-equivalent descriptions of a single world, like a change of coordinates. The hole argument refutes a view nobody needs to hold.

Sam: calling them gauge-equivalent just renames the problem. You still owe an account of what a gauge orbit represents — one world — and why the orbit and not a point inside it is the real thing. Any such account either collapses into relationalism (points aren't individuals at all) or is a promissory note. So you've conceded the anti-substantivalist conclusion.

Alex: no — the field is real, it's just that the field isn't spread over a pre-given stage of points; it's one structure. That isn't relationalism, because relationalism says only relations among material bodies exist, whereas here the field itself is the substance.

Then Sam brought in Leibniz: could the whole universe have been shifted a meter to the left? You'd say no — the shift is a symmetry and nothing marks out one position over another. But the hole argument is just the local version of that same symmetry. If the shifted world isn't a different world, then neither is the hole-shuffled world. So why treat the hole argument as special?

Alex: because the global shift preserves everything, whereas in the hole case you vary the fields inside the hole while holding the outside fixed — and the outside is supposed to fix the inside via the field equations. That looks like a failure of determinism, not merely a redundant description. The global shift has no inside/outside to exploit.

Sam: that difference only matters if you think the outside determines the inside point-by-point, which is the naive view again. Determinism should be a claim about the gauge-invariant content, not about which point carries which value.

They asked me to settle it and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate point by point, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say what both of them are missing. Keep the whole thing under 600 words.
