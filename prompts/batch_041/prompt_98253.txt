I am a senior at a public high school and the student head of our philosophy club, and this year the club is running a mock written examination for a regional high-school philosophy tournament; because the tournament's judges are volunteers with no shared rubric, I have been asked to write not only the questions but also a model answer and a marking scheme that a group of senior students can apply consistently. Our theme this year is paradoxes of practical rationality, and the case we have been arguing about for three weeks is the toxin puzzle: a benefactor offers a large prize to anyone who, at the moment of decision, intends to drink a toxin the following day — a toxin that will make you quite ill for a day but do no lasting harm — while knowing that the prize is paid on the intention alone, and that you may change your mind tomorrow with nothing taken back. My friend Priya, who is on the debate team, says the puzzle proves that a rational person can intend to do something she knows perfectly well she will not do; our faculty adviser, who teaches statistics, keeps insisting that it is nothing but a conditional probability dressed up in a story. I find both answers too quick, so when I drafted the club's house version of the case I deliberately changed two things. First, there is no machine and no lie detector: three of your classmates sit behind a screen and award the prize if a short interview convinces them that you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow; they are unusually good at reading people, they require unanimity, and the prize is paid to you the moment they say yes, with no clawback clause and no further conditions. Second, their verdict is announced at the next school assembly, in front of everyone, so that by tomorrow morning the whole school knows whether you were judged to intend.

I would like you to write the single essay question for the mock examination, a model answer of roughly six hundred to seven hundred words, and the marking scheme. The question should be answerable in forty-five minutes, and it should not be answerable by reciting the standard puzzle together with its usual replies; I do not want a question that rewards a student for naming a position ('resolute choice,' 'sophisticated choice,' or whatever) without doing anything with it. What I want the question to force is a decision about the house version itself: is it still a genuine puzzle, or does the change to a panel of classmates, together with the public announcement, quietly dissolve it? A student should have to say what an intention is such that it can or cannot be produced on demand for a reward; whether an intention formed in order to be believed is still the sort of intention the benefactor's rule requires; and whether the public announcement creates a real new reason to drink tomorrow, or merely a reason to be embarrassed. It should also require students to say whether their answer would change if the panel were replaced by a single machine of known but imperfect reliability, so that the intention becomes evidence about the future action rather than a guarantee of it.

The marking scheme should name five must-hit points worth four marks each, twenty in total, and after each point give one line on the mistake that typically costs students that mark. Please commit to a definite position in the model answer rather than laying out the options and leaving the reader to choose: I want the model answer to argue for one verdict on the house version, and the marking scheme to reward that same verdict while still giving credit to a well-argued opposite answer on the points where the two verdicts share common ground. Finally, tell me honestly where the model answer's position is weakest — the objection you would expect from Priya, or from our statistics teacher — and say plainly whether you think the position survives it.
