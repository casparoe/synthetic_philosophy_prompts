So I'm 67, I spent thirty-one years as a soil chemist, and since I retired I've been working my way through philosophy on my own — mostly the ethics end of it. My wife says I've become insufferable, and she may be right, because here's what's been eating at me for about a month.

There's this notion of "standing to blame." The way I've got it: it isn't the question of whether what somebody did was wrong, it's whether you're in a position to hold it against them at all. You can be dead right about the wrong and still be the last person who gets to say it.

The plain version I keep running into is that the hypocrite has no standing, full stop — the man cheating on his taxes doesn't get to lecture the neighbour about taxes. But I've also bumped into a fussier line, that it isn't the hypocrisy itself that costs you, it's what the hypocrisy shows: if your own conduct is evidence that you don't actually accept the norm you're invoking, you're out, but if it's just weakness — you failing to live up to a norm you genuinely hold — you keep your standing. That second view makes a definate prediction, and I'd like a case that actually tests it instead of just asserting it.

So build me a pair of cases.

- Ordinary life, please. No courtrooms, no historic villains, no brain-in-a-vat machinery. A family, a lab, a workplace, a street.
- In both cases she's a hypocrite by any ordinary standard: she's doing the very thing she's coming down on him for. Same norm, same act type. Don't sneak out of it by making her two behaviours subtly different so that she isn't really a hypocrite — if that's the only way the case can be made to work, say so and tell me why.
- Her own conduct must not be excused, justified, outweighed, or a one-off under duress. No get-out-of-jail cards for her.
- Nothing else about her can be doing the work. She isn't a busybody, she isn't an outsider to the situation, she isn't performing for an audience, and she isn't the person he wronged. Keep her relation to him, the harm, the stakes and the audience identical in both cases. The two should differ in exactly one respect: what her own failure shows about whether she accepts the norm. In one, it shows rejection or self-exemption; in the other, only weakness.
- The verdicts have to fall out of the two views, not be written into the setup. If it only works because you've stipulated that everyone agreed she had no standing, it's no use to me. I want to see the plain view putting her out in both cases, and the fussy view putting her out in one and leaving her standing intact in the other — and I want to see why each says what it says.
- Keep each case to a short paragraph, and be explicit about what sort of blame we're talking about (she's holding it against him, not merely observing that he's a rotter). I suspect a good deal of the trick lives there.
- Then the checks. Which single feature of the pair is doing the work? What happens if I change that one feature back while holding everything else fixed — does the verdict flip? That's my control. And how does somebody who thinks hypocrisy always kills standing go after the second case: do they say weakness is just a quieter form of rejection, that she's exempting herself either way, or that her blame is really aimed at making herself feel better? I want to know whether I've got a counterexample or just a stipulation with nicer furniture.
- Keep the norm itself something no sane reader disputes, so the only argument left is about her standing. Don't hang it on meat-eating or anything where half the room thinks the norm is fake.
- No names and no reading list — I'm not going to chase references.
