I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on rational choice and moral theory, and we have reached the unit on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the result that if each individual's preferences over social outcomes satisfy the expected-utility axioms, if the social preference relation satisfies those same axioms, and if the social ranking respects unanimous individual preferences, then the social welfare function must be a weighted sum of the individual utility functions with positive weights. My instructor presents this as the most rigorous available argument that utilitarianism is the morality a rational agent would choose behind a veil of ignorance, and I want to know precisely where the argument bears weight rather than hear the proof again.

Below are six objections to that reading of the theorem. Please rank them from most to least damaging to the claim that the theorem vindicates utilitarianism, assign each a damage score between 0 and 1, and give one or two sentences justifying each placement. Where you judge two of the objections to be the same objection in different dress, say so explicitly.

1. The theorem's conclusion is a weighted sum, but each person's expected-utility representation is fixed only up to a positive affine transformation. Adding utilities therefore presupposes a common unit of interpersonal comparison, which the axioms themselves do not provide; the theorem appears to derive comparability while quietly assuming it.

2. The derivation requires every person to hold a well-defined ranking of lotteries that give them a chance of living any member's life, not merely their own. This idealizes people's evaluative capacities considerably: it is doubtful that ordinary people have any such preferences, so the pivotal step describes an idealized chooser rather than actual welfare.

3. Passing from a weighted sum to utilitarianism proper requires the weights to be equal, and equal weights require the further assumption that behind the veil each person is equally likely to be anyone. That equiprobability is a moral choice rather than a deliverance of the axioms, so the theorem yields at most weighted utilitarianism, with the substantive work done by an imported assumption.

4. The theorem treats each person's preferences as given and treats their satisfaction as the sole social good. It is therefore silent on the possibility that preferences are adaptive, formed under deprivation or injustice, so the utilitarianism it supports may be insensitive to the provenance of the preferences it aggregates.

5. Applying the expected-utility axioms to the social ranking presupposes that social choices are gambles with well-defined probabilities and that sure-thing reasoning is valid for collective choice. Where the relevant probabilities are subjective, two people may endorse the same policy before the fact purely because they disagree about what will happen — one expecting rain, the other not — so the unanimity that drives the result can be spurious, reflecting opposed beliefs rather than a shared judgment about welfare.

6. The theorem constrains a social preference relation as though society were a single agent with a coherent ordering. But a democratic collective with changing membership need not have any such ordering, in which case the object the axioms are supposed to constrain does not exist and the theorem has no application.

Two further questions. First, which of these objections, if successful, leaves the theorem's formal result intact but forces a reinterpretation of what it establishes? Second, if you had to strike one objection from the list as not genuinely directed at the theorem, which would it be, and why? I am not asking for the proof to be restated, and I do not need sources or readings; I want the ranking and the reasoning behind it.
