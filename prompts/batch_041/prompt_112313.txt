Ok so I'm a science journalist, I write a column about big ideas, and I've spent the last few months reading about time and I've ended up half-convinced of a position I now want you to try to break. Here it is: the direction of time — the fact that the past is fixed and the future is open, that causes come before effects, that we remember yesterday and not tomorrow — is nothing over and above the thermodynamic arrow. The fundamental laws are time-symmetric (or can be written that way), so all the asymmetry comes from the universe having started in an absurdly low-entropy state, and everything else, the psychological arrow, the causal arrow, records, memory, is downstream of entropy increasing. The low-entropy start is just a brute boundary condition we have to posit and that's that.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed this view actually is. Not the undergrad objections, the real ones. Some things I'm already chewing on, so don't just repeat them back to me as if they're news: does it survive the reversibility objection, and does it survive the fact that we seem to have time-asymmetric physics at the particle level that has nothing to do with entropy (I'm thinking of those particle decays that don't look the same run backwards)? And what about the weird cases — Boltzmann-brain style scenarios, or a universe with a low-entropy state at both ends, where you'd get two arrows pointing away from each other in the middle. Would a universe like that be a problem, or is the view flexible enough to just absorb it?

Here's the one that really nags me, and I think it's the test case I care most about: suppose we found a distant galaxy, or a whole region, where everything we could measure ran the other way — light that encoded stars un-forming, entropy locally going down, the whole thing. Is that a defeater, or does the defender just say fine, there's a second low-entropy boundary over there, the boundary conditions are just more complicated than we thought? I want to know whether any observation could actually count against the entropy-arrow view, or whether it's been quietly built so that every possible observation is compatible with it. If it is that flexible, tell me straight whether that's a fatal flaw or just the price of a view that's really about boundary conditions rather than about physics.

Rank the defeaters for me — which would be decisive, which would be awkward but survivable, which a committed defender would just shrug at. And be honest about which of these are things the defender would themselves admit counts against them versus stuff only critics say. I'd personally find a clean counterexample to the 'memory is downstream of entropy' claim alot more persuasive than any cosmological scenario, so weight things that way if you can.
