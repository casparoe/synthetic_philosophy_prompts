I have spent the last few weeks arguing with two friends from a seminar on agricultural ethics, and the three of us keep returning to a comparison that both of them rely on and that neither can agree about: a field under pest pressure is a patient, and an insecticide is a drug. The first friend, who works on antibiotic stewardship in human medicine, uses it to argue that spraying a crop on a calendar rather than when scouting shows that the pest population has crossed the threshold at which damage becomes likely is like prescribing antibiotics for a common cold. Her claim is not merely that this is wasteful; it is that it is a professional and moral failure of the sort we have learned to name in medicine, and that the remedy is structural — a grower should have to obtain something like a prescription from a licensed agronomist before buying a broad-spectrum product, much as a patient cannot simply buy the drug over the counter. The second friend takes the same comparison to license the opposite conclusion. If the field really is a patient, she says, then leaving a serious infestation untreated for fear of resistance is like a physician who withholds a drug from the person in front of her out of concern for the wider population: the resistance problem tells you to protect the drug, not to deny it to the patient who needs it now. She also prefers a different medical frame, chemotherapy rather than antibiotics — the pest is the tumour, the crop is the patient, and a certain amount of collateral damage to pollinators and natural enemies is the price of saving the whole.

What I would like is an audit of the analogy itself rather than a verdict on my friends. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — saying what plays the role of the patient, the physician, the pathogen, the drug, the prescription, the immune system, the side effects, and the public-health authority, and then say where the mapping breaks down. Be explicit about which of the disanalogies matter for the specific uses at issue (the culpability claim, the prescription claim, and the withholding claim) and which are merely decorative. I suspect a few of them matter a great deal: that the pest is not an invader arriving from outside the host but a participant in the interaction; that the identity of the patient is ambiguous between the crop, the agroecosystem of the field, and the grower's livelihood, with the analogy's moral verdict changing depending on which one you plug in; that the patient cannot consent, cannot be consulted, and has no stake in its own continuation of the sort a person has; that the physician here also sells the drug; and that eliminating the pathogen outright is a coherent and often desirable goal in a field in a way that it is not in a body. Finally, tell me whether either friend's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and in particular whether the substitution of chemotherapy for antibiotics strengthens or weakens the second friend's case. If the medical frame turns out to be elastic enough to support both readings, say so plainly, and tell me what analogy would do better — or whether the ethical work here has to be done by an argument that the picture merely illustrates.
