I've spent a long time in the rationalist-and-econ-blog corner of the internet, and I've ended up convinced of the Austrian side of the socialist calculation debate — but in a narrower, more specific form than the slogan version, and I want to know how exposed it actually is. I keep running into the argument in rationalist-adjacent writing on markets as distributed computation and in enthusiasm for prediction markets, so I'd like the version I actually hold to be stress-tested rather than the version that gets thrown around. State the position as I give it, then tell me what would count against it.

The position: no central planning authority — however benevolent, however well-staffed, whatever its data — can in principle achieve the allocative performance of a decentralized price system, because the knowledge it would need is not merely dispersed and partly tacit, but in significant part generated by the competitive process itself. On this reading the obstacle is not computational (we could stipulate unlimited compute) and not a matter of incentives (we could stipulate perfectly aligned, honest officials). It is that the relevant information does not exist in gather-able form prior to the market's own operation. I take this to be a discovery claim rather than a computation claim, and I take it to be distinct from Mises's earlier and stronger claim that rational calculation is flatly impossible without market prices — which strikes me as closer to a definitional point, and therefore more vulnerable.

Now stress-test it.

First, does the thesis survive the idealized planner? Imagine a planner with real-time, complete data on preferences and resources, unlimited compute, and fully aligned agents — i.e., stipulate away the two objections that are usually doing the work. Does anything remain? If what remains is only the discovery or novelty point, is that the same thesis, or a different one smuggled in to save it? I'd like the static-allocation version and the dynamic-discovery version kept separate, because I suspect they have different defeaters.

Second, which of the standard challenges are live rather than merely verbal: Lange-style trial-and-error market socialism, with a planning board that sets prices by iteration and lets managers follow rules; the observation that large firms already plan internally, so the firm-market boundary is a transaction-cost matter rather than a principled barrier; and the mechanism-design tradition, which treats markets as one incentive-compatible mechanism among many. For each, say whether it genuinely threatens the claim or only shows that the claim was never about what it seemed to be about.

Third, and this is what I most want: does the argument survive arbitrarily capable planners — the kind of system that could in principle hold and process everything? If the answer is yes, then the thesis is about the nature of the knowledge rather than its quantity, and I want that argued rather than asserted. If the answer is no, the position was a computation claim all along and I've been defending the wrong thing.

Fourth, the honesty check: this view has a way of being unfalsifiable — market success confirms it, planning failure confirms it, and planning success gets redescribed as markets in disguise (shadow prices, black markets, internal transfer pricing). Can it be stated so that it risks something? What is the sharpest falsifiable version, and what observation would actually move a committed holder?

Rank everything from fatal to merely awkward, mark which of these the position's own defenders would concede rather than only what critics allege, and tell me whether the insulation is a defect or an acceptable feature of a comparative-institutional claim.
