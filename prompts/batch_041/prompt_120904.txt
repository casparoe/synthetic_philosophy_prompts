I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who teaches a seminar on Orwell, and I'd like you to settle something we couldn't. We were talking about whether Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day is an illustration of Orwell's account of language and political thought or a counterexample to it. My friend's line is that the novel refutes the essay: Stevens's prose is everything "Politics and the English Language" demands and more — exact, unhurried, free of dying metaphors, precise about silver polish and pantry inventories and the exact temperature of a room — and since Orwell locates the corruption of language in staleness of imagery and lack of precision, a man who writes as cleanly as Stevens falls outside the essay's jurisdiction altogether. I think that is too quick, and that the novel is the best extended illustration we have of the thesis Orwell states when he says that "the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts" — only with the mechanism doing something the essay never quite imagines.

Here is what I want done with the material. Stevens is a man whose vocabulary of dignity, greatness, loyalty and the good of the house does his thinking for him, and the novel's central political facts — Lord Darlington's German sympathies and the diplomatic weekends at Darlington Hall in the twenties and thirties, the dismissal of the two Jewish maids, the fact that the master Stevens served spent his last years as the dupe of a cause that became the enemy's — are exactly the facts his narration cannot hold steadily in view. Take that seriously in both directions. Where does the novel bear out the Orwellian loop, a language worn smooth by years of service to a cause, now shaping what its speaker is able to perceive at all? And where does it press past the essay? Two things strike me as worth pressing on. First, Stevens's vice is not vagueness but omission: the sentences are clean, and the untruth lives in what goes unsaid, which is a failure mode Orwell's essay has no name for, because his specimens are all bad prose and Stevens's prose is good. Second, Orwell's remedy is essentially to say the thing plainly — the whole exercise is rewriting a corrupt passage into honest English — and the novel suggests that this remedy is unavailable in the case that matters, because Stevens cannot translate his feelings into plain speech without first knowing them, and the language is what stands between him and that knowledge. If that is right, the novel does not merely illustrate the essay; it shows that the essay's cure presupposes a speaker who already has a meaning and merely lacks the words for it.

So what I want is criticism, not a summary of either the novel or the essay: a reading that works through the scenes that matter — the evening Stevens's father dies upstairs while the guests are served below, the exchanges with Miss Kenton that Stevens reports and immediately disowns, the 1956 drive west and the small denials he makes to strangers along the way, and the ending, where he resolves to practise bantering and to serve his new employer as he served the old one — and says what each of them shows about the relation between Stevens's language and his thought. Two constraints. I want a verdict: tell me whether the novel, read honestly, confirms or refutes Orwell, and defend one side; I don't want a survey of possible readings with the difficulties noted on both sides. If you conclude that it splits the difference, then say which half of Orwell's thesis survives and which half dies, and be definite about it. And I don't need citations, a bibliography or a reading list — I have both texts in front of me, so work from whatever general knowledge of them you have and don't quote at length; paraphrase is fine. Begin your answer with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasons, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
