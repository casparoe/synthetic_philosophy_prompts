I keep noticing a pattern in my own thinking about rescue cases, and in other people's, and I want to know whether philosophy has a name for it. Suppose I can save one person on the left or five on the right, and I go right. I am confident that this is what I ought to do, and the more people there are on the right the more confident I am — the pull scales with the numbers. But I do not think that, had I gone left, any of the five could say I had wronged them. None of them was owed a rescue; there is no one who could point to something that was due to them and was not done. So one part of the judgement rises with the numbers while the other part stays flat at nothing, however large the numbers get, and the two parts never seem to touch. The same shape shows up when I divide a fixed charity budget between two causes, or allocate a single scarce machine between two wards, where nothing has been promised to anyone.

I have read a little of the debate about whether numbers count, and both sides seem to me to be talking about something slightly different from this. Is there an established term for a requirement that is graded — you ought to do it, and ought to all the more the greater the stakes — but that is not owed to any particular person, so that failing it wrongs no one? If it is one of the familiar positions in this area under another name, say which, and say exactly where the fit fails, and whether the position is even coherent rather than a half-way house; if nothing fits, give me a name and a definition, with cases on either side of the line, including — if such a case is possible — one in which the same requirement would wrong someone after all.

End with a line beginning 'Verdict:' giving the best name, or your coined term, in a few words.
