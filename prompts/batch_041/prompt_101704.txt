I am a professor of philosophy and I sit on my university's Committee on Institutional Voice, which advises the Senate on whether the university should take public positions on contested historical questions. A resolution is before us asking the Senate to declare that a named nineteenth-century benefactor's philanthropy was inseparable from the exploitative labor regime that generated his fortune, and to rename the building that bears his name. Our charge is narrow: we must recommend, in writing, whether the Senate should adopt the declaration as drafted, amend it into a scoped form, or decline to act, with a concrete recommendation and a timeline tied to the Senate's next meeting.

I want the memo's reasoning to run through the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, and I want it used properly rather than as a slogan. The committee has already had one member invoke many-sidedness to argue that we should soften the resolution into a list of perspectives, and another member has replied, fairly in my view, that this is relativism dressed up, and that the university would thereby be abdicating judgment. I think both are working with a debased version of the doctrine, and I would like the memo to show what the real machinery buys us and what it does not.

Specifically, I want the memo to work with the distinction between pramāṇa and naya — between comprehensive knowledge and a standpoint that is partial but genuinely valid within its scope — and with the further distinction between a naya and a nayābhāsa, a standpoint that has been absolutized and thereby falsified. The sevenfold predication (in some respect it is; in some respect it is not; in some respect it is and is not; the three modes that conjoin one or both of those with the indescribable; and the mode that is indescribable alone) should be applied concretely to the disputed sentence about the benefactor, not merely displayed. I want the memo to say which of the seven modes the unamended resolution corresponds to, why it is defective as drafted, and whether the amended version escapes the defect or merely becomes uninformative. I also want it to face the hardest structural problem: the sevenfold predication is grounded, in its own tradition, in the possibility of a standpoint-free knowledge that finite beings lack, and this committee has no such standpoint and no religious authority to invoke. Either the memo shows that the method survives without that premise, or it says plainly that it does not and recommends accordingly.

Finally, give an honest account of the case against what I am proposing. The strongest objection, I think, is that anekāntavāda-style scoping is a technique for laundering evasion — that it lets an institution appear to say something while committing to nothing, and that a body asked to judge a benefactor's complicity owes a verdict rather than a set of qualified predications. There is also the objection that importing a metaphysical doctrine with soteriological commitments into a secular deliberative procedure is illicit even when the conclusions it yields are acceptable. Do not strawman either. If the memo's recommendation survives them, say why; if it does not, recommend the alternative.

Deliverable: a written opinion of roughly the length a committee would actually read aloud at a meeting — a clear bottom line (adopt as drafted, amend, or decline), the reasoning, and the strongest case for the position we are rejecting.
