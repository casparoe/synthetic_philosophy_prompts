Imagine I'm a journalist working on a feature for a general-interest science magazine, and I've been asked to make the case that some of the driest debates in philosophy of language actually matter for how we think about language today. The piece I'm sketching is built around a real episode: the emergence of Nicaraguan Sign Language. In the 1970s and early 1980s, after a school for deaf children opened in Managua, deaf Nicaraguans who had previously been largely isolated -- many of them communicating only through idiosyncratic personal gesture systems, or "homesigns," that outsiders could barely follow -- came together for the first time in large numbers. What emerged over the following years was not a pidgin of gestures but a full language with its own grammar, developed by the children themselves and elaborated across successive waves of younger learners, who regularized and systematized the material they received from the older ones. I want to use this case to interrogate Wittgenstein's private language argument, and to interrogate the argument right back.

You are a philosopher of language with a deep working knowledge of the Philosophical Investigations and its reception, and I'd like you to think carefully before writing, since I'd rather have a disciplined analysis than a confident one. Start by laying out, in your own words, the load-bearing commitments of the private language argument -- the "beetle in a box," the diarist who records a sensation and has no criterion of correctness, the point that remembering a connection between a sensation and a name already presupposes something like a practice, and the broader rule-following considerations that push toward the idea that meaning requires a shared form of life. Then map the Nicaraguan case onto those commitments: who, on Wittgenstein's account, counts as the relevant community; whether the homesigning children, before they met, had anything we should call a language; and whether the emergence of a shared grammar among people who lacked any common language beforehand counts as a confirmation of the argument, a complication for it, or an outright anomaly. The pressure point I most want you to press on is what I've been calling the bootstrapping problem: Wittgenstein's account seems to require an already-existing shared practice to underwrite the norms of correctness, but Nicaragua looks like a case where such a practice came into being without one. Is that fatal, tolerable, or a sign that the argument needs a thinner notion of "community" -- perhaps something closer to a coordination convention that can arise among strangers rather than a form of life they must already share? Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: tell me what the Nicaraguan case, properly described, reveals about the argument that the argument's own proponents have not fully faced, and where you think the theory is simply silent rather than wrong. Please don't send me toward the literature; I'm after your reasoning and the mapping itself, and I'd rather you flag uncertainty about the empirical details than paper over it.
