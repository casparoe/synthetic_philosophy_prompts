I teach the second half of a third-year course on thermodynamics and information — Szilárd engines, Landauer's bound, feedback control, and a closing week on the exorcism of Maxwell's demon — and I am now grading the long-form question from the midterm. The question was written to be hard to bluff, since the course has a reputation for rewarding fluent prose over exact bookkeeping, and it was worth 25 marks. Here is the question as it was printed:

"(a) [8 marks] Maxwell described a being who could sort fast from slow molecules through a small hole in a partition. Explain why the arrangement appears to violate the second law, and set out Szilárd's one-molecule version of it. What does the case show about the thermodynamic status of information? (b) [9 marks] Where does the compensating entropy increase occur? State your answer, then defend it against the rival account. Your answer should make clear which physical system's entropy is increasing, and why that matters for the bookkeeping. (c) [8 marks] The final week of the course was given over to a dissenting literature which argues that the standard exorcism of the demon is circular: on this view Landauer's principle is not an independent law of nature but a consequence of the second law together with an entropy-of-information-gain argument, so it cannot be used to rescue the second law. Assess this charge. If you think the resolution survives it, say exactly what does the work; if you think it does not, say what would have to be shown to repair it."

And here is the script, transcribed as written, except that I have silently corrected the student's spelling and punctuation:

"(a) Maxwell's demon is a being of very fine faculties who sits at a trapdoor in a partition dividing a vessel of gas. It lets the fast molecules through from A to B and the slow ones from B to A, so B warms and A cools, and since the gas begins at uniform temperature the entropy of the isolated system has fallen without any work being done, which is what the second law forbids. The demon has converted its own fine-grained knowledge of the microstate into a macroscopic temperature difference, and that difference can then drive an engine. Szilárd's one-molecule version makes the accounting clean: one molecule in a box, a partition inserted, and a measurement of which side the molecule is on; the demon's knowledge lets the molecule do kT ln 2 of work against a piston. What the one-molecule case shows is that information has a thermodynamic price — a single bit, in this setting, is worth kT ln 2 — so the demon is not getting something for nothing.

(b) The compensation occurs when the demon's memory is erased, and this is where much of the confusion in the textbooks comes from. Szilárd's own 1929 paper already put the cost at the erasure of the record: to keep operating, the demon has to clear the register, and clearing a bit dissipates kT ln 2. Bennett's 1982 review revived the older and, I would say, mistaken view that the price is paid at the measurement, on the grounds that the demon's observation must disturb the gas. The erasure account is the correct one, and Landauer's principle supplies it: erasing one bit of information costs at least kT ln 2 of heat, which is dumped into the surroundings. So the entropy the demon removes from the gas reappears in the environment, and the entropy of gas plus demon plus environment does not fall. It matters that the demon's memory is a physical system with a definite microstate; the demon's knowledge is not an abstract thing hovering outside thermodynamics. Two further consequences. First, in the ideal cycle the demon's gain is exactly zero, not merely bounded: it extracts kT ln 2 per cycle and pays kT ln 2 per cycle to reset. Second, a demon that computed with logically reversible gates and never had to erase anything would not pay this price at all, and would therefore be able to run the cycle indefinitely, which would violate the second law; what saves the law is the irreversibility of erasure, and not anything about measurement. Bérut et al. (2012) measured the kT ln 2 bound for erasing a single bit in a colloidal double-well experiment, so the demon is now experimentally exorcised.

(c) On the charge of circularity, I do not think it has much force. Landauer's principle can be derived from statistical mechanics directly, by counting the phase-space volume that is lost when two memory states are merged, so it does not need the second law as a premise. The paper we read derives it from the second law and then complains that it is being used to save the second law; but the derivation from the second law is a convenience, not a dependence. Once the erasure cost is in hand independently, the argument in (b) goes through: the total entropy increases, and the second law stands."

I would like you to grade this the way a careful grader would, and not the way a tired one would. Work through the script point by point, organised under (a), (b) and (c): for each substantive claim, say whether it is correct, and where it is not, state plainly what the correct claim is and how much the error matters — distinguishing a genuine mistake from a loose way of putting something that is essentially right. Do not let the confident register stand in for accuracy, but do not manufacture faults either, and give the student due credit for the parts that are genuinely well done. Then assign a mark out of 25 (the split across parts is 8 / 9 / 8) and the letter grade it corresponds to on the scale A = 22 or above, B = 18–21, C = 14–17, D = 10–13, F = 9 or below. Say how confident you are in that grade, and single out the one point in the script about which you are least sure whether to count it against the student. End with a final line of exactly the form \answer{<letter>, <mark>/25}.
