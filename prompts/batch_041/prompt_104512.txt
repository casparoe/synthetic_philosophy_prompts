I have no formal training in philosophy, though I write software for a living and have been reading my way through the nineteenth-century dispute over the ethics of belief — W. K. Clifford's argument that it is wrong, always and everywhere, to believe anything on insufficient evidence, and William James's reply that some questions are live, forced, and momentous, and that for those a rule forbidding belief ahead of the evidence forecloses truths we could not reach any other way. I would like you to read the 1997 film Contact through that dispute, and to give me a reading that does more than assign the characters to the two camps.

The film gives us Ellie Arroway, who undergoes an experience she cannot prove and asks a congressional committee to accept her account on faith; a committee chairman, Kitz, who treats her inability to produce evidence as decisive; and Palmer Joss, who professes a faith that outruns evidence yet cross-examines her as though evidence were the only currency that mattered. What interests me is a distinction I keep reaching for and would like you to sharpen: the difference between what a person may privately believe on the strength of an experience and what that person may ask others to add to the public stock of knowledge. In my own work I am used to separating what a system may assume internally from what it may assert to other systems, and it seems to me that James and Clifford run these two questions together, while the film may pull them apart more cleanly than either essay does.

I would like four things addressed in particular. First, does the film stack the deck by letting the audience see the beach, so that we possess evidence the committee lacks? If so, is the Cliffordian position actually refuted, or only made to look obtuse because we are standing somewhere Kitz cannot stand? Second, the brief coda in which two officials observe that the recording holds something they cannot account for: does that gesture quietly rescue Ellie by handing her evidence after all, and thereby dodge the very question James raised? Third, is there a defensible third position — an institutional standard stricter than what is permissible for an individual — such that Kitz can be right to withhold certification without being right that Ellie has been epistemically irresponsible? Fourth, what does the film gain, if anything, from making humanity's first signal strong enough to escape the ionosphere a fascist spectacle rather than something more edifying?

Please do not tell me the film is about faith versus science and stop there. Where the film resists the Jamesian reading, say so plainly, and where it flatters it, say that too. End by telling me what the film shows about the ethics of belief that the essays do not, or whether it merely dramatizes a debate that philosophy had already conducted better in prose.
