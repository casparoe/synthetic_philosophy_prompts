Question: "Hart and Fuller's exchange over legal positivism and natural law is usually taught through the post-war prosecutions of Nazi-era informers. For the informer case, say what each theorist thought should happen to her, and explain how each one's general account of law leads him to that conclusion."

Student's answer:

"Hart and Fuller are usually taught as the two poles of the debate: Hart thinks law and morality are entirely separate, Fuller thinks unjust rules aren't law at all.

The case is the wife who denounced her husband under a Nazi statute and was prosecuted after the war. The post-war court held the statute was void because it outraged the sense of justice of all decent people -- essentially Radbruch's formula, though Radbruch isn't one of the two theorists here.

Hart didn't think she should go free. He thought she should be punished, but by a legislature passing a retroactive statute, not by a court pretending the Nazi law was never valid. This is where his core and penumbra distinction does its work: rules have settled applications and contested ones, and in the penumbra a judge is legislating, so honesty requires saying so rather than dressing the choice up as deduction.

Fuller thought that framing was wrong, because law isn't just a set of rules but a purposive enterprise, and Hart's picture leaves out the point of the enterprise. Fuller's positive view is the inner morality of law: eight principles like generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, stability, and congruence between official action and the declared rules. A regime that fails these wholesale doesn't produce bad law, it produces something that isn't law. That's why he could accept the post-war courts' reasoning.

And Hart's minimum content of natural law is a bigger concession than he lets on: once you grant that any working legal system has to have some moral content in it, the separation thesis is basically dead."
