I'm the chair of our town's reunion committee and I've been handed the after-dinner speech for the fiftieth anniversary of the flooding of Kessler Hollow — the valley they drowned to make the reservoir. The room is split right down the middle and that's what's making this hard. Half of them are the old-timers, the kids who watched the valley go under or the children of families who got bought out at prices everyone now admits were a scandal, and they have been waiting fifty years for somebody to stand up and say plainly that it was a wrong and that the town should carry it. The other half are their kids and grandkids — people who exist, in the most literal sense, because their parents moved up to the flats above the waterline, met there, married there, and had them there. The old-timers don't always see it, but alot of the younger ones do, and it's made them strange about the whole thing: they can't quite call the dam a mistake without feeling like they're saying they shouldn't be sitting in the room.

I've been reading about the nonidentity problem for months trying to figure out how to write this, and it's made me more stuck rather than less, because every draft I try either smuggles in "and so you shouldn't exist" or else it smuggles in "and it was worth it after all," and I won't say either of those in front of these people. My cousin, who teaches philosophy, says the honest move is to keep the wrongness of the act seperate from the wrongness of the world it left behind, and I think she's right, but I can't make that do any real work in a speech that's supposed to end with the room feeling something other than accused.

So write me the speech, six or seven minutes. It has to land for the old-timers without lying to them, land for the younger ones without asking them to be grateful for a crime, and not turn into a lecture in the middle of a potluck. If there's some form of apology that doesn't require me to wish any of them away, I want it in there; if there isn't, I want the speech to be honest about why not without leaving the room with nothing. Use one concrete thing from the valley as the spine of it — the orchard that's still standing underwater, the church bell they got out in time — pick one and let it carry the thing.
