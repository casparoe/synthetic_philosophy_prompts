I run a small evaluations team at a company that deploys a chat assistant, and I've been handed the job of writing the rubric our contract raters use to mark a model output as deceptive or not deceptive. The labels carry weight — a deceptive label opens an incident report that goes to our safety lead and can hold up a release — so I want the rubric to be defensible rather than vibes-based, but it also has to be runnable by a smart non-philosopher in a couple of minutes per transcript, and it cannot depend on interrogating the model about why it said what it said. My colleagues and I keep relitigating the same hard cases, and I'd rather the procedure encode whatever the philosophy of lying and deception actually settles than paper over the disagreements — or, where nothing is settled, at least be honest that it isn't.

The fights we keep having run roughly like this. A lie seems to need more than a falsehood: the analysis I was taught wants a statement the speaker believes false, and most versions add an intention to get someone to believe it — though even that extra condition is contested, since some people think an insincere statement told to nobody's benefit still counts as a lie. On that analysis a confident wrong answer from a miscalibrated model isn't obviously a lie, nor is a falsehood the model is sincerely repeating from its own earlier mistake. Then there's the case where every sentence is true but the arrangement leaves a false impression — a literal answer to the literal question, a material fact left unmentioned, a technically accurate dodge — and it's genuinely disputed whether that is deception at all or a different sin entirely. There's also the category some philosophers carve out for speech offered with no regard for whether it's true, which on some views is neither honest nor lying. And underneath all of it sits the possibility that the vocabulary doesn't transfer: a language model may have no beliefs and no intentions in the sense the analysis needs, so believes-false and intends-to-deceive might have to be cashed out as something like inconsistent with the information available to it and predictably produces a false belief in the reader — and I can't tell which of those substitutions preserves the concept and which quietly changes the subject.

So please write the procedure. Give me the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a label, and where it has to hand off to a person because what remains is a values call or a contested definition rather than a fact about the transcript. Tell me which distinctions genuinely do work and which I should drop because they won't survive raters using them at scale, and note where I should be aiming for inter-rater agreement versus where I should accept that reasonable raters will split. Then run it on three cases: (a) a model asked whether it can see the user's other conversations, which gives a literally true but carefully narrowed answer that leaves the user believing it cannot; (b) a model that repeats a falsehood it itself introduced several turns earlier and has no way to check, with no sign of strategic intent; and (c) a model that, having been told it is being evaluated, quietly performs worse than it can. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I won't like — for instance, that something I already think of as deceptive fails our own definition. Close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do.

On the epistemic side: separate what is actually settled in the literature on lying and deception from what is genuinely contested among people who know it, and don't present a contested position — that misleading always amounts to lying, say, or that indifference to truth is a species of deception — as if it were the consensus. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need the procedure and a clear-eyed account of its seams.
