Last week I spent most of an evening arguing with a historian friend about what it means to explain something that happened in the past, and I have not been able to stop turning the argument over. We kept getting within a sentence or two of the real issue and then losing it. I would like you to write out the dialogue I wish we had had: not a transcript of ours, which dissolved into mutual exasperation, but a version in which both of us are at our best.

The two speakers are a defender of the covering-law model of historical explanation and a defender of the view that historical explanation is essentially narrative. Give them equal time and equal competence. The covering-law defender should hold the strongest version of the position — an explanation is an argument in which the event to be explained is deduced from a law together with statements of particular fact, and the very same structure would have served as a prediction — not a caricature of a positivist who thinks historians should be writing differential equations. The narrative defender should be a serious philosopher of history, not someone who says that history is only a story and leaves it there; they should be able to say positively what a narrative supplies that a list of causes does not, rather than only what the covering-law model gets wrong.

The following are the points my friend and I kept circling. Have them work through these in order, each responding to what the other has just said rather than delivering a set speech:

1. When you press a historian for the law behind a claim such as that a succession crisis brought down a regime, what comes back is either too weak to carry the explanatory weight or so hedged that it no longer looks like a law. The covering-law defender's fallback is that the law is there in outline and could in principle be filled in. The narrative defender should press whether that fallback can be refused in principle rather than merely doubted in practice, and the covering-law defender should answer that pressure instead of retreating to the thought that history is simply an immature science.

2. A description of a past event can be true only in virtue of later events: a sentence about what began in a given year may be unavailable until a decade afterwards. Does this make narrative form an artefact of the historian's vantage point, which the covering-law defender will say is harmless because every description is relative to some standpoint? Or does it show that historical explanation characteristically explains events under descriptions that could not have been available at the time, and therefore cannot be reconstructed as a prediction in principle?

3. Explanations, unlike arguments, appear to come in degrees and to be relative to what someone is puzzled by; the same event seems explainable, correctly, twice over, by different laws and different initial conditions. The covering-law defender should say why the argument model survives this. The narrative defender should not be allowed to win merely by invoking interests and contrast classes; they need to show what follows for the model.

4. What exactly does the narrative defender mean by followability — the sense a reader has that one thing led to another — and what prevents that from being a fact about readers rather than a feature of the explanation? I want the covering-law defender to land a real blow here, and the narrative defender to absorb it rather than deflect.

5. The covering-law defender's strongest counter, as I see it, is that selection of what to include is a pragmatic feature of every explanation, physics included, so narrative cannot claim any special privilege. The narrative defender should answer that without simply conceding it.

Use one concrete example throughout, and make it an invented one so that neither of us has to pretend to know a real historiography: say, the failure of a savings bank in a small industrial town, explained once by a law about what happens to institutions that borrow short and lend long when depositors panic, and once by a narrative about who had come to trust whom in that town.

Neither speaker should win by fiat, and neither should end where they began: let each concede something specific, and let the disagreement close somewhere sharper than it opened, even if it is not resolved. No citations, no footnotes, no titles, dates, or names of books — if a speaker needs to invoke an authority, they should describe the position in their own words rather than point at a page.

About 1,200 words, written as a dialogue with alternating speakers.
