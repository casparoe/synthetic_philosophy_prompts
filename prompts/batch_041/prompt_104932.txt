I convene a small reading group on the metaphysics of mind, and we have spent several sessions stuck on what I can only call the careenium question, after Hofstadter's thought experiment: a physical substrate of countless tiny moving parts gives rise to stable, higher-level, symbol-like entities — the simmballs — whose interactions look for all the world like inference and memory, and we cannot agree on whether the simmballs push the substrate around or whether it is the substrate that pushes and the simmballs that merely ride along. One faction holds that only the lowest-level physical goings-on are causally productive, so that any statement about a simmball is a convenient redescription, useful for prediction but contributing no causal work of its own; the other holds that the simmball level is genuinely real and does causal work, and that it is a mistake to think the substrate competes with it, since the two are descriptions of one thing at different scales rather than rival forces. Both sides accept the same physics, neither has conceded a point in a year, and appeals to exclusion-style arguments on one side and to real patterns and explanatory indispensability on the other have hardened rather than resolved anything.

I want a diagnosis, not another survey of the positions. Tell me whether this is a substantive disagreement about what causes what, a verbal dispute in which 'cause' and 'real' are being used in two senses, or two groups answering different questions — one about what produces an effect, one about what explains it — under a single heading. I have a hypothesis I would like you to test rather than assume: that the impasse comes from the two sides operating with different standards for what it takes to do causal work, one demanding productive force and the other settling for difference-making within an explanation, and that once this is made explicit the disagreement shrinks to a question about which standard is the right one for the word 'cause'. If that hypothesis is wrong, say where it breaks down and where the real locus of disagreement lies. Conclude with an honest assessment of what, if anything, would move the debate — a distinction, a formal result, or a change of question — or an argument that no movement should be expected. No citations or reading list are needed; reason from the positions as I have described them and from whatever you know of the general problem.

Please lead with your diagnosis in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
