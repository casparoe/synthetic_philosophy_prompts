I'm thirty-eight, I run a two-man landscaping business in a town of about nine thousand people, I never went to college, and what I think about any of this I've worked out from twenty years of watching who gets praised around here and who gets quietly frozen out, a few books from the library, and a long-running argument with my own family. My grandmother came over from Jutland as a girl, and the word she used for the local attitude was Jantelagen — the Law of Jante — which comes from a satirical novel the Danish-Norwegian writer Aksel Sandemose published in 1933, set in a fictional small town called Jante. It's ten rules, all variations on one theme; the first reads "You're not to think you are anything special," and there's an eleventh that works less like a rule than a threat, along the lines of: maybe we know a few things about you. Sandemose wrote the rules as a portrait of small-town suffocation, but in Denmark and Norway the word is now used both as an accusation and, by some people, as a description of a decent way to live. The English-language cousin of it is the tall poppy syndrome — the Australian and New Zealand name for the reflex of cutting down whoever rises above the rest — and that image goes back to a Roman story about a king who walked through his garden cutting the heads off the tallest poppies to send his son a message about what to do with prominent men, though the Greeks told a version of it with wheat.

Next month the local business alliance has asked me to give the after-dinner talk at their annual success-story night — twenty minutes, no slides, then however long the questions run — and instead of telling a warm story about somebody's new patio I want to say something I actually believe. Roughly this: the American Dream gets defended as a claim about opportunity, that anyone here can rise, but the work it does in ordinary conversation is as a claim about desert — that the ones who rose earned it, and the ones who didn't, didn't. And the tall-poppy norm that everyone dismisses as simple envy isn't envy at all; it's a denial of a further claim, that achievement ranks people. I've been trying to pull apart four things that get bundled together: that anyone can rise; that those who rise earned it; that those who rise are worth more as people; and that nobody should be seen to rise. My line is that the third is false, the fourth is the poison, and the first two are separate questions that don't depend on either — and that my grandmother's ethic was right about the third and then slid, badly, into the fourth.

About sixty people will be in that room, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a speech. The audience: mostly owners of small businesses — contractors, a bakery, two insurance agents, a car dealer; several of them immigrants or the children of immigrants, for whom the Dream isn't a slogan but the reason they're here at all; a handful of old-timers who grew up under the don't-get-above-yourself norm and find the whole evening faintly embarrassing to watch; the woman who organizes the dinner, who wants a pleasant night and is already a little nervous about what I'm going to say; two or three younger people who listen to business podcasts in their trucks; the retired schoolteacher who runs the library's reading group and will want to know exactly what I mean by desert; and a reporter from the county paper. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, not the ones a university seminar would raise — ordered by how likely each is times how much damage it would do, and flagged as to which will come first, each phrased the way the person who'd raise it would put it, each with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which two or three I should concede before anyone raises them, which one I should hold even if it costs me the room, and what the single question is that I'm hoping nobody asks. I'm not going to stand up there quoting studies at people, so don't build the replies on research findings; arguments and homely examples are what I need.
