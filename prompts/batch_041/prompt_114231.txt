For the past few weeks I have been puzzling over the Pasadena game, which I came across while reading around on paradoxes of rational choice. The rules, as I have them: a fair coin is tossed until the first head appears, and if that head first appears on toss number n, the player wins a prize whose size is two to the n divided by n, with the sign flipping according to whether n is odd or even, so the prizes grow in size but alternate between gains and losses, and no single outcome is infinite — the trouble, if there is one, is only in the adding up. If you multiply each prize by the probability of the toss sequence that produces it and add the products in the obvious order — first toss, second toss, third toss, and so on — the running total settles down to a finite number, a little under seven tenths. But the very same products can be added in a different order to yield any total you like, or to run away to infinity, and I cannot see what in the description of the game would single out one order as the right one. Some people I have talked to say the obvious order is privileged and the game simply has that value; others say there is no privileged order and so the game has no expected value at all.

A friend in my reading group thinks the whole worry is misplaced, and he makes his case by analogy. A series, he says, can converge only because its positive and negative terms cancel, so that its sum depends on the order in which the terms are taken; the sum in the given order is perfectly well-defined, and the fact that rearranging the terms changes the sum is a familiar curiosity about infinite sums, not a reason to distrust arithmetic or to invent a new theory of addition. In the same way, he says, the Pasadena game has an expected value in the obvious order, the fact that a different order of addition would give a different answer is a curiosity about infinite sums rather than a defect in the game or in the theory of rational choice, and the correct response is to treat the game as a boundary case, decline it or accept it as we please, and go on using expected values as before. So the analogy is doing real work for him: it is what licenses the conclusion that nothing here threatens the standard theory of rational choice.

I would like you to audit that analogy for me. Begin by setting out the correspondence as explicitly as you can — what in the series story answers to the game, to the individual prizes, to the probabilities, to the order in which the terms are added, to the rearrangement that changes the total, and to the verdict that the series has no single sum. Then tell me where the correspondence breaks down, and which of those breaks matter for the use my friend is making of it. I am especially interested in whether the two verdicts are really the same kind of verdict: a series, after all, comes with an order built into it, so that the order is part of what the series is, whereas the game seems to come with a perfectly definite set of possible outcomes and probabilities and no order at all except the one we impose when we do the arithmetic, which makes me suspect that the game is in a worse position than the series rather than merely a parallel one. Finally, tell me whether my friend's conclusion — that expected utility theory is untouched and the game is only a curiosity at the boundary — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly carrying the conclusion all along. If the analogy is not up to the job, propose a better one, and say what it would and would not license.
