I'm assembling an application for a three-month residential fellowship at my department's centre for the study of rationality, and the deliverable is a short research proposal of no more than two pages. My half-formed idea is that the long-running quarrel over whether satisficing is a genuine alternative to optimising has been misframed from the start: both sides argue about whether the satisficer's choice can be *represented* as the maximisation of some utility function, and since almost any choice can be so represented, the argument never resolves. I want to move the disagreement from representation to procedure, by pulling apart two things the literature habitually runs together. Call the first search satisficing: what you do when finding a better option costs something, stopping when the expected gain from further search no longer justifies its cost — plainly a species of optimisation. Call the second evaluative satisficing, which seems to me different in kind: here the agent's acceptance criterion — its aspiration level — is fixed in advance of, and independently of, the particular option set it now faces, and is revised by feedback from its own past outcomes rather than by beliefs about the present problem. If that distinction holds up, the interesting question is not whether the satisficer maximises, but whether a criterion that is thus exogenous to the present choice problem can be given a coherent account of its rationality at all — and whether the account survives once we let the agent hold preferences over how its aspiration level gets set, which is where I suspect it may collapse back into optimisation after all. A colleague insists the whole distinction is verbal, which is part of why I want it nailed down properly rather than left as a hunch.

Please turn this into the proposal itself. State the question so that it has a determinate answer instead of trailing off into "it depends"; explain why the existing positions on satisficing leave it open, referring to arguments and positions as you need to but without citations, since I'll supply those myself; set out the approach that would actually make progress and why it beats the obvious alternative of simply redescribing the satisficer as an optimiser with an eccentric utility function; be candid about what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give a realistic sense of scope for three months. Keep the whole thing conceptual — I am not running experiments or building a simulation, and I have no wish to turn this into a modelling paper.

Give me the proposal as exactly five numbered items, one for each of those tasks, each no more than three sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
