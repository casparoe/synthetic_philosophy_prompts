I've come round to the view that implicature is purely a matter of speaker intention -- a sentence implicates something only because the speaker meant it and meant the hearer to recognise that. So on my view there's no such thing as an implicature nobody intended; where there's no intention there's just an inference the hearer made. What should count against this? I want the cases and arguments that ought to actually move me, sorted by how decisive they are, plus an honest read on whether I can wriggle out of every counterexample by positing a dispositional or unconscious intention, and if so whether that makes the view unfalsifiable and whether thats even a problem.

A few things I'm already chewing on: whatever rides on particular words rather than on intentions (the but, the therefore), the way some seems to imply not all by default, texts whose author is dead, and speakers who use a word without knowing what it connotes. Two I'd like you to think through that I haven't seen discussed much: a translator renders a sentence into another language and the result carries an implication the original didn't have -- did she implicate it? And when a language model emits a sentence that any human reader would read an implicature into, is there an implicature at all or only the appearance of one? Which of all these would a committed intention-theorist themselves concede counts against them, and which would they just wave off?
