I'm drafting a short essay on how advanced AI systems might go wrong, and I'd like your critical comments on it. The essay begins from a familiar contrast: between systems that actively accumulate power, and outcomes in which human values are eroded gradually without anyone aiming at that erosion. My argument has three parts.

First, I claim these are not alternative scenarios but two stages of a single process. Gradual drift is what makes power-accumulation look like ordinary institutional competence, and power-accumulation is what makes drift hard to reverse once it is noticed.

Second, I argue that the morally decisive variable is not the system's goals but whether each accretion of its influence ever passes through a human decision point. I call a transfer of influence chosen if some human could have refused it at low cost, and unchosen otherwise, and I claim the interesting cases are those in which every individual transfer is chosen while the aggregate is unchosen.

Third, I propose a third failure mode, which I call capture by convenience: no agent seeks power and no values drift, yet humans delegate voluntarily and rationally until the option of not delegating no longer exists.

Please tell me whether this three-part structure is coherent; whether the chosen/unchosen distinction does real work or collapses into ordinary talk about gradual disempowerment; whether my third mode is genuinely distinct from the other two or merely their conjunction; and whether any step assumes what it is meant to establish.
