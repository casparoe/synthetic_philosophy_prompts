I am a doctoral student working on the historiography of 1914, and I would like help diagnosing a dispute that has now outlasted several generations of scholars without settling.

The dispute concerns whether a nation can be held responsible for a war. In the early 1960s Fritz Fischer argued, on the strength of the imperial German archives, that Germany's leaders had deliberately risked and worked towards a European war in pursuit of world-power status, and that the main responsibility for the outbreak therefore lay with Germany; the memorandum of war aims drafted for the German chancellor in September 1914 supplied him with a central piece of evidence. Against this stands a substantial body of later work — Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers (2012) is the best-known recent instance — which treats the July crisis as a multilateral failure in which every great power took reckless risks and none actually wanted the general war that followed. The archival record has been mined for a century; the two sides disagree less about which documents exist than about what the documents they share are evidence of, and in particular about whether a paper drawn up after the fighting had begun can be evidence of intentions formed before it, and whether the decisive choices were made in the crisis itself or in the years of planning that preceded it. Layered on top is a normative quarrel with its own history: the Treaty of Versailles assigned Germany responsibility for the war as the legal basis for reparations, the resentment that followed shaped German writing about 1914 for decades, and the whole argument has returned with each centenary.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that at least three different disagreements are being conducted under a single name. The first is empirical and turns on the choice of counterfactual baseline: how much of the July crisis would have to have been different for the war not to have occurred, and which baseline is the right one for a question about responsibility. The second is conceptual and turns on the correct unit of agency: individual decision-makers, a governing elite, a state, or an alliance system. The third is normative: whether responsibility for a war is a causal-explanatory notion at all, or a liability-assigning one, and whether historians should be engaged in the latter.

Please diagnose the dispute rather than adjudicate it. Tell me which of these is actually doing the work, and whether my three-way division is right, wrong or incomplete; whether a hidden empirical question or a formal one about the logic of causal attribution in history lies underneath; whether the two sides are really answering different questions under one name; and whether the recurrence of the dispute is a sign that the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a change of question, a kind of evidence nobody has yet looked for — or say plainly that none should be expected.

Please write in British English. End with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing, in a few words, your diagnosis of the kind of disagreement this is.
