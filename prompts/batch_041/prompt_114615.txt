I've been going down a rabbit hole on this ever since my kid got diagnosed with ADHD and then the clinician started hedging with "maybe some autistic traits too" and "possibly a mood component," so now I'm sitting in waiting rooms reading philosophy of psychiatry on my phone and getting more confused, not less. The thing that's bugging me is that everyone keeps invoking Ian Hacking's looping effects — the idea that classifying people changes the people being classified, so the target keeps moving under the classification — like it's a checkmate, and then they just... move on and keep using their categories anyway. I get that it matters, I don't get what it's supposed to do to a theory of classification. And honestly the whole area feels like a pile of one-off objections (comorbidity is rampant, the same kid gets three different labels from three different clinics, other cultures don't carve it up this way, the reliability push that gave us modern diagnostic manuals seems to have bought consistency at the cost of validity, a category can be a real thing and also a tool of social control at once, and so on) with no one saying which of these are actual requirements and which are just pet peeves of one camp.

So here's what I want, and I want it before I read another book, because right now I can't tell a good theory from a bad one: write me the desiderata — the full spec — that any adequate theory of psychiatric classification has to meet. For each requirement give me the case or consideration that motivates it (why *this* has to be handled and not just waved at), and tell me which ones are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are preferences of a particular school or a particular era of the DSM. I especially want you to flag any subset of the requirements that you suspect can't be satisfied together — if there's a tension between, say, wanting categories to track something real and wanting them to stay responsive to the looping effects, or between cross-cultural applicability and clinical usefulness, I want that named, because that's the part I most want to chew on. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work at knocking out the popular views (the harmful-dysfunction line, the purely pragmatic/toolkit approach, the natural-kind approach, whatever else is in play), without doing a full scorecard — I'll take the scorecard to my reading group. I'm not asking for citations or a reading list, I just want the criteria and the reasoning behind them, laid out so I can apply them myself.

One thing: at the end, tell me how confident you actually are in this list and single out the requirement you're least sure belongs on it — I'd rather have a spec with the soft spots marked than a confident-looking one I can't trust.
