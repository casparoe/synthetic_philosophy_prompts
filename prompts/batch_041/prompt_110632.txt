I'm building the final exam for an upper-level seminar on transitional justice — we spend most of the term on South Africa's TRC, Argentina's amnesty laws and the later push to annul them, and Rwanda's gacaca courts. The problem is that every practice essay I get back is the same old peace-versus-justice seesaw with those three cases dropped in as illustrations. So I want one essay question that forces them to reason instead of recite, and I'm building it around a hypothetical country rather than a real one. Here's the scenario I've drafted:

Calvente's civil war ends in 2004 with a negotiated settlement. The settlement creates a truth commission and offers amnesty to anyone who, within eighteen months, gives a full account of their own acts. Two features make it not quite the usual deal. First, the amnesty doesn't vest on disclosure — it vests only when the commission publishes its final report, and until then it's merely provisional. Second, for ten years after the report, a beneficiary forfeits the amnesty and becomes prosecutable if he either publicly contradicts a finding of the commission or is shown to have concealed a role in planning or ordering killings, as opposed to only carrying them out. About 40,000 people receive amnesty. Fourteen years after the report, a new government reopens roughly 300 files. The reopened cases are almost all against people who were junior at the time but who, on the new government's account, planned or ordered atrocities and understated exactly that in their disclosures. The beneficiaries say the settlement was a political bargain the state has no right to rewrite after the fact; victims' groups say the state never had the power to trade away the right to a remedy, or to bind a later democratic majority.

Please write (a) the essay question itself — 90 minutes, one question — and I want it to turn on which of the reopened prosecutions can legitimately proceed and on what principle, not on whether Calvente should have had a commission at all; (b) a roughly 600-word model answer; and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 that seperates an A from a B. For the scheme, give me the five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, and for each one a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. I especially want the scheme to punish the two moves I keep seeing: treating "the deal was unjust" as though it settles "the deal was void," and treating retroactivity as a single yes/no rather than something that depends on what the amnesty actually promised and to whom.
