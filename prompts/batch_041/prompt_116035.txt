I am an undergraduate in my second year of a philosophy and medicine degree, and I am currently taking a module on the ethics of human enhancement. For my assessed portfolio I have to produce something that tests whether the therapy/enhancement distinction can survive being operationalised rather than merely stated, and I have decided to do it as a parody rather than as an essay. I would be grateful if you would write the piece for me.

The vehicle I have in mind is a fictional payer's coverage rulebook: an invented body, say the Wrenfield Health Assurance Board, issuing its annual Schedule of Covered Restorative Interventions. It should be written in the flat, self-assured register of an insurer's determinations document, in numbered clauses, with no hint that its author sees anything wrong with it. Roughly 700 words, with a title, a one-line issued-by header, and a closing clause on appeals. The header should make plain that the Board is invented and that nothing in the piece is a claim about any real insurer or regulator. It should be funny to someone who knows the enhancement literature and legible to a reader who does not.

The Board's rulebook, which you should set out briefly in a preamble, contains three tests and applies all three to every request. The first is a restoration test: an intervention is covered only if it returns the claimant to the level of functioning typical of a person of their age and sex. The second is an own-baseline test: where the claimant's pre-morbid level is below that typical level, cover restoration to their own pre-morbid level; where it is above, cover restoration only to the typical level. The third is an externality test: an intervention whose benefit consists largely in an advantage over others is not covered.

Then give six to eight determinations in which these tests collide, each with a named fictional claimant, a one-sentence statement of the request, the outcome, and a deadpan justification. I would like the schedule to work through at least the following: a claimant whose own baseline is below the typical level, so that restoration in the rulebook's sense is unreachable and the choice is between covering nothing and covering a lifetime of partial restoration; a claimant whose own baseline is above the typical level, so that a faithful application of the second test requires leaving them worse off than they were; an intervention that prevents rather than restores, and the awkward question of which test it falls under; the same intervention requested by one claimant with a diagnosed condition and by another who wants it for competitive advantage at work; a request whose benefit is plainly positional; a condition the Board itself reclassified by vote the previous year, with the vote recorded; and a request concerning a person who does not yet exist, where the own-baseline test has no baseline to work from and the Board must either invent one or admit that the test does not reach.

I would also like one determination that turns on prevalence: a condition that has become common enough to be statistically ordinary, with the Board having to decide whether the normal is something it discovers or something it sets. Please do not resolve that one cheaply; let the Board's answer be internally consistent, and let the reader see what it costs.

On the satire itself: the humour must come from the rulebook and its application, never from the claimants. No winking, no nudging the reader, no authorial asides about the absurdity of it all; the register should stay deadpan from first clause to last, and a reader should be able to work out what is being satirised without being told. Every absurdity should be a real feature of how such lines actually get drawn rather than an invention of yours. In particular, the piece should run into the problem that the distinction inherits its content from a definition of disorder; the gap between restoring typical functioning and restoring a particular person's functioning; the objection that on a purely welfare-based accounting there is nothing to choose between treating and enhancing; the objection that calling an impairment a deficit to be corrected is itself an expressive act with costs for those who have it; the difference between an absolute gain and a positional one; and the difficulty of applying a baseline test to someone whose baseline has not yet been fixed. I do not want those points stated as objections — I want them to be the mechanism that generates the jokes.

No references, citations, footnotes to literature, or reading lists, and do not attribute any view to a named author; if you need to gesture at a position, describe it in plain terms instead. British English spelling and idiom throughout.
