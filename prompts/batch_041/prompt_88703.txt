Write a pantoum in the voice of a proof-based agent that trusts itself: its one big rule is that whenever it has proved something, that thing is true. The agent is also smart enough to reason about its own proofs. The catch is Löb's theorem -- roughly, for a system like this, proving that something is true-if-provably-so is as good as proving the thing itself, so a self-trusting agent that can do a bit of logic about itself ends up able to prove anything at all, including the negation of anything at all, which is the exact opposite of being trustworthy. Let the pantoum's repeated lines carry that: the lines that keep coming back are the trust principle reasserting itself, and the trick is that each time they return they mean something worse, until the loop shuts and the whole thing detonates. (Quick reminder of the form in case you need it: quatrains where lines 2 and 4 come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last stanza folds back into the opening line.) At some point the agent should reassure itself that it only trusts itself for good reasons -- that should be the most unsettling line in the poem. Someone who hasnt run into the Löbian obstacle before should still get the creeps, and someone who has should feel like the logic was inevitable.
