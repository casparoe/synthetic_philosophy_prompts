I run a small telly drama outfit and I keep having the same arguement in different rooms, so I want to settle it properly.

We adapt real lives. Right now it's a woman who ran a refuge in the 1870s — dead 140-odd years, no descendants anyone can find. Episode three gives her a screaming row with her sister that isn't in any source, because otherwise episode three is her writing letters. Episode five puts her at a deathbed she provably wasn't at, because that's where the guilt in her diary comes from. Our head of legal shrugs: she's dead, you can't libel the dead, crack on. Our script editor says that's a cop-out, we owe her something. Both of them keep saying we owe and we don't owe as if it were obvious what that even means, and I've realised I dont have a view. I'd like one, and I'd like it to be the kind I can actually use — first to make the call, then to defend it to a furious family in a hotel bar.

So before I read one more think-piece about truth versus drama, give me the spec. What does any half-decent account of what we owe the real dead when we dramatise them have to be able to deliver? I want the requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't just collapse, the things it can't contradict — with the case sitting behind each requirement, and a note on which ones are genuine requirements and which are just one camp's taste dressed up as a principle. If some of them can't be held together at once, say so and say which pair, because I suspect there's one and that it's doing all the work.

Cases we keep tripping over, in case they're any use:

- the invented scene that's psychologically bang on and factually invented;
- the composite — two real women quietly welded into one;
- the one we only know from a court record, so the only scenes we can document are the worst week of her life, and inventing her a happier Tuesday might be the fairer thing to do;
- the one who asked for her papers to be burned, and someone didn't burn them;
- the one who was genuinely awful, with a family who'd rather that never got made;
- the one whose own account of herself the archive contradicts — she says she was content, the letters say otherwise, and I can't tell which of the two we're supposed to be faithful to;
- the one who's a symbol to a community, so any version of her gets read as a verdict on them;
- and the caption at the end saying some scenes were imagined — does that discharge anything, or is it a fig leaf?

Two last things. Does it matter that this is telly and not a novel or a history? I think it might, and I'd like you to say why. And if you want to close by saying how the obvious camps come out against the list, keep it to a paragraph.

No reading lists please, and don't tell me which dead philosophers said what — I want the criteria, not the cast.
