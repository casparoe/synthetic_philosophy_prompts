I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on moral psychology and metaethics, and I need one self-contained thought experiment that does conceptual work rather than merely illustrating a familiar point. The seminar's guiding question is whether the empirical psychology of moral disagreement — specifically the apparatus of moral foundations, understood as relatively stable dispositions to be moved by care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation (and the liberty/oppression foundation sometimes added to the list) — can bear at all on the question of who is right when people disagree morally.

To keep the case from collapsing into something already rehearsed everywhere, I want you to build it around a distinction I am stipulating rather than borrowing: treat each foundation as having two separable components. The trigger is the feature of an act or situation that fires the intuition; the warrant is the consideration that would justify the resulting verdict to someone who does not already share the intuition. Your task is to construct a case in which two people have identical foundation triggers and nevertheless reach opposite verdicts, with the divergence located entirely in their warrants.

Constraints:
- Realistic and contemporary: an ordinary institutional, professional, or civic dispute — not a trolley case, not a science-fictional setup, not a lifeboat.
- No disagreement over any non-moral fact. The two people agree completely on what happened, what the consequences are, and who did what. This constraint is load-bearing; if the case smuggles in a factual disagreement it has failed.
- The two people must not differ in the strength, ranking, or salience of their foundations, and neither may be portrayed as irrational, confused, or as suffering from a defective psychology.
- The scenario itself should occupy a single paragraph.

After the scenario, provide the following, in order:
1. For each person, name the foundation being triggered, state the warrant they offer, and demonstrate that the triggers are the same in both cases while the warrants come apart.
2. State what the moral-foundations apparatus, taken as a predictive model that runs from a foundation profile to a verdict, predicts here — and show either that its prediction is defeated, or that rescuing it requires the addition of something the theory does not itself supply.
3. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work. Then remove that feature and describe what the case degenerates into.
4. Give the strongest reply available to someone who insists the psychology of foundations remains relevant to the normative question — that is, an MFT defender who claims your case does not show what you say it shows.
5. Close with an honest statement of what the case cannot show: which part of the question whether the psychology tells us who is right it leaves untouched.

The case is meant to open a seminar meeting, so it must generate its verdicts from the stipulated components rather than stipulating the verdicts directly; a case that simply declares the two verdicts and asserts that they are equally warranted will not be usable. Do not cite any literature — refer to positions and arguments by description, and I will supply references myself.
