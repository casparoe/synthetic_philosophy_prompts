I've been mainlining LessWrong and the old SSC archives since forever, and through a series of poor life choices that has turned into me teaching a fourth-year elective on evolution, mind, and design-without-a-designer. I'm writing the final now.

I want ONE essay question on the cranes vs skyhooks thing and greedy reductionism. Every year half the class answers by reciting the slogan (cranes good, skyhooks bad, Darwin killed the skyhooks) and then waving at Skinner as the greedy reductionist, and I get nothing that tells me whether they understand it. I want something that punishes that.

So here's the new stuff I'm injecting, and the question has to make them actually use it. In the reading group we ended up coining two terms of our own:

- skyhook laundering: using respectable crane-ish vocabulary ("emergence", "self-organization", "complexity", "it's just information processing") to smuggle in something that does the lifting for free.
- crane laundering: the opposite error, dismissing a real bottom-up mechanism by re-labelling it a skyhook because it doesn't feel mechanistic enough, or insisting a skyhook is needed where a crane is already sitting there doing the work.

I want the question to force them to state a test that actually separates a crane from a skyhook (not a vibe, not a slogan), apply that test to three short cases I give them, and then say which of the three, if any, is really a case of greedy reductionism rather than a skyhook. Cases, swap them for equivalents if you think of better ones but keep the flavour: (1) metabolism-first origin-of-life stories where autocatalytic sets are supposed to get you replicators for free; (2) the claim that the self is a center of narrative gravity -- crane, or does it launder a skyhook?; (3) a Hayek-ish claim that market order is spontaneous and nobody designed it. One of those three is a deliberate trap where the obvious label is wrong. I won't tell you which -- nailing that down is part of what I want from the model answer.

Deliverables: the essay question (60-90 min, closed book, no citations needed); a model answer of around 650 words that would get full marks; and a marking scheme out of 25 with the must-hit points and their marks, plus a list of named misconceptions that should cost marks -- I especially want one that catches students who think greedy reductionism just means "saying X is nothing but Y", and one that catches students who think any appeal to emergence is automatically a skyhook, and one line per point on how they usually get it wrong. Finish with a single line saying what one move seperates an A from a B. Don't let the model answer quote the famous slogan verbatim, I want them paraphrasing what the distinction is doing.
