I am taking an upper-level seminar on just war theory, and in section this week a classmate defended the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants with an analogy I cannot shake. His claim was that two opposing soldiers are like two boxers in a sanctioned match: each has consented to the contest, each fights within a shared set of rules enforced by a referee, and neither is doing anything wrong simply by trying to defeat the other. On this picture, asking which soldier is in the right is like asking which boxer deserves to win — it misses the point, because the rules themselves, and not the merits of either cause, are what make the violence permissible on both sides. He then used the analogy to conclude not only that individual combatants on both sides are equally blameless, but that we owe the soldier of an unjust aggressor the same respect and the same honors as the soldier who resisted him.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than simply reject or endorse it. Begin with an explicit table of correspondences: identify what in the analogy is supposed to map onto the consent of the combatants, onto the rules of war, onto the referee or enforcing authority, onto the stakes of the contest, and onto the spectators or bystanders. Then tell me where the mapping breaks down, and — this is the part I care about — sort the breakdowns into those that actually matter for the conclusion he wants and those that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in whether the notion of consent can survive translation from a voluntary sport into a context of conscription, self-defense, and civilians who never agreed to anything, and in whether the analogy quietly imports features of boxing — that the contest is contained, that both parties want to be there, that the harm is bounded by rules both sides can exit — that war conspicuously lacks. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion about equal blame and equal honor survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the work; and if you think a better analogy is available for the same conclusion, say what it is and why it fares better. Please write your answer in American English.
