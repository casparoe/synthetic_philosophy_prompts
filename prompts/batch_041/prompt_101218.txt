The Lucas–Penrose argument — that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind cannot be a machine — has been in play for more than half a century, and the exchange has settled into the texture of a stalemate: each side has a fixed repertoire of moves, each move has a standing reply, and no one's position has moved. The anti-mechanist exhibits a sentence that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic cannot prove but that we, surveying the system from outside, can see to be true, and concludes that we are not that system. The mechanist replies, in one form or another, that the argument requires the anti-mechanist to do something he cannot do without circularity — to identify the system that is supposed to formalize him, or to certify that system's consistency, or to show that his own mathematical output is the output of a single fixed consistent system at all — and that once these demands are made precise the conclusion evaporates. The anti-mechanist answers that the mechanist is describing an idealized reasoner no one claims to be, and the cycle begins again. I am not asking you to adjudicate who is right. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, and I want it to engage the arguments as they actually run rather than their slogans.

Is this a disagreement that a formal result could settle — some sharpened theorem about what a consistent, sound, recursively enumerable reasoner can certify about its own soundness — or is the formal matter already settled, with the real disagreement located elsewhere: in a suppressed empirical claim about whether human mathematical practice is consistent, in a suppressed psychological claim about what we can non-inferentially see to be true, or in a disagreement about what the phrase 'the human mind' even denotes? My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than grant, is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about Gödel at all. They are using 'the human mind' in two senses — one an idealization (a single fixed, consistent, recursively enumerable system, the only kind of thing to which the incompleteness theorems apply directly) and one a natural kind (actual, error-prone, temporally extended reasoners whose collective output need not be the output of any one such system) — and each side quietly helps itself to the other's sense at the very step where the argument either goes through or fails. A second suspicion, which may or may not be independent of the first: the debate also stalls because critics are often arguing that the Gödelian argument does not establish its conclusion, while defenders hear the concession that the conclusion might nonetheless be true as a partial victory, so that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading.

Work out what kind of disagreement this actually is, on the strength of what each side's argument commits it to. Say whether any formal result, conceptual distinction, or empirical finding could move it — in particular, whether the mechanist can make do with a machine that is not a single system but a never-completed sequence of systems, each proving the consistency of its predecessor, and whether that is a genuine reply or a change of subject. And if you conclude that this impasse is not the kind of thing that gets resolved, say why, and what would have to change for the question to become tractable.
