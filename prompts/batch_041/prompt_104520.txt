I am a law and philosophy student writing a position paper for a seminar on the Hart–Devlin debate, and I would like a consistency audit of my own commitments before I commit to a line. Here is where I have landed.

(1) The only legitimate ground for criminalising conduct is the prevention of harm to others, where harm is understood as a setback to someone's interests.
(2) Legal moralism is false. The bare fact that conduct is immoral gives the state no reason to prohibit it, and attempts to rest criminalisation on the erosion of a shared morality are either moralism in harm's clothing or rest on empirical claims nobody has made good on.
(3) The offence principle is sound: serious offence to others can justify criminalisation in its own right. In particular, the distress a person suffers from knowing that others are engaged in conduct she regards as profoundly immoral can rise to the level of serious offence.
(4) The state must be neutral among competing conceptions of the good life, and any criminal prohibition must be justifiable to all reasonable citizens.
(5) Individuals hold rights that constrain what a democratic majority may enact, and those rights are not themselves subject to a vote.

Some of these clearly sit together without effort, but I suspect that at least one pair does not, and I would rather discover the problem now than in the seminar. Please tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one say what the least costly revision would be and what that revision would cost me, including anything it would force me to abandon elsewhere in the list. Where a tension is merely apparent, say briefly what dissolves it, so that I do not mistake a real disagreement for a verbal one.
