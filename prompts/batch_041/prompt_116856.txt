I am to give the principal address at the dedication of a deep geological repository for spent nuclear fuel: a facility designed to be sealed and left undisturbed for ten thousand years, a span longer than any language or polity has so far survived. The audience will be mixed. In the front rows sit the geologists, engineers, and risk analysts who designed the site and who speak, without embarrassment, of discount rates, present value, and expected dose. Behind them are residents of the county that volunteered to host the repository, who have heard the word discount used about their own grandchildren and are not certain they care for it. A handful of officials are present who will be quoted. I want to use the occasion to say something true about what it is to make a promise to people who cannot yet exist, and to do it without either the uplift such occasions invite or the evasion they permit.

Please write the address: roughly twelve hundred words of spoken prose, in which the philosophy does structural work rather than decorating the podium. I want it organized around two distinctions I keep failing to state cleanly when I argue about this in private. The first is between discounting a future benefit and discounting a future person's claim on us. A rate applied to the first is a claim about how much a thing will be worth; a rate applied to the second is a claim about how much a person will count. I suspect the whole quarrel over long-horizon policy is muddled by sliding between the two. The second distinction is between discounting because we are uncertain that the future will arrive or that our efforts will survive it, and discounting merely because the future is distant. I want both distinctions made in public, before people who will notice if I cheat, and I want the address to survive the obvious objections rather than dodge them: from the economist who insists that any positive rate is simply the shadow price of capital and has nothing to do with ethics; from the listener who says that caring equally about everyone ten thousand years out is a counsel no one has ever followed, and that pretending otherwise is a lie told in a kindly voice; and from the person who asks why, if we truly care about distant generations, we are spending this money on a monument instead of on something that would help them.

Do not resolve these difficulties by declaring that we should weight all future people equally. That is too easy, and this audience will hear it as easy. I would rather the address end somewhere more defensible and less comfortable, perhaps by separating what we owe as a matter of justice from what we can honestly promise, and by conceding what the repository does and does not accomplish. You may invoke arguments without assigning them to particular authors; I would rather this not become a roll-call of names. No bullet points or headings; this is to be spoken aloud. End on something the argument has actually earned.
