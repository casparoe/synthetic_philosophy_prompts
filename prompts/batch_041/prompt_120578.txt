I teach a first-year ethics section and I want to run a "which view about tiny harms do you actually hold?" exercise. The case we start from is the old one: one person tortured for fifty years, versus some absurdly large number of people each getting a single dust speck in the eye for a second. Make the number as silly as you like — googolplex, bigger than the number of atoms in the universe, whatever. Almost everyone in the room says torture is obviously worse; almost everyone who does the arithmetic says the specks are. I want a decision tree I can hand out that tells me which position each student is actually in, based on what they say about cases, not on which camp they claim to belong to.

Two things I care about. First, build it from the logical space, not from a list of isms: what are the actual questions you have to have an opinion on before this case has an answer, and what combinations of answers are possible? Then put the positions in that space. Second, a bunch of my students will say "torture, obviously" and I want the tree to tell me *why* — so any two leaves that give the same verdict on the original case need a question further down that pulls them apart.

Some splits I think belong on the tree, though you should tell me if I've got them wrong. Whether "worse" even applies to comparisons this lopsided, or whether the honest answer is that the two options are incomparable / that there's no fact of the matter / that it's vague. Whether there's a level of suffering that no amount of nuisance can outweigh, and if so whether that threshold lives in the *kind* of experience (agony vs. annoyance) or in the *quantity* of it. Whether a harm has to be experienced by someone to count at all — and here's a case I specifically want on the tree: swap the dust specks for something nobody experiences, like a billion sleeping people each having one hair moved a millimeter, or drop people entirely and put specks of dust on a billion rocks. Does your tree have a branch for that, or does the question just evaporate? Whether it matters that the specks land on many different people rather than all on one person — same person getting 10^20 specks in a row is a case I suspect splits people who look identical on the original. Whether the question is about which outcome is better or about what an agent may do, and whether those come apart here. Whether the physical impossibility of the scenario (there aren't that many people, or that much time) is a real objection or a dodge. And whether the intuition "torture, obviously" is evidence or an artifact of a mind that was never built to think about numbers like this.

Give me 8–10 branch nodes and 6–9 leaves. Each leaf gets a name if it has one and "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any two leaves your tree can't separate. Mark the cells in the space that nobody occupies and say why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Dont bother with citations or reading lists, my students can't look anything up anyway. Write it in American English, and tell me which split you're least confident is a real split and what would change your mind about it.
