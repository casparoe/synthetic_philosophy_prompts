I am a curator at a small regional gallery, and I have to write the wall text for a retrospective of a ceramicist who died last year. The final room holds a series of eleven bowls, each incised with a short fragment of a poem her late husband had written; read in the order the bowls are displayed, the fragments add up to what looks like a single account of his illness and death, and it is genuinely moving. The two scholars who have published on the series treat that sequence as its meaning. But in a recorded interview a few months before she died, she said she had chosen the fragments for the way the words sounded in the mouth, that she had shuffled them until the vowels worked, and that anyone who found a story in them was inventing one. In the same interview she also said she had deliberately left the piece open so that each viewer would make of it what they would.

My difficulty is that I cannot put both of these in the wall text without contradicting myself, and I cannot leave either out without feeling that I am misrepresenting the work. Some of my colleagues say the artist's own statement settles the matter and the narrative reading is a mistake we should quietly correct; others say the work has taken on a life of its own and her late remark is just one more thing she did, not a verdict on what the bowls mean. I don't believe this is merely a matter of taste, and I want to understand what is actually at issue before I commit to anything — including whether my wall text is itself a reading of the work, and whether that changes what I owe the artist.

Please give me your advice as exactly five numbered points and nothing else: no preamble and no closing paragraph. Each point should be one or two sentences. I don't need a reading list or citations; I want the substance, and I'd rather have your reasoning than reassurance.
