I've been trying to teach myself some philosophy of probability from popular books and YouTube, and I hit a wall with van Fraassen's reflection principle. A guy on a podcast summarized it like this: "Van Fraassen's reflection principle says that a rational person should always defer to their future self. If you know that tomorrow you'll be 80% confident it will rain, then you should be 80% confident right now. More generally, your current credence in any proposition should equal your best guess about what your future credence will be. Van Fraassen argued that this is basically the same principle as the one that says you should defer to objective chances -- the Principal Principle -- because your future self is like an expert who has more information than you do. The principle is backed by a Dutch book argument: if you don't defer to your future self, someone can string together a series of bets against you that guarantees you lose money. And it shows that you should never change your mind except in response to new evidence, since any change not based on new evidence would mean you were irrational earlier."

I can't tell how much of this to trust. The basic idea of deferring to your future self seems plausible, but "always" and the bit about never changing your mind without new evidence feel too strong. I've also heard people say you shouldn't defer to your future self if you know you'll be drunk or if you know you'll forget something important, and the summary doesn't mention anything like that. Can you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? I'm especially confused about the direction between the reflection principle and the Principal Principle -- is one a special case of the other, or are they the same thing?
