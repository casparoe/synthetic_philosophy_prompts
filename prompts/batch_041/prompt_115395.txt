Question 3 (20 marks). Newton's bucket is commonly offered as proof that motion is absolute. Set out the experiment and the reasoning Newton draws from it; give the relationalist reply in the form Leibniz gives it; and assess how far the experiment settles the dispute between substantivalists and relationists about space. Be explicit about the inferential steps in your reconstruction.

The script reads as follows. Newton's bucket experiment runs in three stages. A bucket of water hangs from a twisted cord. When the bucket is released and begins to turn, the water is at first still and its surface flat, though water and bucket are moving relative to one another. As the water is gradually dragged round, the surface becomes concave. When the bucket is stopped, the water goes on turning and the surface stays concave, though water and bucket are again moving relative to one another. The shape of the surface therefore does not track the relative motion of water and bucket: at one moment the water is flat while the two are in relative motion, at another it is concave while the two are at rest with respect to each other. Newton's two globes make the same point without any bucket at all: two spheres joined by a cord and turning about their common centre in an otherwise empty region would reveal their rotation in the tension of the cord, although there is no external body for them to be rotating relative to. Newton's conclusion is that the water's rotation is rotation with respect to something other than the bucket, namely with respect to space itself. And because the rotation is a real, causally efficacious state of the water, the space with respect to which the water rotates must be real as well. The experiment therefore establishes substantivalism: space is an entity over and above the bodies in it.

Leibniz's reply, in the correspondence with Clarke, is that the concavity is a real effect and so must have a real cause, but that the cause need not be space. The water turns relative to the fixed stars and the rest of the matter in the universe, and it is this relational fact that explains the deformation. To infer absolute space from the bucket is to posit an unobservable entity that does no explanatory work which the relations among bodies cannot do. Leibniz's deeper argument is that absolute space is a metaphysical impossibility, because it would violate the principle of sufficient reason. If space were a substance, God could have created the material world three feet to the east of where it is, and the two worlds would be indistinguishable in every observable respect. Since we could never discover which of the two we inhabit, absolute space commits us to a distinction without a difference. This is an argument from the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and it is what finally defeats Newton's inference. Mach takes up the relationalist side in the nineteenth century, arguing that inertia is not something a body has on its own but arises from its relation to the whole distribution of mass in the universe. General relativity is often read as implementing this idea, since the field equations tie the metric to the distribution of matter. The modern verdict, then, is relationalist, and the bucket argument, whatever it shows about the failure of naive relative motion, does not show that space is a substance.
