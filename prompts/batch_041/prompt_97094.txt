I'm in a reading group that's slowly working through Aristotle's logical works and I've got stuck on the famous chapter of On Interpretation about future contingents — the ninth chapter, the one with the naval battle. I'll try to lay out the bit that's confusing me in my own words rather than reaching for a translation, since we're all working from different editions. The argument as I understand it goes something like: every assertion is either true or false, and if an assertion about the future is true now then the event it asserts is fixed already; if it's false, the event is ruled out. So if it's either true or false now that there will be a naval battle tomorrow, then tomorrow's battle is already settled one way or the other, and the same reasoning applies to everything — nothing would be contingent, deliberation and action would be pointless, and things would happen of necessity. Aristotle then says something that looks like a refusal: in the case of things that are going to happen and could go either way, neither the affirmative nor the negative is simply true or false, though they may be more likely one way. But then he also seems to want to keep the law of excluded middle, and he brings in a distinction about what is necessary when it is — that a thing which is, is necessarily while it is, but that this doesn't make it necessary without qualification.

What I can't get straight is how the two halves of that fit together. Does he genuinely give up bivalence for singular future-tense assertions, or does he keep bivalence and instead mess about with the scope of the necessity — reading the disjunction as necessary but neither disjunct as necessary? And if it's the latter, why does he think the actuality principle (that what is, is necessarily when it is) does any work here, given that it looks like it either proves nothing about the future or proves too much about the past as well? Relatedly, I don't see why the past case is supposed to be different: if I can say truly now that it was the case yesterday that a battle would occur tomorrow, isn't that the same structure coming at me from behind, and if Aristotle accepts the past case while rejecting the future one, what does he take the asymmetry to consist in — our knowledge, the events themselves, or something about the tenses? I'd like someone to walk me through the moves in order, tell me where the ambiguity in the argument actually lies, and be honest about which readings of the chapter are scholarly reconstructions doing heavy lifting and which are genuinely in the text. If a modern tense-logician's framework would tidy it up, I'd like to know what gets lost in the tidying.
