Blaise Pascal's remarks on justice and force are scattered and fragmentary, so any reconstruction of a "Pascal position" has to be argued for rather than assumed. The reconstruction to be used here holds that the just is what is established — that our laws are taken to be just not because reason certifies them but because they are established; that a justice which cannot command force is helpless, while a force that does not answer to justice is tyranny; that because justice is always disputed while force is not, the world has in practice settled for making what is strong pass for just; that the foundations of political authority lie in custom, imagination, and force rather than in reason; that it is dangerous to tell the people that the laws are not just, since they obey only because they believe them just, so that one ought to say that laws must be obeyed because they are laws and superiors because they are superiors; and that a true justice is not to be had in the political order at all.

Analyze the trial and execution of Louis XVI as a case for that position. Treat the following as given, and do not spend the analysis adjudicating fine points of revolutionary chronology or the constitutional arguments: the deposed king was tried before the National Convention, which was at once the legislature and the court; the constitution of 1791 had declared the king's person inviolable, and his defenders relied on that; Saint-Just argued that the king, as an enemy of the nation, stood outside the law and so could not be judged by it, while Robespierre argued against holding a trial at all; the Girondins pressed for a sentence of death to be submitted to the people; the Convention found the king guilty by a wide margin and sentenced him to death by a narrow one, refused the appeal to the people, and executed him on 21 January 1793. Take the episode as a case, not as a puzzle of fact.

Say how Pascal's categories map onto the actors and their options; what his account explains about the trial, and where it misdescribes the episode or falls silent; and what the episode in turn shows about his position — whether it confirms it, refutes it, or presents a kind of case he never anticipated, such as the deliberate founding of a new order rather than the maintenance of an old one. Give particular attention to the revolutionaries' appeal to a justice that was not yet established, and to what Pascal's warning about telling the people that the laws are unjust would predict about a trial in which the legislature itself sat as the court.

Answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
