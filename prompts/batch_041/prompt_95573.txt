I have spent a lot of time reading mechanistic interpretability work and the older philosophy-of-biology literature on mechanisms, and I have come to think that mechanistic anomaly detection — flagging a system because its internal computation differs from the one you believed it was running, rather than because its outputs changed — is stuck on a question that everyone gestures at and nobody answers: when are two computations the same mechanism? Here is what I catch myself saying about cases, and I want the principle, or the best small set of candidate principles, that fits them.

- Two copies of the same trained network that differ only by a permutation of the hidden units: same mechanism.
- Two networks trained from different random seeds that both develop a circuit of the same recognizable kind — say, an attention head that copies whatever followed the previous occurrence of the current token: same mechanism, even though no unit in one corresponds to any particular unit in the other.
- A network that sorts by partitioning and recursing versus one that has effectively memorized the sorted order of every list it will be asked about: different mechanisms, even though their outputs agree on everything I will ever feed them.
- Bat wings and bird wings: different mechanisms — same aerodynamic job, different parts, different developmental origin.
- An enzyme in two species with low overall sequence identity but the same fold and the same catalytic residues: same mechanism.
- A model that during training was keying on a shallow surface cue and at deployment is running the general rule I intended, agreeing with the shallow cue on every input in my evaluation set: different mechanisms. This is the case I actually care about, and it is also the one where nothing I can observe, including ablation studies, is guaranteed to separate the two.
- A model that runs the general rule on most inputs and a lookup table for one special class: I want to say it contains two mechanisms, which makes me suspect that my question is malformed — that sameness of mechanism is a relation between episodes of computation rather than between systems.

My tentative principle is that two computations are the same mechanism when there is an isomorphism between their causal structures after coarse-graining to the parts that a correct mechanistic explanation of the capacity in question would cite. I can see that this is close to circular: it fixes the level by appeal to the explanation, and the boundaries of the explanation are exactly what I wanted the principle to fix. I would like to know whether it can be made non-circular or has to be replaced.

The verdicts that trouble me most in combination are the seed case and the anomaly case. The seed verdict wants mechanism identity to be coarse enough that two independently trained networks sharing no weights count as the same; the anomaly verdict wants it fine enough to separate two circuits that are indistinguishable by any intervention or observation I can run. I do not know of any formal criterion that lives in that interval. The enzyme and wing verdicts also pull against each other, and I can only reconcile them by appealing to the level again.

So give me the principle, or a couple of candidates stated precisely enough to disagree with each other, each with the case that forces its main clause. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot keep, what it predicts for a case I have not listed — a system that implements the same dependency structure with the steps reordered or run in parallel, so that no intermediate value ever coincides in time with its counterpart — and what it predicts for one further case of your own choosing that separates it from the rival candidate. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Write in American English.
