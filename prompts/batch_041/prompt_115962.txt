Last month I showed a friend a short piece I had written about a bad year in my life, and he told me — sincerely, I think — that it was art, because it had infected him with what I felt while writing it. That, he said, is Tolstoy's account of the matter: one person lives through a feeling, fixes it in certain external signs — words, sounds, colours — and the feeling passes to others the way a cold passes; a thing is art to the degree that it succeeds in infecting, the maker's sincerity is what makes the transmission possible, and a work's worth lies in the feeling it carries and in how far and how deeply it spreads. I was pleased, and then uneasy, because the same theory that had just promoted my little piece seemed to require demoting almost everything I love: the difficult, the learned, the obscure, and the merely thrilling come out as counterfeits or trifles, while the plain song anyone can catch comes out on top — and Tolstoy, by his own account, put the bulk of his own celebrated fiction in the discard along with Beethoven and Shakespeare.

I want the analogy audited, not the conclusion defended or attacked. Give me the correspondence table — the maker, the feeling, the sign, the audience, the catching, the susceptibility of the receiver, the sincerity of the source — and say which of those are doing the load-bearing work in his argument. Then where the mapping breaks. Infection is involuntary and sub-rational, whereas the reception of a work is something the audience does; an infectious agent need not itself be ill, whereas the maker must have felt the feeling; a disease is identified by its symptoms and admits of no counterfeits, whereas the distinction between real and fake art is exactly what he needs; contagion is indifferent to the value of what it carries, whereas his point is evaluative; and the headcount of the infected measures nothing in medicine, whereas he treats reach and depth as marks of worth. Tell me which of these disanalogies matter to what he wants the analogy to do and which are incidental.

The question I care about most: does the infection model by itself generate his ranking of good and bad art, or does the ranking come from the religious content of the feeling and so from outside the analogy altogether — the model doing only the definitional work while the evaluation is imported? And if the model cannot rank, is there a repaired version that can, or a different analogy — art as a language, as a gift, as play — better fitted to the work he wants done? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
