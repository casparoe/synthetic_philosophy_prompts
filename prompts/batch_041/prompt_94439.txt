I'm taking a phil of science course right now and we've been doing counterfactual vs. interventionist theories of causation, and I'm definately stuck on one thing. On the old counterfactual picture, whether X caused Y is a fact about the world: if X hadn't happened, Y wouldn't have. On the interventionist picture, whether X counts as a cause depends on which variables you put in the model and what you hold fixed -- change the variable set and the same event can look like a cause, a background condition, or just a correlation. Our prof called this the menu problem and said it isn't just a technicality; it changes what counts as an explanation. I have to write a short story for my final portfolio, and I want to dramatize that without turning it into a lecture.

Set it in a small-town insurance office or a coroner's inquest, maybe after a house fire or a farm accident, where two investigators are both right about the counterfactuals but they've built different variable sets. One blames the faulty wiring; the other blames the county's refusal to fund inspections. Each can defend their causal claim under intervention, and the reader should feel why both are legitimate and why the choice of variables isn't arbitrary but also isn't dictated by the world alone. Make it 1,200-1,800 words, with concrete scenes and dialogue, not an essay. A weird detail I want worked in: one investigator keeps a notebook where she redraws the same event as different causal graphs, and the graphs start to feel like competing moral portraits. End with the inquest's verdict, but make the verdict hinge on which graph the coroner chooses rather than on new evidence. Don't mention any philosophers by name in the story itself; I want the ideas to be visible in the characters' disagreement. And if it's possible, make the story leave open whether there's a fact of the matter about the cause, or whether the interventionist is committed to saying there isn't. I don't need citations or a reading list, just the story.
