Longtermists keep telling me the simulation argument is a side show for how much the far future matters: even if we're almost certainly in a simulation, they say, the astronomical stakes are untouched, so the case for being fanatical about the far future -- trading enormous present costs for a sliver of a chance at astronomically large future value -- survives intact. I dont buy it. Please make the argument the other way: that taking Bostrom's simulation argument seriously (the one that says that if civilizations tend to survive long enough to run lots of ancestor simulations, we're probably in one) should dampen fanaticism about the far future.

Be specific about where the deflation comes from -- e.g. the future we'd be steering is shorter than the billion-year story assumes, or it's a simulated future whose real-world footprint is whatever the simulators allot, or being simulated correlates with not being the civilization whose choices matter -- rather than vague unease. And dont just help yourself to 'simulated beings count too, so the stakes are the same'; if that move works it may rescue the whole thing, so say honestly whether it does, and what's left of the dampening if it does. Last, say whether the same dampening hits any 'our choices fix the value of the future' hypothesis, or whether the simulation case is special.

Finish with a single line of the form Verdict: <dampens-a-lot | dampens-a-little | not-at-all> — <one sentence>.
