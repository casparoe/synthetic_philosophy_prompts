My friend and I have spent several evenings arguing about what it takes for a thing to remain the same thing over time, and on individual cases she is unusually decisive. Her verdicts so far: a river is the same river from one year to the next even though none of its water persists; a chair that is sanded and repainted is the same chair; a city that replaces nearly all its buildings over a century is the same city; a person who changes gradually over a decade is the same person. But a statue that is melted down and recast as a bowl is not the same statue; a table chopped into firewood is not the same table; and a house that burns down, with a different house later built on the same lot, is not the same house. The case we could not agree on, and the reason we were still at it past midnight, is a tree that is gradually carved in place, over a season, into a canoe, with no interruption and the same wood throughout: is the canoe the same thing as the tree, or did the tree cease to exist and a new thing come to be? Please give me your best prediction of her verdict, the rule she seems to be running, any other rule that fits all her earlier verdicts just as well, which of those rules this case actually separates, and the further case that would settle it. In particular, say whether what she is running is closer to perdurantism, on which identity is unbroken spatiotemporal-causal continuity of a single four-dimensional whole, or to a sortal-relative endurantism, on which something survives only so long as it remains the same kind of thing, or to some third option; and assume she is trying to track a real distinction rather than merely stipulating.
