I am assembling a specification for a theory of transformative experience, and I want the requirements settled before I evaluate any candidate account. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the requirement you judge to eliminate the most otherwise plausible accounts, together with your verdict on whether the list that follows is jointly satisfiable — and then give the specification itself, without preamble or restatement of this request. No citations or reading list are needed; the cases and the requirements are the substance, and a table is welcome where it helps.

What the theory is for: it must be adequate to two uses. The first is guiding a person deliberating about a choice they expect to change what they care about. The second is guiding a third party who must decide on someone else's behalf — a guardian, a clinician, an institutional review board — where the person affected will be transformed by the decision and cannot now say what they will want. By transformative experience I mean the class of choices in which the agent cannot evaluate the outcome from where they stand, because undergoing the experience is what would supply the evaluation.

I. Cases the theory must get right, each with what it is doing in the list:

1. Term-limited transformation. Someone enters a five-year commitment — a monastery, an enlistment, an isolated research post — expecting it to remake what they value, with a guaranteed exit at the end. Suppose the person who leaves would not choose to re-enter, and would not call the entry a mistake.
2. Reversal. A person undergoes a change and then its reversal — a conversion and a deconversion, a drug and its antidote. Each self regards the other's values as defective rather than merely different, and neither has an obvious claim to be the real one.
3. Transformation that removes the capacity to value. A treatment leaves someone settled and content but with almost no evaluative capacity: few cares, no projects, no preferences worth consulting. The familiar test — would the informed future self endorse it? — has nothing to consult. The theory must neither declare this a harm on the ground that the future self cannot mind it, nor defer to a self that has no verdict to offer.
4. Epistemic without personal. An experience teaches someone what something is like — a taste, a grief, a landscape, years of a craft — without changing what they care about, and sometimes leaving them wishing they had not learned it. The theory must not treat every transformative experience as a change of values, or every gain in knowledge as a gain in good.
5. Decision by testimony. The agent cannot simulate the outcome and decides on others' reports; a variant in which the reports conflict, and the agent must weigh the testimony of people who have already been transformed and whose values are no longer theirs.
6. The transformation that does not occur. Someone chooses the change and it fails to take; or chooses against it and is transformed anyway. The deliberation was organized around a counterfactual, and the theory must say what becomes of it.
7. Third-party decision. A guardian chooses for a ward; an institution chooses for a class of people. The future self cannot authorize the choice in advance and may repudiate it. The theory must not collapse into paternalism, nor abdicate by saying that only the future self may decide.

II. Distinctions it must respect: epistemic transformation versus personal transformation, which come apart in both directions (cases 1 and 4); a change in values versus an error in values, together with the point that the later self is not automatically the better judge of the earlier one; ignorance of an outcome's value versus uncertainty about an outcome whose value is fixed; regret without mistake, and contentment without vindication; transformation as the object of the choice versus as a side effect, since someone may choose the change because the undergoing is itself the good; reversible in principle versus recoverable in practice, since the transformed self may hold the exit and decline to use it; deciding for oneself versus deciding for another.

III. Constraints it must be consistent with: it must reduce to ordinary expected-utility reasoning in the no-transformation limit, or explain precisely why not; it must not conflate transformation with the well-documented errors of affective forecasting; it must leave room for transformative choices to be made sometimes well and sometimes badly, since an account on which they are uniformly irrational, or uniformly beyond assessment, is not an account of a practice we have reason to think is sometimes conducted competently; it must accommodate that transformation is partly endogenous, in that preparation, framing, and the choice itself affect what the agent becomes, and that some transformations are available only jointly, with others' cooperation; and it must be action-guiding in real time — it must say something about how to decide, not only why deciding is hard.

IV. Which of these are genuine desiderata and which are the preferences of one camp in disguise — for example, the demand that transformative choices be rationally assessable at all, or the demand that the theory issue a verdict on the choice rather than a procedure for making it. Say which you would drop, and what dropping it would cost. Flag any requirement that only looks substantive because it is doing definitional work.

V. Joint satisfiability. I suspect two of the requirements cannot be met together: being action-guiding for the agent as they now are, and not privileging the present self's values over the future self's, which look unsatisfiable without an independent account of whose authority counts. A second candidate pair is the demand that the theory reduce to expected utility in the limit and the demand that it treat a change in preferences as a change in what is good for the agent rather than as a risk to be priced. Confirm, deny, or replace these, and name whichever pair you think is really in conflict; if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say which member you would give up, since that is itself a result.
