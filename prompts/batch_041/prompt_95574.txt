I run a little podcast on the history of analytic philosophy and I've got a guest on next month who works on Frege. Her line, which she's taken some flak for, is that the political diary stuff (the 1924 diary that got published in the Deutsche Zeitschrift für Philosophie back in the 90s, with the antisemitic and anti-democratic bits, plus the fact that he signed that 1914 professors' declaration cheering on the war) has basically no bearing on how we should read the logicism, the sense/reference stuff, the anti-psychologism — that it's a separate file, and mixing them is just moralizing about a dead German. I want to make her work for it. Not in a gotcha way, I actually want a good conversation, but I don't want the usual back-and-forth where she says "genetic fallacy" and I say "but he was awful" and we both go home.

So draft me eight to ten questions, in order, that build. A few things I want covered, and for each of these I want a follow-up that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:

- Get her to distinguish the senses of "bearing on." I've got a rough list in my head: causal influence of the politics on the logic; evidential value about his character or reliability; whether it's an interpretive key to the texts; whether it's methodologically normative (does it make the doctrine complicit in something); and whether it matters for canonicity or for how we teach him. Push her on whether she's denying all five or quietly conceding one and hoping nobody notices.
- The Platonist escape hatch. If thoughts are objective and mind-independent, the thinker's opinions are irrelevant to their truth — fine — but ask her whether that cuts the other way too: it also means Frege himself has no privileged access to his own thoughts, so why should we trust his self-presentation of the program as apolitical? And what would "the program" even be, if not the thing he wrote?
- Continuity vs. rupture. Her story needs the diary to be a late-life departure, a bitter old man after the war and the inflation. Press on whether the earlier rhetoric about logic purging thought of foreign contamination, about rigor as hygiene, is really discontinuous — or whether the diary just says out loud what the metaphors were always doing.
- A question about what evidence would actually change her mind. Give her the chance to say something falsifiable.
- End with one about pedagogy: if she's right, does anything change in the classroom, and if nothing changes, is that itself a problem?

One more thing: I'd like the response to tell me how confident it is in the framing it's giving me, and to flag the single point it's least sure about — I'd rather know where it's guessing than get a smooth set of questions.
