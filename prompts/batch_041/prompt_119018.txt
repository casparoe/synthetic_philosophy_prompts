ok so I've been trying to nail down my views on deliberation-as-an-idealization and I want a consistancy check before I write them up for a seminar.

Here's the package. (1) The right idealization of good reasoning is factored cognition: any thinking task can be split into subtasks small enough that a bounded agent can do each one in a single sitting, with a dumb, transparent aggregator gluing the answers back together. (2) The limit of that process is the HCH-style setup -- a human who, at every point where they'd otherwise have to figure something out themselves, can ask a fresh copy of themselves who sees only the subquestion and nothing about the surrounding context. (3) I take computational limits seriously, so 'ideal' should mean 'the best you can do at a fixed budget of working memory and serial steps', not 'unbounded'. (4) I care a lot about auditibility: the output should be something a human can walk through and check link by link, so that trust in the whole thing is inherited from trust in each step. (5) I don't think there's anything deep about factoring as such -- a single big model that just answers the top-level question is doing the same kind of thing, just more efficiently; the factored tree is a scaffold, not a different kind of reasoner. And (6) on a particular case: if a factored tree and a single model hand me the same proof of a hard theorem, I'd trust the tree's proof more, even holding the proofs literally identical.

Which of these actually fight each other, which only look like they do, and how bad is each one? Rank them by severity, and for each real conflict tell me the cheapest commitment to drop and what I lose by dropping it -- don't just tell me there's a tension somewhere in the set.
