I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, but I work mostly on the moral-psychology end of things, so I'm a bit of a tourist when it comes to the history side. I help run a small interview series in our department, and next week's guest is a visiting scholar whose book-in-progress has been annoying me in a productive way. Her line is that the whole "German rule-following versus American conscience" contrast -- the Kant-and-Hegel lineage on one side, Emerson and Thoreau on the other -- isnt really a disagreement about what our duties are. On her account the two traditions agree, more or less, about the content of our obligations, and disagree only about who holds what she calls dispensation authority: who gets to license an exception to a rule everybody already accepts. In the German picture, exceptions have to be granted by whoever is custodian of the rule, so a private conscientious objection looks like a category mistake rather than a brave act. In the American picture, the individual conscience is itself the custodian, so following an order you think is wrong looks like cowardice even when the order was perfectly legitimate. She's got a subsidiary claim I really want to push on: that moving dispensation authority to the individual has a hidden cost, because it turns every moral failure into a failure of sincerity, which makes the sincerely wrong person almost impossible to criticize. She calls this the conscientious free-rider problem, and I suspect the name is doing more work than the argument is.

Could you draft me eight or nine interview questions, with a follow-up for each one keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not left flat-footed when she gives it? I want at least one on the dispensation-authority thesis itself, at least one on the authorized exception and whether it leaves her any room for legitimate disobedience against a custodian who has it wrong, and at least one on the sincere-wrongdoer problem. I also want something that forces her to say which of the two traditions she thinks has the better of it rather than just describing both, and something about how you'd actually tell them apart in the wild -- she leans a lot on a vignette study of her own where people judge a nurse who breaks a hospital protocol, and I want her to say what would count as evidence against her reading of it. Please dont give me anything that lets her just restate the thesis in different words.
