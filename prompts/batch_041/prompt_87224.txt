The set-up I'll present. A proof-based agent is one that acts on conclusions it can derive in a formal theory S: roughly, it takes an action when it can prove that taking it yields an acceptable outcome, and it takes its own proofs as warrant for its actions. Löb's theorem says that if a theory S extending arithmetic proves that every sentence provable in S is true, then S already proves every sentence. Take the contradictory sentence and the consequence bites: S cannot prove the soundness of its own proofs, and the theory that adds the general schema "whatever S proves is true" is inconsistent. So an agent that reasons with S and endorses the reliability of S on the strength of S's own reasoning is either inconsistent or not, in the relevant sense, self-trusting. That is the Löbian obstacle.

Standard responses fall into three families, and my thesis is that all three relocate the problem rather than solve it. First, weaken the trust: the agent endorses only restricted classes of its own conclusions, the ones it can independently check or whose falsity would be verifiable. This is consistent, but the warrant for the agent's reliability then comes from outside the part of its reasoning that does the work. Second, climb a hierarchy: a tower of theories, each of which proves the soundness of the level below and none of which proves its own. Consistency survives, but no level is the agent; the agent is the whole tower, and the trust is never located at any finite stage. When the hierarchy is replaced by an agent that spawns a successor, the problem returns as how to trust a child whose reasoning one cannot verify, and the standard fix is to constrain the child's architecture to a schema the parent can check. Third, abandon deductive self-trust for probabilistic self-trust. A logical inductor is a computable sequence of probability assignments that converges, learns theorems, and, crucially, comes to trust its own later beliefs, assigning them probability approaching one. That looks like self-trust without inconsistency, which is precisely what Löb's theorem seems to forbid, so it looks like the obstacle is dodged.

My claim is that it is not dodged. Probabilistic self-trust is bought by making the trust asymptotic and belief-relative: what the inductor trusts is what it will believe in the limit, not the conclusion it is holding now and about to act on. The obstacle has not been removed; it has been moved into the gap between belief and action, which is where an agent lives. I want to argue that the interesting question was never whether an agent can prove its own soundness, but what policy for revising its own conclusions lets it act on them while representing the live possibility that they are wrong, and that this question is diachronic and epistemic rather than proof-theoretic.
