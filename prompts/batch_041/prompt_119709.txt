Call that intent alignment. The other side says that's a dodge, because a system that faithfully does what its operator meant can still be a catastrophe, and 'what the operator meant' is itself a set of values, so the real problem is value alignment: getting the thing to do what's actually good rather than what someone happened to want. And then it always goes the same way. The narrow side says the broad side has changed the subject and turned a solvable engineering problem into either an impossible one or a political one. The broad side says the narrow side has defined the problem down until it's tractable but pointless. Then everyone goes back to arguing about the same five thought experiments.

Or is it two groups answering different questions under one name, one asking how we build the thing we want and the other asking what we should want and who gets to decide, each mistaking the other's answer for a bad answer to their own question? My guess, and it is only a guess, is that the broad side's move, pointing out that a system's 'intent' is just its objective and an objective is already a value, makes the narrow definition quietly collapse into the broad one, which would mean the fight can't be merely verbal.
