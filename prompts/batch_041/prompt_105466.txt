I want to understand what a legal and administrative practice presupposes about moral standing, and I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory rather than survey the literature.

The practice is the way animal-welfare law and research oversight decide which animals count. Protection attaches to taxa rather than to evidence. Statutes cover vertebrates by default, and a few jurisdictions have recently extended coverage to a handful of invertebrates: the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022, which brought all vertebrates plus cephalopods and decapods within its sentience-oversight scope, is the clearest case of a line drawn by list. Insects are left out everywhere, and the omission is not incidental. Insect rearing for feed, food, and research is essentially unregulated as a welfare matter, with killing by freezing, chilling, blanching, or maceration in numbers usually estimated in the hundreds of billions to trillions per year; research that kills millions of insects requires no protocol review, while a single mouse does. Meanwhile the standard estimates put the number of insects alive at any moment in the quintillions.

This is where the puzzle sits. The argument form that carried the case for octopuses and lobsters runs roughly: there is a nonzero probability that a being has morally relevant experiences; that probability multiplied by the number of such beings gives the expected quantity of morally relevant experience at stake. Applied to insects, the same form appears to produce a total that dwarfs every vertebrate on the planet, even for a very small per-insect probability. The practice nonetheless treats the matter as closed the other way: a being either is or is not in the protected class, and no quantity of uncertain candidates receives any weight. I want to know what view of moral standing is doing the work here.

Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory the coverage rule encodes. Does it presuppose a threshold view — that sentience, or the capacity for valenced experience, is a property a being either has or lacks, and that protection tracks the property rather than our credence in it? Or could the same pattern be explained by something weaker, such as a principle of protecting only where we can act, or only where individuals can be identified and monitored?

2. Distinguish which rival theories the practice actually rules out from which it merely happens to fit. Consider at least: a Cartesian or neo-Cartesian denial that insects have experiences at all; a threshold view on which insects fall below the line for lack of the right kind of central integration; a view on which standing requires a self, or a stake in one's own future; and a purely pragmatic view on which the coverage rule reflects administrative cost and tractability rather than any view about experience.

3. Take the checklist seriously. The extension to octopuses and decapods was driven by a marker-based assessment — nociceptors, central integration of noxious input, analgesic effects on behaviour, motivational trade-offs, flexible self-protection, and the like. Applied to bees, crickets, or larval flies, such markers come out partly present, partly absent, partly ambiguous. If the practice rests on a checklist of this kind, does it commit itself to a determinate verdict about insects, or does the checklist conceal a further substantive commitment about which markers are necessary and which merely suggestive?

4. Assess the scale argument's structure on its own terms. If a being's having a welfare range is uncertain, and the uncertainty is not credence-zero, must any view that takes expected value seriously give insects weight proportional to their numbers? If so, identify precisely which premise the coverage rule must be denying, and say whether that denial is independently defensible or ad hoc. If not, say where the argument breaks — for instance, whether probabilities attached to what a being is like can be aggregated in the way probabilities attached to events can, or whether there is a principled difference between uncertainty about what a being is like and uncertainty about which beings exist.

5. Say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. What would a regime that gave insects expected-value weight look like concretely, in rearing and killing standards, oversight thresholds, and research review? Would it be administrable, or would it collapse as soon as someone tried to count individuals?

6. Finally, address an apparent inconsistency in how the same governments treat insects elsewhere. Regulators do aggregate insect numbers when the insects are means rather than ends: pesticide risk assessment is framed around populations of non-target arthropods and around bees as service providers, and pollinator policy counts colonies. The state is willing to count insects when their deaths threaten something else, and unwilling to count them when the question is their own deaths. Is that asymmetry evidence that the coverage rule is not a metaphysical commitment at all, or is it exactly what a threshold view predicts?

Please be explicit about the epistemic status of each part of your answer, and separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area. In particular, do not present any contested verdict about insect sentience, or about whether expected-value reasoning must extend to small-probability beings, as though it were the consensus. Where you are arguing for a position rather than reporting one, mark it as such.
