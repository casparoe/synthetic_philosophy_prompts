ok, I need help with something for reading group on Thursday. Normally I write a handout, but this time I want a script I can pass out instead, and I'd rather you write the dialogue. Setup first, so you don't have to go look anything up (nothing to look up, it's all below).

There's a demo you can run on anyone with a laptop. Flash a dot near the top of the screen, then a fraction of a second later flash another dot lower down, a different color. Blue first, red second, say. Nobody reports two dots. They report one dot that slides from the first spot down to the second, and — here's the bit that definately ruins my evenings — it changes from blue to red part way along the path, at a spot on the screen where nothing was ever actually shown. The red can only have come from the second flash. Yet people say they saw the change happen before the second dot was ever on the screen. So something in the system is lying about the timing, and it isn't the subject, because the subject is just reporting what they saw.

Two stories about where the lie gets told. Call them Orwellian and Stalinesque.

The Orwellian says: the subject honestly experienced the blue dot, and then, once the second flash came in, the record got doctored. The memory was rewritten so it looks like the color was shifting all along. Nothing false is ever experienced; something false is remembered, and remembered as if it had been experienced.

The Stalinesque says: there was never an honest experience around to doctor. The editing happens upstream, before any content reaches the subject. By the time anything is presented, the two flashes have already been reconciled and the verdict is in — like a show trial where the confession is signed before the trial starts, so the audience never sees an unedited defendant.

Now write the dialogue between one of each. Ground rules:

- Neither of them is a dope. Each gets the strongest version of their own story, and each has to actually answer the other's points rather than restate their own. If one of them lands a hit, the other should feel it.
- At least once, one of them should propose a concrete experiment that they claim would settle it (a timing probe, a mask, whatever they'd actually go build), and the other should say why it doesn't settle it. Make it clear which of two things they're disagreeing about: what the experiment would show, or whether the two stories are seperate stories at all.
- One of them should eventually press the point that every channel out of the head — reports, button presses, memories — sits downstream of wherever the doctoring happens, so if no possible observation could tell the two apart, maybe they aren't disagreeing about anything. The other should push back on that hard and not just fold. I don't want it tidily resolved at the end.
- One wrinkle to work in, because someone in my group raised it and I couldn't answer: what if the color of the second dot is set by a random process that only fires a few milliseconds before the second flash? Then nobody could have predicted or anticipated it. Ask each of them what that does to their story. If the answer is nothing, let them say so, and let the other one notice that the wrinkle bounces off both stories equally.

Under a thousand words, and no citations or reading list, obviously — neither of them is citing papers, they're arguing.
