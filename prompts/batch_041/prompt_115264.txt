For the past few months a few of us in the office have been carrying on a long, half-serious email thread about whether a person could ever relocate to a place where their words quietly stopped meaning what they used to mean, and I'd like to turn the running joke into something I can actually use. Please write me a parody FAQ — the chirpy, bulleted "Relocation Handbook" that a large HR department circulates before transferring staff to a branch office — for a fictional firm I'll call Meridian Trans-Environmental Relocation Services, which is moving employees to a planet that is a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of Earth, oceans and all, except that what fills the oceans is not what fills ours. The handbook should never wink: it should read exactly like genuine corporate boilerplate while quietly committing itself to the position that the meanings of an employee's words are fixed not by what is inside the employee's head but by the surrounding environment and linguistic community. The humor has to come from getting that view right, so that a philosopher of language laughs rather than winces. So I want the "water" clause handled straight — the point that a transferee's inner life is indistinguishable before and after the move even though their words have come to pick out something different; a "reference warranty" that disclaims liability for whatever a word ends up meaning after arrival; an onboarding scenario involving a colleague assembled on the far side out of swamp matter, a perfect physical duplicate with no history and hence, on the company's own view, no inherited meanings; and a medical-leave clause in which whether an employee's complaint about arthritis in the wrong body part is a real complaint depends on what the local doctors mean by the term, so that a grievance can be valid on one planet and void on the other without the employee feeling any different.

Please also fold in, deadpan, the slow-switch problem — a section on the company's gradual-relocation option, in which staff are moved in stages and at no single moment can anyone point to when their meanings shifted — and a short, slightly ominous "Frequently Raised Objections" subsection addressing the worry that if meaning depends on the environment, an employee cannot know what they mean without first inspecting their surroundings, together with the company's reassuring non-answer. Aim for roughly 750 to 850 words, formatted as numbered clauses with sub-bullets, and let at least one clause contradict another in a way the handbook itself never notices. No citations, no footnotes, no scholarly hedging — I want it to read as an artifact produced from inside the company, not as a term paper about it, and a reader who knows the literature should be able to tell that whoever wrote the boilerplate understood exactly what they were committing the firm to.
