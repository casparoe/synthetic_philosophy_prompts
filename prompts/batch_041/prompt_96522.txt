I have been drafting a short essay on David Lewis's treatment of penal substitution, and I would like your comments. I am not looking for a literature review or citations; I want you to engage with the reconstruction and the argument as I have set them out, and tell me where it is inaccurate, where it is uncharitable, where the reasoning fails, and how it might be improved.

The essay so far goes roughly as follows. The doctrine of penal substitution says that Christ, though innocent, bore the punishment due to human sin, thereby satisfying divine justice and making forgiveness possible. The usual objection is that this is unjust: it is wrong to punish an innocent person, and a just God would not do it. Lewis's response, as I reconstruct it, turns on a distinction between punishment and penalty. Punishment, in the strict sense, requires that the one punished be guilty or at least deserving of the deprivation; it is essentially retributive. A penalty, by contrast, is simply a burden or deprivation imposed by a legal or quasi-legal authority, and it need not attach to guilt in the same way. A fine, for example, is a penalty; it can be paid by someone other than the offender without the payer thereby being punished. On this view, what Christ bears is the penalty of sin, not the punishment. He pays the debt; he does not undergo retributive condemnation.

I then raise a worry. If the penalty just is the suffering that the punishment would have involved, and if it is imposed on someone innocent, it is not clear that calling it a penalty rather than a punishment removes the injustice. The moral sting of punishing an innocent person seems to come from the imposition of suffering on someone who does not deserve it, not merely from the label. Lewis might reply that the penalty is not inflicted as a condemnation of Christ, and that Christ voluntarily takes it on, which changes its character. But voluntary acceptance of an unjust burden does not obviously make the imposition just; it might make it supererogatory, but the authority imposing it still seems to be acting wrongly if the burden is undeserved.

The essay then considers a further move that I associate with Lewis. Perhaps the doctrine is not that Christ is punished instead of us, but that we are punished in Christ. If believers are united with Christ, then when Christ bears the penalty, they bear it too; the substitution is not a transfer of punishment from guilty parties to an innocent third party, but a kind of incorporation. Or, on a Trinitarian reading, God does not punish an innocent other; God in the Son bears the penalty that human sin deserves. I argue that this move does more justice to the doctrine, but it also changes it: it is no longer a straightforward substitution of one person for another, and it raises questions about whether the Son can be said to bear the Father's wrath without dividing the divine will. I conclude that the punishment/penalty distinction is a real and useful conceptual distinction, but that it does not by itself answer the justice objection; the Trinitarian or incorporation move is what does the real work, and that move is doing something different from what the phrase penal substitution naturally suggests.

Please comment on this. In particular: is the punishment/penalty distinction being deployed in a way that is faithful to Lewis, as far as you can tell? Is my objection to the distinction the strongest one, or is there a better line of criticism? Does the incorporation/Trinitarian move genuinely solve the problem, or does it merely relabel it? And is the essay's conclusion defensible, or does it overstate the gap between the two moves? I would also welcome suggestions for making the argument tighter. Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write in a letter or an email.
