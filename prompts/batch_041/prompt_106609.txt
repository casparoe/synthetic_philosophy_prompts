The question was: "Consider three ways in which a physician might bring about the death of a competent, terminally ill patient who has asked to die. In Case A she injects a lethal dose of a drug. In Case B she switches off the patient's ventilator. In Case C she turns a dial that steadily reduces the ventilator's oxygen supply until the patient suffocates. (i) How should each case be classified under the doing/allowing distinction, and on what does that classification depend? (ii) What is the strongest argument for the view that doing and allowing are morally different, and what is the strongest argument against it? (iii) Does the doctrine of double effect illuminate the moral difference, if any, among the cases?"

The student's answer was: "Cases A, B and C can be sorted using the doing/allowing distinction. In Case A the physician performs an act that kills the patient, so this is a doing, and specifically a killing. In Case B she withdraws a treatment she is under no obligation to continue, and the patient then dies of the underlying illness, so this is an allowing, and specifically a letting die. Case C is different again: because the physician actively manipulates the dial and thereby changes what the patient receives, she is doing something to the patient, so this too is a doing. The classification is therefore a straightforwardly factual matter, settled by asking whether the physician's conduct is an act or an omission. The strongest argument for the moral difference is that the agent who does is the author of the death, whereas the agent who allows merely permits a natural process to continue; the strongest argument against it is that the outcome is the same, and the physician's intention may be the same, so that the only remaining difference lies in how we describe the conduct. Since the physician's conduct is necessary for the death in all three cases, the difference between them cannot be a difference in causation, and so the doing/allowing distinction is doing no real work here. The doctrine of double effect is what really explains the difference. It permits a foreseen death as a side effect while forbidding an intended death as a means. Because killing involves an intended death and letting die a merely foreseen one, the doctrine of double effect is simply the doing/allowing distinction expressed in another vocabulary, and the two ideas stand or fall together. This is why the law is right to treat the withdrawal of treatment as fundamentally different from an injection."
