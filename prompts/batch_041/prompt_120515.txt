I've got a student in my Eliot seminar who's built an arguemnt I've been failing to dismantle for two weeks now, and I want a second opinion before I take it into the grad class on Friday. Here's her chain, tidied up.

(1) Eliot's considered view is that a personal God and a personal immortality are not credible — she says so in her letters, she says so in the novels, she never once pretends otherwise — and yet she insists the ethical core of religion comes through the loss intact: duty stays absolute even after its old supports are gone.
(2) The machinery for that comes from Feuerbach, whose Essence of Christianity she put into English: theology is anthropology, the predicates we hang on God — love, wisdom, justice — are predicates of the human species, and the real subject of the sentence is us.
(3) So whatever authority religious morality claimed for its demands was humanity's authority all along.
(4) But a demand has authority over me only if the thing making it has a claim on me — if it has interests I can set back, or can be wronged by me. No claimant, no obligation.
(5) Humanity taken as a species, or as the sum of all human beings, is not an agent and has no interests over and above the interests of the particular people who make it up.
(6) So the authority of any moral demand resolves in the end into the claims of particular human beings — the ones in front of me, whose interests I can actually affect.
(7) Now look at what her characters actually answer to. Dorothea is bound by a promise to a man who is dead and cannot release her or enforce anything; Romola stays in a city that will never thank her; Mordecai gives his life to a people and a future he won't live to see. None of that is exhausted by what any living person in the book can demand of them. What they obey is an ideal — the good that grows in the world through obscure, unrecorded lives, a humanity that doesn't exist yet, the dead and the unborn.
(8) An ideal has no interests. By (4) through (6) it can't make demands.
(9) So either the authority is just the force of our own attachment to our own picture of how things should go, in which case duty isn't peremptory at all and the absolutism is a bluff, or there is something with authority over us that is not any particular human being, which is God with the serial numbers filed off.
(10) Either way the translation fails: it either loses the thing that made her ethic an ethic, or it keeps that thing by smuggling the transcendent back in under a human name.

Something's wrong here, I'm nearly sure, and I don't think it's the Eliot. I want to keep (1) through (3); I think they're right about her and I'd hate to loose Feuerbach, so I'm hoping the damage is downstream. Tell me which step to drop and what dropping it costs me. Is it a local bad inference that a better formulation would fix, or is the whole shape wrong — i.e., can a Feuerbachian have the absoluteness she wanted at all? If the answer is that (5) has to go, say what's left of the humanism; if (4) has to go, say what a Feuerbachian puts in its place, because it can't be nothing. And if you've got a minute, tell me whether the same argument sinks any religion of humanity or just hers — she took the phrase from Comte, though I'm fairly confident she wanted nothing to do with his calendar and his priests, and correct me if I've got that wrong.
