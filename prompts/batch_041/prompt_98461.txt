Okay so I went down a rabbit hole on the absent-minded driver problem and came out with a pile of gut calls that I can't get to fit together, so I want you to tell me what rule I'm actualy using.

Setup in case it's rusty: a guy is driving home, there are two exits, and he's absent-minded, meaning when he arrives at an exit he genuinely can't tell whether it's the first or the second. Exit at the first and he's stranded somewhere useless (call it 0), exit at the second and he's home (4), blow past both and he ends up at a grim motel (1). He knows all the numbers.

My calls:

1. Classic version. I think he should randomize — at whatever exit he's at, continue with probability about two thirds, otherwise exit. And I mean that seriously: this is the rational play, not a cop-out. "Just keep driving" is plainly worse (he gets the motel), and "always take the first exit" is worse still.

2. Same road, but before he sets off he can bring a passenger who CAN see the exits and will tap him when they reach the second one. The passenger is reliable. Obvious: bring the passenger, collect the 4. Feels like the whole puzzle was about information all along.

3. Same road, but he can scribble a note at the first exit ("you already passed one") and read it later — except he's absent-minded about whether he wrote it, so the note is no help. Gut: we're back to the coin.

4. Now change the numbers. First exit is worth 3, second is worth 4, overshoot is 0. Rolling the dice only buys him about 3.06 on average versus a sure 3. My gut here: just take the first exit. I would not be the guy gambling a shot at 0 for a 2% edge.

So 1 and 4 are pulling in opposite directions, right? In 1 I'll happily gamble over a sure 1, and in 4 I won't gamble over a sure 3 for a smaller gain. My tentative rule is: randomize only when the deterministic options are bad enough to be worth insuring against, otherwise take the sure thing. But "bad enough" is doing all the work there and I don't know if it survives.

What I want: the actual principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely, with the case that forces each clause, what it says about a case I haven't listed — say, one where the overshoot payoff is high, so continuing is cheap — and an honest verdict on which of my four gut calls has to go. Bonus if you tell me whether a threshold rule like mine is a real principle or just me being inconsistent with extra steps. Max 450 words, and please skip the decision-theory boilerplate.
