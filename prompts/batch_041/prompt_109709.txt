I am preparing a session of a graduate seminar on medieval Jewish rationalism, and I have set aside the whole of it for Guide of the Perplexed III.32, the chapter in which Maimonides gives his account of the sacrificial commandments. I would like help working out what the passage below actually argues. My students and I keep collapsing into a summary that I have come to suspect is too crude, and I want a close reading of the text itself rather than the received thesis about Maimonides and the sacrifices. The passage is from M. Friedländer's translation.

Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed. Now God sent Moses to make [the Israelites] a kingdom of priests and a holy nation (Exod. 19:6) by means of the knowledge of God. . . . But the custom which was in those days general among all men, and the general mode of worship in which the Israelites were brought up, consisted in sacrificing animals in those temples which contained certain images, to bow down to those images, and to burn incense before them. . . . It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action.

For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner; viz., to build unto Him a temple . . . to have the altar erected to His name . . . to offer the sacrifices to Him . . . He selected priests for the service in the temple . . . By this Divine plan it was effected that the traces of idolatry were blotted out, and the truly great principle of our faith, the Existence and Unity of God, was firmly established; this result was thus obtained without deterring or confusing the minds of the people by the abolition of the service to which they were accustomed and which alone was familiar to them.

Three things in particular I would like you to sort out.

First, the paragraph aligns the legislation with God's "wisdom and plan . . . as displayed in the whole Creation," which is the theme of the chapter's opening account of the gradual hardening of nerves and of the young mammal fed on milk until its limbs can bear dry food. Is the biological analogy doing real work here, or is it ornament? That is, does the passage present the indirection of the Law as a one-off concession to a particular people in a particular condition, or as an instance of a general divine method of bringing things to their perfection by degrees? Which reading the text better supports, and what turns on the difference, is the first thing I want.

Second, I take the counterfactual about a prophet who came today and told us to serve God "in thought, and not by any action" to be doing more than illustrating the Israelites' attachment to old customs. If such a prophet would be rejected now, what does that imply about whether the external forms of worship are conceived as a merely temporary scaffold? Relatedly, "He transferred to His service" seems to presuppose that the very same act — slaughter at an altar — is idolatrous or acceptable depending on its object, not on its intrinsic character. I would like you to say what view of ritual action that presupposition commits him to, and whether the two halves of the analogy pull in different directions: one toward the external service as a phase to be outgrown, the other toward it as permanently necessary to human beings.

Third, Maimonides immediately anticipates the objection that this makes the commandments "should not have been commanded for their own sake, but only for the sake of some other thing," and answers it by appeal to a scriptural precedent for God's deliberately roundabout dealings with Israel — the leading of the people by the way of the wilderness rather than the short road through Philistine country, lest they see war and turn back. How strong is that answer? In particular, how is the concession meant to coexist with Maimonides' insistence elsewhere that the Law is perfect and not to be abrogated? Does the accommodation reading leave the continuing authority of the sacrificial commandments dependent on a fact about the ancient Israelites that no longer holds, and if so, is that a problem the chapter recognizes or one it passes over?

I am less interested in a verdict on whether Maimonides "really" thought sacrifices were dispensable than in a careful account of what this passage, on its own terms, commits him to. Please flag explicitly where the text underdetermines the answer rather than resolving it for me.
