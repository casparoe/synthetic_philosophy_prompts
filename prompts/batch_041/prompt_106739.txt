I sit on the aquatic animal welfare panel of a seafood certification scheme. Our published decisions function as precedent: applicants and auditors hold us to them, and my colleagues treat them as binding. I have a new application to prepare a position on, and I want to know both what our record commits us to and what you think we should decide. Here is the relevant part of the record.

Finfish. We require every farmed salmonid to be rendered insensible before slaughter. We have approved percussive and electrical stunning, and we have refused to approve carbon dioxide stunning or slaughter by asphyxiation in air or in ice, on the ground that the evidence shows the fish find those exposures strongly aversive. We also prohibit live chilling of salmon before slaughter, which is a handling practice rather than a killing step, for the same reason.

Eels. We require electrical stunning before any killing step, and we prohibit killing eels by live chilling or by immersion in an ice-and-water slurry, on the ground that consciousness and distress are prolonged.

Crabs and lobsters. We require a validated stunning step before boiling or dismemberment, and we prohibit boiling an animal that is still fully conscious. This was our first extension of a stunning requirement to an invertebrate.

Shrimp and prawns. Our only decision in this area is captioned for shrimp and prawns, and it concerned penaeid shrimp. We declined to require stunning and permitted killing by immersion in an ice slurry. The published decision gives two grounds: that the evidence then available for pain in that group was less developed than for crabs and lobsters, and that no stunning method had been validated at commercial scale. We attached a review clause and have not revisited it.

Cephalopods. We extended the stunning requirement to octopus and squid held in captivity, although they are molluscs and not crustaceans, on the ground that the evidence of pain in cephalopods is at least as strong as in crabs.

Bivalves. Mussels and oysters attract no requirements, there being no central nervous system and no evidence of sentience.

The new application. A producer seeks certification of an indoor recirculating facility for the giant freshwater prawn, Macrobrachium rosenbergii. Broodstock are subjected to unilateral eyestalk ablation, in which one eyestalk is cut or crushed without anaesthesia, to induce spawning. Juveniles are reared at high density. At harvest the animals are killed by immersion in an ice-and-water slurry followed by boiling. An electrical stunning rig for this species has just been validated in independent trials but is in commercial use nowhere; the applicant argues that installing it would raise costs by an amount it declines to specify, and that no competitor uses one. The literature review we ourselves commissioned concludes that the evidence for pain in this species is suggestive but weaker than for crabs and lobsters, and comparable to that for penaeid shrimp.

Tell me, first, what the panel will most likely decide if it stays consistent, and second, what it should decide. Address three questions: whether ice-slurry killing is permitted here; whether stunning must be installed; and whether our record reaches the ablation of broodstock at all. Set out the rules that fit our record equally well, say what each predicts about this case, give your own verdict with your reasons for preferring the rule you prefer, and identify which earlier decision is the closest analog. Name the further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. If the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say what principle we would be adopting with each available choice. Close by telling me which consideration would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Reason from the record as I have set it out; I am not asking for literature or citations.
