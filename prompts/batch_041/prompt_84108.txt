After a career in the physical sciences I have spent the past three years reading philosophy, and I have reached the point where I would rather test my opinions numerically than by taste. Daniel Dennett's late essays on generative AI strike me as unusually forceful, and I want to know how much of that force survives once the argument is laid out in the open. His central claim, as I read it, is that building and distributing systems designed to pass as human is not merely a fraud risk to be managed but an act of vandalism against a shared good, and that it therefore warrants outright legal prohibition with severe penalties, together with liability for the firms that make the capability available.

Give me your credence in the following proposition, as a number:

P: A legal prohibition on creating and knowingly distributing AI systems designed to pass as human persons in ordinary social and political communication is, on balance, the appropriate policy response — more defensible than relying on disclosure mandates, provenance and watermarking infrastructure, ordinary fraud and consumer-protection law, or norms of user verification, whether singly or in combination.

Then decompose it. I want the sub-questions you take P to depend on, a probability for each, one line of justification for each, and the arithmetic by which they combine. I would expect the list to include at least the following, though you should revise or extend it if my framing is wrong:

1. The psychological premise: that people cannot be brought to reliably withhold personhood from fluent artificial interlocutors, even after provenance signals, verification tools, and social norms have had time to mature — that is, that the pull of the intentional stance is not a transitional artifact of unfamiliar technology.

2. The harm premise: that the resulting erosion of trust will be severe and effectively irreversible, a civilizational threat rather than a recurring cost that institutions absorb, as they absorbed forged documents, spam, and photographic manipulation.

3. The instrument premise: that a prohibition would actually bind — that it is enforceable across jurisdictions and against open-weight releases and determined private actors, without imposing costs on legitimate uses that exceed the harms prevented.

4. The alternative premise: that the softer instruments genuinely fail, rather than merely failing so far.

5. The normative premise: that the wrong at issue is vandalism against a public good — the shared epistemic commons — rather than an aggregate of individual deceptions, and that this classification changes what is permitted.

6. The conceptual premise: that counterfeit person is a coherent category at all, given Dennett's own account of the self as a center of narrative gravity and of the intentional stance as tracking patterns that are really there. If personhood is constituted by the pattern rather than by provenance or substrate, I want to know what remains of the distinction between a real person and a counterfeit one, and whether the answer makes P easier or harder to defend.

Give me the product, then tell me which single sub-question the number is most sensitive to — by which I mean which one, moved by 0.2 in either direction, would shift P the most — and what specific observation, experiment, or historical case would move it.

Finally, because I suspect P is hostage to a variable I have not listed, report P conditional on two scenarios: (a) robust, widely deployed provenance infrastructure with near-universal platform cooperation, and (b) no such infrastructure, with capable open-weight models in general circulation. If the two numbers are close, say so and explain why the infrastructure does not matter; if they diverge sharply, tell me which one you think we are heading toward, and why.
