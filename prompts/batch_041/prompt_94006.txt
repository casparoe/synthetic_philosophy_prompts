My two flatmates Maya and Sam are both in a welfare-economics reading group and they turned a boring practical question — should we chip in for a stairlift for the guy upstairs or a better AC unit for the flat downstairs — into a fight about whether you can compare how well off two different people are at all. Rough transcript, they were going back and forth:

Maya: Any comparison across people is fiction. When I say my knee hurts 7/10 and you say yours does too, that 7 isn't the same unit. Satisfaction scores, quality-of-life years, 'wellbeing' indices — that's one person's vocabulary applied to somebody else. Within a single life we can rank things, because there's a subject whose preferences do the ranking. Across lives there's no such subject.

Sam: But we make these calls all the time and we're not confused. If you've got a broken leg and I've got a papercut, I don't need a common unit to know which of us would be trading up if we swapped. Here's the test I'd actually use: X is worse off than Y just when X would rather be Y. And rough comparison is still comparison — 'clearly worse' and 'about the same' are judgments we get right constantly. Refusing to compare doesn't make the decision go away, it just hands it to whoever's got the most power in the room.

Maya: Two problems. First, run your swap test and it either reports the comparer's preferences — there's no answer to 'what would X rather' once X has Y's tastes and history — or you ask X, in which case the instrument is contaminated by adaptation. The wheelchair user who's adapted reports she's as happy as you are; your instrument says there's nothing to fix, so it will keep telling us the badly off are fine. Second, even granting you rough level comparisons — who's worse off — you still haven't got what the decision needs. The stairlift doesn't have to make him better off than her, it has to gain him more than the AC gains her, and that's a comparison of differences, not of levels. Thats where the missing unit really bites.

Sam: Then compare less subjective things. Pain, mobility, what someone can actually do. Nobody thinks untreated pain and not being able to leave the flat are just a different taste. And your adaptation argument proves too much — you can't use her report as evidence that reports are distorted and also throw the report away. If reports are worthless you can't show the distortion is happening.

Maya: So you've swapped utility for needs. Fine, but then we're not comparing how well off two people are, we're comparing what each of them is missing — a different project with a different name, and you've been quietly doing it for three moves. And 'whoever has power decides' is an argument for doing the comparison carefully, not for it making sense.

Sam: Partial and contested beats meaningless. You've already conceded that we can say he's worse off, that some conditions are bad for anyone, and that people's reports get warped by adaptation. That's plenty of machinery to decide about the stairlift. Calling it 'not utility' doesn't stop it from being a comparison between persons.
