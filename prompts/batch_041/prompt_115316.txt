I recently finished jury service in a criminal trial, and I was the lone holdout for most of the first day. The experience left me preoccupied with how jurors actually reason toward a verdict: not by deducing from premises, but by comparing competing stories and asking which one makes the most sense of the evidence. I would like a serious critical reading of 12 Angry Men through the philosophical debate about inference to the best explanation. Treat the film as a dramatization of that inferential practice, not merely as a morality tale about prejudice. Where does the lone juror’s reasoning genuinely exemplify inference to the best explanation, and where does it substitute mere doubt, stubbornness, or an impossible demand for certainty? Which explanatory virtues—simplicity, coherence, scope, predictive power, consilience—does the film show the jurors actually using, and which does it ignore? I am especially interested in the distinction between an explanation’s explanatory virtues and its probability: does the film track that distinction, or does it let the persuasive loveliness of a narrative stand in for its likely truth? Does the film illustrate the objection that inference to the best explanation can only choose the best among the available hypotheses, not guarantee that the best is good enough? Where does the film resist the philosophical reading—where does it suggest that jury reasoning is something other than inference to the best explanation, perhaps a matter of social pressure, performance, or the strategic construction of reasonable doubt? Finally, what does the film add to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not? Please answer in American English and in no more than 850 words.
