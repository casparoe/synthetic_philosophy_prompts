My reading group keeps re-litigating Malthus and my friend has decided he's the boy who cried wolf. His line: the doom predictions keep failing, the goalposts keep moving ("not now, but in forty years, when the ratio bites"), and at some point you stop listening to someone who's cried wolf that many times. He's using it to say long-run scarcity/limits-to-growth predictions are unfalsifiable mood music rather than forecasts, and that citing one seriously now is a kind of con.

I think the analogy is carrying more weight than he realizes. Give me the correspondence first — what plays the boy, the villagers, the wolf, the false alarms, the ending, and the moral — then where the mapping breaks. I especially want the break that matters for the thing he's actually using it for: does the fable's own logic really support "stop listening," or does it flip once you notice the boy was lying on purpose and it was the villagers who got eaten? Second, does "he was early, not wrong" have any principled content at all, or does it just mean no failure can ever count against the theory because some auxiliary assumption (tech change, fertility, trade) always absorbs the hit — and if so, is that a defect of the theory or just of long-run prediction in general? And if the fable is the wrong picture, what's a better analogy for a prediction that keeps missing its date?
