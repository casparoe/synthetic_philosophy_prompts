Compare Hume's account of the standard of taste with a position we may call aesthetic calibrationism. On Hume's view, beauty is not a property residing in objects but a sentiment produced in the perceiver; yet he also proposes a standard, namely the convergence of verdicts among critics who combine delicacy of sentiment, practice in comparison, and freedom from prejudice. Aesthetic calibrationism, by contrast, holds that beauty is the disposition of an object to produce a distinctive pleasure in perceivers whose responses have been calibrated through wide and repeated exposure to works across cultures and periods, so that the standard is neither a mind-independent property nor a private sentiment but the stable output of well-calibrated perceptual-cognitive systems. Which of these two views offers the better account of aesthetic normativity, and in particular: does Hume's appeal to qualified critics commit him to a constitutive standard or merely a regulative ideal for settling disputes? Does calibrationism preserve or distort Hume's sentimentalism by relocating the standard into the causal history of the detector? And which view better explains the possibility that an entire culture of apparently well-calibrated judges could be systematically mistaken about the beauty of a work?
