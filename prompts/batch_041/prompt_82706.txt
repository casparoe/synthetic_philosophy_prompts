My department runs a joint logic-and-philosophy-of-mind reading group and this term we've been grinding through the Lucas–Penrose family of arguments — the ones that say Gödel's incompleteness theorems show a human mathematician can do something no machine or formal system can, so the mind isn't a machine. Two of the regulars have been rehashing the same fight for ten weeks: one is an anti-mechanist who thinks the arguemnt basically works, the other is a mechanist who thinks it either begs the question or proves far too much. They've finally agreed to do an "adversarial collaboration" and I've been volun-told to draft the joint statement. Can you write it?

What I need first is a numbered list of claims both of them could actually put their names to — about exactly what the incompleteness theorems do and don't establish, about what it would even mean to claim the mind 'is' a machine or a formal system (and whether that's the same claim as 'is a Turing machine'), about what the theorems are simply silent on, and about which idealizations each side is or isn't entitled to (treating the mathematician as never mistaken, as having unbounded time and memory, as reasoning inside a consistent system, and so on). Then a disagreements table: one row per point where they genuinely split, with the anti-mechanist's position and the mechanist's position each written in a way that side would accept as its own rather than a strawman, plus a column for your judgment of which rows are real disagreements and which are just the two of them using 'knows' or 'machine' differently.

The part I care about most is the cruxes, and I have a hunch they cluster in three places. (i) Whether the anti-mechanist is entitled to trust that her own mathematical reasoning is consistent, without being able to say which formal system she instantiates — I've been calling this 'system-blind self-trust' in my notes — and what the mechanist can say about that trust. (ii) The 'disjunctive' move: I may not know which machine I am, the anti-mechanist says, but for any consistent machine its Gödel sentence is true and I can see that, so I outrun all of them — does that actually go through, or does it quietly need soundness rather than mere consistency, or does it only deliver a conditional that the machine could prove about itself too? (iii) The 'proves too much' worry: if the argument worked, wouldn't it also show that a suitably constructed machine can't 'see' the truth of its own Gödel sentence, i.e. that no machine can be a machine — and if so, isn't the whole thing self-undermining? For each of these cruxes, please also write down (a) the case, argument, or formal result that both sides would agree bears on it — no citations, just spell the thing out — and (b) what each side predicts about how it comes out. If any chunk of the dispute turns out to be verbal, say which chunk and why. Keep the whole thing a document both of them could sign, not a dialogue and not a verdict on who's right.
