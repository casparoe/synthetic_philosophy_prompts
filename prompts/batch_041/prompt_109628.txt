I have been reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections for about fifteen years, and the rule-following material — the cluster of problems that gets nicknamed Kripkenstein — is the one I keep coming back to and keep failing to settle. I can recite the canonical setup and the canonical responses myself (finitely many past applications, infinitely many functions consistent with all of them, dispositionalism, the appeal to communal practice, quietism, primitivism), and I am not asking for any of that back, nor for citations, names of theorists, or a reading list; what I want is to find out what principle my own verdicts add up to, because I suspect they do not add up to anything coherent, and I would rather be told which verdict to give up than be handed a survey. So here are the verdicts, roughly in the order I hold them.

My three-year-old nephew calls every vehicle a truck — cars, buses, tractors. He is wrong about trucks; his word does not mean vehicle, he is simply not competent with it yet. My neighbor Dan has used "soup" for thirty years to cover chilis, stews, and chowders; if I ask for soup he may bring me chili, and if I object he says the chili is a soup and that I am being fussy. I think Dan's "soup" means something a little broader than mine, and that "the chili is soup" is true as he says it. A man I knew after his stroke answered 5 to 68 + 57, and to every sum in that range, and could not be brought to see the error, though he still sat down to do his accounts; I think he no longer meant addition by "+" at all, whatever he took himself to be doing. My partner, exhausted, once said the keys were on the table when they were on the counter, and corrected herself a second later: a slip, and "on" had not moved. On an island I read about, the word for the region we divide into blue and green covers both; children learn it, and the islanders find our distinction fussy rather than wrong; I think their word means blue-or-green, that they are not mistaken, and that we are not mistaken either — just different partitions. A woman who was the last fluent speaker of her language, and who has lived alone for forty years, now says "soon" of things weeks away, consistently, and would be puzzled if I called that a change in her; I lean toward saying her word has changed, that she is the authority on her language now, and not that she has spent four decades in error. A pocket calculator returns 5 for 68 + 57, and so does a language model I tried, and I do not think either of them means anything at all, not even something wrong. And a team of clerks who each add one column of a long sum, none of whom could do the whole thing, return a wrong total: I want to say the team got the addition wrong, and that the team means addition, even though no member of it grasps the whole rule.

My tentative line, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is this: a speaker means whatever norm her use is answerable to, and she is answerable to a norm only insofar as she participates in a practice, which requires that she hold herself to the norm and be corrigible — a mere dispositional profile, like the calculator's, does not qualify. Deviations from the norm she holds are errors; deviations that she has made her own standard, and could in principle teach and be corrected on, are new meanings. That gets the nephew, the stroke patient, and the slip as errors, Dan and the island as new meanings, and the calculator as nothing — but it makes the last speaker an error, which I do not believe, it leaves the team's meaning a puzzle, and it leans on a speaker's opinion about her own practice in a way that ought to be suspicious. Please tell me what the best principle is — mine repaired, or a replacement — stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forces it, its verdicts on two or three cases I have not listed (especially one where a clause does real work and one where it strains), and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot keep. Take a definite position: I would rather have a principle that costs me a verdict, and be told which one and why, than a catalogue of options with the choice left to me.
