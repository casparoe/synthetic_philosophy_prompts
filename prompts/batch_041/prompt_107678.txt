I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I keep bumping into the same op-ed move: America is Rome in the late Republic. The version that's stuck with me is more specific than the usual empire-in-decline stuff. The arguer says the Republic didn't die from a Caesar, it died the moment a majority got tired of a tribune vetoing its bills and just had him deposed; after that no procedure was sacred, the Senate started leaning on its emergency decree, reformers went around the constitution, the army turned into a general's private asset, and one-man rule was the only exit left. The lesson drawn is always some version of: watch the filibuster, the debt ceiling, emergency powers, because that's definately how it started for them.

Can you audit how far that actually holds? Give me the mapping first — what plays the Senate, the tribune, the veto, the emergency decree, the army — then tell me where the mapping breaks and which breaks actually matter for the lesson they're drawing. And be straight about whether the worry survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument. If there's a better analogy for what's really bugging them, I'd take that too.
