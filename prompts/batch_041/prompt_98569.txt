I produce a small interview series on applied ethics, and next week's guest is a philosopher of biology who defends an unusual line on insect sentience. She accepts the scale argument—that if there is even a modest chance that the vast number of insects affected by human activity are sentient, the expected disvalue is enormous—but holds that its practical upshot has been badly overstated. Her distinctive claim is that sentience is a graded property and moral weight should track the degree of sentience, so the astronomical headcounts are largely offset by near-zero per-capita weights; on her view the argument licenses only cheap, low-cost precautions rather than major reforms. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press the internal machinery of this position instead of letting her restate it. Include at least one question on whether her offsetting claim requires a threshold or a nonlinear weighting of moral weight against degree of sentience—and if the weighting is linear, why the product does not still swamp everything—with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; one on how a graded view avoids reintroducing a sharp cutoff when some insects fall below whatever minimal integrative capacity she posits; one on the objection that the same expected-value structure extends to mites, nematodes, and other organisms she presumably wants to exclude, with a follow-up; and one on what she thinks actually follows for a concrete practice such as insecticide use, including a follow-up that presses the possibility that wild insect lives are net positive and that reducing their numbers could be harmful.
