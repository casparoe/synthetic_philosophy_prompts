I sit on a hospital system's ethics committee, which reviews proposals for new markets in health-related goods and services. We have no formal doctrine, but our track record is as follows. We rejected a proposal to let patients sell one of their kidneys, on the grounds that it would exploit poor donors and crowd out altruistic donation. We approved, with conditions, a proposal to let a research consortium pay healthy volunteers for participation in a risky first-in-human trial; the conditions were that payment not be contingent on completing the trial, that it be capped, and that an independent monitor screen for undue inducement. We rejected a proposal to let shareholders sell their votes in a corporate election, on the grounds that voting is a fiduciary act whose point is not to be traded. We approved, with conditions, surge pricing for bottled water and generators during a declared emergency, but only after the company agreed to a price cap and to supply essential medicines at pre-emergency prices; our reasoning was that prices can allocate scarce goods and signal where need is greatest, but that some goods must not be rationed by ability to pay. We rejected a proposal to let individuals buy carbon offsets for their own flights and call it climate neutrality, because it would let people discharge a duty without changing the underlying conduct and because the offsets might not be additional. We approved, with conditions, a market in personal data in which users could opt in, share revenue, and exclude health data, on the grounds that people have an interest in controlling their data but that some data are constitutive of privacy rather than mere commodities. A recurring theme in our reasoning, though never adopted as a formal test, has been a distinction between price as a discovery mechanism, a way of surfacing dispersed information about demand and cost, and price as a rationing device, a way of allocating a good by ability to pay. We have tended to permit the former and resist the latter.

Now a new proposal is before us. A private clinic wants to create a marketplace in which patients on a waiting list for a scarce, life-saving treatment can sell their place in the queue to the highest bidder. The clinic would take a 15 percent fee and use the proceeds to fund treatment for uninsured patients. Sellers would be adults who are competent, have been fully informed, and are not being coerced; buyers would be wealthy patients or their families. The treatment is not experimental, but it is in short supply, and the queue is currently managed by a combination of medical need and waiting time.

The question is not what I personally think is just. It is what our committee should decide if it wants to be consistent with its own precedents. Identify the candidate principles that could explain our past rulings—at least three, and make sure they are genuinely different, not restatements of one another. For each, say what verdict it predicts on the queue-sale proposal and why. Then give your best guess about which principle we are implicitly committed to, with reasons, and say which past ruling is the closest analog. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so, and explain what principle we would be adopting if we approve and what principle we would be adopting if we reject. Finally, name a further case—one not in our track record—that would best distinguish between the surviving principles, and say what verdict each principle would give in that case.
