I'm a TA for a 200-level epistemolgy course and I've got one 75-minute session with about 18 students to make the regress problem feel like a live bind rather than a taxonomy they memorize. Last year I put foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism on the board and asked which is right; they dutifully said coherentism is circular, foundationalism is arbitrary, and infinitism is impossible, and nobody felt anything. This year I want them to actually run into it. My half-baked idea is a tabletop thing where someone makes a claim, someone else can always ask 'why should I believe that?', and every answer costs something: you can stop with a bedrock card, but then you've just asserted it; you can point to another belief you already played, but then you're circling; or you can keep drawing new justification cards, but the deck is finite and there's a clock. I want rules, materials, roles (challenger, defender, maybe a referee who can call 'that's not a justification, that's a restatement'), and a scoring/payoff scheme that doesn't need real money. The structure should push them toward one horn, then let them feel the cost of switching to another, so the debrief can map what happened onto the three positions without me having to lecture first.

Can you design the whole thing -- setup, a 75-minute timeline, exact win conditions, and a debrief that names each move only after they've made it? Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm worried they'll just agree to be nice, or that the 'infinite' defender will realize the deck is finite and game the clock) and how to handle that. And be honest about which feature of the regress problem the activity can't reproduce: I assume it can't really make infinitism attractive, because in the game running out of cards looks like failure, whereas the infinitist says the chain never ends and that's fine; if so, say so and tell me what I have to supply by talking.
