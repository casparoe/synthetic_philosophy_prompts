I am writing a novel in which two of the principals — a senior philosopher of a stubbornly old-fashioned analytic bent and his one-time student, now a colleague of sorts — have it out in a borrowed seminar room over the better part of an afternoon, and I want that argument to be the intellectual spine of the third act. The elder character defends a principle he takes to be nearly self-evident: that knowledge is closed under known entailment, so that whoever knows a proposition, and knows that it entails a second, knows the second as well. The younger defends a rival picture on which a belief counts as knowledge only when it tracks the truth — when the believer would not have held the belief had it been false, and would have held it had it been true — and he maintains, against the elder, that once you accept the tracking condition you must give up closure, because a person may know some perfectly ordinary thing while failing to know a skeptical denial that the ordinary thing entails. I would like you to write the dialogue between them: a genuine exchange, not a pair of monologues, in which each answers the other's actual points, each is allowed to be sharp, and neither is permitted to be a fool. Both should be competent; the elder should not simply wave the flag of common sense, and the younger should not simply recite his formula. Let them press each other and revise under pressure.

I have given them a test case drawn, as it happens, from my own trade, and I need to know how a competent defender of each side would actually handle it. The younger proposes that the elder may know he is sitting in a chair, and may know that sitting in a chair entails that he is not a character in some novelist's manuscript whose every sensation and belief has been scripted for him, and yet may fail to know that he is not such a character — since in the nearest world in which he were a character, his scripted beliefs would be just as they are, and he would believe himself to be no such thing. The elder, I assume, will not let this pass. I want the dialogue to work through, at minimum: whether the tracking condition really returns the verdict the younger claims on the scripted-character case, and whether the elder can turn that case against him; how the elder should respond to the younger's insistence that we can know ordinary things while remaining ignorant of the skeptical denials they entail, given how bizarre it sounds to say that one knows one has hands but does not know that one is not a handless brain kept alive in a vat; and the difficulty of saying which belief-forming method is supposed to be held fixed in the younger's counterfactual, since the verdict seems to shift depending on how finely or coarsely we describe the method.

Toward the end, I would like one of them to force the other to concede something, even if only a qualification, and I would like your own closing sentence or two on who has the stronger position and why. Write it as polished prose in American English, roughly a thousand words, with attributions and stage business kept light so that the argument itself carries the scene.
