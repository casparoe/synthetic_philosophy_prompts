I am a software engineer at Meridian Instruments, with no training in philosophy, and I have been asked to give the five-minute speech at the ceremony where the Founder's Wing is renamed for Ruth Adeyemi. She designed the company's first field instrument, her name was dropped from the record within a decade of her death, and I found her notebooks and drawings last year while I was rebuilding our internal archive; that is how the speaking slot fell to me, and her granddaughter will be in the second row.

The difficulty is the founder. The same archive holds his internal memos from the 1950s and 1960s: arguments against putting women into engineering roles, and a note opposing the promotion of a Black technician on the grounds that customers would not accept it. It also holds a reply from his own chief engineer, written in the same year, saying the policy was wrong and would cost the company its best people — which makes the defence that he was simply a man of his time hard to hold, since the people around him were plainly not all of that time. On other matters he was better than his era required: he kept that technician on the payroll through a year in which we nearly closed, and refused to sign an industry pledge that would have cost him nothing. Someone on the planning committee objected that the whole exercise is presentism, and I did not know enough to answer him.

The room is split. Retired employees who worked under the founder will hear the renaming as a verdict on their own working lives, and I will lose them if I let it sound like one. Newer engineers expect the old name gone and will hear any qualification as cowardice. The board has asked for remarks that honor the past without litigating it, which I do not believe is possible.

Please write the speech. It has to take an actual position on what we are doing when we judge a man long dead and unable to answer, and it has to make something of the awkward fact that his contemporaries disagreed with him — my suspicion is that what people call the standards of a time is usually just the standards of whoever was loudest. It should keep three things apart: explaining why he acted as he did, excusing it, and deciding what goes over a door today. I would rather it be wrong in a clear way than balanced into saying nothing. It should also say what the renaming is for, not only what it is against, and it should be something I could still say about our own memos, since our successors will read them.

No hedging, no quotations from historians, no jargon — the room is not academic. If my own instinct is thin, do not build on it merely because it is mine. Roughly seven hundred words.
