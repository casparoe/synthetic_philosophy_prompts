For the past several months a small reading group I'm in — two philosophers of mind, a computational neuroscientist who works on population-level coding, and me, a cognitive science graduate student — has been circling Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment without ever converging, and I've started to suspect that we are not disagreeing about the thing we think we are disagreeing about. The setup we've been working with is this: a vast arena contains an enormous number of tiny magnetic balls. The balls cling to one another in shifting clumps; the clumps persist long enough to be identified and labeled, and their formation and dissolution track regularities, so it is natural to describe a clump as a symbol and to say that clumps pass information to one another. The question that keeps derailing us is whether, when one clump appears to bring about the formation of another, the clump is doing any of the pushing, or whether all of the pushing is done by the individual balls and the clump-level story is only a convenient redescription. One philosopher insists that the balls do all the shoving and that clump-pushes-clump talk is at best a summary of ball-level events; the neuroscientist insists with equal heat that if you intervene on the clump you change the outcome, and that this is all anyone could reasonably mean by the clump being a cause; the other philosopher keeps raising the worry that if the ball-level facts are already sufficient for everything the clumps do, then the clumps are excluded from doing any causal work. The same exchange, in slightly different words, has now happened at least four times.

Two things about our impasse seem to me worth flagging before I ask for a diagnosis. First, we never settle whether we mean by cause what makes something happen or what makes a difference to whether it happens, and I have noticed that we also slide between asking whether clumps are real and asking whether clump-talk is useful. Second, no experiment on a careenium could settle the matter, since the ball-level and clump-level descriptions are not rival empirical hypotheses about what is there, yet the disagreement does not feel merely verbal to anyone involved. My tentative hypothesis is that the impasse is a collision of two legitimate questions being asked under a single heading, and that some but not all of the disagreement is verbal in a way neither side will concede.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict: what kind of disagreement this actually is; which parts of it are merely verbal and which are not; whether the exclusion worry is a genuine argument or an artifact of a particular picture of levels and of what a level has to be; and what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction between senses of cause, a criterion for when a higher-level description earns causal standing, a demonstration that the ball-level and clump-level claims can both be true, or an argument that the question as posed is ill-formed and ought to be replaced. Please engage with what each side is actually claiming — ball-level sufficiency, the interventionist criterion, and the exclusion claim — rather than with a generic survey of downward causation, and tell me honestly whether our frustration is a sign that the question is hard or that it is badly put.
