I am the program director of a small endowed foundation — roughly forty million dollars, three grant cycles a year, individual grants between two hundred thousand and three million — whose charter names the long-term future of civilization as its ultimate beneficiary. I write the recommendation on every proposal; a five-person board votes; I have about an afternoon per proposal; I am not a philosopher and neither are they. Three of the six proposals before me this cycle share one shape: a small or unestimated probability of an outcome described as astronomical, permanent, or literally infinite. One trustee has started arguing that the simulation argument — the trilemma about ancestor simulations, the third horn of which says we are almost certainly simulated — gives us reason to stop treating the far future as the thing worth protecting and to spend instead on nearer-term, surer benefits. Another trustee replies that if that reasoning counts, it also counts against next year, and that in practice it is a way of dressing up what he already wanted to fund.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run, built on the expected-value framework as it functions in these debates and on the cluster of arguments about unbounded magnitudes and vanishing probabilities — the fanaticism problem — with the reality-doubt arguments as one family within it. I need the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which questions end the matter with a no; which are questions of value that the board must decide and I must merely frame; which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure; and what the procedure cannot do.

It has to tell me how to handle the reality-doubt arguments in particular. My suspicion is that the crux is whether such an argument discounts everything we might do by the same factor — in which case it cannot reorder any two proposals — or whether it attaches specifically to the length of the causal chain and the remoteness of the effects, in which case it does real work but is a time discount in disguise. Work that out inside the procedure, with a test I can apply to a specific argument, rather than asserting one side.

The procedure must also handle, at minimum: outcomes claimed to be infinite; probabilities that are asserted rather than estimated; the difference between the probability that a plan fails and the probability that the world is not as we take it to be; whether the beneficiaries are the kind of beings whose welfare we count; and how much credit we may take for a good outcome that others would probably have produced without us.

Then run it on three proposals. First, a five hundred thousand dollar grant to a group that puts a one-in-ten-thousand chance on averting a permanent, civilization-ending stagnation, with no way for me to check the estimate. Second, a comparable grant with a certain but modest benefit — a program that measurably improves the lives of a few thousand people now. Third, a proposal of your choosing, designed so that the procedure returns an answer I will not like; do not soften it, and explain why the procedure lands there.

Write it as something usable at my desk: a numbered procedure with the exits and hand-offs marked, the applications worked through, and a closing section on where it will misfire. No reading list and no citations, and do not assume I know the literature — whatever the procedure needs from the theory, put it inside the procedure.
