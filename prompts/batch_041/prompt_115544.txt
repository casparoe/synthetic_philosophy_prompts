Second-year PhD here (metaphysics, mostly free will and time). I've been handed one 75-minute session with 14 second-years on future contingents, and the only rule is that it has to be interactive — they have to do something, and I'm not allowed to stand up and lecture about the sea battle for an hour. I've got a half-formed idea and I'd like you to turn it into something actually runnable, or tell me it won't work and give me something better.

What I want them to leave with is the *feeling* of the two pulls that make the problem hard: that a claim about tomorrow is already true or already false right now, before anything happens; and that what they're about to do is genuinely up to them. I don't want them to be able to just list the three standard responses at the end. I want them stuck for a few minutes, in the way you get stuck when both seem obviously right.

My rough sketch. Everyone gets chips. There's a forced binary choice coming later in the session — two boxes, two coloured tokens, whatever — and before it happens they place bets on what the others will pick. They also each write a sealed prediction of their *own* later choice. Then, for contrast, they bet on a second set of things that are already settled but hidden from them (something I've done and sealed before the session starts). Then the choices happen and everything settles.

What I'm really after is the moment where they notice that a record of the future written down before the fact doesn't feel like a record of a coin that's already been flipped, even when the record turns out right. I'd like to stage that with a sealed "oracle" record of everyone's choices that turns out to be correct. I don't mind having to rig it, or picking someone who's reliably predictable, as long as it's honest and the getting-it-right is itself something we then look at. I don't want them to just feel conned.

Then I want a contrarian: a volunteer who, when told the oracle has it written down that they'll pick X, does the opposite. They should get a strong hit of "look, my future isn't written, I can always falsify it" — and then the debrief should show them that this only shows the record was false, not that the future was open. (There's a name for this move in the foreknowledge literature and I can never remember whose it is, doesn't matter.)

So give me the whole thing: materials, rules, roles, the exact sequence with timings, a payoff scheme with no real money, and where I have to bite my tongue and not explain. Then a debrief that maps each stage onto the actual machinery — why the betting makes bivalence feel compulsory, what the sealed coin is doing that the prophecy isn't, why "the record was written earlier" and "the record's truth depends on what you end up doing" come apart, what the contrarian did and didn't show. I want them left with the three options — deny that these claims have truth values yet; keep the truth values but make the truth depend on what happens; or accept it's all fixed and explain why deliberating still isn't pointless — with a feel for what each one costs, not just a list. Plus the honest bit: what will predictably go wrong the first time, and which part of the problem the game simply can't reproduce so I have to supply it myself. No readings or citations, they won't do them anyway.
