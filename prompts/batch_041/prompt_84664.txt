Write me a satire piece for a members' club that has got itself into a genuine constitutional tangle, and write it as the club's own tribunal ruling on the mess — a headnote, numbered paragraphs, a short dissent at the end, deadpan the whole way through, 600-700 words. You'll have to invent the club; say the Nether Wallop and District Angling and Philosophical Society, an institution with a standing orders booklet, a treasurer, and a long history of people drafting amendments in the car park afterwards.

The facts are these. Rule 1 of the standing orders says any rule may be changed by a two-thirds majority of those present at a general meeting. Some years later someone added Rule 14, which says Rule 1 itself can be changed only by a unanimous vote. Last month 61 of the 90 members turned up. A motion (a) repealing Rule 14 and (b) dropping Rule 1's threshold to a simple majority passed 41-19. Then, on the strength of that, a second motion abolishing the annual subscription passed 34-26. The treasurer says the second motion failed, because Rule 14 was plainly still in force at the moment the first motion was counted and it wasn't anything like unanimous; he's refusing to bank the money and has threatened to resign. The same meeting also purported to amend the Rules Commitee's own terms of reference, so the commitee has to decide whether it still has jurisdiction to decide any of this at all.

What I want is for the jokes to come out of the logic, not out of whimsy. Every move the tribunal makes should be one a real lawyer or logician would recognise as fair: whether a rule that has just been repealed still governs the meeting at which it was repealed, whether repealing an entrenching clause requires the entrenchment's own permission, the awkward fact that the founding rule was adopted by acclamation before there were any rules about adopting rules, the difference between changing a rule and changing the rule about changing rules. Nobody is allowed to win by pointing out that it's only a club, or that the rules are just a game — the tribunal takes the rules with total seriousness and never winks. Give the treasurer one line in his own words so we can see he's the only one who has noticed the circle. It should be genuinely funny to someone who's read their Hart and perfectly readable to someone who hasn't, and the dissent should leave the reader honestly unsure whether a rulebook can ever ground its own amendment, or whether the buck always stops somewhere that nobody ever voted for. British English please, and keep the club's voice dry.
