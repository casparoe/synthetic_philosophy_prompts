After thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, I have spent the early years of my retirement reading philosophy of science, and I keep discovering that the methodological maxims I absorbed at the bench behave rather differently once they are asked to do philosophical work. The one that troubles me most is the maxim we used to end arguments with, usually credited to the statistician George Box: all models are wrong, but some are useful. In the laboratory it did a single job — it silenced the colleague who objected that our model assumed a frictionless surface, a point mass, or an infinitely large reservoir — by reminding us that a model is an instrument rather than a portrait. I have since learned that Milton Friedman, in the lead essay of Essays in Positive Economics (1953), advanced something far more aggressive, a principle Paul Samuelson is said to have nicknamed the F-twist: that the realism of a theory's assumptions is simply irrelevant to how the theory is to be appraised, and even that the more significant a hypothesis is, the more wildly inaccurate its assumptions tend to be, since a good hypothesis abstracts away everything that does not bear on prediction. His stock illustration is that firms may be treated as if they maximized profit even when no manager performs any such calculation, the test being the accuracy of the predictions rather than the fidelity of the psychology.

I cannot tell whether the maxim I relied on for so long is a mild and innocuous version of Friedman's thesis, an altogether different claim that merely sounds like it, or something that, taken literally, is simply false. What I would like is a map of the distinct precise claims that the maxim could be making, laid out as a numbered menu ordered from the nearly tautological, through the substantive but defensible, to any that are straightforwardly false, with a note on which reading is actually in play when a scientist deploys the maxim to close a dispute about assumptions. Please take the ambiguity of both halves seriously: wrong might mean false as a description of the world, false in its stated assumptions, incomplete, or not even the kind of thing that admits of a truth value; and useful might mean predictively accurate, explanatory, heuristically fertile, or merely convenient for some local practical purpose.

For each reading I would like a verdict — platitude, live and defensible, or untenable — and, more importantly, a statement of whether it supports Friedman's conclusion that the realism of assumptions is irrelevant to appraisal, leaves that conclusion untouched, or actually tells against it. If it should turn out that the reading which does the real work in Friedman's argument is one that no working scientist would recognize as what they meant by the maxim, I would like to be told so plainly.
