I live in an eleven-unit housing co-op, and I have somehow ended up chairing the committee that decides what to do with a one-time surplus of about sixty thousand dollars. Four options are on the table: replace the roof a few years early, build the playground the families have been asking for, move the money into the reserve fund, or split it among the households as a rebate. I assumed this would be easy. It has not been. When I polled everyone, I found that a majority prefers the roof to the playground, a majority prefers the playground to the reserve fund, and a majority prefers the reserve fund to the roof. The rebate is nobody's first choice but almost everybody's second, and it loses to each of the other three head to head. The roof is also the only option with a real deadline attached to it. Worse, the winner changes depending on how we vote: deciding the options two at a time gives us the roof, a single vote for your favourite gives us the playground, and ranking everything and dropping the least popular until one survives gives us the reserve fund. Each method felt perfectly fair while we were using it. Two neighbours have now proposed things I cannot judge. One wants to hand each household a hundred points to spread across the four options however they like, so that the people who feel strongly about the playground can outweigh the people who mildly prefer the roof. The other wants to fold this decision together with the separate vote we are about to hold on parking, on the theory that whoever loses on the roof should be able to trade that loss for a win on parking. What should we actually do? Is there a fair way to settle this that I can defend to everyone, or is the co-op's preference nothing more than whatever our procedure happens to produce? And if no procedure is simply the correct one, how should I choose between them — and what do I tell the neighbours who are starting to suspect that whoever picks the method picks the winner?
