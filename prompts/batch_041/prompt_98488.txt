I'm a first-year PhD student in a robotics lab that's gone all-in on inverse reinforcement learning, and for the past few weeks I've been stuck at the lunch table while two of the senior students, Dara and Kenji, have been going at the same question over and over: can IRL actually recover what someone wants, or is the whole thing a bit of a con? I've stopped trying to referee and I honestly can't tell who's winning, so I want you to do it move by move.

Dara's line is that you watch a person's behavior, then posit a reward function that makes that behavior optimal — but that's just fitting. Any finite set of demonstrations is consistent with infinitely many rewards, and worse, any two rewards that differ by a potential-based shaping term, something of the form gamma times a function of the next state minus that same function of the current state, induce exactly the same optimal policies in every environment. So the demonstrations can't possibly tell you which one the person "really" has. The best you get, she says, is one representative of an equivalence class, and the choice among representatives is a modeling convention, not a discovery about the person. Kenji's reply is that this is just the ordinary gap between finite data and any hypothesis class — maximum likelihood has the same issue and nobody says statistics is a con — and that once you assume the demonstrator is only *approximately* optimal under some noise model, and collect data across many environments and tasks, the equivalence class collapses and the reward is identified in the limit. Dara comes back that the "approximately optimal, with this particular noise model" assumption is doing all the work, that different rational-noise models give you genuinely different recovered preferences, and that nobody has an argument for why a human's irrationality has the shape the model assumes, so you're smuggling your answer in through the back door. Kenji says fine, but that's true of every model of any cognitive thing — you always need some inductive bias, and calling it smuggling would rule out all of cognitive science; the real question is whether the bias is defensible, not whether it exists. Then Dara says the difference is that everywhere else the bias is independently testable against held-out behavior, whereas here the very thing you'd test against, namely what the person actually wants, is the thing you're trying to infer, so it's circular — and Kenji shoots back that the held-out behavior *is* the test, she's just refusing to count prediction as evidence.
