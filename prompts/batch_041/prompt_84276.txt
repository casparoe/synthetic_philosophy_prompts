I am a philosophy professor assembling materials for a unit on the Meno problem, and I would like to settle my own view before I have to teach it. I have been writing down cases and recording my untutored verdicts on each. In every case, the two people involved end up believing the same true thing and then behave identically; only the route to the belief differs. I want to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to, where it breaks down, and which verdicts I should surrender. Please write in American English, and please give me the principle itself rather than a survey of what others have said about the problem.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. The diagnosis. Two residents both conclude correctly that a patient's confusion comes from an interaction between two of her medications. One derived it from the chart and the pharmacology and can defend it step by step. The other arrived at it by an unexplainable hunch that happened to be right and cannot say why. My verdict: the first is better off. The difference is real, though smaller than the first resident's confidence would suggest.

2. The idle lemma. Two people both believe, correctly, a small lemma buried in the middle of a long proof they will never use, mention, or build on. One once followed the derivation and has a sound memory of the result, though he can no longer reproduce the steps. The other has simply believed it all along, on no basis whatever. My verdict: the first is still better off, even though nothing in either life will ever turn on the difference.

3. The deferential witness. A woman saw an accident, remembers it accurately, and has a true, well-grounded belief about what happened. She is, however, deferential to authority, and if a confident official told her she had it wrong, she would abandon her belief. A second person holds the same true belief on the strength of a rumor he is too stubborn ever to relinquish. My verdict: the first is better off, even though her belief is the more fragile of the two. This seems to me to embarrass the familiar suggestion that knowledge outranks true belief because it stays put.

4. The terminal diagnosis. A man knows his illness is terminal: he has read the results and understood them. Another man holds the same true belief on grounds that would collapse under any reassurance from a doctor, and he will in fact never be contradicted. My verdict: the first man is worse off in the way that matters to him, but I do not take that to show that his epistemic position is worse. I want to keep those two kinds of value apart, though I doubt my principle respects the distinction.

5. The license to assert. Two people both believe, correctly, that a particular candidate will carry a particular state in an upcoming election. One has polling and a model; the other had a dream. My verdict: only the first may flatly assure others, only the first may stop checking, and only the first may be relied upon. I take this to be where the value of knowledge is most plainly visible.

6. The reciter. A child has memorized a true sentence from a chemistry textbook in a language she does not understand and will never use; she can produce it on demand and would assent if asked. I cannot decide whether to call this knowledge, mere true belief, or neither. The uncertainty bothers me, because my principle is stated in terms of knowledge and I need to know what falls under it.

Putting these together, my tentative principle is this: knowledge is worth more than true belief because knowledge, and only knowledge, issues a license — a license to assert without hedging, to act without further inquiry, and to be relied upon by others — and it is the possession of that license, rather than any advantage in stability or in eventual success, that makes the difference. The principle predicts that where nothing is to be asserted, acted upon, or relied upon, the difference should shrink to nothing. Case 2 is precisely such a case, and my verdict there is that the difference does not shrink to nothing.

Please do the following. First, state precisely what my verdicts commit me to, including any verdict I have misdescribed and any distinction I have run together. Second, tell me whether a single principle can capture my five verdicts, and if not, whether the simplest principled account must overturn one of them; if it must, name the verdict and say what should replace it. Third, repair my tentative principle if it can be repaired, or replace it, and state the result in a form that can be tested. Fourth, apply the result to two cases I have not listed: one that separates the surviving accounts from one another, and one that I would probably find counterintuitive but that you think the principle nevertheless gets right. Fifth, tell me the strongest objection to the account you end up recommending and what it costs me to accept it. Where a verdict of mine cannot be accommodated, say so plainly rather than papering over it.
