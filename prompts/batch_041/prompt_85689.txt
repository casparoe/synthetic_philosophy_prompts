I am a novelist in the last stretch of a manuscript, and I have written myself into a corner of an unexpected kind: a dinner-table scene in which a character of mine — call her Ines, a philosopher of probability with a taste for closing arguments — is expected to settle a wager about the two-envelope problem. You will know the setup (two envelopes, one holding twice what the other holds; she takes one, opens it, and may then keep it or exchange it for the other), but the argument she is asked to demolish runs as follows. Let the opened envelope hold a. The other holds either a/2 or 2a, nothing favours either possibility, so the expectation of switching is (1/2)(a/2) + (1/2)(2a), which is one and a quarter times a; therefore she should switch, and having switched should switch again, and never stop. Her interlocutor supplies the familiar retort — that no probability distribution over the amounts can make halving and doubling equiprobable for every a, so the whole thing is a fallacy and the puzzle dissolves. My difficulty is that the familiar retort has a hole in it, and Ines is too good to use it: a woman who has actually opened an envelope and read a number off the page does have a perfectly respectable posterior over what lies in the other one, and she cannot be answered by announcing that the prior was impossible. She has to say exactly what goes wrong for that woman, and why.

I would like you to take the role of a decision theorist who has thought hard about this problem, and to give Ines her answer. Argue for a definite verdict — that the paradox is a fallacy and not a genuine paradox — and defend it by locating the precise step at which the reasoning breaks, showing what the correct treatment of the opened envelope actually looks like, and separating the unconditional argument, which is sound and yields the symmetry between the envelopes, from the conditional argument, which is not, saying exactly where the two part company. She must also answer an objection from the far end of the table: suppose it is common knowledge that the smaller amount was fixed by a coin toss at ten or twenty, so the pair is either ten and twenty or twenty and forty — does the switching argument now succeed, and if it appears to succeed for one value and fail for another, what does that show about the original argument's conclusion that one should switch regardless of what one sees? Finally, she should say what, if anything, the puzzle teaches about the limits of expected-value reasoning, and whether the version in which the player may exchange the envelope any number of times adds anything or merely repeats the first mistake in a louder voice.

Begin by setting out, in a sentence or two each, the candidate diagnoses you can see, and then choose among them and argue for your choice at length. I want commitment rather than a menu: do not lay out four resolutions and leave me to pick, and do not hedge with the observation that much depends on how one formalises the problem. If the verdict I have asked you to defend turns out to be indefensible, say so plainly and argue the contrary — but argue it. Where a step in your reasoning is delicate, mark it as delicate rather than smoothing it over, and tell me, at the end, which single step you would abandon first if you were forced to give one up. Write it as an argument Ines could deliver, not as a lecture to me.
