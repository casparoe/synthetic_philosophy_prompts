I'm in a reading group working through Mill's On Liberty and I've hit a paragraph in the second chapter that I can't get my head around. Here it is:

"But the peculiar evil of silencing the expression of an opinion is, that it is robbing the human race; posterity as well as the existing generation; those who dissent from the opinion, still more than those who hold it. If the opinion is right, they are deprived of the opportunity of exchanging error for truth: if wrong, they lose, what is almost as great a benefit, the clearer perception and livelier impression of truth, produced by its collision with error."

And two other bits I keep flipping back to: "To refuse a hearing to an opinion, because they are sure that it is false, is to assume that their certainty is the same thing as absolute certainty. All silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility." And: "And the world, to each individual, means the part of it with which he comes in contact; his party, his sect, his church, his class of society".

I get the general shape — don't silence opinions because you might be wrong — but the details bug me. Mill slides from "they are not infallible" to "all silencing is an assumption of infallibility", and I don't see how that follows; you can act on very strong evidence without claiming to be infallible. And is the bit about "the world" meaning just the bit you happen to be in contact with a premise in the argument, or just a diagnosis of why people are so cocksure? What I really want help with though is the first passage: it looks like Mill is running two different arguments at once — one where suppressing a possibly-true opinion robs us of truth, and one where suppressing a false opinion still costs us the livelier impression of truth you get from seeing it collide with error. Those seem to need different things from us. Can you walk me through what the passage is actually arguing, step by step, and say which of the two arguments is doing the real work? And one more thing while your at it: Mill also has that example about someone saying corn-dealers are starvers of the poor — fine in a newspaper, punishable if shouted to a hungry mob outside a corn-dealer's house. How is that not just silencing an opinion because of what it might cause? If you can, say what the passage would have to mean for that example to be consistent with it.
