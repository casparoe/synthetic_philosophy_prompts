I've been running a small reading circle out of the back room of a pub for about a year now — nobody in the group has studied philosophy formally, myself included; we're just people who like to argue about books — and this month we read Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France alongside a few pages of Chesterton, largely because one member keeps insisting that Chesterton's fence and Burkean conservatism are the same idea said twice, and I wanted the group to settle it. To give the discussion something to bite on, I set a short written question and asked everyone to answer it in a few paragraphs before we met. Now I'm the one who has to mark them, which I'm not really qualified to do, so I'd be grateful if you would grade one of them for me and, more usefully, explain in detail where it is right and where it goes wrong, so that I can give the group a fair account when we meet.

Here is the question I set: Burke is often called the founder of modern conservatism, and Chesterton's fence is often offered as the conservative's rule of thumb about reform. Are these one idea or two? In your answer, explain what each says about the presumption in favour of inherited institutions, what that presumption rests on, and the conditions under which an institution may legitimately be changed or done away with. Then say whether Chesterton's fence is best understood as a restatement of Burke's position or as something importantly different.

Here is what the member wrote:

"Burke's central insight is that a society is not a fresh contract among the individuals alive at any given moment but a partnership across the generations, binding the living to the dead and to those not yet born. Because our institutions are the slow deposit of generations of experience, they should be treated as presumptively wise rather than as scaffolding to be redesigned from scratch. This is what lies behind his defence of prejudice: he meant by it not bigotry but the inherited, half-articulate habits of judgement, which carry more practical wisdom than any single reasoner could reconstruct alone. It is also why he drew such a sharp line between reform and revolution — reform repairs within the inherited frame, revolution tears the frame down. His famous image of the fence across the road makes the same point: the reformer who wants to clear it away must first be able to say why it was put there.

Chesterton, writing much later, turns this into a rule. The fence is any inherited law or custom; the impatient reformer sees no use in it and wants it gone; the wiser man answers that if you cannot see the use of it, you certainly may not remove it. So the fence stands as a veto: for as long as you cannot give an account of a practice's purpose, it must stay. On this reading Chesterton is simply Burke made memorable — the same presumption of inherited wisdom, dressed up as a parable. The only real difference between them is rhetorical.

What both writers are protecting is the same thing: the limits of individual reason set against the inherited stock of social knowledge. Neither is a defender of mere stagnation; Burke welcomed reform, and Chesterton's fence, once its purpose is understood, may be cleared. But the burden of proof lies with the would-be reformer, not with the institution."

What I'd like from you is a grade — something simple, a mark out of ten with a one-line verdict — together with a point-by-point commentary that justifies it, walking through the answer claim by claim. I don't want a general impression; I want to know which sentences are sound, which are not, and, wherever something is wrong, what the correct statement would be and why the error matters. Please also say how confident you are in each of your judgments, and single out the one point in your assessment that you are least sure about, since I would rather flag my own uncertainty to the group than pass on a correction I cannot defend.
