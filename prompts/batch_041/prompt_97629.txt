I teach a graduate seminar in aesthetics and philosophy of mind, and I have been cornered by a question I cannot answer to my own satisfaction — not by a student, but by my closest friend, who has spent eleven years as a hospice nurse. She told me last week, with real embarrassment, that she can weep for twenty minutes over the death of a character in a novel — most recently a boy who dies of a fever in a book she had already read twice, so she knew the whole time exactly what was coming — and yet has never once cried at the bedside of a patient, including patients she had cared for daily for months and loved. She is not cold; she is the nurse families ask for by name. But the asymmetry disturbs her. She has begun to wonder whether her tears at the novel are the counterfeit ones and her steadiness at the bedside is the honest response, or whether it is the reverse: that the book is the only place her grief is permitted to surface, and that the job has quietly taught her to feel nothing in the presence of the real thing. She mentioned, as if it were a further symptom, that she cried for days over a companion in a video game who betrays her and then dies — more than she cried at her own grandmother's funeral — and that she feels vaguely ashamed of this. She wants me to tell her whether she is being irrational or emotionally dishonest, and whether she ought to do anything about it.

I would rather give her a real answer than consolation, so please don't simply tell me to reassure her. Help me understand what is actually going on in a case like hers: whether an emotion directed at something we know to be invented can be genuine, or whether it must be a different kind of state altogether from grief at a real loss; what the asymmetry between her fictional tears and her professional composure reveals — a defect in her, a feature of how these emotions work, or neither; and whether the fact that she already knew the boy would die changes the diagnosis. I am also curious whether her shame rests on a presupposition that a careful account would reject. Then tell me what, concretely, I should say to her and whether she should try to change anything. Open with your bottom line in one or two sentences, then explain it; keep the whole reply to at most 450 words, in American English.
