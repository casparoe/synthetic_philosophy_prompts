I'm running a small reading group on formal epistemology — mostly philosophy grad students who've had a basic probability course, plus a couple of friends from cognitive science who are comfortable with Bayes but haven't done much philosophy of probability. Next month's session is on the Judy Benjamin problem, and I'm the one leading it. I know the setup: Judy parachutes into a territory divided into Red and Blue, each split into Headquarters and Second Company. She starts with equal credence in all four quadrants. Then she gets a radio message that she interprets as a conditional: if she's in Red, then she's in Second Company. She doesn't learn she's in Red, or that she's in Second Company; she only learns the conditional. The question is how she should update. The usual conditionalization rule doesn't apply because she didn't learn an unconditional fact. And there are at least two plausible updating rules that give different answers — one that minimizes the change in the conditional probabilities, and one that minimizes the change in the overall distribution (something like relative entropy). The problem is that it's not obvious which rule is right, or whether the question even has a unique answer.

I don't want to just explain the problem to them; I want a plan for how to teach it in about 45 minutes. What has to come first, what depends on what? I'd definately like to use a different carrying example than the military one — maybe a detective who learns something like: if the butler did it, then the weapon was a candlestick, with some degree of confidence. What should that example have and lack so that it doesn't smuggle in the answer? Which misconceptions will they form — I'm guessing they'll think it's just about ambiguity in the message, or that you can just conditionalize on the material implication and be done, or that the different rules are all equally arbitrary — and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off? What's a good check for understanding at the end, and what should I openly refuse to explain at this level (e.g., the full formalism, the proof that the rules diverge in general)? Also, if there's a way to connect it to something they already care about — like updating on conditional advice from an unreliable AI, or a doctor's conditional prognosis — I'd love a suggestion for where that would fit.
