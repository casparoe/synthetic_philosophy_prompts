I am teaching a unit on L. A. Paul's account of transformative experience: her distinction between experiences that are epistemically transformative, whose subjective character you cannot know without undergoing them, and those that are personally transformative, changing what you care about; her argument that the combination defeats rational choice about whether to become a vampire or a parent; and her proposal that we choose instead on the basis of the revelatory value of discovering what the experience is like. I want to pair the unit with Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016), in which the linguist Louise Banks learns the Heptapod language, comes to experience time nonlinearly, and then chooses to have a daughter whose death at twelve she has already, in some sense, witnessed.

Read the film through Paul's framework and tell me where it illuminates and where it resists. Does Louise's foreknowledge dissolve the epistemic problem or relocate it: does she know what it will be like for the person she will have become, and does a memory of an experience she has not yet had count as knowing what it is like? Given the film's picture of time, is her choice a decision at all, or something nearer to assent, and what becomes of the revelatory solution when the discovery precedes the experience rather than following it? Paul's account is also relentlessly first-personal, whereas here the transformation falls on Ian and on the daughter through a choice that was never theirs; does the view need a relational or second-personal notion of transformative experience, and what would that notion have to say? Finally, press on the film's premise that a new language restructures what one can think and value: does that make Louise's change epistemic, personal, or a case in which the distinction cannot be drawn? Use the film as a case that pressures the theory rather than illustrating it, and end by saying what it shows that the arguments alone do not.
