Write a joke about the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem.

I want the humour to come out of the theorem itself, not out of the familiar observation that people shade their reports when it pays them to. Someone who knows the result should laugh because the joke gets the internal structure right — because it turns on which hypothesis is doing the work — and not merely because they recognise the topic. Please do not build it on the moral that elections are rigged or that politicians lie; that is the headline, and it is not yet a joke.

Here is the material the joke should exploit. The theorem concerns deterministic rules that take a profile of ordinal preferences as input and return a single winner: if the rule's range contains at least three alternatives and the rule is not dictatorial, then it is manipulable, meaning that at some profile some voter does better by reporting something other than her true preference, whatever the others report. What makes the result worth joking about is where the pressure comes from and where it can be relieved. Two alternatives are safe, since with two options majority rule gives every voter a dominant strategy. Restricting the preference domain is safe: if preferences are single-peaked, the rule that selects the median reported peak makes truthful reporting weakly dominant. Replacing a winner with a lottery over winners is safe in a different way: picking one voter uniformly at random and letting her choose is anonymous, ex post efficient and strategy-proof, and it is the only rule satisfying all three. Attaching money to the decision buys efficiency together with dominant strategies in the Groves manner, though generally at the cost of budget balance, which expected-externality mechanisms restore only once the incentive requirement is weakened to Bayesian equilibrium. And the revelation principle means that no ingenious indirect mechanism escapes the theorem; only a changed specification does.

So the punchline should be anchored to one of those joints — the third alternative, the restricted domain, the return of a set rather than a single winner, the lottery, the transfer, or the shift from dominant strategies to Bayesian play. It should be a joke that would become false, or would collapse into triviality, under a different hypothesis, and a listener who knows the theorem should be able to tell which hypothesis is being exploited.

Keep it short: a couple of exchanges, or a few lines at most. Give it a concrete setting. Write it for someone who knows the result but not the specialist literature, so let it work from the mathematics rather than from any named paper, textbook or author. Then add two or three sentences identifying the hypothesis or escape route your joke exploits, and saying what a reader who missed that point would take the joke to be about.
