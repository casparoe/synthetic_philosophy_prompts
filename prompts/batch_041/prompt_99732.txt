I teach a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar in medieval ethics; there are 14–16 students, and they have read some Aquinas but not this material closely. I want a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel the force of his actual position on killing a tyrant — not the cartoon version in which tyranny is unjust and therefore may be overthrown, and not the quietist version in which subjects must simply endure. What I want them to run into is the practical squeeze: that disturbing a tyrannical government is not itself sedition, and yet the very same considerations that license the disturbance also fence it in, so that a would-be liberator can end up occupying the structural position of the seditious party. They should arrive at that squeeze through the mechanics of the exercise, before anyone names it.

A few constraints on the design. Invent the setting — a small city-state and a ruler, with enough concrete detail to make the stakes vivid — rather than using a real historical case, so that no one can short-circuit the exercise by recalling how the real case turned out. The rules must make at least three things mechanically live rather than merely mentioned: (1) private initiative is always available but is marked as private, while legitimate authorization is a scarce public thing that can be withheld, contested, or dissolved; (2) delay has costs that compound, so that toleration is not a free option and the case for acting grows more urgent the longer the group deliberates; (3) each participant has some genuine private grievance against the ruler, so that acting on the common good and acting on one's own injury are distinguishable in what the players do, not just in what they say. At least one role should have a strong reason to prefer that the ruler survive, and that reason should be defensible rather than villainous. There should be a real chance that the group's intervention makes things worse, and the rules — not the facilitator's narration — should be what makes that chance visible and costly. No lecture at the start, and the words "sedition," "tyrannicide," and Aquinas's name should not appear until the debrief.

For the debrief, I want to work from a short passage rather than from my own summary. Please build the debrief around this line from the Summa: "Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government." The same article adds that it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely. The debrief should map specific moments in the run onto those clauses — the authorization condition, the inordinateness condition, and the reversal by which the ruler turns out to be the seditious one — and should be honest about which of those conditions the activity made feel inevitable and which it left as a stipulated rule. I would also like the debrief to press the students on the practical question the text raises but does not settle: how one is supposed to know, in advance and under pressure, whether the disturbance will be inordinate, and what it does to the position if that judgment is not available to the people who must act.

Please give me the design under these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths: Overview (100–150 words); Setup and materials (200–250 words); Roles (300–400 words); Rules and payoffs (400–500 words); Run of play, minute by minute (300–400 words); The squeeze (150–200 words, explaining exactly where and why participants should feel the position's force); Debrief (400–500 words); What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it (250–300 words); What the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself (200–250 words).
