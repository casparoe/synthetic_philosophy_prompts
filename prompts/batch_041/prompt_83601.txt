our council's scrutiny committee chair has got it into his head that the fix for planning is sortition — pick thirty residents at random, like a jury, and let them decide. his whole case is that we already trust twelve random people with a bloke's liberty, so trusting thirty random residents with a bypass route is nothing much. and he keeps pulling extra bits out of it: the planning inspector should sit there like a judge and tell them what they're allowed to consider, they shouldn't be let loose commissioning their own evidence, their decision should be final with no appeal to the secretary of state, and you should sit once and never again, same as we don't have professional jurors. he even says juries can ignore a law they think is barmy, so the panel should be able to set aside national planning policy if it wants.

how far does the jury analogy actually hold here? give me the correspondence table — who or what plays the judge, the defendant, the law, the two sides, the verdict, the appeal — then the places it breaks, and what it's quietly importing from jury trial that a planning decision just doesn't have. then tell me whether his four conclusions (inspector as judge, no own evidence, no appeal, one term) survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying them the whole way. if there's a better analogy for what a sortition panel is actually doing, say what it is. British English please.
