I have been trying to settle what I actually believe about linguistic relativity, and I would rather work from a set of verdicts I am confident about than from a general thesis. Here are the cases and my verdicts on them.

Suppose a language has no numerals beyond one, two, and many. I am confident that its speakers can see that one pile of pebbles is larger than another, and equally confident that they will do poorly at any task requiring them to produce exactly seven pebbles or to carry an exact quantity across a delay. Verdict: the missing words cost them a task, not a concept.

Suppose a language uses a single term where I use separate terms for blue and green. I expect its speakers to see the difference, and to be slower and less sure than I am when sorting similar shades or remembering which shade they saw. Verdict: the difference lies in naming and memory, not in what they can see.

Suppose a language describes where things are by fixed compass directions rather than by left and right, so that a speaker says the spoon lies to the north of the plate. I think its speakers genuinely think about arrangements differently, and not merely talk about them differently. I would call this a case of language shaping thought, and I would not be satisfied by the reply that they merely have a different habit of speech.

Suppose a language obliges every speaker to mark how they know what they are asserting — seen, inferred, or reported. I think speakers of such a language become more alert to the sources of their own beliefs than I am, and again I mean this as a difference in thought and not only in grammar.

Now the cases that pull the other way. Suppose a language has no word for freedom, or for irony, or for any other single concept I care about. I do not think its speakers are unable to conceive of the thing; I think they would conceive of it under some other description, and that a patient translator could render the concept given enough words. I think the same about every proposition I can state: there is no thought I can have that is in principle out of reach for a speaker of any other language. And when I switch between the two languages I speak, I believe I am having the same thoughts with different emphasis, not different thoughts.

The case I cannot settle is this. Suppose a grammar obliges a speaker to mark a past event as witnessed or as reported, so that there is no way to say in a single sentence that something happened without committing to how one knows it. I think such a speaker can still have my neutral thought — one can think about the evidence without asserting anything — but cannot say it. If that is right, then sayability and thinkability come apart, and I no longer know which of the two my earlier verdicts were about. If the compass-direction and evidentiality cases show language shaping thought, they must shape something more than what can be said; but if all they shape is what is habitually said, then my sense that those speakers think differently was an illusion.

I would like you to tell me what thesis, if any, these verdicts add up to. State the strongest version of relativity that survives all of them, say which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and say what it predicts for a case I have not mentioned — a language with no grammatical tense at all, whose speakers nevertheless have words for before and after. Be precise about the difference between a language obliging a category and merely offering one, since I suspect most of my verdicts turn on it, and about whether the thesis you give me concerns what can be thought, what is easily thought, or what is said.
