I'm putting together the take-home final for my 300-level ethics of war course and I want one essay question on conscription. Problem is every version I write gets me the same canned answer — conscription violates autonomy, the all-volunteer force is a poverty draft, dignity of the person, etc. — and they've had all of that in the readings all term. I want something they actually have to think through.

The angle I'm chasing: most of the conscription debate quietly assumes the conscripts are going to get used, i.e. that there's a war. But the countries that actually still conscript, alot of the time, are ones whose security doctrine is basically "we are not going to come fight you, but if you come here it'll be a nightmare" — Switzerland, Finland, Sweden now that it's brought it back. In those places the conscript army's main job looks like deterrence: make the place too expensive to take. Nobody's planning to invade anybody. So:

Does the deterrence-only justification for conscription survive the objections that are supposed to sink conscription generally? The usual objection is that you're forcing people to gamble with their lives for a state project they may not endorse. But in the neutral case you're forcing them to train and stand ready to gamble their lives for a policy whose whole content is that they probably won't have to. Does that make it better, worse, or just differently bad? And then the part I really want: if the good you're buying is deterrence, a smaller professional force with good kit can deter too. So what does mass conscription add that a professional force doesn't? If the answer is "it's cheaper," does cheapness get to do any moral work, or is that just admitting you're buying bodies at a discount?

I've been calling this the difference between "readiness conscription" (you're conscripted so that there's a visible reserve, and the reserve's whole point is that it never gets used) and "mobilization conscription" (you're conscripted because the war effort needs bodies). I genuinely don't know if that distinction is doing any work or if I'm just relabeling. Part of what I want the students to do is tell me.

So please write me:

1. The exam question itself, ~150 words. Take-home, they get a week. Build into it a stipulated case so they can't answer from memory: a small neutral state, call it whatever you like, 8 million people, currently runs six months of universal male conscription plus a reserve, and there's a proposal to scrap it, stand up a smaller professional force, and spend the savings on drones and territorial defense. The question should ask whether scrapping conscription changes the moral status of the arrangement, not just its efficiency.

2. A model answer, around 700 words, written as if by a strong student.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 that separates a high pass from a distinction by naming the specific moves each has to make. I want the scheme to punish two things in particular: (a) treating "deterrence" as obviously good without saying what makes a threat credible rather than a bluff, and (b) smuggling in the assumption that conscripts consent just by virtue of being citizens.

And if the framing is confused — if the readiness/mobilization thing is a distinction without a difference, or if asking about a country that isn't planning to fight is just a different question from the conscription debate everyone else is having — tell me that up front instead of writing the exam anyway. I'd rather know than get a polished exam built on a muddle.
