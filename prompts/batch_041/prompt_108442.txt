I am reading David Hume's essay "Of Suicide" and I am stuck on a particular stretch of its argument. Here is the passage:

"To prove, that Suicide is no transgression of our duty to God, the following considerations may perhaps suffice. In order to govern the material world, the almighty creator has established general and immutable laws, by which all bodies, from the greatest planet to the smallest particle of matter, are maintained in their proper sphere and function. To govern the animal world, he has endowed all living creatures with bodily and mental powers; with senses, passions, appetites, memory, and judgment; by which they are impelled or regulated in that course of life, to which they are destined. These two distinct principles of the material and animal world continually encroach upon each other, and mutually retard or forward each other's operation. The powers of men and of all other animals are restrained and directed by the nature and qualities of the surrounding bodies; and the modifications and actions of these bodies are incessantly altered by the operation of all animals. ... But tho' the provinces of the material and animal powers are not kept entirely separate, there result from thence no discord or disorder in the creation: On the contrary, from the mixture, union, and contrast of all the various powers of inanimate bodies and living creatures, arises that surprizing harmony and proportion, which affords the surest argument of supreme wisdom.

"The providence of the deity appears not immediately in any operation, but governs every thing by those general and immutable laws, which have been established from the beginning of time. All events, in one sense, may be pronounced the action of the almighty: They all proceed from those powers, with which he has endowed his creatures. A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation."

My difficulty is this. Hume appears to be arguing that suicide cannot be a transgression of duty to God because God governs the world through general and immutable laws, and human powers are themselves part of that system. On this view, a person who takes his own life is no more an interference with divine providence than a house that falls by its own weight. But if that is the argument, it seems to prove far too much: it would show that no human action whatsoever could be a transgression of duty to God, since every action is equally an exercise of powers God endowed and equally falls under the general laws. That cannot be what Hume wants, because he still treats duties to God as a category. I need help with three things.

First, what exactly is the argument in the passage supposed to establish? Is it a claim about all actions, or only about the specific charge that suicide is an act of usurpation of God's prerogative?

Second, how does Hume avoid the "proves too much" consequence? Is there a distinction between acting within the system of general laws and violating a duty to God that does the work, and if so, what is it? Or does Hume simply accept that the category of duty to God is not about interfering with providence at all, and that his argument only dismantles one particular way of grounding that duty?

Third, the argument appears to rely on a conception of providence as general rather than particular — the deity governs "not immediately in any operation" but through laws established from the beginning. If an opponent holds that God's providence is immediate and particular, does Hume's argument still have any force against that opponent, or is it merely an ad hominem against a deistic view of providence? I would like a careful reconstruction of the argumentative steps, an assessment of where the real philosophical work is being done, and an explanation of whether the argument is best read as a standalone proof or as a dialectical move against a specific theological position.
