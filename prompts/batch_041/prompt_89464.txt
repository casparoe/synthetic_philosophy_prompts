im putting together a small anthology of poems about philosophy of language for a reading group, and i need one for the twin earth session. not the standard version though — the one where a guy and his molecule-for-molecule duplicate on another planet both say water and mean different stuff. everyone writes that poem and it basically writes itself. i want the messier case.

heres the setup. one guy. whole life on earth, ordinary, says water all day long, means H2O. one night he gets swapped onto the twin planet — atom for atom, in his sleep, no gap in consciousness, nothing to notice — and from the inside its the same as it ever was. same brain doing the same thing. same noises coming out of him. but at some point the stuff he's been calling water stopped being the stuff he grew up calling water, and theres no moment he could point to, and he'd fail any test that asked him to name it. he isnt confused about anything. he knows what he means. he just cant say what he's referring to.

can you write me a pantoum out of that. (if the full form is too tight, quatrains where the 2nd and 4th lines of a stanza come back as the 1st and 3rd of the next, with the last stanza picking up the first, is fine — same idea.) four or five stanzas. the returning lines should be the things he keeps saying, word for word identical every time they come back, and by the end they should sit differently than they did at the start even though not one word inside them has changed. the pantoum earns its keep here because a line coming back is hard to tell apart from a line that just rhymes with something — you lose track of whats new and whats returned — and i want that uncertainty doing real work, not decoration. but i dont want the shift to be a pun or a cute trick of the line sitting next to new words. it should feel like the ground moved under him, and the poem should never say when.

constraints, and these actually matter:

- nothing in the poem can tell us which side of the swap he's on. no clue from the inside. he cant feel it and neither can the reader — by the end i want the reader in exactly his spot, unable to say whether theyve just read the before-poem or the after-poem.
- dont let him notice a difference in his concepts or in how the word feels. the second he does, its internalism and the poem is dead.
- no resolution. dont say when it happened. dont have him decide whether he's still the same man. and dont let the poem imply his word definitely did change — for all anyone knows, maybe it never did.
- it should end on the strange part: he has perfect access to his own thoughts and no access at all to what theyre about. i dont want that announced as a thesis, i want it to be what the poem is doing when it stops.

accuracy-wise: a philosopher of language shouldnt wince. no new facts learned, no new beliefs, no gap in memory, no describable moment of doubt, no identity crisis. he isnt anxious. he's just talking, and the only thing thats moved is outside his head.

no jargon on the page. keep it plain.
