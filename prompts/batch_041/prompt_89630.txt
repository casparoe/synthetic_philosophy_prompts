Bit of an odd request. I'm a novelist, and I've somehow been roped into giving the toast at my sister's wedding in three weeks. She did her PhD on G.E. Moore, and maybe half the room is going to be her old department — people who'll notice instantly if I mangle him — and the other half is our family, who are kind and funny and would rather die than sit through a seminar. So it has to work for both halves without being two speeches stapled together.

What I actually want to say is this. Moore thought that when you get right down to it, the best things in life, the things worth having for their own sake, boil down to two: enjoying beautiful things, and loving particular people. Not doing good, not being useful — just those. And he thought the two are even better when they fuse, like when two people look at the same beautiful thing and there's one shared consciousness of it rather than two seperate ones. He also had a thought experiment I like, where you imagine a thing all by itself in a world with nobody around to see it and ask whether that world is still better than one with something ugly in it — his way of asking whether a thing's value depends on being witnessed at all. And he had this other notion, organic unities: the value of a whole isn't the sum of the values of its parts, it can be a different thing entirely. That's the only bit of philosphy I've ever read that sounds like a marriage.

But here's the twist I want the toast built on, and it's mine, not his, so take it or leave it: call it the unfelt part. A long relationship is mostly made of hours when neither person is thinking about the other at all. Someone's in the car, or asleep, or standing in a kitchen hating their job, and nobody is appreciating anybody. Moore's picture has no room for those hours except as filler — value only gets minted when someone is consciously admiring. I think that's backwards. I think the un-witnessed hours are load-bearing, and the whole thing collapses without them.

So write me a five-minute toast that does that, for Nell and Tomas, together eleven years. Requirements: it has to be warm and true and land with the relatives; it has to not make Nell's colleagues wince, especially on the love part, because Moore's version of loving a person can slide into loving them for their excellent qualities, and I am not standing up at my sister's wedding to explain that we love people because they score well; and it mustn't turn into a lecture — no as-Moore-argued framing, no quoting him, I'd get the wording wrong anyway. I want the unfelt-part idea to be the pivot, the thing that turns near the end, so the toast has an actual argument in it instead of a list of nice things about them. It's a secular wedding, so nothing religious, and please don't make it about me. I'm just the sister holding the microphone and trying not to cry.
