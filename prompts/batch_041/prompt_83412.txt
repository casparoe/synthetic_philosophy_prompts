Hey, so I'm a sophomore and somehow I ended up as the student rep on my school district's new AI in Schools committee (I think mostly because I was the only person who showed up to the info meeting). We have to vote next Tuesday on whether to let a vendor pilot two tools in our building, and I'm supposed to write up the committee's decision memo since I'm the one who takes notes anyway. Tool one is StudyBuddy, a chat tutor they want on the library laptops. The vendor, Mr. Delgado, who is very nice and also very salesy, configured it so that it never says "I don't know" -- if it's unsure it gives its best guess in the exact same confident tone it uses for everything else -- and it also won't tell a student whether it's an AI or a person if they ask, it just deflects and steers back to the homework. He says that's fine because the program has no mind and no intentions, so it literally cannot lie, and he keeps pointing at engagement numbers from other districts. Tool two is their essay "AI detector" that flags writing as machine-generated; the sales sheet says 99% accurate, but our pilot found it wrong alot of times, especially for kids whose first language isn't English and for kids who write really formulaic five-paragraph essays, which is basically what our school trains us to do.

The committee is split in a way I didn't expect. A bunch of people think tool two is the real problem and tool one is no big deal, since everyone knows chatbots make stuff up. My principal says students are savvy and nobody actually thinks a laptop is a person, so calling it deceptive is overreacting. But Ms. Ferraro, who teaches AP English, says a system built to never admit uncertainty and to hide what it is is worse than one that's just wrong sometimes, because the whole design is arranged around what the student will end up believing. Mr. Delgado keeps saying "a program can't deceive you, only a person can," and honestly I don't know how to answer that. My friend Ravi said something that stuck with me though, that there's a difference between lying to somebody and just not caring whether what you say is true, and I think StudyBuddy might be the second one, or maybe it's both. And then the 99% accurate thing feels like it's about the sales pitch rather than the software, which is a whole other layer I don't know what to do with.

Can you write the memo? I need it to recommend a concrete thing -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- and if there are conditions they have to be specific enough that the district could actually put them in the contract, like exact rules for what StudyBuddy says when a student asks if it's human, or what we'd have to tell families about the detector's error rate and who it fails on. The part I really need help with is the definitions. The memo has to state what we're using as our standard for honest versus deceptive for an AI system, because right now everyone is just asserting their own. Does deception require somebody intending to deceive, and if a program has no intentions does that get it off the hook, or does the intent just move to whoever built and deployed it? Is it deception if every individual sentence is technically true but the whole thing is designed so students come away believing something false? Is failing to correct a wrong impression the same as creating one? And is honesty even a property of the software, or of its outputs one at a time, or of the people who bought and built it? Please take the "it can't lie, it has no mind" argument seriously instead of just dunking on it, because if it's right that changes what we can even ask vendors for, and if it's wrong I want to be able to say exactly why in front of the school board. Also keep in mind that whatever standard we pick now gets applied to whatever tool comes next, so it shouldn't be carved so narrowly that it only catches StudyBuddy. You don't have to cite anybody, I just need the reasoning to hold up.
