I've been argueing with a friend about whether the big 3G licence auctions of 2000 were basically a dollar auction in slow motion, and I want a verdict rather than vibes. The case as I have it -- work with this rather than going off to check, though say if something looks off. Britain sold five 3G licences that spring for sums nobody had predicted. Germany went next: twelve frequency blocks, a licence to any bidder holding at least two of them and no more than three to any one bidder, so somewhere between four and six licensees; seven bidders turned up, the thing ran from 31 July to 18 August, and it raised about 50.8 billion euros. The bidding only stopped once everyone had quietly settled on two blocks each; before that several bidders were bidding on three, which pushed the price up and threatened to cut the number of winners to five or four. Two of the winners later handed their licences back. And the twist that matters here: the losers definately paid nothing. They went home with their deposits.

So run the case through the dollar auction -- the game where a dollar goes to the highest bidder but the runner-up also pays what they bid. Do the mapping properly: who's the auctioneer, what exactly is the prize, what does dropping out cost you, why is the next bid rational for whoever is currently in front, and what would the theory say the bidders should have done instead. Then tell me where it breaks, and press on three things. The dollar auction's engine is the runner-up's bill and here there wasn't one, so if the bidding still went past anything the licences were worth, what was doing the work -- and does that make the dollar auction the wrong model rather than just an incomplete one? Second, in the dollar auction the prize is worth a fixed amount to you whoever else is playing; here a licence is worth more the fewer people hold one, and some of the German bidding looks aimed at shrinking the field rather than at winning. Can the theory absorb that, or is it a different game? Third, the auctioneer: in the dollar auction they gain from keeping the game running forever, and here the seller was a state that had to live with the aftermath -- an industry loaded with debt, networks arriving years late, huge write-downs, two licences handed back. Fixable detail, or structural limit?

Then turn it round. What does the episode show about the dollar auction, and about escalation of commitment more generally? Was this escalation irrational in the sunk-cost sense, or is it better read as rational play inside a badly designed game, and can you actually tell those two apart from the outside? And what would the theory say would have stopped it, versus what plausibly did?
