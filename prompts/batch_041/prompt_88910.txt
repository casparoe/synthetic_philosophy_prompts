I lead a monthly lunchtime discussion group at my company for engineers with no background in philosophy, and next month I am on the hook for the inefficacy objection — the family of arguments that since one vote, one hamburger, or one short-haul flight makes no measurable difference to the outcome, the individual act cannot be wrong for the reason usually given. I am not asking you to argue the point; I am asking you to plan the session. What must the group already accept before the problem even looks like a problem? I suspect they need a working distinction between making a difference and being part of something, but I do not know what else has to come before that, or in what order, and I would rather not discover the dependency structure by watching the discussion stall.

I want the whole session to hang on a single carrying example. I was going to use the marginal meat purchase, though I am wary that the hamburger version smuggles in something that does the work for us; I also considered an engineering analogy (no single commit takes down the build), which may or may not import a distortion. Tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack, and whether either of mine survives. I plan to organize the hour around three rival models of how individual acts add up — linear increments, thresholds, and participation in a practice — so tell me whether that is the right spine or whether two of them collapse into one. Then give me the misconceptions to expect, in the order they will surface, the point in the sequence where each gets headed off, the single question at the end that shows whether the idea landed, and what I should say out loud that we are not going to settle at this level.

Finally, tell me which step in your dependency chain you are least confident is really a dependency, and what would show that it is not.
