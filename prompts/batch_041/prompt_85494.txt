I've spent the last week losing a comment thread — the kind that runs to four hundred replies on a rationalist blog — about Gödelian anti-mechanism, and I've concluded that the reason nobody can win it is that nobody ever says what the argument is supposed to deliver, so every reply quietly moves the target. I want the specification instead: the desiderata that any argument of the Lucas–Penrose family, the claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mathematical mind cannot be a formal system or an algorithm or a Turing machine, has to satisfy before it should move anyone. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that generates it — the mathematician who might after all be inconsistent, the machine that asserts the Gödel sentence of some weaker system, the regress of ever-stronger systems, the fallibility of actual working mathematicians, and whatever else you think belongs — and then say honestly which of these are genuine requirements and which are merely what one camp happens to want. Where two requirements pull against each other, say so and tell me which you would sacrifice; my own suspicion is that the demand that the argument be about real, finite, error-prone mathematicians and the demand that it credit us with knowledge we cannot survey are in exactly that position.

Take the role of a philosopher of mathematics and mind who has followed this literature closely. Think carefully, and before writing anything, brainstorm the stock cases and objections the debate turns on, so that your list is generated from them rather than from a template. Two things I want worked in as test cases, because I think they are where the usual discussion goes soft. First, the target: most versions slide between the mind is not a Turing machine and the mind is not any formal system at all, and I want the list to say which of those the argument has to establish, and whether one set of requirements serves both readings. Second, self-referential software — programs that search for proofs about their own source code and then rewrite themselves on the strength of what they find. I do not care whether such a thing is a good engineering idea; I want to know whether its bare coherence changes what an anti-mechanist has to deliver, and specifically whether the step that no machine could recognise the truth of its own Gödel sentence survives once machines can reason about, and alter, their own code. Treat that as a pressure test inside the list, not as a topic in its own right.

Structure the answer as follows. "Framing", about 120 words, saying exactly what is being denied and what counts as a machine for these purposes. "The requirements", 500 to 700 words, the bulk of the piece: numbered items, each stating the requirement, the case behind it, and a label of load-bearing or preference of one side. "Conflicts", 200 to 300 words, on the pairs that cannot be jointly met and which horn you would take. "Scorecard", 200 to 300 words, covering Lucas, Penrose, and the strongest pro- and anti-mechanist replies you know, each in two or three sentences against the list, ending with your judgement of which single requirement eliminates the most candidates. A table is welcome in the scorecard if it earns its place.
