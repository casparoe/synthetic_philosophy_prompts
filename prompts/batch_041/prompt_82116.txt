I am the curator of the small municipal museum in a river town that was built around a wool mill, and on Friday evening I give the opening remarks for our new permanent exhibition on the mill fire of 1931. The room will be divided. Half of it is the town: people whose grandparents worked in the mill, who grew up on the story that the fire was the owner's doing — he had refused for three years to pay for a sprinkler system, and the town has never forgiven him. The other half is a regional conference of economic historians, in town for the weekend, who will hear any single-cause claim as an amateur's mistake. The exhibition's principal funder and lender is the owner's grandson, eighty-four years old, in the front row. Its centrepiece is the mill office clock, stopped at 4:40 a.m.

My own research has left me convinced that the fire was overdetermined, and I want the remarks to be honest about it. Three independent routes led to that fire, each of them sufficient on its own: rotted wiring in the carding room, a fire left smouldering in the loading yard the evening before, and the mill's habit of stacking oil-soaked waste against a wooden wall. None of the three was necessary. The town's remembered cause is a different kind of thing altogether: the missing sprinklers did not start the fire, they decided how much of a fire there would be, and a refusal to act is a cause of a different order from an act. The clock, meanwhile, does its own quiet work in the vitrine — it tells visitors that something happened at a moment, and that the moment is the explanation.

Write the remarks. Five to seven minutes, spoken aloud, so no citations, no names of scholars, no footnotes.

The remarks must not say the fire was inevitable, and must not say that no one was responsible; they must neither exonerate the owner nor condemn him. They must explain, in language the millworkers' grandchildren can follow, how it can be that no single act would have prevented the fire and yet the burning-down of the mill was avoidable, and why blame does not need the test of 'without this, it would not have happened'. This must be done without the words 'counterfactual', 'but-for', or 'necessary and sufficient'; the word 'overdetermined' may appear once. At least once, and explicitly, the remarks must decline to name a single cause, and must make that refusal feel like something given to the room rather than withheld from it. There must be a sentence the grandson can live with, and it must not be a lie. The remarks must handle the clock without letting it mean what it seems to mean. Do not resolve the question at the end.
