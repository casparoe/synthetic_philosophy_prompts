I'm in a year-long seminar on the concept of societal collapse, and the discussion has been going in circles for months in a way I can no longer ignore. The pattern is always the same. Someone raises Easter Island: the familiar story of a society that cut down its own forest to raise statues, wrecked its environment, and then fell into famine and warfare. Someone else counters that this narrative is largely a colonial invention — that the island's population was modest and stable until Europeans arrived, and that what looks like a self-inflicted collapse is really the aftershock of contact, disease, and slave raiding. We turn to the Maya: one camp treats the abandonment of the Classic-period cities of the southern lowlands as the textbook case of a civilization that failed, while the other insists that the political order dissolved but the Maya themselves endured, so that calling it a collapse mistakes the unit of analysis. The Bronze Age follows — palace economies and long-distance trade networks on one side, the continuity of ordinary settlement on the other — and the same impasse repeats. No one has moved in eight months. My suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that the disagreement has drifted away from the past and toward the word: the two sides seem to be arguing about what 'collapse' means, and the archaeology is often beside the point.

I can see two readings of this, and I want to know which is right. On the first, the impasse is at bottom verbal: 'collapse' is doing several jobs at once — demographic decline, the breakdown of a governing apparatus or an economy, and the loss of a whole way of life or tradition — and each camp defends one of these as the real meaning while quietly substituting a different one; separate them and much of the dispute evaporates. On the second, the disagreement is substantive even after the senses are fixed, because the sides genuinely disagree about the evidence in particular cases — what the soils and the radiocarbon dates show, how large the population was, when the decline began — and no amount of definitional hygiene will settle that. There is, I suspect, a third possibility: that the dispute is partly normative, since 'collapse' carries a judgment that 'transformation' or 'reorganization' does not, and the choice of word may be doing evaluative work that neither side will own. I don't want a verdict on who is right about Easter Island or the Maya. I want a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement this actually is, what each side is committed to once its commitments are spelled out, whether the impasse is evidence that the question is ill-posed or merely hard, and — given your diagnosis — what would count as progress, whether that is a distinction, a stipulation, a decisive piece of evidence, or a change of question. Please answer in no more than 600 words.
