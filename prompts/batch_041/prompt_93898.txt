I retired four years ago after thirty-five years as a medicinal chemist — the first half in a pharmaceutical company, the second in a university department whose money came largely from industry — and I have spent the years since reading philosophy in a manner my former colleagues find eccentric. I am now partway through a part-time MA, and I have been let onto the programme of a one-day workshop on freedom and expertise at my old university next month. My paper argues that the freedom of a scientist is badly captured by either of the two liberties Isaiah Berlin distinguished. Negative liberty, as I understand it, says I am free to the extent that nobody interferes with my research; but for years I worked under agreements that gave the sponsor sight of every manuscript before submission and, in principle, a veto over whether it appeared, and nobody ever actually stopped me doing anything, so on that account I was free the whole time, which strikes me as plainly false. I once spent a fortnight softening a sentence I knew to be true because of who was paying for the study, and I would say I was unfree in that fortnight even though the sponsor said nothing and may never have read the sentence at all. Positive liberty, by contrast, tells me that freedom is self-mastery, or living under norms I can call my own; that seems to me to redescribe the difficulty rather than resolve it, and to locate the trouble in my inner state when what troubled me was my position.

What actually troubled me, I want to argue, is the standing capacity of other people to interfere with me at will — funder, editor, senior author, the committee that owns the metric — whether or not they ever exercise it. A researcher who must keep a sponsor sweet in order to keep a laboratory running is unfree even in a year when the sponsor does nothing at all. This is the neo-republican thought about arbitrary power rather than interference, the conception of liberty as non-domination that Philip Pettit has made current, and I take it to be the most promising of the three notions. I should be candid in the paper that I am a newcomer and that my grip on the literature is patchy; I would sooner have the objections than the encouragement.

The audience is what worries me. It is the political theory group at my old university, and I know roughly who will be there: two liberals in the Berlin tradition, one of them a historian of political thought who will not care for my borrowing and will want to know whether I have understood what the distinction was for; a political economist of libertarian temper who will say that dependence is simply the price of a voluntary bargain, and that I am complaining about the market rather than about unfreedom; a moral philosopher of Kantian sympathies who will insist that non-domination is just autonomy in sociological costume, which is to say positive liberty again, with all the usual worries attached; one neo-republican who has published on Pettit and who, I suspect, will tell me that domination is a relation between agents capable of interfering and not a description of a structure or a market, and that I am stretching the concept until it snaps; and two or three science-and-technology studies people who will want to know why this is philosophy and not politics under another name. Please write me a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defence. I want the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this room, in roughly the order they will arrive and ranked by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that particular objector would phrase it — the libertarian's version of the dependence point is not the historian's version, and I need both. For each, give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft; tell me what I should concede at the outset rather than defend, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session, and which question I am hoping nobody asks. I have no use for a reading list or for the standard first-year objections any tutorial could produce — I want the ones this room will produce, and I should like the whole thing in British English.
