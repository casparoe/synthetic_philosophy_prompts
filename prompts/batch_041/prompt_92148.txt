I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past four years I've sat as one of the two public members on the board of the Harbor Point Maritime Trust, a small coastal foundation that owns and operates a 1911 wooden sardine carrier called the Coralie — the last working example of her type in the region, and the centerpiece of the trust's schools program. At next week's board meeting we have to settle a question we've deferred for two years, and which the trustees have started rolling their eyes about whenever I call it our Ship of Theseus: which of two vessels the trust will license to carry the name Coralie, and which will receive the annual maintenance grant and the heritage listing that are attached to the name. Vessel A is the one that still sails. Between 1972 and 1996 she was restored plank by plank, and by the end of that process not one original timber remained; the replaced pieces were catalogued and put in storage, and in 2003 a local shipwright reassembled roughly 85 percent of the original planking — trimmed and re-bent to fit, on new frames with new fasteners and a modern keel — as Vessel B, a static hull now on display in the town museum. Vessel A keeps the original name board, builder's plate, and mast step, and still does everything a Coralie has ever done: she takes school groups out, races in the summer regatta, and is the boat people in town mean when they say they learned to sail on the Coralie. Vessel B is, as the museum's curator puts it, the only one of the two that is actually made of the ship. Our 1971 charter is silent — it speaks only of "the Coralie" and of keeping her in commission — and the state listing must be renewed this spring, so the name cannot be left dangling any longer.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate to the other trustees before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — which vessel gets the name, the grant, and the listing, and on what timetable — and give the reasoning in terms my colleagues can actually argue with, including a fair statement of the strongest case for the vessel you rule against. Two of them have already staked out positions I find too quick. One says the answer is obvious because the Coralie just is the boat that has always been called that; the other says this is purely a matter of what we choose to call things, so we may as well pick whichever vessel serves the education mission best. Another trustee has circulated a page of notes asking whether "the Coralie" is a thing at all or a title we hold in trust, and whether questions of this kind have a right answer or merely a defensible one. I'd like the opinion to take that possibility seriously rather than brushing past it, and to say plainly what would have to be true for the losing claimant to be right. You don't need to cite anyone; the trustees want reasoning they can interrogate, not references.

Finally, since I'll be defending this document to people who know that vessel far better than I do, be explicit about how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of, and why.
