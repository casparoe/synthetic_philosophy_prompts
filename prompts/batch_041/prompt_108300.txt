I'm in a reading group on early modern philosophy and we've been doing Descartes on animals. The bit everyone latches onto is the garden automata: he pictures the hydraulic statues in the royal gardens at Saint-Germain that start moving when a visitor steps on a hidden tile, water pushing through pipes and valves, and then says the animal body is the same kind of thing -- nerves and muscles and animal spirits doing what pipes and water do -- so no mind is needed to explain the dog running or the dog yelping when its kicked. Our discussion leader used this to say Descartes' position is basically an inference to the best explanation and still stands: we can build machines that walk, avoid obstacles, and now even talk, so the fact that animals do these things gives you no reason at all to posit a mind in them. A guy in the group who works in robotics pushed back and said the analogy proves way too much -- his lab's quadrupeds would pass Descartes' own second test, acting appropriately across novel situations, and chatbots would pass the first, producing new utterances that fit the context, so if the garden automaton shows animals have no minds then the same reasoning hands minds to the robots, which he doesnt believe; and anyway the statues are triggered by a hidden tile, they don't feel the foot land. We went around on this for an hour and got nowhere.

So take the garden-automata analogy seriously and audit it for me. Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the water, the pipes, the valves, the hidden tile, the visitor's foot, the engineer, the onlooker -- and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies into the ones that actually matter for the use our leader was making of it (proving animals have no minds) and the ones that are merely picturesque (nobody cares that the statues were cast in bronze). Two things I especially want your take on: first, is there a circularity here, in that the analogy only gets its force if you already accept that everything going on in a body is mechanical, which is the very thing at issue? Second, someone in the group claimed Descartes actually allowed animals sensation, fear, hunger, and denied them only thought, and that this is a respectable reading now -- does that rescue the analogy or does it leave the analogy doing no work at all? Don't hedge at the end: tell me whether the leader's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it doesn't, what a better analogy would be -- one that keeps the mechanist insight without smuggling in the conclusion it was supposed to support.
