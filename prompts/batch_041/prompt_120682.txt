Agencies that regulate health, safety, and the environment do two things that sit oddly together. On one side, they price the benefits of a rule by reading value off people's actual trade-offs: the extra pay workers demand for jobs that carry a higher chance of dying on the job is taken to reveal what a small reduction in the risk of death is worth to them, and that figure is then entered in the ledger as the benefit of a regulation, so that a rule survives only if its costs fall below the value it assigns to the lives it saves. Where a good has no market at all — the continued existence of a species, clear views at a national park — the same agencies fall back on asking people in surveys what they would be willing to pay, which looks like an abandonment of the commitment to actual choices. On the other side, many of these same governments run behavioral-insights teams that redesign forms, defaults, and pension enrolment on the explicit premise that people's choices are warped by inertia, present bias, and the framing of options, so that what a person picks is not what she wants.

I want the implicit theory of utility that each of these practices presupposes. For each, say what it must assume about what welfare consists in, and about when a person's choice is authoritative evidence of her own good — then say whether a single conception of utility can license both, or whether the state is quietly committed to two incompatible pictures, one in which choice is the measure of value and one in which it is a symptom to be corrected. Distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with: does the revealed-preference machinery need the metaphysical claim that choices disclose true preferences, or only a methodological decision to treat them as if they did? Where is the arrangement philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of welfare would license it equally? And if the presupposed view were replaced by its rival — choices taken as incorrigible, or latent preferences taken as the only thing that counts — what would change in how the benefit side of a regulation is computed, and would nudging appear as a benefit or as a cost?
