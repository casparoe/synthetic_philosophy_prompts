Alright, I need help with an argument a friend in my reading group keeps throwing at me. It's the old sea-battle thing, I know, and I've already been told the usual move ("future statements aren't true or false yet"), so please dont just recite that back at me — the version below has a step I've never seen in the textbook treatments and I think thats where the trouble is, but I cant put my finger on it. He's happy to accept the conclusion, by the way; he says he's some kind of hard determinist. I'm not.

The set-up: tomorrow a jury votes. Tonight they're still deliberating — arguing, unsure, one of them might change her mind.

(1) Either the jury will convict tomorrow or it won't. No third option.
(2) If they will, then it's true right now that they will; if they won't, then it's true right now that they won't. So one of those two is already true tonight.
(3) For a claim to be true, something has to make it true, and you can't be made true by something that doesn't exist yet — that's backwards. A claim is true because of the way the world is, and the only world there is right now is tonight's world.
(4) So if it's true tonight that they'll convict, tonight's world already contains what makes it true.
(5) And whatever makes it true also fixes it. If the grounds are already in place, the conviction is already settled, not still open.
(6) What's settled can't be changed by anyone — you can't change the past, and by (4) and (5) the verdict is in the relevant sense already past.
(7) So the jury's deliberation tonight is doing nothing. Their arguing, their doubts, her change of mind — none of it can affect an outcome that was fixed before they sat down.
(8) Nothing in the argument turns on it being a jury. Run it for anything anyone does and you get that nothing was ever up to anyone — including whether I send this email, and including whether anyone deserves blame or credit for anything.

I can't take (8). But here's the part that really trips me up: my friend says the obvious escape — deny (2), say future claims aren't true or false yet — costs you even more, and I think he might be right. His reasoning: if it's neither true nor false tonight that they'll convict, then tomorrow, once they convict, it becomes true that they were going to convict. So a fact about tonight — namely, what was going to happen — got created tomorrow. But if facts about tonight can be created by tomorrow's events, then the past isn't fixed, and (6) dies for a completely different reason. Worse, if the past can gain facts it can lose them, and then nothing is safe.

So: I'd like to keep (1), and obviously I'd like to keep (8) being false. Which step do I give up, why does it look so obviously fine at first, and what does giving it up actually cost me? And is he even right that the escape route carries that price — or is that bit cheating somewhere too?
