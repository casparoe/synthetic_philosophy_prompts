I chair the board of a small settlement fund — the Wren Valley Community Trust — and we have to vote a week from Thursday on a claims rule that's turned out to be much harder than anyone expected. Background, briefly: the Wren Valley dam went up in 1964 and flooded four villages; the families were resettled onto a tract outside Carverton. In 2019 a historian found that the geological survey used to justify the siting had been doctored — the engineers knew the valley wasn't the best site and picked it because the land was cheap and the villagers had no lawyers. The utility settled, and the money now sits in our fund, which is supposed to compensate "those who suffered loss as a result of the resettlement." Our payout formula, inherited from the settlement agreement, is but-for: a claimant gets the difference between their actual life and the life they'd have had if the dam hadn't been built. That works fine for the surviving elders — lost farms, lost livelihoods, we can price those. The problem is the second and third generations. Our actuary ran the numbers and thinks maybe 6% of the descendant claims can clear the but-for test, because for most of them the honest counterfactual isn't "you'd have had a worse life," it's "you wouldn't have been born at all" — their grandparents met in the resettlement tract, or married later, or moved to Carverton, and the particular people sitting in our waiting room are the downstream product of that. Under the charter, unspent money reverts to the utility, so if we read the rule strictly we're handing a nine-figure surplus back to the company that did the doctoring, which nobody on this board can stomach.

So I need a written opinion I can circulate before the vote. Three positions are on the table and I want the memo to actually choose between them. (1) Strict but-for: only claimants who can show they'd have existed anyway and been worse off get paid; the rest get nothing, and we return the surplus. (2) What our treasurer calls the lineage rule: the wrong was done to the villages as continuing communities rather than to individuals, so compensation flows to whoever now carries the community — a per-capita payment to all descendants, counterfactual identity irrelevant. (3) A well-being floor: anyone whose life falls below a stated threshold gets a fixed sum, identity questions off the table entirely. My niece, who's doing a PhD in philosophy, says this is all a version of the nonidentity problem and that position 1 is either incoherent or monstrous, but our lawyer says philosophical labels don't decide charters, and honestly I think the treasurer's objection has teeth too: if we pay people because an injustice shaped who got born, where does that stop — do we owe everyone whose parents met at a seperate terrible party? Please recommend one of the three (or a modification) with the reasoning spelled out, engage the strongest version of whichever side you're rejecting, and give me something concrete to implement: who decides individual claims, what the standard of proof is, and a timeline. No citations or reading lists — the board won't read them and I can't check them anyway. Also, I'm a retired hydrologist, so if the memo can avoid assuming I know the jargon, that helps.
