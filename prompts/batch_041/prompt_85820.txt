I'm in my final year of high school and I'm writing a big essay for a combined philosophy/psychology course on whether psychiatric diagnoses pick out real things or whether we sort of invent them by naming them. My teacher keeps going on about Ian Hacking and his looping effects — the idea that once you hand people a label like multiple personality or fugue or anorexia, some of them start describing themselves and their symptoms in terms of the label, which changes what the disorder looks like, which changes the label, round and round. Fine, I get that. But I've ended up defending a position and I want you to try to wreck it. Please do it as someone who actually knows both the philosophy of psychiatry literature and the empirical side (epidemiology, clinical trials, the biomarker and genetics stuff), not as someone who just delivers a general it's-all-complicated speech.

My position — let me call it the layer-cake view — is this: looping effects are real and interesting, but they happen at the surface, at the level of how people describe, express and perform their distress. Underneath there's still a genuine dysfunction, a brain-or-psychological thing that goes wrong whether or not anyone has a name for it, and that's what psychiatric categories are ultimately tracking. So depression in 1890s Vienna and depression on a campus now might look and feel pretty different, and the loop might be why, but they're the same underlying kind. Same for all that stuff a few years ago where teenagers suddenly started having tics after watching videos of people with tics — I'd say that's a genuine functional disorder that the videos triggered and shaped. And the historical ones, fugue, hysteria, shell shock, I'd say those were real conditions whose expression got scripted by the culture, not things the culture literally created out of nothing.

So what would count against this? I want actual defeaters, ranked, not a survey. Which bits of evidence, cases, arguments or formal results would a defender of the layer-cake view have to take seriously, and which would be pretty much fatal as opposed to merely awkward? I'm especially interested in cases where the loop seems to reach below the surface: is there any real case where the mechanism or the biology itself seems to change along with the label, and if so does that break my view or can I just say the mechanism is being modulated rather than created? The cases I find hardest are the ones where a whole disorder basically vanishes — if fugue was a real dysfunction, where did it go? — and the cross-cultural stuff where the same distress shows up as bodily symptoms in one place and as psychological symptoms in another; I can't tell if that's surface variation or something worse for me. I also want to know what defenders of the view would concede themselves counts against them, not just what critics shout from the other side. And be straight with me about whether the layer-cake view has quietly made itself unfalsifiable, because the obvious escape hatch is to say the real dysfunction is definately there even if we can't find it, and I suspect I'm doing that — if I am, tell me whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just the price of doing business.

Please start with some brainstorming of the different kinds of defeater a view like this could have before you sort and rank them from fatal down to merely awkward. At the end, tell me how confident you actually are in what you've claimed, and single out the one point you're least sure of — I'd much rather have a hedged answer that flags its weak spot than a confident one that pretends this is all settled.
