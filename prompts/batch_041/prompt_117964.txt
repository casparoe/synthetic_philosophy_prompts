A couple of people in my reading group have been going at this for weeks and I want to see it written out properly. Call them R and C.

R thinks complexity theory is doing real metaphysics: when we prove something is NP-hard we've discovered a fact about the world, as objective as any fact in physics, and P vs NP is the deepest open question we have, because if P≠NP then there are truths about perfectly finite, mundane objects that no being could ever come to know — not just in practice, but in principle. She also likes to say complexity is what you get when you take epistemology seriously: it draws the horizon of knowability.

C thinks that's a load of dressing-up. "Complexity" is always complexity for some model of computation and some asymptotic idealization; every real input is finite and small; worst-case hardness tells you nothing about the instances you'll actually meet; and what R calls a fact about the world is a fact about what our favourite algorithms can't do yet.

Write the dialogue. Let them press each other properly — R should have a real answer to the "it's relative to your model" move, C should have a real answer to "then why does the distinction keep showing up in nature", and I want them to actually work through the case where someone finally proves P = NP but the algorithm is O(n^1000) with a galactic constant. Does R's metaphysics survive that, or does C win? And get C to say what would change her mind. No name-dropping, no "see so-and-so's paper", just the arguement.
