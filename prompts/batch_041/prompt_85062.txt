I'm in a graduate seminar on social choice and collective decision-making, and we've spent three weeks on the discursive dilemma — the pattern in which a group that votes separately on each of several logically connected propositions can end up endorsing an inconsistent set of them even though every member's own set is perfectly consistent. Our running example is a hiring committee of five that has to settle three questions: whether the candidate clears the research bar, whether she clears the teaching bar, and whether to hire her, where hiring is supposed to follow if and only if she clears both. Majorities can approve the hire while majorities reject each of the two bars, which is exactly what happened in our mock run, and the committee's written report came out looking deranged — it recommended a candidate it had just judged unfit on both counts.

I've been defending a strong reading of this and would like it stress-tested. The reading is that the dilemma is not an artifact of majority voting or of a badly designed committee, but shows that no procedure can be at once fair — in the minimal sense that each member's judgments count equally and no member's judgments are systematically ignored — and collectively coherent — in the sense that the group ends up with a consistent set of judgments closed under the entailments connecting them. And I want to go one step further: that this is not a problem to be engineered around, but shows the ideal of the group as a genuine reasoner, a single mind with a view of its own, to be incoherent for any agenda rich enough to be interesting. So: what would count against that? I want the defeaters laid out and ranked, from the ones that should make me abandon the strong claim down to the ones that are merely awkward for it. In particular, which assumptions are actually doing the work — unrestricted agendas, equal treatment of members, the demand that the group's output be a deductively closed and consistent set of judgments, the demand that it depend only on members' judgments and not on the reasons behind them — and what would the world have to look like for each of those to fail in a way that matters? I keep being told the impossibility dissolves under domain restrictions such as single-peaked or unidimensionally aligned judgments, or under a thinner conception of collective rationality, or if the group reports a ranking or a decision rather than a set of beliefs. I want to know which of those are real escapes and which are the theorem restated in friendlier vocabulary, and whether any of them is a live option for an actual committee rather than a formal curiosity; the same question applies to the general family of Arrow-style impossibilities that my professor treats as the grown-up version of the point.

I'd also like an honest account of what a defender of the strong reading can concede without giving up the view, and of whether the strong reading has quietly made itself unfalsifiable — say, by treating every proposed fix as a redefinition of 'fair' or 'coherent' rather than as a counterexample, and whether that insulation is a defect or just what a claim of this kind looks like when it is conceptual rather than empirical. Tell me which parts of my position are results in social choice theory, which are claims about the metaphysics of group agency, and which are empirical bets about how real committees behave, and what evidence would bear on the last of these; if some result shows the strong reading collapsing into a much weaker and more boring claim about which sets of axioms can be jointly satisfied, I'd rather hear it now. The thing that would move me most is a case in which an actual group, on a genuinely rich agenda, produced a coherent collective verdict without covertly handing some members' judgments extra weight — or a demonstration that no such case could count, which would tell me something about the view too.
