I write long-form essays on science and intellectual history for a general-interest ideas magazine, and for the past several months I've been reporting on a feud that has run for decades without either side landing a clean blow. On one side are the historians and philosophers of history who hold that historical explanation is constitutively narrative — that to explain why something happened just is to tell a story about agents, intentions, and consequences, so that narrative form is not a decorative wrapper around the real explanation but the explanation itself. On the other are the quantitatively minded social scientists and psychologists of judgment who hold that narrative is a bias engine: that the very features which make an account readable — a beginning and an end, a turning point, a cast of characters who meant to do what they did — also manufacture the impression that the outcome was bound to happen, so that historians ought to prefer accounts that survive having their plots stripped away. My editor is tired of the he-said/she-said treatment and has asked for something better, so I've persuaded both camps to sit down for a genuine adversarial collaboration; what I need from you is the document they could actually both sign. I want the agreed-claims section to be substantive rather than ceremonial: what both sides accept about the incompleteness of archives, about the fact that every account selects, about what "inevitability" does and does not mean when historians use the word, and about the standing laboratory results on hindsight bias. Then the disagreements, each formulated in wording its own side would accept as fair — I want a hostile reader from either camp to finish the document without feeling misrepresented — followed by the cruxes, phrased as sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side. Two candidates occur to me: whether hindsight bias is a distortion at all or merely what rational conditionalizing on evidence looks like from the far side of the event, and whether the demand for a plot is a defect peculiar to narrative or a feature of any explanation that has to be about something in particular.

To keep the two sides from talking past each other I've been using a small private vocabulary in my notes, and I'd like the document to keep it. "Outcome leakage" for the contamination of a reader's sense of prior likelihood by knowledge of how things turned out. "Arc pressure" for the demand that an account have a shape, so that episodes fitting the arc get credited with more causal weight than the record supports while episodes that don't are quietly dropped. And "ex ante grip" for the degree to which an account, presented with its ending removed, would have constrained a competent contemporary's expectations — as distinct from "retrodictive fit," the mere fact that the account makes the known outcome look necessary. For each crux I want the case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, followed by each side's prediction, written down in advance, about what that case should look like if its own view is right; the Cuban Missile Crisis and the 2008 financial crisis are the two episodes both camps have already conceded are well documented and genuinely contestable, so use those, and say explicitly what each side would predict about, for instance, how readers' sense of inevitability changes when the same evidence base is presented as a plotted narrative rather than as a bare sequence with the causal weights marked. If there is a crux on which neither side will accept any possible observation as decisive, say so, and say what that implies about the dispute.

One more thing I'd like you to take seriously rather than gesture at: whether part of this quarrel is verbal. The word "narrative" is doing at least three jobs — a genre of prose with a plot, the minimal temporal-causal structure any explanation whatsoever must have, and a substantive claim about the ubiquity of agency in human affairs — and I suspect at least one of the disagreements dissolves once the camps are forced to say which job they mean. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and close with a short note on which disputes are genuine, which are terminological, and what the difference tells us about why the fight has lasted this long. No bibliography, citations, or reading lists: this is a piece of argument, and I would rather have the two camps' own formulations than a survey of who said what.
