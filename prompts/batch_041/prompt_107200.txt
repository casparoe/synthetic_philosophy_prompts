QUESTION (20 marks)

In lecture I set out two conditions that a conspiratorial explanation has to meet before we'll call it warranted.

(C1) The comparative condition. The conspiratorial hypothesis must explain the body of evidence better than the official, non-conspiratorial account of that same evidence. Fitting the evidence, or being consistent with it, is not enough — usually both accounts fit. The question is which one explains it better.

(C2) The independence condition. Any conspirator the hypothesis has to add, beyond the ones it started with, must be given a motive that can be stated without appealing to the conspiracy itself. You may not introduce a new conspirator whose only job is to explain why the cover-up held.

Two stipulations that matter:
(i) C1 and C2 are conditions on the hypothesis, not on the person who holds it. How dogmatic, paranoid or gratified a believer is has no bearing on whether the hypothesis meets them.
(ii) The relevant body of evidence is what was available to the agent at the moment she formed the belief. Nothing that emerged afterwards counts.

The case. Marbury is a town of about nine thousand. A plastics plant that had operated there for thirty years closed in 2009. In 2013 a cluster of eleven childhood leukaemia cases was identified within a two-mile radius of the old plant. The regional health authority's report concluded that the cluster was consistent with normal statistical variation given the town's small population, and recommended no further investigation. A local campaign group put forward what the local paper called the Marbury cover-up: the plant's parent company had known since the 1990s that one of the solvents it used was leukaemogenic; the lead author of the health authority's report had previously done paid consultancy for the company; and the two had worked together to keep an internal 2011 study out of the report.

At the time Priya formed her belief, the publicly available evidence was: the cluster itself; a leaked internal email, ambiguous in content, in which the report's lead author and a company scientist exchanged messages about what they called the 2011 file; the lead author's consultancy history; and the health authority's statistical report. Priya read all of this and concluded that the Marbury cover-up is true. (For what it's worth, eighteen months after Priya formed her belief the company did release an internal 2011 study on solvent exposure.)

Assess whether the cover-up hypothesis satisfies C1 and C2, and say what follows about the warrant of the cover-up hypothesis and about the warrant of the official account.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

The question asks me to test the Marbury cover-up hypothesis against the two conditions from lecture, and then say what follows for the warrant of the hypothesis and of the official report.

C1. C1 asks whether the conspiratorial hypothesis explains the evidence better than the official account. Both accounts fit the cluster: the official account says eleven cases in a town of nine thousand is within the range you'd expect by chance, and the cover-up hypothesis says the cluster is the expected effect of solvent exposure. But the cover-up hypothesis also fits the leaked email and the lead author's consultancy history, and the official account can only write these off as coincidences. So the cover-up hypothesis fits more of the evidence, and C1 is satisfied.

C2. C2 says that any conspirator the hypothesis adds, beyond the ones it started with, needs a motive that can be stated without appealing to the conspiracy. The hypothesis adds the lead author of the report, and her motive is easy to state independently: she had a paid consultancy relationship with the company and stood to lose it. So on its face C2 looks satisfied. But I think the real point of C2 is to rule out the self-sealing conspiracy theory, the one that treats every disconfirming item as further proof of the cover-up. Priya clearly isn't doing that — she doesn't treat the report's denial as evidence of a cover-up. So C2 is satisfied.

Assessment. Both conditions are met. The cover-up hypothesis is therefore warranted: it explains the evidence better than the official account, and it doesn't need to be propped up by the believer's psychology. The official account, by contrast, is not warranted, because it is the rival that lost on C1 and two rivals can't both be warranted. And in fact we now know the 2011 study existed — the company released it a year and a half later — which is more or less what the hypothesis predicted. So the official report's conclusion that the cluster was normal statistical variation is false, and Priya knows that the cover-up is true.
