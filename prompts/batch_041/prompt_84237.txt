At a departmental reading group last week, two graduate students in philosophy, Ana and Ben, became locked in an argument about a draft by a visiting speaker. The draft defends a view it calls stratified expressivism. On that view, a moral sentence such as Lying is wrong has two separable layers: a truth-conditional content layer that is invariant across embedded and unembedded occurrences, and a practical-commitment layer that is contributed by the force of the utterance. In unembedded assertions the commitment is discharged; in embedded contexts, such as the antecedent of a conditional, the commitment is only hypothetical. The speaker claims that this solves the Frege-Geach problem, because the truth-conditional layer explains compositionality and validity, while the commitment layer preserves the expressivist idea that moral judgments are practical rather than descriptive. I was a bystander and did not take a side. The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Ana: This cannot be expressivism. If moral sentences have truth-conditional content, then moral judgments are beliefs about how things are, and the view is simply cognitivism with a pragmatic afterthought. The practical-commitment layer does no semantic work. It also cannot explain moral disagreement: two people can agree on all the truth-conditional content and still disagree morally, so the disagreement must lie outside the meaning of the sentences.

(2) Ben: That misreads the proposal. The truth-conditional layer is not a layer of moral facts; it is a commitment-role content, fixed by the inferential relations among moral and non-moral sentences. The view remains expressivist because the fundamental explanatory notion is the practical commitment; the truth-conditional layer is a representational bookkeeping device that lets us track commitments under supposition. And it does explain disagreement: the disagreement is in the commitments, which are part of the meaning, not an optional add-on.

(3) Ana: Then the meaning of an embedded moral sentence is incomplete. If the commitment is part of meaning, the antecedent of a conditional does express a commitment—just a hypothetical one—and the Frege-Geach problem simply reappears as the problem of explaining hypothetical commitment. If the commitment is not part of meaning, the view is not expressivist. Either way, the proposal either collapses into cognitivism or fails to solve the problem it was designed to solve.

(4) Ben: Hypothetical commitment is not mysterious; it is the same thing as suppositional reasoning. When you suppose that lying is wrong, you temporarily adopt the practical stance of disapproving of lying, and you reason from it. Modus ponens is valid because accepting the conditional plus accepting the antecedent yields the commitment to the conclusion. The truth-conditional layer represents the role of the commitment in inference; it does not replace it. This is not cognitivism, because the commitment is not a belief about a moral fact. And it is not ad hoc, because it is motivated by the embedding data.

(5) Ana: But if the truth-conditional layer already fixes all the inferential transitions, the commitment is epiphenomenal. If it does not, the compositional semantics is incomplete. You keep sliding between two readings of truth-conditional content. On the first, it is ordinary truth-conditional content, and the view is cognitivism. On the second, it is commitment-role content, and then it is not truth-conditional at all, so you have not explained how moral sentences embed in truth-functional contexts. Commitment-role is a label, not a semantics. Consider negation: Lying is not wrong and It is not the case that lying is wrong are not equivalent, but your view seems to treat them as the same commitment-role content.

(6) Ben: Negation is a higher-order commitment operator; it takes a commitment-role and yields a contrary commitment-role. The distinction is between content and force. The truth-conditional layer is content; the commitment is force. Frege-Geach is a problem about content, not force. The expressivist can say that the content of a moral sentence is its commitment-role, and the force is the expression of that commitment. In embedded contexts, the commitment-role is present but not asserted; it is used to determine the conditional's content. That is exactly how expressivists should handle embedding.

(7) Ana: If the content of the moral sentence is a commitment-role, then it is not truth-conditional in the ordinary sense, and you still owe an account of how it composes in truth-functional contexts. Calling it content does not make it compositional. And if it is truth-conditional in the ordinary sense, then the commitment is not part of content, and the view is not expressivist. The proposal is an unstable hybrid.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each numbered turn, rate the move as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and give a brief justification. Then answer the central question: is stratified expressivism a genuine solution to the Frege-Geach problem, a disguised form of cognitivism, or an unstable hybrid? Finally, identify what both parties are missing: what would a defender of the view need to add to make it coherent, and what would an opponent need to concede if the view can be made coherent? Treat the draft's view as fully described by the summary above, and do not appeal to external sources; reason from the materials in the exchange.
