I teach the graduate seminar in philosophy of probability, and I am drafting the take-home component of the final examination. I want a single essay question, answerable in roughly ninety minutes and worth twenty marks, on the reference class problem, constructed so that it cannot be answered by reciting the familiar framing that every probability judgment presupposes a reference class and that the choice of class is underdetermined by the evidence. The question must force students to engage a specific proposed solution rather than to restate the problem, and it must not require any citation or acquaintance with particular authors; a student who reasons carefully from what is given should be able to do well.

Build the question around the following proposal, which I have been floating in lecture as a candidate dissolution of the problem and which students are to treat as the object of criticism:

A stability rule for reference classes. Among all reference classes that contain the individual in question and are statistically well populated, that is, large enough to yield a reasonably stable frequency estimate, assign the individual the probability given by whichever class has an estimate that is least sensitive to small perturbations of its membership criterion. Where two or more classes tie for least sensitivity, average their estimates.

The question should direct students to do three things. First, state the reference class problem as an argument rather than a slogan: make explicit the premises that generate the difficulty and identify exactly which premise the stability rule is meant to supply. Second, determine whether the stability rule in fact selects a unique class, or whether the appeal to stability merely relocates the original indeterminacy, for instance by presupposing some measure of how large a perturbation is or some ordering of membership criteria that the rule itself cannot supply; students should test the rule against a concrete case of their own construction, ideally one in which a broad class and a narrow class both contain the individual and give divergent estimates. Third, say whether the problem, if it is genuine, is a defect of probability as such or an artifact of one particular interpretation of probability, distinguishing at least the frequency, propensity, and credence readings, and explain what the resulting quantity is supposed to be the probability of when the subject is a single unrepeatable event. A strong answer will also notice whether any invocation of a total-evidence requirement helps or simply reproduces the difficulty.

After the question, provide a model answer of about six hundred words and a marking scheme totaling twenty marks. The marking scheme should be itemized by the specific moves a top answer must make, with the marks attached to each, and should name the specific errors that ought to cap an otherwise competent answer below a distinction, together with a one-line note on how candidates typically commit each error. No citations are expected in either the question or the model answer.
