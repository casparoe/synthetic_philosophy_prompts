I'm a science journalist who writes about decision theory, and once a month I run a ninety-minute ideas lab for about twenty readers — mixed backgrounds, a handful of graduate students, mostly curious non-specialists. For next month I want an activity that makes them feel the force of counterfactual mugging rather than hear about it. The structure I want them to run into: a coin decides which of two worlds they are in; in one world they are asked to pay a small cost, in the other they receive a large reward, but only if they would have paid in the first world — so the reward turns on a branch of the world that, from where they sit, never happened. I want the payoffs arranged so that refusing is at least as good in every world they can observe, and yet the group divides, and the division is not merely risk aversion or confusion.

Design it: rules, materials, a token- or candy-based payoff scheme with no real money, and a timeline for the session. I'd like at least three rounds — one where they choose on the spot, one where they commit to a policy in writing before the coin is revealed, and one where the reward depends on the XOR of their choice and the hidden coin, that is, on whether exactly one of the two is true, so that afterwards no one can tell from the outcome alone whose policy was right and the group is forced to grade policies rather than results. The mechanics must be fully transparent; I'm not willing to deceive them about the rules. The idea should surface in what happens, not in what I say: they should feel the pull of paying before anything is named, and the vocabulary of decision theory should appear only in the debrief.

Then give me the debrief, mapping particular moments in the activity onto the argument — why "I would pay" is not a prediction about the world they are actually in, what the XOR round does to the assumption that outcomes can grade decisions, and where the intuition that paying is irrational is coming from. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it without lecturing; I expect at least one participant to insist the setup is physically impossible and another to collapse the whole thing into ordinary risk. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
