I'm in the final stretch of a philosophy of mind course, and our unit on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view, usually associated with Keith Frankish, that experiences do not actually have the phenomenal properties we naively take them to have, and that our impression of their presence is itself a kind of introspective misrepresentation — has left me with a question I cannot shake. In seminar we kept sliding between two things that strike me as separable. The first is a metaphysical claim: there are no phenomenal properties, so nothing in the world answers to what we thought the phrase about what experience is like was tracking. The second is an epistemic claim: first-person reports about experience carry a kind of authority that third-person evidence does not, so a subject's sincere report that she is in pain settles certain questions in a way no scan or behavioural measure can. Several people in the room treated these as a package, as though accepting illusionism means losing the authority along with the properties, and I suspect that is too quick — but I cannot work out what actually follows from what.

What I would like from you is a careful account of the most important implications of illusionism, taken as a position in its own right, for how much weight we should give first-person testimony about experience, specifically in cases where the stakes are practical rather than merely theoretical: clinical pain assessment for patients who cannot self-report reliably, welfare decisions about animals, and the increasingly live question of whether a system that claims to suffer should be believed. If illusionism is true, does the report that something hurts become merely a report of a certain representational state that misrepresents itself, and if so, what changes about how such reports should figure in moral and clinical deliberation? I am especially interested in whether anything survives of the idea that the subject has privileged access to something, even if not to phenomenal properties, and in how the illusionist's own account of introspection should constrain the science of consciousness — that is, whether the field can keep treating phenomenal consciousness as its explanandum or should replace it with the mechanisms that generate the sense that there is something it is like to undergo an experience.

Please be explicit about how confident you are in each of the claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
