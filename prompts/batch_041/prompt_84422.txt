I work on the cost-effectiveness side of a health authority — my job is, in effect, to add up QALYs across patient groups and rank interventions by the total — and I have been uneasy ever since a philosopher a colleague invited to our journal club characterized the standard objection to what I do in a way I cannot assess. What he said was roughly this: "The separateness of persons is the objection that finishes off utilitarianism, and by extension any procedure that sums benefits across people. Utilitarianism treats society as though it were a single person: it pours everyone's pleasures and pains into one ledger and then maximizes the total. But persons are not parts of a super-organism; they are distinct lives, so weighing my suffering against your happiness as though the two were entries in one book is a category mistake — there is no single subject whose good is being maximized. Once you see that persons are separate, you see that utilitarianism licenses sacrificing some people for the greater good, which is exactly what it is to treat a person as a mere means. Rawls settled the matter: because persons are separate, the principles governing a society have to be chosen behind a veil of ignorance, and no party behind the veil would accept the utilitarian sum. Note that the objection is not that utilitarianism is too demanding or too hard to apply — it is that its central act of aggregation is incoherent. Contemporary utilitarians have largely conceded the point and retreated to rule utilitarianism."

I would like a careful audit rather than a verdict on the man. Which of his claims would someone who works on this objection accept as a fair statement of it, which are simplifications that would make them wince, and which are straightforwardly wrong? I am especially interested in three things: whether the "category mistake" charge is the objection itself or an exaggeration of it; whether Rawls's veil-of-ignorance argument is really the same argument as the separateness-of-persons objection or a distinct one that has been folded into it; and whether his closing claim about contemporary utilitarians is true — I have been told before that rule utilitarianism is a minority position, but I do not know what the majority actually says in reply, and I suspect the phrase "retreated to" is doing a lot of work. Since this bears directly on what I do all day, I would also like to know what exactly the objection, if sound, would condemn in a QALY-style appraisal of the sort I run, and whether there is a version of it that a defender of aggregation can accept without giving up the practice. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — is the summary fair, unfair, or fair in outline but wrong in emphasis — and then give the reasoning claim by claim, without preamble and without restating my question.
