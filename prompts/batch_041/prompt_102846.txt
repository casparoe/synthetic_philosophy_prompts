Supererogation is usually introduced with heroic cases — the bystander who wades into a freezing river to pull out a stranger, the donor who surrenders a kidney to someone she has never met — and I can accept that such acts are neither required nor forbidden. What I cannot accept is that the category deserves to be treated as a genuine philosophical puzzle rather than a piece of moral bookkeeping, and I suspect the received view is confused at precisely this point. My argument runs as follows. If an act is not required, then the agent who omits it does nothing wrong, and in that respect stands exactly where the agent who performs it stands, since both have done only what they were at liberty to do; and if the two agents are equally within their rights, it seems arbitrary to insist that one of them acted better. Suppose instead that performing the act really is better — that the rescuer is admirable in a way the onlooker is not. Then the onlooker was under some obligation to act, and we are calling that obligation supererogatory only because we are reluctant to blame her for neglecting it. So either the category collapses into the merely permissible, or it collapses into ordinary duty supplemented by a sentimental unwillingness to enforce it. I take this to be a serious difficulty for the standard account, and I would like to know how a defender would answer it — in particular, whether the notion survives once one separates what we are prepared to demand of people from what they are actually required to do. One further case leaves me equally stuck: two people make the same large donation, but for one the sum is trivial and for the other it means genuine sacrifice. If the act performed is identical, is only the second donation supererogatory, or does lying beyond the call of duty depend on the agent's circumstances rather than on the act?
