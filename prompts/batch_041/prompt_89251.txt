I sit on my university's academic freedom and professional standards comittee. We meet Thursday to decide what to do about a mandatory online module that all first-year health-science grad students have to pass. The module is supposed to teach them to spot hidden bias in clinical judgment. It works by showing vignettes and scoring answers on what the vendor calls the counterfactual endorsement test: an answer is flagged as bias if the student would not endorse it after full reflection, full information, and no social pressure. A religious student group has complained. They say the module flags as bias any answer that treats certain moral convictions as non-negotiable, like that torturing a child for fun is wrong no matter the consequences, or that a promise to a dying patient can't be traded off against hospital convenience. The complainants say those are moral preferences or commitments, not biases, and that the module can't tell the difference. The vendor says if you exempt every judgment that feels identity-constituting, the tool catches nothing, because bias always feels like who you are from the inside. Half the comittee wants to pull the module. Half wants to keep it and add a seperate disclaimer. One subcommittee has drafted a possible condition: any flagged answer that a student declares as a commitment that is part of who they are gets routed to a faculty panel, which asks whether the commitment can be stated in terms other reasonable patients could accept and whether it would predictably distort care for patients who don't share it. The vendor says that just moves the same problem one level up. I need the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote: a clear recommendation (keep, pull, or keep with specific conditions), the reasoning, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on the other side. Also say what would change your mind, which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Keep it under a page and a half, and don't give me a reading list; I need something I can defend in the meeting.
