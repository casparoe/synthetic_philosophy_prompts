I am a strategic analyst whose working vocabulary comes from crisis-bargaining models of war, deterrence theory, two-level games, and the offence–defence balance. I am reasonably fluent in the standard apparatus: bargaining ranges and reversion outcomes, commitment problems, private information and incentives to misrepresent, costly signals, audience costs, escalation dominance and escalation ladders, the security dilemma, and the distinction between revisionist and satisfied states. My Hegel, by contrast, is thin: I have read the Philosophy of Right once, some years ago, and a few commentaries, so I need you to do the philosophical work rather than assume that I can supply the dialectical background myself.

I would like you to restate Hegel's account of war — the account developed in the Philosophy of Right, not the caricature of a thinker who simply celebrates bloodshed — in the vocabulary I actually use. Treat the following as part of that vocabulary, and employ them wherever they earn their keep:
- recognition rents: payoffs that a state can receive only from other states' acknowledgement, rather than from material resources;
- internal-audit value: the information a crisis produces about whether a state's own members will bear the costs it asks them to bear, and about whether its domestic order can sustain the commitments it makes abroad;
- ethical slack: the portion of a state's capacity that is not required for survival, and which it can spend on projects that sustain its self-conception;
- sovereignty premium: the value a state places on not being subject to any higher tribunal, and on being the final judge in its own case.
Do not smuggle in the assumptions of my framework and call the result a translation; where my terms force Hegel into a shape he would not accept, say so. I am not asking you to defend Hegel; I am asking what my framework can and cannot carry.

Then give me the residue report. For each of the following, say whether it survives translation, survives only with distortion, or has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all: (a) war as a moment of ethical life rather than an instrument of policy; (b) war as a preservative against the corruption of civil society and the ossification of the state; (c) the state's sovereignty as unlimited by any international tribunal; (d) world history as the court that judges states and their wars; (e) the thought that war can reveal or actualise the ethical unity of a people in a way that peace cannot. For each residue, state whether the failure is a defect of my framework — something it cannot represent — or a sign that Hegel's claim is less substantive than it sounds, or both. Finally, tell me which single part of Hegel's account you think my framework should be revised to accommodate, and which part I should be content to leave behind.

No citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and no quotations from Hegel: I want the translation and the residue, not the original text or pointers to the literature. Write in British English.
