Ok so I'm a second-year history PhD and I've somehow become the department's unofficial defender of counterfactuals, which is not a job I applied for. It started at a family dinner — my grandmother left the city she grew up in about four years before it stopped existing as an independent place, and every time it comes up someone says well it was going to happen anyway, they'd have gotten it eventually, and I could never explain why that sentence makes me want to put my head through a wall. Then in a seminar I said something glib about "inevitable" being a claim about a reference class rather than about the world, my advisor told me that was either trivial or false and to pick one, and now I have to produce something. So I want a short story.

Here's the setting I keep circling, use it or push it around: a port republic, call it Sarre, that over about two centuries gets slowly absorbed into a bigger continental power. Sarre has an institution — a guild of chroniclers, the Concordance — whose whole prestige rests on something they call a counterfactual trial. They take a year, they perturb one decision (a vote, a marriage, a grain embargo, a bad winter), they re-run the following decades against the archive of everything the city ever wrote down, and they read off what happens. The results are monotonous: run it a thousand times, a thousand absorptions. Sometimes it takes forty more years, sometimes the empire ends up with a different name and a different flag, but Sarre always becomes a province. The Concordance's official doctrine — they call it the Settled Account — is that this settles it, and the doctrine is doing real work in the present: it's why the families who negotiated the terms are honored rather than prosecuted, and why the people who fought in the streets are remembered as romantics who got alot of other people killed for nothing. The thing my protagonist (a junior chronicler, early twenties, not a hero, bad at politics) discovers is not a conspiracy — it's worse and smaller than that. The trials need dense continuous records to run at all; you can't perturb a year you only know three facts about. And dense continuous records in Sarre only begin after the chancery reforms, and the chancery reforms are themselves one of the moves that locked the city in. So the instrument works perfectly and can only be pointed at the window where the outcome was already decided. Every counterfactual it can run confirms inevitability, the years when an alternative might still have been live are exactly the years it is structurally blind to, and the blindness is unprovable, because the absence of records is the thing you'd need records to argue about. I don't want this to end as aha, the archives were lies — the archives are honest, they're just thin, and nobody in the story is lying except maybe to themselves.

What I want from the story: it should actually do the philosophy instead of gesturing at it, so that by the end the reader can see how "it was inevitable" can be true relative to one reference class and false relative to another without that collapsing into everything-is-relative. Show the pull of the Settled Account honestly — the base rates really are overwhelming, the chroniclers aren't stupid or evil, some of them are the sharpest people in the city and one of them is genuinely kind to her. No time travel, no Rome, no library-of-alexandria stuff, and please don't hand me a scene where a wise old historian explains the outside view to the protagonist; I'd rather the concepts show up as texture — as the way people in the room talk, as what the forms they fill out are asking for, as what the trial room smells like after the lamps have been burning for nine hours. End with her having to decide whether to publish, and don't tell me which way is right. Maybe 2,500 words or so, and if the ending has to be unresolved that's fine, I'd take that over a moral.
