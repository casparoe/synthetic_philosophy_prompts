Ok, this is a working note rather than a polished brief — seminar prep. I do a session on the China/Singapore performance-legitimacy line, the idea that a regime can earn its keep by delivering growth, order, housing, safety and general satisfaction whether or not anyone gets to throw it out, and I'm bored of the discussion collapsing into 'yes but no elections'. I want a designed case that pressures the *content* of the doctrine instead.

So build me a two-regime thought experiment. Present day, boringly realistic, no fictional tech, no external threats, under a page. Two countries delivering the same basket of goods to the same standard, same institutions on both sides (whatever electoral machinery I stipulate, it's identical in each), both rulers having genuinely made the choices that produced the goods, and no repression, no cooked statistics, no hidden coercion, and citizens who know perfectly well what kind of regime they're in. One difference only: in A, if the government fell tomorrow the goods would carry on without it — the prosperity sits on things that dont need these particular people; in B, if the government fell tomorrow the goods would go with it, because it all rests on their deals, their discipline, their networks, their credibility.

Then walk me through it. Does the performance-legitimacy defender have to say B has a *stronger* claim on its people than A, on the grounds that more of the good is attributable to the rulers? If so, say plainly that the doctrine is liscensing rule by hostage — 'remove us and the prosperity goes' — and make that consequence explicit rather than letting it hide behind the word 'performance'. Give me the verdicts side by side: the performance-legitimacy defender, someone who thinks legitimacy has to come from who decides rather than what gets delivered, and a thoroughgoing prudentialist who says neither case is about legitimacy at all, only about reasons of interest to comply. Then tell me which single feature is doing the work, and run the check: if I make B's goods just as robust, or A's just as fragile, does the case die — and if it dies, does it die because the doctrine was never more than prudence wearing a normative hat? Last thing, be honest about whether I've stacked the deck: if my two regimes aren't as identical as I've claimed, or if the case only bites by smuggling in a democratic premise the doctrine is entitled to reject, say so.
