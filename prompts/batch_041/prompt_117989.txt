I am a graduate student in philosophy, and last month I had one of those arguments that turns out to be two arguments wearing a trench coat. A close friend and I both think the law should recognize same-sex marriages, but when I pressed her on why, it emerged that we hold incompatible accounts of what marriage is for: she thinks the institution exists chiefly to secure the setting in which children are raised, and I think child-rearing is at most one of its purposes and not the one that explains why the state is involved at all. Since then I have been noticing the same pattern everywhere — people who agree about the legal verdict and disagree about the point of the institution, and people who disagree about the verdict while agreeing about the point — and it has convinced me that the usual 'for' and 'against' framing hides most of the actual disagreement. I would like you to build me a taxonomy of the positions on what marriage is for, written for undergraduates in a philosophy of law seminar who have read some of the debate but keep collapsing every view into one of those two buckets.

Please structure the taxonomy as a tree whose internal nodes are claims rather than labels, so that a student can work down it without knowing in advance where each answer leads. Each branch point should be a question a student could answer on the basis of cases and convictions rather than by naming a camp: whether marriage has any essential purpose at all or only purposes the parties assign it; if it has one, whether that purpose is procreation and family life, the comprehensive union of the spouses themselves, the provision of care and dependency support, or something civic; if procreation is central, whether the spouses must be the biological source of the children or need only supply a rearing context; whether the state's interest in marriage is children-centered, spouse-centered, perfectionist, or nonexistent; and whether an account of the institution's purpose settles at all what the law ought to do. The leaves should carry names, and each should get a sentence identifying its most awkward commitment. I want at least these views to appear as leaves: the conjugal view, on which marriage is a comprehensive bodily and emotional union ordered to procreation; the revisionist view, on which it is an intense loving commitment whose point is the spouses' mutual good; a child-centered view that keeps procreation central but treats sexual complementarity as contingent, so that it can license same-sex marriage while still regarding childless marriages as marginal or defective; a minimal-marriage or flexible-contract view that treats the legal institution as a severable bundle of terms available to caring relationships generally, romantic or not; the view that the conjugal account is the correct account of marriage but is not the sort of claim the state may make a condition of legal recognition; the view that the injustice of exclusion is a matter of equal civic status rather than of what the relationships are for, so that the question about purpose is beside the point; and the abolitionist view that the state should stop recognizing or privileging marriage altogether. Add any further leaves the logical space demands.

Build the branches so that they are tested by concrete cases, and use these where they discriminate: a couple who marry and decide never to have children; a couple who want children and cannot have them; a marriage never consummated; a couple who raise children together for decades and never marry; two long-lived friends, or two siblings, who are each other's primary caregivers and want the legal package; a three-person household; and a marriage contracted for a visa. Where a combination of answers is occupied by nobody, say so and diagnose it — is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can pose, flag the pair rather than papering over it. The whole instrument is a failure if it sorts people by which label they like rather than by what they say about the cases, so please check that two people who reach the same verdict for different reasons land in different leaves, and end with a short note on which disagreements the tree reveals to be merely verbal. No citations or reading lists are needed, and please write in American English.
