I'm a graduate student in a philosophy-of-biology reading group that has spent the term circling the evolution of communication, and I'm trying to turn a seminar paper into something submittable. The standard story about honest signaling — that a signal is credible because it is costly, and that honesty is secured when the cost falls harder on the signaler who has less to advertise — has always seemed to me to be doing two jobs at once without noticing it: explaining why a receiver should believe the signal, and explaining why a low-quality signaler does not simply pay up and lie. I've written a draft arguing that these two jobs come apart, and I'd like you to read it and tell me whether the central distinction is doing real work or is just a relabeling of things everyone already says. Please be blunt about the parts that fail, and skip the prose-level nitpicks — the argument is what I need help with.

The draft, in full:

> Everyone knows the shape of the argument: a peacock's tail is expensive, and because it is expensive only a healthy peacock can afford one, so the hen may read the expense as evidence of health. Generalizing, a signal is credible in proportion to what it costs, and it is honest because the cost falls heavier on the signaler who has less to advertise. Call this the toll model of credibility: you buy your way past the receiver's skepticism.
>
> I want to argue that the toll model is asked to do two distinct jobs and does only one of them well. The first job is to explain why a receiver should take the signal at face value. The second is to explain why a low-quality signaler does not pay the toll and lie. Differential cost answers the second. It does not answer the first, and in many cases the first is done by something else entirely: a receiver who reasons "that must have been expensive, therefore it is true" leans on a background assumption — that the expense is genuinely beyond the reach of pretenders — which is precisely what the second job was supposed to secure. The toll model is therefore not a general account of credibility; it is one mechanism among several for making a signal unfakeable.
>
> If we sort signals by where the cost of a fake would fall, four kinds appear. Handicaps: the faker pays, and pays more than the honest signaler. Locks: nobody pays, because the signal is physically tied to the thing signalled — a frog croaks deeply because the frog is large, and there is no way to be small and croak deep. Audits: a third party is paid to check, and the faker is exposed by an inspector rather than by the arithmetic of his own budget. Leaks: the faker pays again, but in the opposite direction — what is expensive is not producing the signal but suppressing it. Blushing, a cracking voice, the fatigue that shows through in the last mile of a race, the safety record a firm cannot keep out of the newspapers: these are credible not because they cost something to display but because they would cost something to hide, and the signaler would rather not be displaying them at all.
>
> The fourth category is the one I care about, because it inverts the toll model's psychology. In a handicap the signaler is buying credibility; in a leak the signaler is failing to buy silence. And I suspect a great many signals usually filed under "costly" are leaks in disguise. Take the credibility of long-term consistency: someone who has behaved generously for thirty years is believed, and we say the behavior is credible because faking it would be exhausting. But the exhaustion is not a toll paid at each act of generosity; it is the cost of sustaining a persona across thirty years of unscripted situations. The expense is in concealment, not in display — a different mechanism with different predictions. For one thing, a leak should grow less reliable as the signaler gains control of the channel, which is why institutions invest so heavily in public relations, and why the credibility of a firm's safety claims is not a function of how much it spends advertising them.

Here is what I most need from you. First, is the fourth category genuinely distinct from the second, or is the difference only that one infeasibility is physical and the other motivational — and if so, does that difference carry any explanatory weight? Second, does the third category collapse into the first, since the inspector's effort is itself a cost someone bears? Third, is the taxonomy exhaustive on its own organizing principle, or does reputation in repeated interaction fall outside all four — my worry is that sustained honesty is secured neither by a toll nor by a lock nor by an inspector, but by the deferred threat of being disbelieved later, and I can't tell whether that is a fifth route to credibility or just an audit stretched over time. Fourth, on the long-term-consistency example: am I smuggling a cost back in under a new name, or is the concealment/display contrast a real distinction? And finally, if the paper can only carry two of the four categories, which two would you keep, and what would you say is the single strongest objection to the whole framing — the one a referee would reach for first?
