Write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers arguing over whether an individual has any moral reason to change her own high-emission habits when her own contribution makes no perceptible difference to the climate. Let the first press the inefficacy objection in its strongest form: that a difference no one could ever detect is no difference at all; that expected-impact reasoning smuggles in probability estimates about effects she can never verify; and that appeals to "what if everyone did it" or to "complicity" either collapse back into causal talk or quietly assume the very collective duty they are meant to ground. Let the second answer each of these actual moves rather than a stock position, and let neither side win by caricature.

To keep both from recycling familiar examples, route the middle of the dialogue through a mechanism that neither character has encountered before: a conditional-pledge platform on which a user binds herself to cut her flights only if at least a stated number of others do the same, where that threshold may or may not end up being reached. Have them disagree about whether making such a pledge is itself efficacious — whether it changes the probability that the threshold is crossed, whether that counts as making a difference, and whether whatever answer they reach carries over to ordinary, unpledged consumption. Close with the two of them stating exactly what they still disagree about and what fact or argument would settle it.
