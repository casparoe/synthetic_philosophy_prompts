Some friends and I got into an argument after a bad movie night -- we'd all paid for tickets to a sequel none of us were enjoying, and three of us sat through the whole thing while the other two left after twenty minutes and got ice cream. The ones who left said staying was textbook sunk cost fallacy; the ones who stayed said something like "well, I paid for it." I realized I don't have a stable view here at all, so I've been collecting my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them or if I'm just inconsistent.

Here's where I land. Finishing a bad meal because you ordered it: mistake. Sitting through the bad movie because of the ticket: mistake. The Concorde thing, and governments pouring more into a failing war because of the soldiers already lost: mistake, though I feel differently about the war case than the movie, because honoring the dead seems to be doing real work there and I'm not sure it's the same fallacy wearing a costume. Staying in a relationship that's clearly over because of the years already in it: mistake, but I'll admit it feels less like a mistake than finishing the meal, and I can't say why. Now the ones that don't fit. I know someone who paid a big nonrefundable deposit on a gym and then actually went every week, and I don't think she was being irrational at all -- the money was gone either way, but it changed what she did. Same with a small business owner I know who kept a doomed product line going two extra years specifically so that suppliers and rivals would see he doesnt quit; he says it paid for itself in later negotiations and I believe him. And then the case that really breaks me: a widow I know kept her late husband's failing hardware store open for years after he died, losing money the whole time. By the standard story that's a sunk-cost disaster. I don't think it's that at all, and I get genuinely annoyed when people call it one. But I also think the person who stays in a dead-end job because "I've already given them twelve years" is making a mistake, and I can't articulate what's different between her and the widow.

A friend of mine who does game theory keeps telling me the whole framing is wrong -- that when you're uncertain about your own future self, your past investment is evidence about what kind of person you are, and acting on it can be a way of keeping a commitment to that self rather than a fallacy. I don't know if I buy it but it's made me take some of these more seriously. So: is there one principle that gets all of these verdicts, or close? If I have to give one up, which one, and why that one? And please tell me what your principle says about two cases I haven't listed: a lottery player who keeps buying tickets because of what he's already spent, and a researcher who's spent fifteen years on a program that hasn't worked and won't quit because the grad students who built their careers on it would be stranded. Don't worry about citing anyone, I just want the principle and the reasoning.
