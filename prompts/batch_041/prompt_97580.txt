I am a novelist who has been sitting in on a graduate seminar on chance and inference, and I have agreed to grade one of the final examination questions. The question set for the exam was this: Explain how Kolmogorov complexity and the Solomonoff prior are supposed to underwrite Occam's razor, and assess the claim that Solomonoff induction solves Hume's problem of induction. In your answer, address what the prior assigns to a hypothesis, what optimality result is claimed for the mixture predictor, and what difference the choice of universal Turing machine makes.

One student's answer read as follows. The core idea is that we can measure the simplicity of a hypothesis by the length of the shortest program that outputs it. This is Kolmogorov complexity, K(x). A hypothesis with smaller K is more probable a priori, because the Solomonoff prior assigns probability 2^{-K(h)} to hypothesis h. When we observe data, we update by conditionalizing on the data. Solomonoff's mixture predictor is a weighted average of all computable hypotheses, weighted by their prior. A key theorem is that this predictor is optimal: for any computable sequence, its cumulative prediction error is bounded, so it eventually predicts as well as the best computable hypothesis. This is why it is often said to solve Hume's problem: it gives a universal inductive method that is guaranteed to succeed on any sequence that has a computable regularity. The choice of universal Turing machine does not matter, because any two universal machines can simulate each other, so the complexity of a string is the same on all of them. In practice, we can compute the Solomonoff prior by enumerating programs and summing 2 to the power minus the program length; it is not feasible for long strings, but with enough computing power it would be. Occam's razor is thus vindicated as a theorem, not a heuristic.
