Hey, I teach a final-year module that's half history, half philosophy of history, and I'm writing the exam. The last essay is the one I actually care about: it's on the theme we've run all term, when does a legend become history, using Troy, Arthur and the Exodus as the three running cases. The problem is that every question I've tried lets them just recite the standard fact-sheets ("no Egyptian evidence for the Exodus", "there's a burnt layer at Hisarlik", "Geoffrey of Monmouth mostly made Arthur up") and then stop. I want a question that makes them do conceptual work instead. So I'd like you to act as an external examiner who knows this material cold — archaeology, the texts, and the methodology debates — and write me the question plus everything I'd need to mark it. Dont worry about citations or a reading list, it's a closed-book exam, no books allowed.

Here's the angle I've been pushing at them and I want the question to test whether they've actually absorbed it. I keep telling them that when someone asks "did the Trojan War really happen", they're usually asking one of three quite different things and sliding between them without noticing: (i) the event question — did the things the story narrates actually occur; (ii) the descent question — is there a causal chain that carries the content of the story back to some real event, as opposed to the story being invented later and read backwards onto the past; and (iii) the datum question — what does the mere fact that people told this story tell us about the people who told it. My provocation, which I want the question to force them to either defend or knock down, is that the evidence can really only bear on (ii), that (i) mostly can't be settled either way, and that most of the shouting in this area is people arguing about (i) while waving evidence that only speaks to (ii) — or, going the other way, minimalists quietly swapping (iii) in for (i) and presenting it as a finding about what happened. And then the sting I want them to face: the three cases look like they should be graded very differently, but I'm not sure the evidence actually grades them differently. Troy has its hints (the Hittite material some scholars match to a place and a king they read as Wilusa and Alaksandu, plus a destruction layer); the Exodus has the Merneptah reference to Israel and not much else Egyptian; Arthur has Nennius, then Geoffrey, and effectively nothing behind either of them. Yet we cheerfully call one of these historical, one contested, and one literary. Is that grading earned by the evidence, or is it cultural, and what would have to be true for the grading to be earned?

So please write me (a) the essay question itself, phrased so it can't be answered by reciting the three fact-sheets, (b) a model answer of around 700–800 words that actually takes a position on my provocation and on whether the three-way grading is earned, and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — including at least one mistake that a very well-read student is especially likely to make. Think it through carefully, and start by brainstorming the ways a strong student could push back on my three-question split before you commit to the model answer.
