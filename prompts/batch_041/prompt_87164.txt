Ok so I got into this at a family dinner and now I definately can't let it go. Somebody brought up that old Chinese line that a white horse is not a horse, and everyone laughed and said it's a trick, he's playing with the word is, and moved on. I'm not a philosopher, I've never studied any of this, I just read a bit online and now I have a notebook going. The thing is, the more I poke at it the less it feels like a trick, because when I try to sort out my own examples I can't find the line between the ones where it's obviously a cheat and the ones where the thing really has stopped being the thing.

So here's my list, the ones I'm sure about and the ones I'm not. Tell me if there's one rule that gets all of them, or if I have to give some up.

- A white horse is a horse. Obviously. If that's the famous claim then it's just false and I don't see the fuss.
- A dead horse is a horse. Pretty firmly. Even though a dead horse can't do one single thing that makes a horse a horse.
- A busted gun is a gun. Same deal, it can't shoot, but it's still a gun.
- A fake gun is not a gun. This feels just as firm as the white horse one and I can't say why the two don't go together.
- A toy gun is not a gun. Same.
- A stone lion is not a lion. Same. But a dead lion is a lion, so why does dead keep it and stone lose it?
- A rocking horse... I honestly go back and forth. Some days it's a horse, some days it's furniture.
- A sawhorse isn't a horse and a pommel horse isn't a horse, and I'm fine with that. But a seahorse I want to say isn't a horse, and it feels like a different kind of isn't from the sawhorse. Is that a different question from the one I'm asking?

My half-baked rule: the extra word keeps the thing being the thing as long as it doesn't take away whatever makes it that kind of thing, and if it does take that away, it isn't that thing anymore. White doesn't take anything away from a horse. Fake and toy and stone take away what makes a gun a gun and a lion a lion. But dead takes away a lot and I still say horse, and busted takes away the shooting and I still say gun, so my rule's broke somewhere and I can't see where.

Two things I want out of this. First, whatever rule you land on, what does it say about the actual white horse claim — false, true, or is he asking something else entirely? I read that one of his moves is: ask for a horse and any horse will do, brown or black, but ask for a white horse and the black one won't do. And I read that the same crowd has a pile of other sayings attached to them — a chicken has three feet, a white dog is black, a dog is not a hound, the wheel never touches the ground, fire isn't hot — and some of those don't look like they're about the extra word at all. They look like a different puzzle, about parts, or about motion, or about the words themselves. Which of them is my rule even supposed to touch, and which am I wrong to put in the same drawer?

Second, give me a case I haven't listed that splits whatever rule you give me, and one where you think I'd get it wrong.

And if the honest answer is that this is two or three different puzzles wearing one coat, say so. I'd rather have that than one tidy rule that quietly cheats on one of my cases.
