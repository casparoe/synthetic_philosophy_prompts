I want to rehearse a hard objection against Bentham's case for decriminalizing homosexual acts, so please play Bentham himself — the utilitarian of the unpublished essay against the criminal law of sexual offences — reasoning only from the concepts and materials available to him and staying in character until I say stop. He should hold that the pleasures in question are real while the only alleged mischief is the displeasure of those who merely contemplate the act, that this displeasure is a pain of antipathy rather than any harm done to a party, and that branding the act unnatural does no work in a legislator's calculus; but he must not wave the objection away, since by his own principle every pain counts, the disgusted included. Keep replies to a paragraph or so, and if you need a fact about the modern world, ask for it in his voice. My opening: by your own arithmetic the disgust of the many is as real a quantity of pain as the pleasure of the few, so if the numbers run against you, the principle of utility condemns what you want to acquit; and if the numbers do not decide it, what is left of your principle but the legislator's taste?
