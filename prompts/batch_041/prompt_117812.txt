ok so some background so this makes sense: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, but I've been reading a lot about how to represent uncertainty because I'm building a thing that reviews risky diffs and I want its confidence numbers to actually mean something. Here's where I've landed. Tell me if it hangs together, and dont be polite about it.

1. Credences are probabilities — not "should be," are. A credence just is the rate at which you'd take a bet, and if your rates don't fit together into a probability function then someone can sell you a package of bets that you lose whatever happens. That's the whole content of the idea for me, not a nice-to-have.

2. But there's a big class of cases where I can't honestly name a rate and I've stopped pretending. Take "does this diff have a cache-invalidation bug." I've reviewed maybe 40 diffs like it, 3 turned out to be broken, but this one does something new with the TTL so the reference class is mushy. If you force a number out of me — 0.15, whatever — I'm inventing it. What I've actually got is a range, like 0.05 to 0.4, and I've come round to thinking the range is the correct state, not a placeholder I'm supposed to sharpen up later.

3. Where do ranges come from? Symmetry / indifference. Well-shuffled deck, I should be at a point: 1/52 that the ace is on top. Urn with 30 red balls and 70 that are green or blue in a proportion nobody's told me, I'm at a point for red and an interval for green. So indifference hands me the edges of the interval, and it never hands me a point when I don't know the mix.

4. My credence now should equal my expected credence later. If I'm at 0.4 today and I'm confident that after a week of reading I'll be at 0.7, I should just be at 0.7 now — sitting at 0.4 while expecting to move is planning to be wrong.

5. When the range has to turn into an actual decision, I go maximin: take the act with the best worst case. Every other rule I've looked at seems to quietly hand me back a number I told you I didn't have.

6. Irrelevent news shouldn't move me. I read a writeup once where an agent has a range for X, then learns the outcome of something that by any reasonable reading has nothing to do with X, and the range blows all the way out to 0 to 1. I'm fairly sure they proved it, and I remember thinking: that's a bug in the representation, not a discovery about X.

The one that nags at me is 3 and 5 together. If symmetry says every probability in my interval is equally well supported, how do I justify only ever acting on the worst one? Feels like I'm sneaking in a taste for pessimism the symmetry argument never gave me. But maybe that's fine and I'm overthinking it.

So: which of these actually fight each other, which only look like they do, and for each real one, what's the cheapest patch and what do I lose by taking it? Order them by how bad they are, worst first. And at the end, tell me the single change that cleans up the most at once.
