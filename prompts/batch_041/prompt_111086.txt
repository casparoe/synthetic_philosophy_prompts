I am a novelist at work on a book in which an executive brings about a foreseeable harm he insists he never intended, and the moral weight of the whole chapter turns on whether we say he harmed his neighbours intentionally. I have been reading about the experimental philosophy of intentional action and about the asymmetry philosophers now call the Knobe effect, and I keep sliding between explanations of it — which is awkward, because my characters slide with me.

Please act as a philosopher of action and an experimental methodologist. Think carefully about the space of explanations before you draft anything, and brainstorm first the cases that would prise them apart; then give me a decision tree I can actually work through, taking me from my own reactions to the cases to a named position on what the asymmetry shows. Branch only on concrete cases or claims on which the candidate positions genuinely come apart — for instance, a bad side effect for which nobody could be blamed; a good side effect that nonetheless violates a norm; an agent ignorant of the side effect; a corporate agent rather than an individual. The leaves should include at least the view that the folk concept of intentional action is itself normative; the view that the asymmetry is a conversational implicature and the folk concept is not normative at all; the view that blame comes first and the ascription of intent is downstream of it; the view that what drives the asymmetry is norm-violation rather than badness as such; and the debunking view that the asymmetry is a bias to be explained away rather than evidence about the concept. Phrase every node as a question I can answer without already knowing which position it leads to.

Flag any leaves your tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers nobody occupies, and whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. No reading list, please — I want the instrument, not the literature. Write in British English.
