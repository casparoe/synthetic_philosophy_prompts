I have this dumb weekly argument club with two friends, and we've spent like three months on the last chunk of Wittgenstein's notes — the ones he wrote at the very end of his life, where he's chewing on Moore holding up his hand and saying he knows it's a hand. I don't have a philosophy background, I studied accounting, so take this as an outsider's read, but the thing that grabbed me is that the propositions he's interested in aren't known and aren't doubted either. They're not the sort of thing you could prove or check; they hold fast because everything else is checked against them, and the doubting itself already assumes them. And the bit I can't stop thinking about is that he also seems to allow that this bedrock can shift — that the channel the river runs in was itself carved by the water at some point, and could carve differently. That's the part my friends and I keep going around in circles on, and it makes me want to write something mean.

So here's what I want from you: a parody employee handbook / FAQ, deadpan corporate voice, from the compliance department of an invented mid-size insurance company (call it Harrowgate Mutual or whatever you like) that has decided to implement this stuff as actual policy. There's a Foundation Assumptions Office. The FAQ should explain what a foundational assumption is, why it's exempt from the audit process, what happens when an employee raises a doubt about one, how the company decides which assumptions get promoted into the foundation, and what the procedure is for a foundation assumption that starts to erode. Make it around 750 words, formatted as numbered Q&As or handbook clauses with headings. The jokes should come from the actual logic: the circularity of trying to justify the thing you doubt everything else against, the fact that these are held by acting rather than by being seen to be true, the fact that you can't list them and also treat them as unquestionable (the listing is itself a doubt-shaped act), the fact that a new hire is trained into them rather than convinced of them, and the fact that the bedrock moves, slowly or in a flood, and nobody can be in charge of that from outside. Whoever runs the office should be perfectly sincere. I don't want a wink — the document should read like a real compliance artifact that happens to be insane.

It needs to be funny to someone who's read the notes and readable to someone who hasn't, so keep the jargon out and let the absurdity do the work. Don't lean on any specific numbered passages or quote anything; I'd rather you just get the ideas right in your own words. Target is the practice of institutions declaring some assumptions beyond questioning, and also the philosophical itch that you could ever put the hinges in a list and hand the list around — I think that's the joke, but you tell me. And I want a definite answer, not a menu: at the end, add a short author's note (maybe four or five sentences) saying where the parody takes liberties with the real ideas, which ones you had to flatten to make the form work, and your actual verdict on whether the satire is fair to the view or whether it's a cheap shot that just makes institutional stupidity look like philosophy. Take a side on that, don't hedge it.
