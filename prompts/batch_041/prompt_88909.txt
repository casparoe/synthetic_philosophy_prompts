I'm a second-year law student taking a jurisprudence module and we've just spent two weeks on the Hart-Fuller debate and I honestly cannot tell what is still being argued about. I'd like you to tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, because my suspicion is it isn't the one on the tin.

Here's the setup as I've got it. Hart says law and morality are conceptually seperate: you can have a perfectly valid legal rule that's morally monstrous, and calling it law doesn't commit you to endorsing it. He also thinks rules have a settled core and a fuzzy penumbra, and that hard cases fall in the penumbra, so deciding them is a genuine choice rather than the discovery of some higher law. Fuller fires back that this is a picture of law as a one-way projection of authority, and that a legal system has an inner morality of law all its own — you can't even manage to make law if you're arbitrary enough — so the Nazi statutes weren't really law, or weren't fully law, and the post-war courts weren't just choosing freely. Then Hart replies that this just smuggles morality into the definition.

What bugs me is that they seem to agree on all the boring facts. Both think the Nazi law was wicked. Both think the grudge informer case is horrible. Both think judges sometimes have to decide hard cases and that this is hard. So why can't they meet? My hypothesis, tell me if it's dumb: they aren't really disagreeing about whether law and morality are connected, they're disagreeing about what a theory of law is *for*. Hart wants a concept that tracks what officials actually do, descriptively; Fuller wants a concept that does work when you're deciding whether to obey or apply the thing. If that's the diagnosis then it's nearly a verbal dispute, except that the "nearly" is doing enormous work, because choosing between those two purposes looks like a moral or political choice and not a neutral one.

But then I wonder if it's actually a fight about necessity — Hart's no-necessary-connection versus Fuller's there-is-one — and whether "necessary" means the same thing on both sides at all (logical necessity? conceptual? just a strong tendency?). And relatedly: does Fuller's inner morality make a wicked but orderly regime impossible, or only less likely? Because if it's only less likely then it's an empirical claim about how legal systems tend to work and Hart seems to win by default; if it's impossible then it's a conceptual claim and I want to know what makes it conceptual rather than a very strong empirical regularity. And is the live version of this now just Dworkin versus everybody, which feels like a different fight wearing the same coat?

So, what kind of disagreement is this really? Verbal, two questions under one word, a hidden empirical question about how legal systems function, or a genuine clash of bedrock intuitions about whether an unjust command can obligate anyone? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction that dissolves the thing, a piece of evidence, or an argument that it can't be dissolved and that's fine? I'd rather have the diagnosis with reasons than a summary of who said what.
