Question 2 [20 points]

(a) [6] Give an intuitionistic proof of ¬¬(p ∨ ¬p). Natural deduction or a Hilbert system, your choice.

(b) [7] Using Kripke semantics, show that p ∨ ¬p is not a theorem of intuitionistic propositional logic.

(c) [7] A classmate says: "Since the intuitionist can prove ¬¬(p ∨ ¬p), and the only thing standing between intuitionistic and classical logic is double negation, the intuitionist and the classicist don't really disagree about anything. Whatever the classicist proves, the intuitionist can prove too -- just with two extra tildes in front." Evaluate this claim. (The disjunction property and Glivenko's theorem may be useful here.)

Student's answer:

(a) I need to show ¬(p ∨ ¬p) → ⊥. Assume (1) ¬(p ∨ ¬p). Now suppose (2) p. Then p ∨ ¬p by ∨-intro, which contradicts (1). So ¬p by ¬-intro, discharging (2). But then p ∨ ¬p by ∨-intro again, contradicting (1). So we've derived ⊥, and therefore ¬¬(p ∨ ¬p) by ¬-intro. □

(b) A Kripke model is a set of nodes with an accessibility relation and a monotone forcing relation, and a formula is a theorem iff it's forced at every node of every model, so one countermodel is enough. Take the model with a single node w, and let p not be forced at w. The left disjunct fails, since p isn't forced at w. For the right disjunct: ¬p is forced at w iff p is not forced at any node accessible from w; since there are no accessible nodes and p isn't forced at w, ¬p isn't forced at w either. So neither disjunct holds at w, so p ∨ ¬p fails at w, so it isn't valid and hence isn't a theorem. ■

(c) The disjunction property is the key to why LEM has to go: if intuitionistic logic proves A ∨ B then it proves A or it proves B, and since it proves neither p nor ¬p it can't prove p ∨ ¬p. But this doesn't show the classmate is wrong, because of Glivenko's theorem: A is a theorem of classical propositional logic iff ¬¬A is a theorem of intuitionistic propositional logic. So every classical theorem has an intuitionistic double-negation, and the intuitionist can prove the ¬¬-version of everything the classicist proves. The formulas the classicist proves and the intuitionist doesn't are exactly the ones where the extra ¬¬ can't be dropped -- LEM, double-negation elimination itself, and so on. So every classical proof can be turned into an intuitionistic proof of a slightly weaker statement, and that's the whole difference: it comes down to which formulas you're willing to assert outright rather than to anything about what's provable. The classmate is basically right -- intuitionistic logic is classical logic with the double negations left in, and you get classical logic back just by allowing yourself to drop them.
