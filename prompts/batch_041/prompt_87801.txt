You are a philosopher of education who knows Alfred North Whitehead's writings on education in detail and who has also spent real time inside secondary schools — you are equally at home with his account of the rhythm of learning and with a department meeting about pacing guides. You think carefully about how ideas constrain form, not just content.

I chair the planning committee for a two-day professional-development workshop for high school department heads. Our theme is "depth over coverage." The committee has adopted as its framing motto three words borrowed from Whitehead — romance, precision, generalization — and has already printed them on the tote bags and the program cover. I have to give the opening remarks, roughly twelve to fifteen minutes, and I want them to do two jobs at once: set the intellectual frame for the two days, and quietly repair what I think is wrong with our own motto.

Here is my problem. Half the room are veteran teachers who have lived through a decade of reform initiatives and who will decide within the first ninety seconds whether this is another fad. A quarter of the room are administrators who like a clean framework and who will repeat the three words all year whether or not they still mean anything. And a small handful have actually read Whitehead, and they will notice if I flatten him. The flattening I am worried about is this: we have turned the three stages into a staircase — first you give them romance, then precision, then generalization, then you are finished. My reading is that his rhythm is not a staircase. It is a cycle that recurs within every subject, at every level of schooling, and even inside a single lesson, and the stages are defined by their relation to one another rather than by their position in a sequence: precision entered without a prior romance produces exactly the dead, unassimilated learning Whitehead warned about, and romance that never matures into precision curdles into mere enthusiasm. I want the speech to carry that correction without making the committee look foolish in front of the people we still need to persuade.

Write the opening remarks. Requirements:

- The structure of the speech should embody the rhythm rather than merely describe it. Think hard about what that commits you to: if the speech is to move through romance, precision, and generalization, it cannot open with a definition of the terms, and it cannot close with a summary of what was said.
- Give one concrete, recognizable classroom example for each stage — the kind of example a chemistry teacher or a history teacher would recognize as their own week, not an idealized scene from a school with no bells and no testing calendar.
- Include one honest concession: name a real cost or risk of teaching this way, and do not dissolve it with a happy ending.
- Anticipate, without ever naming it as an objection, the worry that romance is a luxury for schools whose students are under pressure to hit measurable targets. Address it so that a principal in the back row finds it credible rather than preachy.
- Do not quote Whitehead verbatim, and do not cite editions, page numbers, or the titles of particular essays. Paraphrase his ideas in your own words; the audience does not need a literature review, and a misremembered quotation would do more damage than a good paraphrase.
- Land the ending on something the audience can act on Monday, not on a feeling.

Before you write, spend a short section thinking on the page: what does it actually mean to structure a speech by a rhythm rather than by a list of points, and which of the three stages is hardest to dramatize in a keynote? Then write the speech. Then, after the speech, add a short note to me — clearly not part of the speech — about the one place where it is most likely to be misheard by the teachers who distrust theory, and what I should do about that in the discussion afterward.
