I've just started a type-design program, and a disagreement between two of my instructors has become pressing, because I'm midway through a display face and no longer know what I'm supposed to be preserving.

One of them holds what I'll call the backbone thesis: every 'a' — single-storey, double-storey, cursive, blackletter, the geometric circle-with-a-tail — is a surface realization of one underlying skeleton, a small set of relational roles (a counter, a stem, a junction, a terminal, a direction of stroke), and a shape is an 'a' exactly to the extent that it preserves that skeleton. Identity, on this view, is structural and mind-independent; the thousands of 'a's you've seen are variations on a single schema. The other holds what I'll call the boundary thesis: there is no skeleton. 'a' names a region in shape-space that competent readers of the script have learned to carve out, and the region is fixed by recognition rather than geometry. On this view a loopy geometric 'a' and a blackletter 'a' need share no structural feature whatever; they are united only by the fact that trained readers will call both 'a'.

Please compare the two theses and tell me which, if either, is right, and what each does that the other cannot. Test them against the cases I keep running into: the single- versus double-storey 'a' (do they even share a skeleton?); a perfectly well-formed 'a' rotated ninety degrees or mirrored, so the skeleton survives but the identity does not; a cursive 'a' whose bowl is left open; a shape that reads as 'a' only because it sits in the middle of a word and would read as nothing in isolation; an 'a' abstracted in a logo down to a bare triangle; and a shape structurally identical to an 'a' that functions in its context as a caret or an arrow. Tell me which thesis each case favours and why, and whether this is genuinely a disagreement between two theses or one thesis stated twice in different vocabularies.

Structure the answer as: "The two theses" (~200 words, each stated in its strongest form); "What each explains" (~350 words, the work each does that the other cannot); "The hard cases" (~400 words, working through my list); and "What I should do" (~150 words, which thesis to design from and what would change your mind).
