Two designers I share a studio with have been circling the same argument for weeks, and I'd like an outsider's ruling. The occasion is a lowercase 'a' that Ruth drew: it keeps a bowl and a stem, but the bowl is squeezed into a narrow almond and the stem is detached, floating just to the right of it; set in a word at text sizes it still reads as 'a'. Ruth says the drawing settles nothing and the letter settles everything — every 'a' shares an essential core, a bowl plus some mark that keeps it from collapsing into 'o', 'e', or 'd', and a typeface is judged by how far it can push that core without losing it. Omar says there is no core: the same drawing set in a different face would read as an 'e', and what makes a shape an 'a' is only its place in a system of mutually distinguishing shapes, together with the reader's expectations — conventions, both of them. Ruth answers that a single-story 'a' (a bowl with a stem) and a double-story 'a' (a bowl under a curved hood) are built from different parts, yet both are unmistakably 'a'; so the essence cannot be any particular set of strokes, and must live at a more abstract level, which is what she meant all along. Omar says that reply proves his point: an essence abstract enough to cover both forms is abstract enough to cover nearly anything, so 'essence' has quietly become 'whatever readers will accept' — his view with extra steps. Neither budged, and I genuinely couldn't say who had the better of it. Please adjudicate move by move: which claims are right, whether Omar's collapse objection lands against Ruth's abstraction, whether either of them is committed to something the other has refuted, and what, if anything, both are missing.
