I want to write a short story that puts the relational view of personhood under real pressure — the idea that you don't get to be a person on your own, that you become one through other people, a person through other persons. My premise: a near-future town adopts a civic "recognition index," a running score of how many people are actually holding you in mind as a person — remembering you, counting on you, who'd notice if you vanished. It's sold as a kindness, a way to route care to the isolated. My protagonist is an old man whose number is sliding, and the town slowly starts treating the number as the fact. What I want the story to be about is the question the relational view can't dodge: if personhood is something other people give you, is it something they can take back, and is there any sense left in which he's still a person once the last person who remembers him stops? Please don't hand me the sentimental "he was a person all along" ending — I want it to sit in the tension between the relational insight (we really do become ourselves through others, that part isn't a metaphor) and the gut feeling that dignity can't be a popularity contest. Give me at least one character who thinks the index just makes visible something true about us, and one who thinks it's a machine for manufacturing outcasts. 2000 words or so.
