I have recently joined the ethics committee of the hospital where I work, and in my first months I was twice drawn into disputes over whether a patient should be declared dead: once when a family refused to accept a determination of brain death, and once when a transplant team was pressing for a declaration so that procurement could proceed. I could argue about the individual cases, but I found I could not say what a definition of death is supposed to accomplish in the first place, and it became clear that everyone in the room was quietly working from a different answer. Before we settle on committee policy, I want the specification rather than another round of cases.

Set out the desiderata that any adequate account of death — its definition, the criterion drawn from it, and the tests performed at the bedside — must satisfy: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the findings it cannot contradict. Give the case or consideration behind each requirement rather than merely asserting it.

I expect the list to have to deal with at least the following: patients in a persistent vegetative state, and patients who are locked in, aware but almost entirely paralysed; bodies kept going on ventilators, and more awkwardly the documented cases of bodies sustained by extracorporeal support, or in pregnancy, after the functioning of the brain has ceased; controlled donation after circulatory death, where the declaration follows the cessation of circulation after only a few minutes of observation and everyone involved knows in advance that organs will be taken; anencephalic and severely malformed newborns; and the ordinary fact that the tests differ from hospital to hospital and country to country, while death is supposed to be one thing everywhere.

Be explicit about the distinctions the account must not collapse: definition versus criterion versus bedside test; the death of an organism versus the death of a person, or the loss of the capacity for consciousness; dying versus being dead; a biological claim versus a legal or social one.

Then separate the requirements that are genuine constraints on any account from those that are merely commitments of one camp. Where several cannot be held together, say so and explain the conflict. I suspect that the demand that death be a single biological fact, the demand that the determination be conservative enough to license the removal of vital organs, and the demand that the criterion be uniform across every kind of body will not all survive contact with one another, and that is the part I most want to see. A colleague has also proposed that the criterion be indexed to the individual, so that a patient's own advance directive could select among them; tell me whether that is a genuine desideratum or a category mistake. Finish by naming the single requirement that does the most to exclude the current candidates.

Tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
