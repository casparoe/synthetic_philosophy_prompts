"(a) Take an urn containing 90 balls. 30 are red; the remaining 60 are black or yellow in some unknown proportion. One ball is drawn at random. Consider four bets, each paying $100 on the indicated color(s): A wins if red; B wins if black; C wins if red or yellow; D wins if black or yellow. Most people strictly prefer A to B, and strictly prefer D to C.

(b) This pair of preferences violates the independence axiom of expected utility theory. The independence axiom says that if you prefer one lottery to another, you should still prefer it once both are mixed with a third lottery at the same probability. Here A and B differ only in which color wins, and C and D differ in the same way, so by independence my preference between A and B ought to match my preference between C and D. People's choices don't match, and that mismatch is the paradox.

(c) The pattern is consistent with treating the unknown proportion as a range rather than a point value, so it isn't automatically inconsistent with anything. Whether it's unreasonable is a further question, and the honest answer is that this is genuinely contested. The standard expected-utility line, associated with Ellsberg himself, is that the ambiguity-averse choices are irrational: they amount to failing to treat equally likely events equally, and a rational agent who grasped the axioms would correct them. Against this, one can argue that a preference for familiar odds over unfamiliar ones is a legitimate preference about the situation rather than a mistake about probability, and that expected utility has no right to legislate it away. I lean toward the second view, though I don't think the first can just be dismissed.

(d) The two are closely related but not the same. Allais involves known probabilities and a violation of the independence axiom proper, whereas Ellsberg involves unknown probabilities. So they're two different failures of the same theory rather than two versions of one failure."
