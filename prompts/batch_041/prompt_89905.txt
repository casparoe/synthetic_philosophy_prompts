I hold the view that to have rational preferences just is to have preferences that can be represented by a utility function which the agent maximises in expectation, so that intransitivity, incompleteness, and violations of the independence or sure-thing principle are all rational defects; the proof, I take it, is the exploitation argument — such an agent can be walked, through a series of trades each of which they accept, into an outcome they prefer less. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward, and I suspect several of the following are more serious than they are usually allowed to be: the resolute agent who binds themselves or adopts a standing policy and is therefore never actually pumped; the agent whose ranking of outcomes depends on the path by which they were reached, so that the final trade in the sequence is not one they would accept; the case of incomplete preferences, out of which nothing can be pumped but which are also not representable as expected-utility maximisation; and the charge that the argument is pragmatic through and through, showing that such an agent will lose a particular game rather than that their preferences are irrational. I am also unsure how much of the package the argument really reaches: it seems cleanest for intransitivity, and for incoherent degrees of belief in the probabilistic case, and it is not obvious that it touches completeness or independence at all. Which of these would a committed defender concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated by quietly redefining rationality as 'whatever cannot be exploited', and whether that is a vice or a legitimate refinement. State how confident you are in each of your claims and single out the point you are least sure of. Please answer in British English.
