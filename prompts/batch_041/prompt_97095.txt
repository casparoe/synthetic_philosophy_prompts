My younger sister is a GP in a mid-sized town and a lab there just started pushing a cheap blood panel that flags what they call an "elevated inflammatory marker" (I forget the number, six or seven). She keeps getting people who came in for something totally unrelated, feel completely fine, and leave with a flagged result. The lab's leaflet says the marker indicates a biological abnormality, full stop, and that whether you slap the word disease on it is just semantics. Half of these people start acting like patients anyway — joining forums, asking for the drug, telling HR. The other half are people with the exact same numbers who are genuinely miserable, and nobody would blink at calling them sick.

Then the clinic got into a fight with the regional health board, which wants to screen the whole town and treat everyone who flags, on the grounds that a body malfunctioning is a body malfunctioning and you don't need anyone's opinion to know it. A patients' group fired back that this is just relabeling normal variation as pathology, and that what we call disease is really about which problems a society is willing to treat as problems — they point out that the town has a name for something basically harmless and calls it an illness, while refusing the word for something that actually wrecks people's lives, mostly depending on who's got it. Now my sister wants my opinion and I don't really have one, because both sides sound right.

So: is a flagged result a disease even when the person is fine? Does it change anything that the same biology ruins someone else's life? And when the town and the lab disagree about what counts, is one of them just wrong about the facts, or are they answering different questions? Practically — what should she actually tell the woman who's fine but flagged, and should her answer shift if that woman is anxious about it?
