I chair the interdisciplinary research committee of the Larkspur Foundation, and on Thursday we must vote on two competing grant applications in what our program officers call the plant cognition area. One team, led by a plant physiologist, proposes to study whether damaged leaves produce a systemic signal that alters future growth; the application describes the signal as a representation of the damage and argues that the plant therefore has a minimal kind of cognition. The other team, led by a philosopher of biology, proposes to study the same signaling pathway but insists that calling the signal a representation is a category mistake: on their view, representation requires decouplable content, meaning the state can occur without the condition it indicates, and the possibility of misrepresentation, meaning the state can be wrong, and the plant signal has neither. The two applications cite some of the same experiments, agree on the physiology, and even agree that the signal guides adaptive behavior. They disagree about whether that agreement settles the question of representation. My committee is split: some members want to fund only the second team on the ground that the first is trading on an equivocation; others want to fund both and let the science sort it out; a third group wants to table both until the applicants agree on terminology.

I have been reading about verbal disputes and the branch of philosophy that asks what ontological disputes are really about, and I think our decision turns on whether this is a merely verbal dispute or a substantive one. But the standard tests do not seem to help. The parties do not agree on all the facts, because they disagree about which facts are relevant; nor do they agree on all the practical consequences, because the first team thinks the representation vocabulary licenses a research program that the second team thinks is confused. One of our members, a logician, has proposed the following test, which I would like you to apply: a dispute is merely verbal if, after replacing each party's disputed term with the party's own preferred definition, the parties' remaining claims are logically compatible and make the same predictions about every possible observation; if the replacements yield different predictions, or if one replacement makes a whole class of questions askable while the other forbids them, the dispute is substantive. She calls the first clause the substitution test and the second the downstream-question test.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action, whether to fund one, fund both, fund neither, or fund with conditions, and it should state the conditions and a timeline. It should apply the two tests explicitly to the plant signaling case, say what the tests leave underdetermined, and engage seriously with the strongest argument on the side you reject. The memo should be written for a committee of scientists and philosophers who will not all share your background, so define any technical terms you use. Please write in American English.
