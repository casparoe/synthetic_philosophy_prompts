I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been turning over an idea I ran into in a popular book about minds and machines. The author tells a story about a solitary wasp: she stings a cricket, drags it back to her burrow, leaves it on the threshold, and goes inside to inspect the nest before hauling the cricket in. If you move the cricket a few inches while she's inside, she comes back out, drags it to the threshold again, and inspects the nest again — and you can keep this up all afternoon. From the outside the sequence looks purposeful, but it seems to be a fixed script that can only be run from the top and that treats any perturbation as a cue to start over rather than as information to re-plan around. The author's word for this, which I've adopted, is sphexishness, and I've started seeing it everywhere: in insects, in the bureaucratic forms I fill out at the town hall, in the non-idempotent deploy scripts I've written, and, less comfortably, in a few of my own habits. I'd like to get a real grip on the idea by ranking cases against one another instead of reading more theory, and I'd rather the ranking rest on a defined dimension than on my gut.

So please rank the following ten cases from most sphexish to least sphexish, and present the result as a tier list — say four tiers, with an explicit ordering inside each tier. Treat sphexishness as the degree to which a behaviour (a) cannot be interrupted and resumed mid-sequence, and (b) responds to a change in the environment by restarting the routine from the beginning rather than by re-planning toward the same goal; where those two pull apart for a given case, say which you weighted more heavily and why. For each case give a short justification and a score between 0 and 1 for calibration (0 = a flexible agent that re-plans, 1 = a pure start-to-finish script). I'd also like you to flag the cases whose surface appearance is misleading — the ones that look flexible but on inspection are rigid, and the ones that look rigid but are in fact re-planning — and to tell me which single case, if I re-described it slightly, would most disturb the whole ordering and in which direction. I'm far less interested in where you draw the line between mechanical and free than in the relative placement of these ten, so don't agonize over the bottom of the list.

1. The wasp and the moved cricket, as described above.
2. A web-spinning spider whose web-repair routine, if a strand is broken partway through the repair, starts over from the outermost anchor line rather than patching the break.
3. A deploy script that is not idempotent: if it fails halfway, re-running it corrupts the state, so you must restore from a backup and run the whole thing from the beginning.
4. A robot vacuum that pauses and resumes cleanly when you pick it up mid-run, but that, when carried into a different room, begins its entire coverage pattern from the starting corner again.
5. A novice cook who discovers halfway through a recipe that an ingredient is missing, abandons the dish, and orders takeout.
6. An experienced cook who discovers the same thing and substitutes an ingredient to keep the dish on track.
7. A commuter whose usual route home is closed and who, rather than detouring, drives back to a familiar landmark and starts the route again from there.
8. A chess engine that, after each opponent move, discards its previously planned line and searches afresh from the current position toward the goal of winning.
9. A surgeon who, on encountering an unexpected condition mid-operation, aborts the planned procedure and re-plans the whole operation from scratch around the new goal of stabilising the patient.
10. A language model that is told partway through a task that one of its earlier assumptions was wrong, but keeps building on that assumption because the earlier turns are already fixed in its context.
