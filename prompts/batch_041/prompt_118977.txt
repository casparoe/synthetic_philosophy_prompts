I run a monthly sci-fi-and-philosophy night at a pub near me — about a dozen people, half of them with PhDs in something completely unrelated — and I've stupidly agreed to lead the session on the dark forest idea, the one people get from Liu Cixin's trilogy, where the galaxy is full of civilisations hiding from each other and the only rational response to detecting another one is to wipe it out before it wipes you out, since you can never know its intentions and it might leap ahead of you technologically overnight. I've got the skeleton of the argument down (finite resources, the chain of suspicion, the sudden technological explosion, first strike as the dominant move), and I've been arguing about it for weeks with a friend who says its basically Pascal's wager with spaceships — an unbounded downside means any nonzero probability of hostility settles the question — while I keep thinking it must be smuggling in something about one-shot games, because the universe is very old and very repeated and that's exactly the situation where you'd expect cooperation to evolve.

The text I want to pair it with is Ender's Game, and I honestly can't decide which way the novel cuts. On one reading it's a straight dramatisation of the hypothesis: humanity has been invaded twice by something it never managed to understand, and rather than wait around for a third it annihilates the buggers' home world, and the whole thing happens under exactly the kind of radical uncertainty about the other side's intentions that the dark forest treats as unavoidable. On the other reading the book is an argument against the hypothesis: the twist is that Ender thinks he's running a simulation, and the deeper twist is what he learns about the buggers afterwards — that they had a side to the story, and that the catastrophe came down to a failure of communication rather than an unbridgeable hostility, so the chain of suspicion turns out to be a human artefact rather than a law of nature.

What I want is a reading that holds both of those together instead of picking a side: map the novel onto the argument as carefully as you can — who the players are, what each side could and couldn't know, what the strike was actually a response to, whether the technological-explosion premise does any real work in the story — and then tell me straight where the mapping breaks and where the novel's own events contradict the thesis or do work the argument can't do on its own. I'm especially interested in whether Ender's ignorance is a narrative cheat that lets the book have it both ways, or whether it's the point: that a species can run the dark-forest calculation and still be catastrophically wrong, and that rational in the game-theoretic sense and justified in the moral sense can come apart. Tell me which two or three scenes I should put on the handout (the room will mostly have read it, but years ago), and finish by saying what the novel adds to the debate that the bare argument doesn't, if anything. Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I can't use them and neither can the room — and we're only doing the first book, not the sequels, so keep it to that.
