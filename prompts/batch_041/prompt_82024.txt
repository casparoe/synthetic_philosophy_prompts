I have been trying to develop an account of constitutional deadlock that cuts across the usual presidentialism-versus-parliamentarism debate. As I read the standard debate, it turns on the form of the executive — whether it is separately elected and fixed in office, or responsible to the legislature — and it explains instability by citing the pathologies of the separately elected executive: competing claims to democratic legitimacy, rigidity of tenure, winner-take-all elections. I want to test a different cut. My claim is that the operative variables are (a) how many bodies in the system hold an independent electoral mandate, and (b) what kind of discharge path exists for a deadlock between them. Stated as claims:

1. A deadlock requires two independently mandated bodies in conflict over something that cannot simply be left undone.
2. Discharge paths come in two kinds. Automatic paths allow the deadlock to persist without anyone acting — a failed bill is simply a failed bill, and the status quo holds. Discretionary paths require an actor to choose to end the deadlock; call that actor the tiebreaker.
3. Automatic paths fail when the contested item is money. A government that cannot be funded cannot govern, so the status quo is not stable and the deadlock cannot be left alone.
4. Where supply can be blocked and no automatic path exists, the deadlock will be resolved — if it is resolved at all — by a discretionary tiebreaker.
5. A tiebreaker's authority rests on its distance from the electoral game, but exercising the power necessarily makes it a player. Discretionary tiebreaking is therefore self-undermining: the more decisive the intervention, the more it politicizes the office that made it.

The case I want to run through this is the Australian constitutional crisis of 1975. Here it is as I understand it; if I have a detail wrong, flag it and move on, since I do not want the analysis to collapse into a fact-check. Australia is a Westminster parliamentary system: the government is responsible to the House of Representatives, and the Governor-General is the Queen's representative, appointed on the advice of the Australian prime minister. But the Senate is also directly elected and holds the formal power to block money bills. In 1975 the Senate deferred the budget bills in order to force the Whitlam government to an election; the opposition, led by Malcolm Fraser, had made the blocking of supply its strategy. Whitlam refused either to resign or to advise a dissolution, insisting that the government's mandate came from the House. The Governor-General, Sir John Kerr, dismissed Whitlam, commissioned Fraser as a caretaker prime minister, and Fraser obtained supply and then a double dissolution; the ensuing election was won by the opposition. The Speaker of the House appealed to the Queen, who declined to intervene. The episode has been contested ever since, and the Governor-General's reserve powers and the Senate's power over supply remain live constitutional questions in Australia.
