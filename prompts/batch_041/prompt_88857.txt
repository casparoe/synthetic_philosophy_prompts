I run the ops for a small community "library of things" in a town of about 30,000 — we lend tools, run a repair café, have a shared garden plot, a community fridge, and a little fleet of cargo bikes. We've got around 250 members, two part-time staff, and a volunteer board. Lately every monthly meeting turns into alot of the same arguments: the 3D printer in our makerspace is always tied up by the same four guys, the garden's irrigation timer gets over-ridden so half the beds get soaked and the rest dry out, the community fridge is either empty or full of stuff nobody wants, and the cargo bikes are never where they're supposed to be. My board wants me to write up a procedure for deciding when we should step in with rules — reservations, time limits, fees, quotas, maybe even kicking someone out — and when we should just add capacity or leave it to people to work out. I've read a bit about the tragedy of the commons and public goods, and a friend who studies environmental governance said the key questions are whether the resource is rivalrous and how hard it is to exclude people, but I can't turn that into something I can actually use at a meeting. I think the fridge might be more of a public good and the printer more of a commons, but I'm not sure if that changes what I should do. I don't want a reading list or citations, I just need a procedure.

Can you write me a decision procedure I can actually run? I'm thinking: a sequence of questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says 'no new rule' or 'new rule needed,' and where it hands the decision to the board because it's really a values call about fairness or who belongs. It has to be usable by a volunteer coordinator who's never taken a poli-sci class, and it should keep the distinctions that actually change the answer — like whether the thing is rivalrous, whether exclusion is feasible, whether users are identifiable, whether the resource is a public good on top of a common-pool resource — and drop the ones that don't. Please don't just tell me to 'use the design principles' — I need the actual questions to ask, in order. I've got maybe 20 minutes per case, any fee or exclusion has to be approved by the board, and we can't afford a lawyer. Also, alot of our members are students or renters who move away every year, so the usual 'we'll all see each other again' enforcement mechanism is kinda weak. Then run it on three cases: the 3D printer, the garden irrigation, and the community fridge. At least one of them should come out in a way I won't like — I'd rather find out now than at the meeting. And please say what the procedure can't do, or where it will misfire.
