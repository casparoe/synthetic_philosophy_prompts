Im a novelist — three books, none of them philosophical, which will become obvious in a second — and I'm writing a chapter set in a near-future pain-and-sleep clinic that has gone all-in on a theory I keep seeing philosophers fight over: the one that says phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, that there's no actual felt quality to experience, just the brain's false report that there is one. I want to drop in, as an in-world artifact, the clinic's patient welcome leaflet / FAQ, deadpan, maybe 600 to 700 words, laid out like a real leaflet with numbered questions and answers, plus a little boxed note for relatives.

The joke has to come from getting the theory right. It should nail the distinctions the illusionists actually insist on: that they aren't denying experience, only denying that experience has phenomenal properties; that the real puzzle is why we're so sure there's a hard problem in the first place (the meta-problem, or whatever they call it); that introspection is itself a representation and representations can misrepresent; that the illusion is a real phenomenon to be explained and not something anyone's saying doesn't happen. And it should land the objections people make to all that — that it just changes the subject, that it denies the blindingly obvious, that it's a verbal dispute dressed up as a discovery, that you can't even state it without presupposing what it denies — without ever naming any of those objections out loud. An illusionist should read it and wince with recognition, not feel strawmanned.

The trick I'm after, and the reason it gets wierd: the leaflet keeps insisting that nothing practical changes, that your pain is still bad and still gets treated, while its own wording keeps accidentally implying that everything changes. Keep it flat and institutional, like a real hospital leaflet, no winking, no cartoonish bits. Someone who's never read a word of this stuff should still find it funny and slightly unsettling.
