I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy working on epistemic logic, and I am the instructor of record for a graduate seminar on knowledge and belief. I am midway through grading the midterm and would like an independent read on one student's answer before I return the papers.

The question on the exam read as follows:

(a) In a standard possible-worlds model of knowledge — a set of worlds W, an accessibility relation R, and the clause that a subject knows φ at w just in case φ holds at every world v such that wRv — show how the model generates the problem of logical omniscience. Be precise about which closure properties the model forces and which it does not.

(b) Describe one standard response to the problem and say whether it addresses the difficulty.

The student's answer was:

"(a) Take a model with a set of worlds W, an accessibility relation R, and a valuation. The subject knows φ at w just in case φ is true at every world accessible from w. For knowledge, as opposed to mere belief, we require R to be reflexive, so that whatever is known is in fact true.

Now take any logical truth φ. Since φ is true at every world in W, it is true at every world accessible from w, and so the subject knows φ. The agent therefore knows every logical truth. The model also validates the K axiom, so that from Kφ and K(φ→ψ) we may infer Kψ, and it validates necessitation, which lets us infer Kφ from the theoremhood of φ. Putting these together: whenever φ→ψ is a logical truth, we get K(φ→ψ) by necessitation, and then Kψ from Kφ. So the subject's knowledge is closed under logical consequence, and she knows every logical truth. That is the problem, since real reasoners are neither closed under consequence nor in possession of all the logical truths they would need in order to be so closed.

The difficulty is usually stated as one about closure, but I think the deeper issue is the reflexivity of the accessibility relation. Because R is reflexive, every world is accessible from itself, and the model in effect identifies knowledge with truth: the subject knows every proposition that is true, including facts about the distant future and about events she has no way of having encountered. That is the consequence the problem is really about.

(b) One standard response is the awareness approach. It distinguishes a subject's implicit knowledge — what she is committed to, given the model — from her explicit knowledge, and adds a separate component recording which propositions she is aware of; explicit knowledge is then implicit knowledge that falls within her awareness. A second response, impossible-worlds semantics, enlarges the space of worlds so that there are worlds at which logical truths fail; since a logical truth need not hold at every world, the subject need not know it.

In my view the cleanest fix is to drop the K axiom and to replace the reflexive accessibility relation with a non-reflexive one. Once K is gone, knowledge is no longer closed under material implication; and once reflexivity is gone, the subject no longer automatically knows every logical truth, since we can no longer derive K(φ→ψ) from the theoremhood of φ→ψ. This leaves us with a model much closer to the epistemic situation of finite reasoners."
