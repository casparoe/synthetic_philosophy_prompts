I need a decision problem built to order. Its purpose is to test a claim about the independence axiom — namely, that the Allais-style patterns reveal something about how options are described rather than about probability as such — so the case is meant to work as a counterexample to the thesis that any adequate theory must assign an option its value as a function of the reduced distribution over prizes. Because it is a counterexample, its verdicts must be generated from the models specified below and from explicit numbers, not stipulated: if the chooser's pattern has to be assumed rather than derived, the case has failed.

Take the following as given; you need not attribute any of it to a source.

- A lottery is a list of prizes with probabilities summing to one. Its reduced distribution is the one-stage distribution over prizes obtained by collapsing all stages and sub-events.
- Independence: for any lotteries A, B, C and any p in (0, 1], A is preferred to B if and only if the mixture that yields A with probability p and C with probability 1 − p is preferred to the mixture that yields B with probability p and C with probability 1 − p.
- A distributional model is any model that assigns an option a value determined solely by that option's reduced distribution over prizes. Expected utility and cumulative (rank-dependent) probability weighting are both distributional in this sense.
- A branch-weighting model assigns an option the value obtained by summing w(p_i)·u(x_i) over the branches as they are described, where u is a utility over money, w is a strictly increasing function with w(0) = 0 and w(1) = 1, and the weights are not renormalised to sum to one.

Construct the following case. Three options, each a monetary gamble, all available to one chooser at the same time, all with realistic amounts (hundreds of dollars at most) and realistic framings (raffles, side bets, bonus schemes — not urns of coloured balls). Two of the three must have exactly the same reduced distribution over prizes and differ only in how one prize's probability is partitioned into named sub-events: same prize, same total probability, only the number of separately described sub-events differs, and the two descriptions must be equally natural — two sources of the same prize, say, not one plain description and one artificially gerrymandered one. The chooser is fully informed: she knows the two options have the same reduced distribution and can compute it, and the case must not depend on her being ignorant, distracted, or deceived. The third option is an ordinary single-branch gamble. Present the case itself in one paragraph, with a table of prizes and probabilities alongside it.

The case must satisfy three requirements. First, expected utility must rank the two description-identical options equally, and so must every distributional model, whatever its utility or weighting function. Second, under the branch-weighting model, instantiated with an explicit u and an explicit w and worked through arithmetically, the third option must fall strictly between the two description-identical options, so that the branch-weighting chooser's ranking of the three options, as described, constitutes a strict cycle. Show the numbers. Third, state what property of w the cycle requires, whether that property holds for the shapes of w usually taken to describe how people weight probabilities, and what difference it would make if the weights were renormalised to sum to one.

Then the analysis. Give the verdict of expected utility, of a cumulative distributional model of your choice, and of your branch-weighting model, for each of the three options, and explain what drives each verdict. Identify precisely what the branch-weighting chooser violates here — independence, reduction of compound lotteries, or something weaker, such as a requirement that an option's value not change when one event is split into sub-events whose probabilities sum to its own — and justify your choice. State which feature of the case is doing the work, and what becomes of the case when that feature is removed: what happens if the partition is collapsed, and what happens if the third option is a sure amount rather than a gamble. Say exactly what the cycle is a cycle in — preferences over options or preferences over descriptions — and whether that distinction bears on whether the case is a counterexample to anything.

As a cross-check, say whether your branch-weighting model, with the same u and w, can reproduce the two standard Allais-style choice pairs, with numbers, and if it cannot, what would have to change. Pair 1: a sure $1,000,000, against a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000 and a 1% chance of nothing; Pair 2: an 11% chance of $1,000,000 and an 89% chance of nothing, against a 10% chance of $5,000,000 and a 90% chance of nothing. Most people take the sure million in Pair 1 and the ten-percent shot at five million in Pair 2. Pair 3: an 80% chance of $4,000 and a 20% chance of nothing, against a sure $3,000; Pair 4: a 20% chance of $4,000 and an 80% chance of nothing, against a 25% chance of $3,000 and a 75% chance of nothing. Most people take the sure $3,000 in Pair 3 and the twenty-percent shot at $4,000 in Pair 4.

Finally, the defence. How would a sharp defender of expected utility attack your case? They will want to know why the cycle is not simply a mistake on the chooser's part, or a preference over descriptions rather than over outcomes, and they will press on whether the two description-identical options really are identical in every respect the chooser could care about. Give their strongest move and your reply to it. Throughout, state how confident you are in each of the main claims you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
