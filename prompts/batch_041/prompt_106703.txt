Hey — I'm a third-year student on the decision theory module, and I also run the little student interview thing the department does before the visiting speaker's talk. Next week's guest is someone defending the updateless/timeless answer to counterfactual mugging, i.e. the line that you should pay up when Omega comes asking. I've got about forty minutes with him and I don't want to burn it on him just restating the standard story for the tenth time. Could you draft me 8-10 interview questions, each with a follow-up that's keyed to the answer he's most likely to give? Press where it actually hurts, not the generic stuff.

A few things I definately want in there, and I've cooked up some variants of my own that I want to put to him, so please build questions around these rather than the textbook versions:

1. My "predictor-free" mugging. Strip out Omega entirely. No predictor, no simulation, no reading you off. Instead a random process before you were born fixes both (a) whether your house gets termites and (b) a fact about your brain that makes you the sort of person who'd hand over the money. So the correlation's there, but nothing is predicting you and the termite outcome doesn't depend on your choice in any way. Does his answer flip? Follow-up: if he says the correlation is all that matters, why isn't he then also paying in every ordinary case where your character and the outcome share a common cause — and if he doesn't, what's the principled difference that stops his view collapsing into the evidential-decision-theory money pump?

2. The XOR blackmail case (Omega sends you a letter saying he's sent it iff your house is infested XOR you pay him). Ask what his view prescribes and whether the usual diagnosis — that this is just EDT being money-pumped while CDT gets it right — is fair, or whether updatelessness gets dragged under too. Follow-up: he'll almost certainly say the difference is that real mugging has counterfactual dependence and this case doesn't, so press on exactly where that dependence lives once the predictor is perfect. It looks like a definitional/logical dependence rather than a causal one, and I want him to have to say which kind he needs.

3. The nature of the coin. In one version the coin is really just a determinate fact you don't know (like a distant digit of pi). In another it's a genuinely indeterministic quantum measurement made after your decision, so at decision time there's no fact of the matter about how it came up. Ask which version his argument actually needs. Follow-up: if he needs the deterministic one, does the whole thing become a claim about logical uncertainty rather than about decisions, and does he accept that framing?

4. A fallible predictor with known accuracy p. At what p does the answer flip, does it flip smoothly, and is p = 1 special (a discontinuity)? Follow-up: he'll probably say that for any p above a half you still pay if the prize is big enough — so ask whether the argument is then just ordinary expected-value reasoning that even a causal decision theorist would accept once you've written the correlation down, and if so why the case is philosophically interesting at all.

5. Embedded version. Make the predictor a physical agent inside the same universe, running a simulation of you that costs it resources and can mispredict because of computational limits. Does the updateless argument survive when prediction is an embedded, resource-bounded process instead of an idealised demon? Follow-up: he'll say the idealisation is fine for a thought experiment, so ask whether the idealisation is doing any load-bearing work that would quietly vanish for realistic agents.

6. Self-locating version. The message is addressed to whichever copy of you receives it, and you're uncertain which copy you are. Does that uncertainty move the answer, and in which direction?

7. Precommitment. Everyone already agrees you should precommit if you can. Ask him to state exactly what updatelessness adds beyond that, and what an agent who genuinely can't precommit should do at the moment of asking. Follow-up: if he says "choose the policy you'd have wanted to have chosen", ask what the actual decision procedure is when the policy choice and the act happen at different times and you can't bind yourself.

8. The stopping rule. If he pays in the mugging and (if he does) in XOR blackmail, where does it stop? Is there a version of the argument that obliges you to pay anyone who credibly claims to have simulated you? Make him state a principled stopping rule rather than just saying each case is judged on its merits.

Try to keep each question short enough to actually say out loud in an interview, and make the follow-ups genuinely depend on the reply he's most likely to give rather than being a second unrelated question. Finish with a last line that reads exactly: Verdict: <number>, where the number is the question you reckon he's least likely to have a ready answer to.
