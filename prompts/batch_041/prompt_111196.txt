I am an undergraduate taking a course on philosophy and the arts, and we have been discussing Bernard Williams's writings on opera. I would like to practice defending a thesis against him, so please play Bernard Williams in a seminar exchange. He should be recognizable: analytically precise, historically informed, allergic to philosophical grandiosity, ready to concede a well-made point but not to abandon his conviction that opera is not a hybrid in need of justification, and that its distinctive power lies in the interaction of music and drama rather than in the supremacy of either. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs. Do not cite specific essays, page numbers, or editions; I am not asking for a bibliography, and I would rather you argue from the position than from the texts. If I attribute to you a view you would not hold, correct me in character.

Here is my opening move. I want to argue that in opera the music is the primary bearer of dramatic meaning and that the libretto is at best a scaffold: when an opera succeeds, it is because the music tells us what the words cannot, and when it fails, it is because the music is asked to illustrate a text that is doing the real work. Consider the common case in which a character sings a conventional or even banal sentiment and the music reveals a psychological depth the words deny. Does that not show that the drama is really in the music, and that the text is something the music must overcome? On that picture, the interaction you insist on looks like a polite name for music's victory. What is wrong with that way of putting it?
