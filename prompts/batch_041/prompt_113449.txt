I am writing the central section of an essay on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I want to build that section around a reading of Kurosawa's Rashomon rather than around a stock example, so I need a reading that takes the film and the paradox equally seriously, and that does not simply use one to illustrate the other.

Let me set out the paradox as I intend to use it. If every truth is knowable — for any truth p, it is possible that someone should know that p — then, granted that knowledge is factive and that knowing a conjunction requires knowing each of its conjuncts, every truth is in fact known. Suppose instead that some truth went unknown; the proposition that p is true and that no one knows it would then be true, and it could not be known, since knowing it would require knowing p, which would make its second half false. An unknown truth therefore implies an unknowable one, and, more awkwardly still, no one could ever know, of any particular truth, that it was the one that would never be known. My reading of the upshot is that a world containing unknowable truths is not one that anyone inside it could identify as such.

The film is the byword for the opposite: a rape and a killing in a grove, four mutually incompatible accounts of them, and three men sheltering from the rain at a ruined gate who cannot reconcile what they have heard. The received view is that the film shows the truth to lie beyond anyone's reach. My hunch is that the paradox makes that view unavailable, and that the film, knowingly or not, does something else — that it shows truth being withheld rather than being unknowable, which is why it matters that the frame story gives us a woodcutter who eventually admits he saw the killing and lied to stay out of trouble.

What I want from you is this. First, which of three things the film actually dramatises: that there is no single fact of the matter, that there is a fact but it is unreachable in principle, or that there is a fact and someone in the film's world knows it and will not tell; and why the paradox leaves the film only the first and the third. Second, whether the film appears to know which of the three it occupies, and where it betrays that it does not. Third, where the reading strains and where I should concede: if the honest verdict is that the film's real interest is in the ethics of testimony — who is permitted to give an account, whose account is believed, what telling the truth costs the teller — rather than in the metaphysics of knowledge, then say so plainly, tell me which scenes carry that weight, and say what the film contributes to the debate that the paradox by itself does not.

Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way you would put it in a letter.
