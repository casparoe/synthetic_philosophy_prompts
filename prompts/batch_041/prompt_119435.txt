I'm trying to put together an advesarial collaboration doc and could use your help drafting it. The two camps have been going at it about corruption — clientelism, gift-giving to officials, and whether "corruption" as a category is basically a Western export.

The Universalist: corruption is just the misuse of an entrusted public role for private benefit. That's a functional definition, it travels, and if a practice fits it, it's corrupt whether or not the locals have a word for it — local acceptance doesn't launder it either, since people can be socialized into accepting stuff that's still wrong. Patronage, clientelism, gifts that buy favor: corrupt, period.

The Contextualist: corruption isn't a thin functional term, it's a thick one — it doesn't just describe, it evaluates, and the evaluation is baked right into the description. Applying it presupposes a whole background: a sharp split between public role and private person, and a norm of impersonal impartiality. Where that background is missing or shaped differently, the concept doesn't cleanly apply, and slapping it on misdescribes what's actually happening (think a gift that constitutes a relationship rather than a bribe) — and it's a bit of cultural imperialism to boot.

I want the document the two of them could both put their names to. So:

- a numbered list of claims they can both sign (about what corruption-talk does, about the cases, about method)
- the real disagreements, each phrased so the side that holds it would accept the wording — no strawmen
- the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling it would actually move at least one of them. I think one is whether "misuse of an entrusted role" already smuggles in a public/private distinction, and another is whether the impartiality norm is a moral truth or just a local ethical form
- for each crux, a case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance

Two things I want you to stress-test the doc against. First, this case: a society where the patron-client bond is itself the recongized form of political accountability, so the "corrupt" act is the officeholder favoring a stranger over their own client — corruption ends up with the opposite valence from ours. Does that break the universalist definition, or does it just relocate where the power was entrusted? Second, whether part of the disagreement is really verbal — two different concepts both wearing the word "corruption."

Format: agreed-claims numbered, then a disagreements table (claim / universalist wording / contextualist wording / what would actually move each side). And at the end, tell me honestly whether the whole thing just collapses into it-depends-what-you-mean-by-corruption, or whether there's a real disagreement left underneath.
