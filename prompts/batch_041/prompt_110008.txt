Ok so a friend in my reading group dropped this on me and I cant stop chewing on it. He says it shows the Church-Turing thesis is either empty or else nobody knows what kind of claim it even is. Here's his argument, tidied up a bit:

(1) Every claim is one of three things: a definition (a stipulation about how to use a word), a mathematical theorem, or an empirical hypothesis.
(2) The CTT isn't a theorem. The thesis says that anything computable by an effective procedure -- any finite, mechanical, pencil-and-paper method -- is computable by a Turing machine. But "effective procedure" is an informal, intuitive notion; you can only gesture at it. You don't prove theorems about gestures, you prove them about formal objects.
(3) It isn't an empirical hypothesis either. Ask what would count as evidence against it and you get nothing. Someone proposes a counterexample: here's a function no Turing machine can compute, and here's a method that computes it. But any method you can actually run is a finite mechanical procedure, which is exactly what "effective" means. So either the method is effective, in which case by the thesis it's Turing computable and there's no counterexample, or it isn't effective and there's still no counterexample. No possible observation could shake it, and a claim that nothing could disconfirm isn't a scientific hypothesis.
(4) So it has to be a definition. "Effectively computable" just means "Turing computable."
(5) But definitions aren't true or false, nobody discovers them, and they don't constrain the world in any way.
(6) Which is absurd, because we treat the CTT as a real discovery, one that definately tells us something about which physical processes can exist at all. And it's not like one guy's convention: Turing machines, the lambda calculus, recursive functions -- all these very different setups turned out to carve out exactly the same class of functions. That convergence feels like evidence for something, not like a bunch of people independently landing on the same notation.

So which step do I give up? I'd rather keep (6); I really do think the thesis says something. But (1) looks like common sense, (2) looks obviously right, (3) is the one I keep staring at, and (4) and (5) seem to follow from it. If I have to sacrifice one, what does it actually cost me? And is the argument quietly leaning on something it shouldn't -- because it also bugs me that the same shape of reasoning would make "temperature is the mean kinetic energy of the molecules" come out as a mere stipulation, and that can't be right.
