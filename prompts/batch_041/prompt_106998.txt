I have been trying to state, for myself, the conditions under which it is rational to accept a proposition — where accepting means taking it as a premise and relying on it in reasoning and action, not merely assigning it a high probability. I keep arriving at a rough rule and then finding cases that embarrass it, and I would like you to tell me what principle, or small family of principles, actually fits my verdicts, and which verdict I should abandon if none does.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (i) In a fair lottery with a thousand tickets and one winner, I think it is rational, before the draw, for me to accept of any particular ticket that it will lose; I would gladly take an even-money bet that ticket #1 loses. (ii) I also accept that exactly one ticket will win — that is part of the setup. (iii) I do not accept that every ticket will lose, since I know that is false. (iv) Now consider a book of three hundred claims, each independently checked and each with a probability of roughly 0.99 of being true; I think it is rational for the author to accept each of the three hundred claims individually. (v) I also think it is rational for the author to accept that at least one of the three hundred is false — that modest disclaimer seems to me not merely permissible but epistemically admirable. (vi) But I do not think the author may accept that all three hundred are true. (vii) By contrast, when I accept that it rained overnight and accept that the street is wet, I also accept their conjunction; deductive closure seems plainly right for claims like these, and I use the conjunction as a premise without hesitation. (viii) Finally, stakes matter to me: if the very same lottery ticket, at the very same 0.999 probability, were the thing I had to stake my child's life on, I would not accept that it loses — not in the sense of relying on it — even though my credence would be unchanged.

My tentative rule is that one may accept a proposition just in case its probability exceeds some threshold, and that the threshold rises with the stakes of being wrong. But I can already see this rule straining: it tells me to accept each lottery proposition in (i) and each book claim in (iv) while forbidding the conjunctions in (iii) and (vi), which sits badly with the closure I endorsed in (vii), since closure would let me assemble (i) into the very conjunction I reject in (iii). And (viii) seems to demand that the threshold be context-dependent in a way I cannot yet state. Please tell me what principle or principles best capture these verdicts; state them precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; say what they predict for cases I have not listed — for instance, whether I may accept the conjunction of two lottery propositions each at 0.999, or whether the author may assert the conjunction of her book; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, (i) through (viii), the best principle cannot keep, and why that verdict is the one that should go. I am less interested in being told that this is a known paradox than in seeing the principle worked out and stress-tested.
