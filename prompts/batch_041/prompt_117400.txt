My mate Priya and I have been going at Quine's gavagai business for about three weeks solid now — it started when she made me read the jungle linguist bit before a seminar, and it has somehow become the main topic of conversation in our flat — and the infuriating thing is that we agree about the case itself and disagree about what it shows. She reckons the indeterminacy only looks dramatic because Quine had already decided the linguist's sole evidence is the natives' yes and no noises, and that the moment you let the linguist ask about the villager's eyes and brain and what it's actually attending to, the whole rabbit-versus-rabbit-stages problem evaporates. I think that's cheating, because the question was never about which evidence we happen to have, it was about whether there's a fact of the matter at all. Our friend Tom meanwhile insists it's just the quus thing from Kripke's Wittgenstein wearing a pith helmet, which I don't think is fair but can't quite say why.

Anyway, I've been volunteered to run the reading group, and I'd rather turn this into a proper sorting instrument than another shouting match, so please build me a decision tree. It should start from a commitment like: I accept that the gavagai case is a genuine puzzle, i.e. that a linguist could have all the assent and dissent data in the world and still be torn between a manual on which gavagai picks out rabbits and one on which it picks out temporal stages of rabbits. Every branch node should be a question someone can answer by thinking about a concrete case or a concrete claim, not a question with the name of a position baked into it. So, for instance: does the indeterminacy turn up when you translate your own language, or only when you go abroad? Is it a fact about words (which thing a term latches onto) or about whole sentences and translation manuals? If two rival manuals agree on every sentence, is anything left over? Is the linguist's evidence exhausted by assent and dissent, or does it include what the native can perceive and discriminate? And is the underdetermination merely epistemic — cleverer questions would settle it — or is there really no fact of the matter? Six to eight nodes, five to seven leaves, each leaf carrying the usual name where there is one (Quine's own line, Davidson's benign version, the it's-just-behaviourism-in-disguise line, Kripkenstein-style meaning scepticism, the anti-indeterminacy line that perceptual content pins reference down, and a deflationary view that translation is a practical art rather than a fact-stating business), or marked unnamed if nobody actually defends it. Where the tree genuinely can't seperate two positions, say so instead of papering over it, and where a combination of answers leaves a gap with no occupant, tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Two extra things I want in there, because they're what came up in the argument and I suspect they're doing most of the work. First, swap the linguist for a machine translation system trained on nothing but the villagers' assent and dissent behaviour, which reproduces all the right behaviour; then a second version where we can also look inside the thing and find a stable internal structure that lines up with rabbit-stages rather than rabbits (or the other way round). Does either version settle anything? Tom says the second one does; Priya says neither does, since an internal structure is just one more manual; and I genuinely can't tell whether we're now arguing about translation or about what a representation is supposed to be. Second, a node about the child raised in both villages at once — I half think indeterminacy can't survive a bilingual, and half think it survives absolutely everything.
