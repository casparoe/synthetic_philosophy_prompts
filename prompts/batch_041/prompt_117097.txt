My department has just voted to replace our Western civilization requirement with a two-semester global history sequence, and I have been handed the job of making it cohere. I have a year before the first offering. I do not need a reading list; I need a defended judgment about which unresolved conceptual questions in the Eurocentrism debate are load-bearing for a design like this, and in what order I should settle them. A sequence with visible seams that I can defend is worth more to me than a seamless one with a hidden center.

Two hunches I would like tested rather than assumed. First, that Eurocentrism lives less in which societies appear than in the shape of the story — a single timeline, a causal grammar of development, a climax — so that adding content without changing the form changes little. Second, that the global turn carries a bias of its own, favoring societies that left traces of long-distance exchange, so that history from everywhere quietly becomes history from everywhere that was in touch.

So which open questions matter most? The ones I keep circling are whether periodization can be salvaged at all, or whether a global sequence has to be stitched from regional timelines that never fully merge; whether categories such as religion, state, and economy can be carried across the whole world without doing violence to the cases; and whether a single narrative can hold more than one temporality — societies that are not behind but on different clocks — without flattening them into one now. Rank these, and the ones I have missed: which are upstream of which, which are tractable in a year of reading, which deserve to be deprioritized because settling them would not change the syllabus, and what would move a question up or down the list.
