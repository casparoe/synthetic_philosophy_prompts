Next month I will give the toast at the fortieth-anniversary dinner of two old friends, and I want it to rest on a philosophical account of value rather than on warm feeling. The account is G. E. Moore's, in the final chapter of Principia Ethica: his considered answer to the question of what is good in itself is that the greatest intrinsic goods we know of are certain pleasures of human affection and the enjoyment of beautiful objects, and that the best conceivable whole is one that combines the two; and his principle of organic unities holds that the intrinsic value of a whole is neither identical with nor proportional to the sum of the values of its parts. I want both ideas to do structural work in the toast—to determine what can and cannot be said—rather than to be cited and then set aside.

The occasion supplies the material. The couple have been married forty years. They raised two children, weathered a long illness, survived a stretch of estrangement in the middle years, buried a parent, and made together a garden that has quietly become a local landmark. I want the toast to claim, and to make the claim good, that their marriage is valuable in itself and not merely as a means to their happiness, and that its worth cannot be computed by adding up the good years and subtracting the bad ones. It should bring in Moore's second good—the enjoyment of beautiful things—by tying their shared devotion to the garden to their affection for one another, and it should put his method of isolation to use: invite the room to imagine the marriage with all of its effects on other people stripped away, and to see whether anything worth valuing remains. Moore's own thought, that a beautiful thing is good even if no one is there to enjoy it, is available here if it helps.

The room is mixed, and this is the hard part. Perhaps a third of the guests are philosophers; several of them know Principia Ethica well and will wince at any misstatement of Moore, at any whiff of hedonism, and at any lapse into the naturalistic fallacy—so the toast must not say that the marriage is good because it is natural, or because it produces happiness, or because it is what people are supposed to do. The rest are family and neighbors who have never heard of Moore and will not sit still for a lecture. The toast should run about four minutes spoken, should move the room rather than instruct it, and should carry the family along rather than leaving them behind.

Write the toast as finished prose that I can deliver as it stands.
