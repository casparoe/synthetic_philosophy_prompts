I'm a graduate student in a philosophy program, and my two closest friends in the department have been circling the same argument since a seminar on aesthetics last fall. She is a pessimist about aesthetic testimony: her view, roughly, is that no matter how trustworthy the person speaking, you cannot come to know that a painting is beautiful, or that a novel is sentimental, or that a piece of music is graceful, merely by being told; you have to encounter the work yourself, and a belief that rests only on someone else's say-so is defective in a way that matters. He is an optimist: he holds that this is a romantic myth, that testimony transmits justification in aesthetics just as it does in science or history, and that his friend is dressing up a taste for firsthand experience as an epistemological principle. They have now had this argument a dozen times, always landing in the same place — he says that his friend told him a movie was good and he believed her, so testimony plainly works; she says that this is not aesthetic testimony at all — and I would like to see what happens if they are forced to take each other seriously for the length of one real conversation.

Please write the dialogue they would have. Neither may be a strawman: the pessimist should be given the strongest version of her position, the optimist should press her where she is genuinely vulnerable rather than collecting easy points, and each should answer the other's actual formulation rather than sliding past it. I especially want them to get past the opening skirmish and reach a precise disagreement. Two things I would like them to fight about. First, whether the trouble with a belief held on aesthetic testimony is epistemic — she says you lack justification for it — or merely practical, namely that you hold the belief but can do nothing with it, cannot appreciate the work, and cannot answer a challenge except by citing your informant; he will want to know why that is not equally true of testimony in the sciences. Second, whether her view commits her to saying that someone who cannot experience a work at all — a listener who is deaf and is told about a symphony, say — can never know that the symphony is beautiful, and if so whether she accepts that consequence or refines her position to avoid it. I would also like them to say something about whether aesthetic testimony differs from moral testimony, since a parallel worry is often raised there, and to consider the case of a critic who describes a work in such loving detail that you feel you can almost see it, which he will treat as testimony doing its job and she will treat as something else masquerading as testimony.

Let the exchange run long enough to develop a real back-and-forth, with each turn responding to the last, and let it end in a sharply stated disagreement rather than a resolution or a polite truce. Please write in American English, and don't worry about citing sources or supplying a reading list — I care about the argument, not the bibliography.
