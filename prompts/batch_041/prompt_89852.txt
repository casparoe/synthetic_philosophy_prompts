So I'm writing this novel where two of my main characters — a statistician and an ER doctor — spend basically the whole book arguing about whether a certain test result means the patient is in trouble. I wanted them both to be smart and honest, no bad faith, no secret agenda, no one secretly knows something they're not saying. Just two careful people looking at the same thing and landing in different places, and the reader has to sit with that.

Then a friend of mine who does econ tells me about this Aumann agreement theorem, and he says it proves that if two people are rational and each knows the other's opinion, they have to end up agreeing — that two rational people staying in disagreement is literally impossible. Which is a problem, because two rational people staying in disagreement is kind of my entire book.

So do I have to make one of them irrational or lying? Or is the honest fix to have them realize by the end that they actually agree, and the whole argument was just a misunderstanding? That feels like it would gut the story, and it also feels false to life — I know plenty of smart, careful people who look at the same evidence and don't come around. How do writers who care about this stuff handle it? Is there some way for two characters to be genuinely rational and still not agree, or is Aumann right and I should just accept that one of them has to be fooling himself? I'd rather not make a character dumb just to keep my plot going, but I also don't want to write something a mathematician would laugh at.
