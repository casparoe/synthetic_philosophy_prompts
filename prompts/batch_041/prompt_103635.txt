Eighteen months ago I left a policy job to write a book about a factory occupation that happened in the mid-1970s in the town where I grew up, and I have spent much of that time discovering that the field I am writing into is split by a dispute that no one seems able to settle. The split is easy to describe and, so far as I can tell, has not moved in decades. On one side are historians for whom the occupation is an episode in the restructuring of an industry: it is legible in company ledgers, ministry files, union bureaucracy records, and the slow movement of capital, and what needs explaining is the structure that made the occupation possible and the outcome it produced. On the other are historians for whom it is a rupture in the lives of the people who did it: legible in testimony, rumor, songs, and the meanings participants gave their own action, and what needs explaining is how people without power nonetheless acted. Each side treats the other's central evidence as more or less beside the point. The structuralists say retrospective memory is construction; the others say the administrative record is the voice of the powerful and documents only the categories the powerful already had.

What I want from you is not a verdict on which side is right but a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that it is at least three questions wearing one name: whose lives are the object of history, at what scale explanation succeeds, and whether the historian's stance is that of analyst or advocate. A fourth possibility I am less sure about is that the two sides mean different things by explanation itself — one reconstructing a mechanism, the other reconstructing a meaning — in which case they may never have been contradicting each other. A fifth is that the dispute is now largely institutional, kept alive by departments, journals, and hiring lines rather than by any live intellectual stake, and that the participants rarely have to answer one another's strongest objections.

Test these against what each side actually says, in its strongest form rather than its caricature, and tell me which parts of the disagreement are substantive, which are verbal, and which are disguised empirical disputes. I would particularly like to know whether the two positions differ in what they predict about evidence: if I found that the occupiers were executing a script handed down by a national organization, would that count against the history-from-below account, or would it be absorbed by it? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a change of question, a testable claim, a formal result of some kind — or argue that no progress should be expected here and say why. Finally, since I have to write this book either way, I want an honest answer about whether a project like mine can genuinely avoid taking a side, or whether it is committed to one the moment it chooses its sources. If the impasse turns out to be real but harmless, say that too.
