A guy in my book club — retired doctor, reads a ton of popular science — went off on a whole thing about heritability last week and I can't stop chewing on it. Here's basically what he said, as close as I can remember:

"Heritability is the most misread number in science. It's not about you as an individual, it's about a population. When someone says the heritability of IQ is 0.7, they don't mean 70% of your IQ came from your genes — they mean 70% of the differences between people in that group trace back to genetic differences. It's a ratio: genetic variance divided by total variance. And it says nothing about whether a trait can change. Lots of highly heritable things, like certain inherited metabolic diseases, are completely treatable, because heritability is only ever measured against the range of environments people happen to be in. But the part everyone misses is this: as we make society more equal — same schools, same food, same healthcare — heritability should go down, because we're squeezing out the environmental differences. So a fairer world literally means genes matter less. And the reverse is the giveaway: when a trait is strongly heritable inside a group, that's good evidence the differences between groups are genetic too. That's why the stubborn gaps in things like educational attainment between social groups look mostly baked into the genes."

I know "heritability isn't destiny" is the standard caveat and he said that too, so it's not like he's a crank. But something is bugging me and I can't find the seam. Which bits would an actual geneticist nod along to, which are subtly off, and what specifically is wrong with the wrong ones? I'm fairly sure the group-gap thing is dodgy, but honestly the "fairer world means genes matter less" bit sounds like it should be true and I can't see why it wouldn't be. Would love it seperated out.
