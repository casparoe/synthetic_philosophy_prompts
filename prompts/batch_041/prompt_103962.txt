I have come around to the organicist answer to the special composition question: composition occurs only when the parts are caught up in the life of a single organism, so that the only composite material objects are living things. On this view the cat is one thing, the cat together with the mat it sits on is not, and a heap of sand, however it is arranged, never adds up to an object over and above its grains. I would like a careful map of what would count against this position — not the textbook objections, which I already know, but the defeaters that a sympathetic defender ought to feel the force of.

Work through the following and tell me which are genuinely damaging and which are not. First, the marginal cases at the edge of life, and whether they help or embarrass the criterion: lichens, which look like one organism but are a partnership of a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium; siphonophores, which are colonies of zooids that function as a single animal; slime molds, which alternate between independent cells and a single crawling mass; biofilms; and the holobiont picture, on which a host and its microbiome together count as one biological individual. Do these pressure cases show that the organicist criterion is unstable, or can the view absorb them by leaning on a robust notion of biological individuality? Second, the non-living replicators — viruses, viroids, plasmids, prions — and whether the view is forced to say that a virus is not one thing at all. Third, the status of life itself as a predicate: does the criterion have a determinate extension, or is being caught up in a life a matter of degree, so that the view inherits all the vagueness of biology's own demarcation problem? Fourth, the problem of the many as it recurs for organisms: the cat and its many candidate parts, and whether the organicist has any better answer here than the universalist. Fifth, death and suspended life: does the cat cease to be a composite object the moment it dies, and what about a frozen embryo or a dormant seed — is a whole that is temporarily not living still a whole? Sixth, whether the view predicts anything its rivals (mereological nihilism, universalism, and the various brutalist or deflationary answers) do not, or whether it merely redescribes the same facts under a biological label. Finally, the relation between this metaphysical thesis and biology's own treatment of individuality as a pragmatic or modelling choice, and whether the philosopher is being asked to legislate where the biologist sees a decision procedure.

For each defeater, say whether it is decisive, seriously awkward, or merely embarrassing, and rank the whole set from fatal to trivial. Where possible, distinguish what the position's defenders would themselves concede as counting against them from what only its critics press. Then give me a straight assessment of whether the view has been insulated against refutation by its qualifications — in particular by the loose phrase about being caught up in a life — and whether that insulation is a vice or simply the price of any restricted answer to the question. Tell me, too, which of these considerations would personally move you most if you held the view, and what single observation or argument would be most likely to make an organicist abandon it.
