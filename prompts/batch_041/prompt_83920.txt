Bit of an odd one. I sit on the consumer appeals panel at my state's department of insurance — we're the last stop for people who think their insurer stiffed them, and when we take a case we write up a recommended decision that goes to the commissioner. I have to circulate a draft before Wednesday's vote and I keep putting it off because I genuinely don't know which way to come out.

Here's the case. A woman's husband signed up with a company that runs "transfers" to the lunar settlement. You lie down in the machine here, it maps you down to the last synapse, your body is destroyed, and about forty minutes later a body is assembled at the other end and wakes up. He woke up, he knew his kids' birthdays, he called her, he's been living his life up there for two years. The policy has an exclusion: no death benefit where the insured's body was voluntarily destroyed and "a functioning continuation of the insured's mental life is established." The insurer says that's exactly this. She says the man who walked into the machine is dead and the thing on the moon is a copy that thinks it's him, and she wants the $500k. She's not a crank — she's a nurse and she's read alot about this.

Two things make it worse. First, the same company sells a seperate non-destructive scan, where you keep your original and a backup sits in a vault. We already paid a claim where a guy made a backup before heart surgery, died on the table, and the backup got switched on. Nobody on this panel thought twice about it. And we've got a pending mess where a guy made a backup and then *didn't* die, so now there's two of him and both are claiming the same pension. Second, last year we had a claimant with a degenerative condition who had his neurons swapped for prosthetics a few thousand at a time over fourteen months — same memories, same guy waking up every morning — and we treated him as the same person the whole way through. So if slow replacement is fine, what exactly is wrong with the fast kind? My colleague keeps calling this the Parfit thing and wants to just read the exclusion literally and be done in ten minutes. I think she's wrong, but I want her argument in there properly, not strawmanned.

Draft me the opinion. I need a concrete call on the widow's claim and on the two-copies pension mess, plus a rule the department can actually apply going forward — something the next panel can use without re-litigating the metaphysics from scratch. And it has to be honest about the strongest thing on the losing side. You don't need to cite cases or statutes, there's nothing on point yet anyway.
