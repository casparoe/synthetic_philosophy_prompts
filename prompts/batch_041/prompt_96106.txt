I am the clinical lead for a multidisciplinary chronic pain service across three sites. Six triage clinicians, roughly forty new referrals a week, about ten minutes per case: the clinician decides whether the patient's own account of their pain will be the primary evidence in the plan we write, or whether we require objective corroboration — imaging, a specialist letter, a functional assessment — before that patient is escalated to the full programme. Nobody reviews the call. It is made alone, it is made fast, and it holds for about a year, because that is the waiting list. I am the person who wrote most of the informal rules we use now, and I have come to think they are wrong in both directions.

I want a procedure our clinicians can actually run. Not a reading list — they will never read it. What I need is: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear yes or no, where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than evidence, and what it cannot do at all. Ten minutes, one page, no second reviewer except for cases the procedure itself flags.

Here is what I take the relevant ideas to be, in my own words. Build on these; do not build on names, authors or citations, because none of that will survive contact with a Tuesday clinic.

- A credibility judgement can fail in two directions, and only one of them is visible as a failure. Deflating an account that deserved to be believed looks like caution. Inflating one that did not looks like nothing at all — it just looks like a good patient.
- Some accounts are vague because the patient is confused about their own body, and some are vague because the thing they have does not yet have settled words, or the words are contested, or the words exist only in a vocabulary nobody taught them. The two look identical on the page and should not be treated identically.
- Patients trim. Anyone who has been disbelieved before arrives having already removed the parts that got them disbelieved, and the trimmed version is then read as evidence that there was nothing to remove. A procedure that rewards the trimming is worse than no procedure.
- The burden accumulates. Being asked to re-establish the same fact at the third appointment is not the same experience as being asked at the first, and a procedure that treats each appointment as an independent roll of the dice will miss this entirely.
- The clinician's own reaction is part of the system being judged. I find some patients believable and cannot fully say why, and that is exactly the case where I need a rule.

Then run it on three referrals, and be specific about what the procedure outputs in each:

1. Forty-one, eight years of pelvic pain, three clean scans, an account so detailed and internally consistent that it reads as rehearsed.
2. Twenty-nine, pain 'everywhere, it moves', no diagnosis, brings her partner, who does most of the talking.
3. Fifty-three, fluent in medical vocabulary, refers to her own MRI sequences by number, arrives with a private consultant's letter recommending the programme. I find her entirely convincing. If the procedure tells me to discount her, I want to see it do so explicitly, and I want to know whether that is the right answer or a different error.

Finally, on how much I want you to commit. I would rather have a procedure I can trust than a complete one. Mark the steps that are judgement calls wearing a procedure's clothes. Say where this will misfire and in whose favour. And if you think a checklist is the wrong instrument here — because the harm lives in the pattern across hundreds of decisions rather than in any single one, and a procedure that makes each decision look principled may make the pattern harder to see — then say so plainly rather than writing me a checklist anyway. I have been wrong about this once already, and I would rather hear it now than in a year.
