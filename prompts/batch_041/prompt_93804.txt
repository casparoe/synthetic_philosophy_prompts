Hi there. I'm a retired field biologist (thirty years chasing birds around, mostly), and since I put down the binoculars I've been trying to teach myself philosophy — badly, but happily. I've gotten stuck on the ring of Gyges bit in the Republic, where the shepherd finds a ring that makes him invisible and Glaucon says basically anybody would do the unjust thing once they knew they could get away with it, and then dares Socrates to show justice is worth having for its own sake and not just for the reputation and the rewards. I know that's Philosophy 101, so I don't want the standard tour. I'd like you to get into the weeds with me.

Two things keep nagging at me. First, the story is usually told as if the ring is a rare miracle, one lucky shepherd. But look around — it feels like we're all getting a cheap, leaky version of the ring now. You can say and do things at very low risk of being held to account, except the cloak has holes in it, and how big the holes are varies from person to person and place to place. Second, it bugs me that Glaucon asks the question about a single person. What I actually care about is what happens when everybody has a ring, or a half-ring. Is morality meant to be a strategy that holds up in that kind of world, or a personal disposition that's supposed to hold up even when it doesn't?

So here's my ask. I've got maybe ten decent years left and a small bit of savings, and I'd rather spend them thinking and writing about this than on one more bird list. Act like a philosopher who works on ethics but who also actually understands game theory and the social science of cooperation, and help me figure out which open questions in this neck of the woods are worth the years, and in what order. Start by brainstorming the candidate questions out loud before you commit to anything — I want to see the space before you prune it.

Some candidates I've half-formed, but don't let me limit you: whether why-be-moral is even one question or three stitched together (a motivational one, a justificatory one, a prudential one) and which of them Glaucon is really pressing; whether the right reply to Gyges is to make people virtuous or to build a world where injustice doesn't pay — institutions, accountability, reputation tech — and whether those are substitutes or complements; what an equilibrium even looks like when concealment is cheap but imperfect, i.e. does partial accountability stabilize cooperation or just select for better concealment; whether the gap between being just and being believed just can be closed at all or is just a permanent feature of social life; and whether the whole framing sneaks in a picture of morality as a restraint on self-interest that a naturalist like me should maybe just reject.

For each question you keep, tell me why it's upstream of the others or not, how tractable it is for a lone amateur rather than a research team, and what evidence or argument over the next couple of years would move it up or down my list. And please say which of the popular answers to Glaucon are, in your view, dead ends that merely sound profound. One more thing — don't give me a reading list or citations, I can't check them and I'll just feel bad about it. I want your defended agenda, not a survey. Think it through carefully, and be willing to tell me if I've framed the whole thing wrong.
