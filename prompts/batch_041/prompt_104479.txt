I have no philosophical training and have never studied any of this formally, but I have been reading Maimonides' Guide of the Perplexed on my own, and the section on the sacrificial laws has stayed with me. As I understand his explanation, the commandments concerning sacrifice were not given because sacrifice is what God most wanted from us; they were given because the people who received them had grown up in a world in which every serious religious act took the form of an animal offering, so that a law which simply abolished the practice would have demanded something no one at the time was capable of doing. The practice was therefore kept but redirected — the same gestures and the same animals, now offered to the one God, under rules that strip away the surrounding practices Maimonides took to be idolatrous. The more I turn this over, the more it resembles an argument I keep hearing from a friend who works in addiction services. She defends methadone and needle exchanges on the grounds that you do not get to skip a person's present situation: a demand that someone give up everything at once is not a standard but an excuse for refusing to help, and it is better to work with the behaviour people actually have, in the form they actually have it, than to insist on the behaviour they ought to have had. She calls this meeting people where they are; Maimonides, as far as I can tell, is doing something of that sort with an entire people. I should say that another friend reads the same laws as a training in the seriousness of taking life, and while I find that attractive I am not asking about it here — I want to keep the comparison to these two rationales.

What I would like is a careful comparison of those two ideas — not a general essay on divine accommodation, nor one on pragmatism in legislation, but a comparison of these two rationales specifically, treated as rival answers to the question of why a lawgiver would permit or prescribe something he would prefer not to exist at all. I want to know how much of the structure is genuinely shared: a concession to an incapacity, a redirection rather than an abolition, a second-best that is nonetheless commanded and therefore binding. And I want to know where the comparison quietly stops working. My own guess is that the load-bearing difference lies in the metric. In the harm-reduction case the harm being reduced falls on the person being accommodated, so the concession can be defended in terms of her own good; in Maimonides' case what is being avoided is a wrong done to God, which is not obviously anybody's welfare, and I would like to be told whether that difference is fatal or whether there is some way of describing both cases in a single currency. I am also struck by a difference in the shape of time. Harm reduction is usually defended as a step, with the eventual aim being that the person no longer needs the substitute at all, whereas on Maimonides' account the sacrifices are not a stage on the way to their own disappearance — they simply continue, which makes the concession look less like a bridge and more like a permanent arrangement.
