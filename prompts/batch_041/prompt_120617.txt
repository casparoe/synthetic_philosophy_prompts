I am setting the final examination for an upper-level seminar in democratic theory, enrolling advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students, and I need a single essay question on the Condorcet jury theorem. The difficulty I keep encountering is that candidates can state the theorem and its assumptions and then stop, without showing that they see why the assumptions do any work. I want a question that makes it impossible to paper over the gap between the theorem as a mathematical result and the theorem as a premise in an argument for majority rule.

The question should turn on the distinction between a voter's being more likely than not to be right and the voters' errors being independent, and it should be anchored in a concrete case so that a candidate cannot retreat into the general statement. Please build it around the following case, which I have invented and which should appear in the question as given. A citizens' panel of 999 members must settle a factual question: whether a reservoir is contaminated. Every member watches the same televised demonstration by a single expert. The demonstration is correct with probability 0.7. Each member follows the demonstration with probability 0.9; the remaining tenth form their own judgment, independent of the demonstration, which is correct with probability 0.55.

The question should require candidates to work out how reliable the panel's majority verdict is; to compare that with what a straightforward application of the theorem to each member's individual reliability would predict; to identify which assumption of the theorem the case violates, and to explain why the violation is not the familiar complaint that voters are ignorant; to explain why enlarging the panel would not improve its reliability; and to say what the case shows about appeals to the theorem in defence of majority rule. It should also require them to say what, if anything, changes when the question before the panel is evaluative rather than factual -- whether the reservoir ought to be decommissioned, say -- and whether the theorem can be brought to bear on that question at all.

Please provide three things. First, the essay question in the form in which it would appear on the examination paper, no longer than 150 words and without sub-questions that disclose the intended structure; the paper is three hours with a choice of questions, and candidates will spend about 45 minutes on this one. Second, a model answer of roughly 750 words, written as a strong candidate would write it. Third, a marking scheme worth 25 marks that identifies the five or six moves a top answer must make, assigns marks to each, and adds for each move a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs candidates marks -- errors that candidates actually make, not straw men.
