I'm writing an essay on the analogy-centered theory of cognition — the Hofstadter-and-Sander line that analogy-making is not one faculty among many but the basic operation of thought, so that perceiving, categorizing, and reasoning are one process run at different scales — and I want to argue for a deliberately sharp version of it: that there are no literal concepts, only concepts that are bundles of past cases, so applying any concept is a judgment of sameness relative to a purpose. Before I commit to that, tell me what would count against it. Which observations would genuinely tell against it — a creature, a person, or a machine that categorizes or reasons with no discernible analogy to a prior case — and which apparent counterexamples a defender could absorb simply by redescribing them as analogy with zero slippage, so that I can separate a real defeat from a rearguard restatement? What does the sharp version predict that its rivals don't, and which findings would its own proponents concede cut against them? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a defect here or the unavoidable price of a thesis this general. I'll add that what I would personally find most persuasive is a clean, concrete case of thought that cannot be redescribed as analogy without draining the thesis of content.
