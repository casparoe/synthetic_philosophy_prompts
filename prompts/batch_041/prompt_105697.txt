I am assembling the materials for a graduate seminar on modeling in the social and life sciences, and one session is devoted to the instrumentalist doctrine that a model's assumptions need not be descriptively accurate — that a hypothesis is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions rather than by the realism of its assumptions. This is the position Milton Friedman set out in his 1953 essay on positive economics, the one now usually nicknamed the F-twist. My students read the doctrine and then produce either a shrug or a caricature, and I want them to see the dispute conducted at full strength. So I would like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers of science who hold opposing views of the doctrine. Neither should be a stand-in for a position to be knocked over: the defender of the instrumentalist line should know the standard objections and have real replies to them, and the critic should be arguing from a considered alternative rather than from the bare fact that assumptions are sometimes false. They should respond to each other's actual words, concede where a point lands, and press where it does not.

Please have them argue about a particular case rather than about the doctrine in the abstract, and I would rather you invent the details than reach for a famous textbook illustration. Use this one: a compartmental model of an infectious disease that assumes a population mixes homogeneously, that the transmission rate stays constant over the period of interest, and that people do not change their behavior in response to the policy being studied; the model is used to estimate that closing schools would have cut cases in a particular city by roughly forty percent, and its defenders note that it reproduced the observed case curve in three other cities and ranked a set of candidate policies in the same order that later data supported. The instrumentalist should be able to explain why the falsity of the mixing and behavioral assumptions does not touch the model's usefulness, and should be able to handle the objection that a model false in exactly these respects might be getting the right answer for the wrong reason. The critic should be able to say what goes wrong when assumptions are false in a way that matters, distinguishing the case where an assumption is a deliberate distortion that isolates a factor we want to study from the case where it quietly does the explanatory work and turns the conclusion into an artifact of the setup — at which point the instrumentalist should press the critic on what criterion could license that distinction other than the predictions themselves, since the critic's own favored kind of idealization also proceeds by asserting something false. I would like each of them to concede at least one point to the other before the dialogue ends, and I would like the exchange to close without a tidy resolution, on a clear statement of exactly what the disagreement turns on. The dialogue should run to about fifteen hundred to two thousand words. Neither character needs to cite any literature — I want the substance of the arguments rather than a survey — and I would prefer that they argue in their own voices rather than in the register of a journal article.
