ok so I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy of education, and I've somehow been voluntold to give the opening remarks at our division's fall TA training day. Twelve minutes, right after the free coffee and the icebreaker nobody likes. The room is mostly first-time TAs from across the division: a chunk of chem and bio people who showed up because they think they want "active learning techniques," some humanities folks, our teaching center's staff (lovely people, but they have a very specific way of talking about all of this), and two or three senior faculty who will definately know who Whitehead is and will be watching me the whole time.

Here's my problem. The teaching center sent around a one-page prep sheet that boils the whole thing down to a loop they call "hook-drill-apply," and the blurb next to it basically says: first get them excited, then make them practice the exact steps, then let them use it. On paper that's... fine? But I keep feeling it's a flattened, checklist version of Whitehead's rhythm of education (romance, precision, generalization), and that the flattening actually matters. The sheet treats the three moments as a recipe you run once per lesson and then you're done, whereas I think Whitehead's whole point is that the rhythm recurs, and that the real danger isn't skipping a stage so much as precision that was never earned by romance, or what he called inert ideas, i.e. stuff absorbed without ever being used or tested or thrown into new combinations. There's also a version of this where precision looks like the enemy, and I don't think that's right either. I think he'd say precision in isolation is barren, but that skipping it just leaves students with vibes and no grip.

I want the talk to actually be shaped by the Whitehead idea and not just name-drop him in paragraph two. So: help me write it. It has to land for the chem TAs who have never read a word of philosophy, give the teaching center people something to nod at instead of feeling attacked, and still be honest enough that the faculty in the back don't think I'm using a dead metaphysician as garnish. If there's a place where the honest thing to say is that the three-stage story is messier than the handout makes it sound, say so, and tell me plainly which parts of the reading are settled among people who study Whitehead and which are genuinely contested, rather than smoothing it all into one confident line. I'd much rather the speech contain one real hedge than four fake certainties.
