Me and a colleague have been going in circles for months about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — the one that says true moral responsibility is impossible because it would require being causa sui, and that this goes through whether or not determinism is true, since indeterminism would just swap your character for a coin toss. I'm the Strawsonian in this fight: nobody is ever truly morally responsible, and the whole point is that the argument isn't about determinism at all. She's the compatibilist who thinks the argument is a non-starter — either the premise that being responsible for what you do requires being responsible for how you are is simply false, or true responsibility is a philosopher's fiction and the ordinary stuff (answerability, accountability, being a proper target of a demand for reasons) never needed self-authorship in the first place.

Write the joint statement we should have produced. I want a numbered list of claims we can both sign — about the argument's structure and what it does and doesn't presuppose, about what we actually agree on regarding determinism, about the phenomenolgy, and about what does and doesn't follow for blame, punishment and apology. Then a disagreements table, each row worded the way the person who holds that view would want it worded. Then the cruxes. I suspect one is whether the transfer from responsibility for the deed to responsibility for the doer is a conceptual truth or just a premise compatibilists may reject, and another is whether the argument quietly presupposes a self that's prior to its own traits — i.e. whether a Parfit-ish reductionist or a bundle theorist about the self can just shrug at it. For each crux give a case or argument we'd both agree bears on it, with each of our predictions written down in advance.

Two test cases to run through it if they help. First: someone at 40 deliberately remakes her own character over twenty years through real effort, and at 60 does something awful — is the 60-year-old responsible in the Strawsonian sense, and does the twenty years of self-work buy her anything? Second: whether the argument proves too much, since it looks like it would equally strip us of responsibility for our beliefs and our reasoning, including the reasoning that's supposed to get us to the argument's conclusion. And if part of our fight turns out to be merely verbal — say, about whether responsibility means desert or just answerability — say which part that is, and don't paper over the rest.
