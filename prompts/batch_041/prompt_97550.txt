I am a teaching assistant grading the final examination for an undergraduate course on the ethics of war. One of the questions, together with one student's answer to it, is reproduced below. Please grade the answer and provide itemized comments that justify the grade.

The examination question was:

In this course we have treated the moral equality of combatants as a central doctrine of traditional just war theory. (a) State as precisely as you can what the doctrine asserts about the permissions and prohibitions that apply to individual soldiers once fighting is under way, and explain the theoretical work it is meant to do. (b) Consider the following case. State A has launched an unprovoked war of conquest against State B. A's government has told its citizens, falsely, that B has invaded and occupied A's territory; the ordinary A soldier believes this and understands himself to be defending his homeland. A's soldiers are conscripts; those who have refused to serve or attempted to desert have been threatened with reprisals against their families. In the field, A's soldiers engage only B's combatants and observe the ordinary in bello rules. State B is fighting a straightforwardly defensive war and its soldiers do likewise. For a single firefight between one A soldier and one B soldier, is it permissible for each to try to kill the other? Answer under the traditional doctrine and under the revisionist critique, and say where and why they diverge. (c) State which position you find more defensible, and give your strongest reason for it.

The student's answer was:

The moral equality of combatants is the doctrine that, once a war is under way, soldiers on both sides have the same permissions and prohibitions under jus in bello. On Walzer's version, the justice of the war — the question of jus ad bellum — is set aside when we ask what soldiers may do in the field: an infantryman on the unjust side and an infantryman on the just side are alike permitted to kill enemy combatants and alike forbidden to target noncombatants. The doctrine does several kinds of work. It explains why we do not treat enemy soldiers as criminals merely for having fought. It underwrites the symmetry of the laws of war, since rules that bound only one side could not function as law. And it spares soldiers the impossible task of adjudicating their state's cause before they can know whether they are permitted to fight.

The revisionist critique denies the symmetry. On McMahan's account, whether you may kill a person depends on whether that person is liable to be killed, and a person is liable when he is responsible for an unjust threat. An unjust combatant is fighting for an unjust cause; he is therefore responsible for an unjust threat and therefore liable. A just combatant, who is not responsible for any unjust threat, is not liable. The permissions are therefore asymmetric: the just combatant may kill the unjust one but not the reverse, and an unjust combatant who kills a just one commits murder.

Applied to the case, A's soldier is prosecuting a war of conquest, so on the revisionist view he is liable and B's soldier may kill him. B's soldier is defending his country and is not liable, so A's soldier may not kill him. On the traditional view the firefight is one of mutual permission: both may try to kill, because both are combatants and the in bello rules apply symmetrically. The injustice of A's war makes A's soldier blameworthy for the war, but it does not alter his permissions in the field. The two soldiers are equally responsible for the killing that occurs, so neither can claim the moral high ground, and this is precisely what the doctrine is meant to secure.

I find the revisionist critique more defensible. It seems false that a soldier conquering another country and a soldier defending his own are doing the same kind of thing and are equally entitled to kill. The A soldier is engaged in an unjust project whether or not he knows it, and ignorance does not make an unjust threat just. It may be objected that ordinary soldiers cannot be expected to know whether their cause is just; but that shows at most that they are excused, not that what they do is permitted.

Please assess the answer on its merits. I would like (1) an overall grade — a letter grade together with a rough percentage — and (2) a point-by-point set of comments identifying what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and how serious each error is, with each comment tied to the specific sentence or claim in the answer that occasions it. Where the answer misstates a position, say what the position actually is and why the student's version of it is inaccurate. Distinguish errors that undermine the answer's central argument from errors that are minor or merely imprecise, and make clear whether, in your judgment, the errors should pull the grade below what a reader skimming for the main points would be inclined to give. The student was not asked to cite sources, so do not penalize the absence of citations; grade the reasoning and the accuracy of the philosophical claims.
