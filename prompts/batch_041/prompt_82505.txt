A mate from my reading group has been banging on about this one for weeks and I've run out of replies.

The setup, so it's clear what we're actually arguing about. Take an agent whose starting point is what he calls a universal prior (Solomonoff's, I think): every hypothesis about the world gets a weight of two to the minus the length of the shortest computer program that would print that world's history out, so short descriptions get exponentially more weight, and nothing else is assumed at all. Then it does ordinary Bayesian updating on what it sees, and picks whatever action has the highest expected value.

His argument:

(1) "My computation is being run once" and "my computation is being run a million times, with the world arranged so that the copies' fates depend on what I do now" are almost the same length. Once you've paid for me and for the simulation, saying "a million" costs about twenty bits and "a million million" costs about forty. So the many-copies hypothesis starts out only about a millionth as probable as the single-copy one — not astronomically less probable, just a millionth.

(2) Expected value maximisation tells you to trade a small probability of a huge good against a large probability of a small one. A millionth of a reward that is a million million times bigger wins against anything on offer in the boring hypothesis.

(3) So for whatever ordinary thing I'm contemplating, there's a barely-longer hypothesis on which doing something else entirely — ditching my family, giving everything away, doing whatever some hypothetical simulator wants — pays off astronomically for astronomically many copies of me.

(4) So the agent does that. Every time. Its ordinary values never get to decide anything. The prior is malign, he says, and consequentialism is what makes it malignant.

(5) And it isn't a quirk of the numbers I picked: because the prior ranges over all programs, and short programs can name enormous numbers, there's no ceiling on the size of the reward in (3) and no floor on its probability, so nothing ordinary ever survives the comparison.

What I can't do is give up either of the two things he says I have to. I want to keep the prior, because "simpler is likelier, and nothing else is assumed" looks like the only principled place to start. And I want to keep expected value maximisation, because the moment I stop multiplying value by probability I lose every long-shot-high-stakes argument I actually believe — the ones about small chances of very bad futures, which is more or less the whole of what I think matters.

The bit that smells to me is this. The same trick in (3) seems to run in the other direction too: whatever I'm about to do, there's a barely-longer hypothesis on which a simulator punishes it and rewards the opposite. So the argument doesn't seem to pick out any behaviour at all — it just says "you're hijacked", without saying by what. He says that's the whole point, it's malign in every direction at once, and a bit of reasoning that licenses every action equally is exactly what "malign" means. Maybe. But I can't shake the feeling that reasoning which licenses every action equally is less a discovery about the prior than a reductio of one of the steps.

So which step goes? If it's (1) or (2), tell me what the extra bits or the extra probability really cost — I don't believe "specifying the simulator's reward policy is expensive" for a second, but maybe I'm being glib. If it's (3) or (4), tell me what principle stops the comparison, and then tell me honestly what that principle costs me elsewhere: in particular, whether it kills the small-chance-of-ruin reasoning I said I wanted to keep, and if it doesn't, what the difference between the two cases is that isn't just that I like one of them. And if the honest answer is (5), or the conclusion, then say so — I'd rather know now than keep pretending I can have both.
