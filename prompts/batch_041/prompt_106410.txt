I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I'm trying to work out what the professor who runs our seminar on contractualism is actually committed to about aggregation. She is a Scanlonian in the strict sense — she holds that "an act is wrong if its performance under the circumstances would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced, general agreement" — and she is emphatic that the unit of justification is the single person's complaint rather than the sum of complaints. I have kept a running list of her verdicts from this term's case discussions, and I want to know what she will say about the case we have been assigned for next week.

Her verdicts so far: (1) A technician is trapped in a broadcast transmitter room and is being painfully burned by the equipment; the only way to stop it is to cut the feed to a match being watched by millions, who would merely be disappointed. She says the feed must be cut — the technician's complaint settles the matter, and the fact that the disappointment would be shared by millions adds nothing to its weight. (2) A hospital can spare one patient a permanent, disabling injury only by requiring thirty people to spend twenty minutes each on paperwork; she says do it. (3) We must choose between leaving one person blind and leaving three people each with a permanently damaged hand; she says leave the three with the damaged hands, since the person who would be blinded has the stronger complaint, and the fact that three would suffer rather than one adds nothing. (4) A rule that makes a hundred people wait an extra ten minutes at a clinic would spare one person a two-day wait; she says impose the ten-minute waits.

Those verdicts kill off the crude aggregative views — she is plainly not minimising total burden, and not minimising the number of people burdened — but they appear to leave several readings standing: a strictly individualist rule, on which the worst individual complaint on each side decides and numbers never count at all; a two-tier rule, on which serious burdens are compared one complaint at a time but small burdens may be pooled across persons; and a same-kind rule, on which burdens of the same kind are summed but burdens of different kinds are not. Next week's case is an airport scheduling decision: either one identifiable passenger misses a connection and loses two days to rebooking and a hotel, or each of 100,000 passengers waits an extra forty minutes in a security queue. Predict her verdict, tell me which of those readings (or some third rule I have not thought of) her track record actually supports, what each predicts about the airport case, which one you would bet on and why, and which further case would most cleanly separate them — including, if a pooling rule is live, what it commits her to about where the line between serious and small falls. Keep the whole answer under 400 words.
