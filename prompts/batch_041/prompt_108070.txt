The opening chapter of a well-regarded popular book on Adam Smith, lent to me by a friend, offers the following précis of his ethics:

'Smith's whole ethics rests on sympathy, which he takes to mean feeling what another person feels — sharing their pain or their joy. From this he builds the impartial spectator: when we want to know whether an action is right, we imagine a spectator who has no stake in the matter and ask what such a person would approve of. The device is the ancestor of the modern ideal-observer and veil-of-ignorance traditions; in effect, we strip away the information that tells us who we are and see what remains. Since sympathy is the sole source of moral approval, what the impartial spectator ends up approving of is whatever makes for the greatest happiness, and Smith is a utilitarian in disguise. And because he concedes that custom and fashion can corrupt our moral sentiments, he must also concede that the impartial spectator is really the voice of his own society internalised — which is why the gulf between his two books, the one explaining us by sympathy and the other by self-interest, cannot be closed, and why he has no answer to the relativist.'

I am not equipped to adjudicate this, but I suspect it is a competent summary that has gone subtly wrong in a few places. Please audit it claim by claim, telling me for each whether a specialist in Smith would accept it as it stands, treat it as a defensible simplification, or reject it as something Smith explicitly denies; where a claim is a half-truth, say which half is doing the work and which is being smuggled in. I am particularly unsure how far the comparison with ideal-observer and veil-of-ignorance theories is meant to extend. My own hunch is that the relativist charge is the one that really bites, so I should like the strongest case for it set out, the best reply available to Smith, and your own judgement of whether that reply succeeds.

Then tell me whether the book's concluding verdict survives the corrections: if the summary is roughly right about the mechanics but wrong about the standard of right, does the conclusion still follow, and which single correction matters most for the rest?
