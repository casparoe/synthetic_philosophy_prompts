I am a novelist, and the book I am at work on turns on a single question of psychology, so I would rather get that question right than write around it. My narrator gives a sustained, coherent, and entirely sincere account of why she has done what she has done, and over the course of the story the reader comes to see that the account is wrong — not merely incomplete, not self-serving in the ordinary way, but wrong about the causes that actually moved her. I want the book to be a study in self-deception rather than a puzzle about whether there is any fact of the matter, and before I commit the whole structure to that, I want the specification of what an adequate theory of introspection must deliver.

So please write the desiderata for a theory of the reliability of introspection: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the findings it must be consistent with, and the questions it must at least be able to answer — each with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and each marked as non-negotiable or as the prejudice of one camp. I care about the following in particular.

First, whether reliability has to be indexed to a domain. A person can say with authority that her tooth aches or that the light has changed, and be hopeless about why she has suddenly taken against a colleague. Does an adequate theory have to draw that line, and where, and on what grounds?

Second, the difference between a report being accurate and its having authority — the first-person privilege that makes my narrator's testimony about herself different in kind from what a good friend could tell me about her. Must authority rest on accuracy, or can they come apart? I need to know whether the form of my book is defensible or merely conventional.

Third, whether a theory must allow that a report can be fluent, confident, sincere, and still not be caused by the state it purports to report — as in the familiar experiments where the reason a person gives is demonstrably not the reason for her choice. If a theory forbids that, it forbids my novel.

Fourth, what to make of the fact that I answer the question of whether I believe something not by looking inward but by looking at the matter itself. Is that a requirement any theory must accommodate, or a description of a shortcut that happens to work?

Fifth, the baseline. Reliable as compared with what — chance, a stranger, an instrument? I need to know whether my narrator's errors are rarer or commoner than the errors an attentive friend would make about her.

Sixth, whether reporting alters what is reported. My narrator writes her account down; is a theory obliged to say whether the act of telling changes the state told?

Then tell me which of these cannot be had together — I suspect the demand that the first-person report be authoritative in a way a third-person report cannot be, the demand that it be causally downstream of the state it reports, and the demand that self-deception be a genuine error rather than an alternative construction are not jointly satisfiable — and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. Finish briefly with how the main families fare against the list: the picture of introspection as an inner sense, the view that self-reports are inferences from one's own behaviour and circumstances, and the view that they express commitments rather than describe states. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are what I need, and the criteria are the reason I am asking.
