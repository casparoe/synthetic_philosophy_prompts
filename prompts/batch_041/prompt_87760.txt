So this is a bit of an odd one and it's not hypothetical, it's my actual family, and the reunion is in three weeks. My aunt has spent about four years on the family tree and she's found out that my great-grandad — who came over from Ireland in the twenties and is basically the founding saint of our family story — had a wife and two kids in Dublin that he walked out on, and there's a whole branch of the family over there who mostly don't know where he went. The version we grew up with is that his first wife died young. She didn't. My aunt wants the reunion to be where it comes out: she's made a display board with both families on it, she wants the names read out including the first wife's, which none of us has ever said aloud, and she's set up a little fund the rest of us are meant to chip into for the Dublin descendants — who by the way never asked for any of this, and one of them told her on the phone that she doesn't want to be anybody's project.

My dad, who's hosting and giving the toast, thinks it's a mistake. His line is that the people who were actually hurt are dead, the ones who'd recieve the apology aren't the ones it's owed to, and all my aunt is doing is making sixty people who never met the man feel guilty about him for the rest of their lives; he reckons a family is allowed to move on, and if there's money going spare it should go to my cousin's kids school fund rather than a plaque. My aunt says a family that won't say out loud what it did is still doing it, and that what people call moving on is just what you call it when you've decided the victims don't matter. I'm doing the slideshow, so I sort of have to pick a side. My flatmate, who did an MA in philosophy and is no help whatsoever, says there's a philosopher who reckons you only owe this kind of remembering to people you're already bound to, so we're basically off the hook — which sounds to me like exactly the sort of thing someone says to get out of it, but I can't say why.
