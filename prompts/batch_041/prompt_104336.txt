I keep getting into arguments about when it's actually wrong to not beleive someone, and I've noticed my gut verdicts don't obviously run on a single principle. Please don't just report what some famous philosopher supposedly said — I want the actual principle, stated so I could apply it myself. Here's where I land:

- A doctor shrugs off a woman's abdominal pain as anxiety; it turns out to be something serious. Unjust, obviously.
- My brother lies about everything. He tells me the road is flooded, I ignore him, I wreck the car. He was right, and I still don't think I wronged him.
- A guy at a party has a thick accent and keeps mangling a technical term. I catch myself deciding he doesn't know what he's talking about, even though I understood him fine and he's right. Unjust.
- Someone from a group with a genuinely higher base rate for a certain medical condition tells me about something totally unrelated — a plumbing problem — and I quietly discount them. I say unjust, even though I'd happily use that base rate for the medical thing.
- Same person, but now they really are telling me about the medical thing, and I weight their word lower because of the base rate. Honestly I think that's fine, or at least not an injustice.
- Here's one I can't resolve: someone has a terrible track record at a skill, so I never believe them about it. It turns out they never got to practice because they were kept out of every room where the skill gets learned. I want to say my discount is unjust, but it's not about their group and it's about the right topic, so my other verdicts don't get me there.
- Someone bad at reading social situations misses that a colleague is quietly sabotaging her. Nobody disbelieves her — there's no moment where she says something true and gets shot down. She just can't make sense of what's happening and blames herself. I think that's an injustice, but I can't point to anyone who did anything wrong.
- Flip it: a woman says she's being harassed, and her manager genuinely doesn't think the behavior counts as harassment, though he has the word and uses it for other cases. Nobody dismisses her testimony as such — they just disagree about whether it applies. I want to say that's not an injustice, just a bad argument, and I'm not sure that survives contact with the previous case.
- One more, about patterns: a rushed doctor misses an atypical presentation of a disease in a man. No injustice there. The same doctor misses it in nine women in a row and never notices. I think the ninth is an injustice even though each visit, taken alone, involved no prejudice and no sloppiness.

My draft principle was: a credibility judgment is unjust when the hearer's discount isn't tracking evidence about this speaker on this topic. That handles the accent and unrelated-base-rate cases, but it makes the flooded-road verdict come out unjust (his record is evidence about him on this topic), and it does nothing with the last two, which look like they're about patterns and concepts rather than any single judgment.
