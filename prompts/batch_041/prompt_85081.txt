I serve on the formation committee of a small religious congregation of women religious — six of us, two lay, advising a provincial superior — and I need the written opinion I will circulate before our meeting on the 14th. Our founder, Mother Élodie Marchand, died in 1911; for thirty years the novitiate has used a short devotional life written in the 1950s, and the provincial has asked us to select the text that will form novices beginning next September, and to say in the same breath whether the old devotional life should be quietly withdrawn. Only two manuscripts are in front of us and there will not be a third, because there is no money for one. The first was commissioned from a writer long attached to the congregation; it follows the old conventions of the edifying life — signs of grace in childhood, an unbroken ascent in virtue, favors reported after her death — and it leaves out the two things the archives actually contain: her refusal, in 1894, to readmit a young sister she had herself dismissed for insubordination, who died of tuberculosis eighteen months later in a rented room in the town; and the four years of desolation, documented in her own letters, in which she wrote that she could not pray and believed she had been abandoned. The second manuscript is the work of an academic historian given unrestricted access to the archive; she prints both episodes, argues that the dismissal was a failure of charity aggravated by the founder's horror of scandal, reads the congregation's early expansion as partly the expression of a temperament that needed to command, and insists throughout that she takes no position on the reported supernatural events, since that is not her business.

The committee is split, and the split is not really about the two books. One member argues, and I think she may be right, that the whole point of the genre is admiration: a model one cannot imitate is not a model, that the old writers understood this and edited accordingly, and that a novice handed a woman who could not pray for four years and who let a young sister die in poverty will learn only that she herself is not called to sanctity. The contrary position, which I also find hard to dismiss, is that the edifying life is a species of deception that destroys itself the moment the reader discovers it — our novices have phones, they will find the letters — so that the admiration the first position wants is best secured by candor, by a woman who failed and went on; and that the premise behind Plutarch's kind of life-writing, that the study of a life's small acts is itself a discipline of self-improvement, requires those acts to be real acts rather than exemplary fictions. There is a further wrinkle neither side has touched. The devotional life and the historian's life seem to rest on different theories of what a life is for — one treats the founder as an icon to be venerated whole, the other as a case to be weighed in parts — and I suspect whichever we choose will quietly settle, for the next generation of our sisters, what learning morally from a life is even supposed to be.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action that we can actually execute with the resources we have — one of the two manuscripts, or some third arrangement (a preface, an appended selection of the letters, a companion volume, a withdrawal from circulation, a staged adoption) — with a time frame that fits the September novitiate and says who must do what. It should give the strongest version of the position it rejects rather than knocking down a weak one, and it should say plainly what the committee's choice commits us to about the nature of exemplars, since I expect the provincial to ask me that directly and I do not want to improvise an answer at the table.
