Two of the regulars, Nadia and Peter, went at it for the last forty minutes of the session and I honestly couldn't call it. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

1. Peter: if the self just is its roles, there's no standpoint left from which to criticise a role. His example was a woman whose role as daughter-in-law requires her to defer to a controlling mother-in-law. On this picture the best she can do is be a better daughter-in-law; she can't say the role itself is what's hurting her. That isn't ethics, he said, it's script-following with extra steps. And the same point scales up — the theory can never condemn a whole social order, only coach people into performing their parts more gracefully.

2. Nadia: that's a cartoon. Roles aren't scripts handed out at birth; they get interpreted, contested, renegotiated, and the virtue the tradition cares about is precisely the skill of doing that well. Also nobody has one role. The same woman is a mother, a sister, a neighbour, a citizen; those roles make conflicting demands on her, and the conflict is the engine of criticism. You don't need a self standing outside all the roles, you need several roles inside one life.

3. Peter: fine, but that only buys you intramural critique. If every critical standpoint is itself a role, you can never say the whole set of roles is rotten — only that this one is out of tune with that one. And then the sharper point: the picture now needs something that isn't a role, namely whoever is doing the negotiating among the roles. That looks like a self behind the roles, which is the one thing the view said it didn't have.
