I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training beyond a couple of online courses and way too much time arguing in group chats, so excuse the terminology if I get it wrong. I've got kind of obsessed with this puzzle about dispositions — the idea that a thing can have a tendency or a power even when it isn't doing anything, like a glass being fragile while it just sits on the shelf, or a virus being infectious while it's frozen in a lab. The standard story, as I understand it, is that to call something fragile is roughly to say that if you stressed it the right way it would break, and most of the literature seems to be about how much work that "roughly" is doing. There are the cases where the trigger itself stops the thing from doing what it otherwise would (the toy example I keep hearing is a machine that reads your mind and, the instant you're about to drop the glass, reinforces it so it won't break after all), and cases where something else blocks the effect without changing the glass at all (it's wrapped in bubble wrap, or glued to the table). I've decided neither of these is a real problem for the view that dispositions are real, and here's my position: a disposition is a genuine, intrinsic feature of a thing, and what makes it *that* disposition is the manifestation it's oriented toward — the effect it's pointed at — not any counterfactual about what would happen in this or that situation. The counterfactual is a useful but defeasible symptom of the disposition, not its content. So finks (where the trigger cancels the effect) and masks (where something else blocks it) just show the symptom can fail while the dispostion is still there, the same way a smoke detector can fail to go off while there's still a fire. I'll even go further and say the trigger isn't really part of what the disposition is either — it's just one way of bringing the manifestation out.

What I want from you is an honest map of what would count against this, because I have a nagging suspicion I've built something that can't lose, and I can't tell if that's a bug or a feature. Concretely: which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results should actually move me, and which ones would a defender of my view just wave off as missing the point? What is my view committed to that might turn out to be unacceptable — say, about how we tell one disposition from another, or whether we could ever know a disposition is present at all, or how dispositions are supposed to relate to laws and to counterfactuals if I've cut the counterfactual out of the content? What does my picture predict or forbid that a rival doesn't — the plain counterfactual analysis, or the view that a disposition is just its physical basis and nothing over and above it? And be blunt about whether I've made the thing unfalsifiable: if every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by saying "oh, the disposition was there but it was finked or masked," is there anything left that could count against me, and is that actually a problem or just the price of any view that posits properties you can't see? Please rank the defeaters from "this should genuinely change my mind" down to "annoying but survivable," and tell me which ones I'd have to concede even on my own terms.
