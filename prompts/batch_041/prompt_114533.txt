I'm in a philosophy of religion reading group that's spent a few weeks on divine hiddenness, and last session a friend of mine (a theist, for what it's worth) floated an analogy that I think is supposed to make the whole problem go away. His version: a parent playing hide and seek with a small child deliberately stays hidden and stays quiet, doesn't call out, doesn't cough, because the entire value of the game is the child coming and finding them — a parent who shouted their location every ten seconds would turn the reunion into a non-event, and the child would never have had the chance to want anything. He says God is like that, only the game is a whole life, so the hiddenness isn't indifference, it's the condition of a relationship of free love rather than one of puppets who have no option but to believe. And he says the complaint that a loving God would make himself obvious is like a child demanding the parent climb out of the wardrobe right now — the demand misunderstands what's on offer. I want to take this seriously, but I also smell something, and I can't tell whether the smell is the analogy being bad or just me not liking where it lands.

So: give me the correspondence laid out properly — who or what plays the parent, the child, the hiding, the searching, the eventual reveal, the rules of the game, and who's watching whom the whole time — and then tell me where the mapping actually breaks, and which of the breaks matter for the work he's asking it to do versus which are just noise. Two things nag at me and I don't know if they're the real problem. First, in hide and seek the child *saw* the parent go and hide, knows the parent is in the house, and knows the parent is listening and will come out the moment the child starts to cry; in the thing being modeled the seeker isn't unsure *where* God is, they're unsure whether there's anyone there at all — not a gap in location but a gap in existence, and I suspect the analogy only ever had the first kind. Second, the game is voluntary, has a time limit, and is guaranteed to end in a reunion with the parent present; strip those three things out and I don't see what's left. Does the analogy survive the subtraction, or is the guaranteed reveal quietly doing all the argumentative work? He has a patch ready, by the way — he says God leaves traces that only become readable later, like a note in a language you grow into — so tell me whether that patch holds or whether it just relocates the problem. And if the analogy does collapse, is there a better one that gets him the same conclusion, or does the conclusion actually depend on the guarantee he smuggled in? His opponents in the group say the analogy cuts the other way: a parent who watched their kid conclude they were an orphan and did nothing would be cruel, and we'd never call that good parenting.

I'd like you to be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely live disputes among people who actually work on hiddenness — I keep being told some particular reply is "the standard answer," and I'd rather find out which claims are consensus, which are contested, and where you're the one making a judgment call.
