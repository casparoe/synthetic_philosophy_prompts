One of my friends in our little reading group has extremely firm opinions about who's to blame for what, and I've started keeping a list of her verdicts because I'm pretty sure she's running some implicit rule and I want to know what it is before Thursday, when I'm presenting a case at the seminar and she's definately going to have something to say. She hasn't read any of the moral luck literature — I asked her once and got a blank look — so there's no point asking her which camp she's in. I have to reverse-engineer her.

Here's what she's said so far. Two nurses on the same ward, equally careful, both have given the same drug hundreds of times; one patient has a freak reaction and dies, the other nurse's patient goes home fine. Her verdict: neither of them did anything wrong, neither should be disciplined, but the one whose patient died has a tab open with that family that her colleague doesn't have, and it would be a mistake to tell her to just move on. Two fifteen year olds drag race down a residential street, equally reckless, equally indifferent to who might be out there; one car mounts the curb and kills a woman walking her dog, the other one doesn't. Her verdict: the one who killed someone has vastly more to answer for and the court should come down on him much harder — and, in the same breath, the two boys are the same boy underneath, the lucky one isn't a better person, just a luckier one. Then: a woman who's been gentle and generous since she was small, for whom kindness costs nothing, versus a man who's selfish and hot tempered by nature and who's spent twenty years forcing himself to be decent. Her verdict: the man deserves far more credit, and the woman's goodness isn't an achievement, even though there's nothing to criticize in her. And: a man raised in a compound that taught him, with no available counterevidence and no way out, that a certain ethnic group is subhuman, and he repeats it. Her verdict: he isn't to blame, you can't blame someone for what they had no way to see, though she wouldn't have him at her table.

Now the case I'm presenting Thursday. A woman is driving her kid to school, well under the limit, both hands on the wheel, no phone, when a boy chases a ball out from between two parked cars about four feet in front of her. She brakes, but she's already on top of him. He dies. The police report finds no negligence — she wasn't speeding, nothing. The family isn't blaming her, but they've asked her to come by the house, and they've also asked if she'd help with the funeral costs. What does my friend say about her? Does she get a tab open here, is there something outstanding between her and that family, and if so, is it the kind of thing the family can rightly ask for — money, an apology, her standing in their kitchen — or is it hers alone and they'd have no standing to demand it? And does her line on the drag racer commit her to saying this driver has something to answer for too, when there's no fault anywhere in the story?

I'm less interested in yes or no than in the rule she's actually running. I think there's at least two rules that fit everything above and that come apart on this one, maybe a third, so tell me which she's more likely running, what each one predicts about my driver, which you'd bet on and why, and which case would actually settle it. Lead with your answer in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no throat clearing, and don't restate the cases back at me.
