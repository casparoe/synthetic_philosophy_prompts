Typogenetics is the miniature genetics in which strands built from four letters are rewritten by enzymes that the strands themselves specify: strands serve as data, enzymes as the rules that operate upon them, and an enzyme never acts upon another enzyme. I am planning a seminar in which I want to relax that one restriction and allow enzymes to act upon enzymes as well as upon strands, so that the rewriting machinery becomes capable of rewriting itself. Please describe the most important implications of this single change. I am particularly interested in three things: what it does to the isomorphism between typogenetics and Gödel's construction, that is, the idea that a formal system can encode the very rules that generate it; whether self-replication in such a system becomes easier, harder, or different in kind; and whether a diagonal argument can be run in the extended system, and if so, what it would establish. I would also welcome any implication you consider more important than these, even if it is one I have not anticipated. Please write the answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, tables or bold text, in the manner of a letter, and write in British English.
