I've been chewing on something that came up in a reading group on professional ethics, and I'd like you to help me get it straight. Consider how we handle rescue. A firefighter, a lifeguard, or a member of a mountain-rescue team is under a standing obligation -- built into the role and typically into the employment contract -- to go into the burning building or the riptide; declining is a dereliction and can be disciplined or prosecuted. The very same person, off duty and off contract, walking past the identical emergency, is in most common-law jurisdictions under no legal duty to intervene at all, and if they do intervene we call it heroic. Yet the on-duty rescuer who does precisely what the job requires is not usually called heroic, while we still hand out medals and commendations for acts that were, formally, already required of them, and we describe colleagues as exceeding the call even inside a role that already demands a great deal. So one and the same act -- running into the fire -- looks supererogatory, obligatory, or both depending on who performs it and under what arrangement, and the surrounding practices seem to want it both ways at once.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of action who works on supererogation and on role-based and institutional obligation, and to reconstruct the theory of the obligatory/supererogatory boundary that this cluster of practices presupposes. I'm not chiefly interested in scoring named theories; I want to know what the practice quietly commits us to. Is the boundary a property of the act and its moral cost, or is it relative to the agent's role, contract, and the reasonable expectations others may hold of them? Which rival pictures of supererogation does the practice rule out? Are its parts mutually consistent -- does obliging someone, or paying them, drain the supererogatory status out of the act, and if so, what exactly is the medal for? And where is the practice underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories of supererogation would license the very same arrangements? Think carefully and weigh the rival reconstructions before you settle; I've read these forums long enough to distrust a reconstruction that smuggles in the conclusion, so flag the load-bearing step rather than smoothing it over.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no closing summary: the first should state the implicit theory the practice presupposes; the second should say whether that theory makes the boundary act-determined or role-relative; the third should name what the practice rules out; the fourth should say where the practice is underdetermined or internally inconsistent; and the fifth should tell me which argument or consideration would change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
