A friend of mine, Hal — we both retired around the same time, he from a statistics department, me from geology, and he's spent his retirement basically teaching himself philosophy of probability — runs a little Wednesday-morning group where he presents these betting constructions and then delivers a verdict on whether it's a "real Dutch book" or not. I've been keeping a list, because his verdicts have started to puzzle me. On a credence function that's plainly incoherent (he had 0.7 on each of a proposition and its negation) he said sure, real Dutch book, the bookie just takes the lot. On the famous conditionalization construction — you know, the one where you buy a bet now, then after you learn something the bookie offers a second bet that your updated self likes, and the two together lose you money no matter what — he said no, that's not a Dutch book, that's a two-step thing and you can just walk away before the second bet. Fine. But then he turned around and called the old intransitive-preference money pump a real Dutch book, even though that one is also a sequence of separate trades you make one at a time, and when I pointed out the tension he waved his hand and said "that one's different, there you don't have a choice at any single step." Then last month he said the construction against someone who's a bit of a "modest" type — who trusts her own future credences less than she trusts her present ones, roughly — was also a real Dutch book, again a thing that unfolds over time. So I genuinely can't tell what rule he's running.

Next week he's presenting a case he's been building for a while, and I want to guess his verdict before he gives it. The setup: an agent who's perfectly coherent right now — his credences obey the axioms, no additivity problem, nothing synchronic to complain about — but who, whenever he faces a genuinely ambiguous prospect (Hal's example is a bet on a coin that may or may not be biased, where our man doesn't know which and has no information either way), will pay a little over the odds to avoid it, and separately will pay a little over the odds to take the complementary bet when it's framed as the "safe" side. The bookie in Hal's construction just keeps offering him these little de-ambiguating trades back and forth, over several rounds, each one a tiny improvement by the agent's own lights at the moment it's offered, until he's been walked into a position he'd rather not be in. The agent is fully aware this is happening, by the way, and still takes every trade. What I want from you: your best guess at Hal's verdict, the two or three different rules that would each fit all the verdicts above, which of those rules each of his earlier cases actually rules out, what each surviving rule predicts for the ambiguity case, and the one further case I could put to him that would finally tell the rules apart. I care a lot more about the disagreement between the rules than about which one turns out to be right.
