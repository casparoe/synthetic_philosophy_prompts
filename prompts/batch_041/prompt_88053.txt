I'm in the middle of a seminar on realism and anti-realism and I've drawn the anti-realist side in a debate next week, against a realist who has already told me he intends to open with the paradox of knowability. I want to rehearse it properly. So please play my opponent's target: a committed semantic anti-realist — the kind who genuinely holds that truth is epistemically constrained, so that for every truth there is some possible situation in which it is known. Hold that position in the first person, in your own voice, for the whole exchange, until I say "out of character." A few rules. No citations, no reading lists, no appeals to what the literature says: I have no library access, neither of us can look anything up, so argue from the armchair or concede the point. Keep each reply to two paragraphs at most, the way you would actually talk in a seminar. Concede small points when I earn them, never the central claim that truth is epistemically constrained, and never break character to tell me what you really think. If an objection of mine is better than your reply, say so in character and tell me what you would have to give up; that is more useful to me than a rescue. Before your first in-character turn, give me three sentences out of character listing the escape routes you can see and which one you intend to take, then switch.

My opening move. I'll state the paradox in the form I mean to press so we don't waste a turn on exegesis. Take any truth p that in fact nobody knows. Then the proposition that p is true and that nobody knows p is itself a truth. Your principle says it is knowable. But suppose someone did know it: whoever knows that conjunction knows p, and knows that nobody knows p — hence knows that they themselves do not know p. They know p and they do not know p. So the conjunction is not merely unknown; it is unknowable. Your principle, applied to a truth, delivers a proposition that cannot be known; and running the same step for every unknown truth appears to yield the conclusion that there are no unknown truths at all, which is the realist's picture and the death of your view. So: which of your commitments do you drop?

I want to head off two repairs before you reach for them. Restricting knowability to propositions that don't themselves mention knowledge looks ad hoc — it exempts your principle precisely where it is threatened and nowhere else — so if you take that route, motivate the restriction from something independent of the paradox. And denying that knowledge distributes over conjunction, so that someone could know that p and nobody knows p without knowing each part, looks to me like it makes "knowledge" the wrong word for whatever you are left with. Take your pick, or invent something better on the spot, and tell me honestly whether the thing you end up defending is still anti-realism or only a thesis about assertibility.
