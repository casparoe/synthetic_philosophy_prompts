I run a six-person analytical unit that puts probabilities on political and operational risks. Twice a month, under deadline, I have to turn the analysts' individual estimates into the single number that goes into a decision memo; the decisions at stake are expensive and hard to reverse, the director signs off and will not accept a range, and I have to be able to explain the number to people with no statistical training. My analysts are senior and will resist anything that treats their disagreement as noise to be averaged away. Write me a decision procedure I can actually run in a ninety-minute meeting.

Ground it in the following idea, which I want used properly rather than gestured at: agents who share a prior and whose prior, estimates, reasoning, and rationality are common knowledge cannot knowingly disagree, so any disagreement that survives is either private information not yet pooled or a divergence in the priors themselves. Two consequences should shape the procedure. Because the result turns on common rather than mutual knowledge, the procedure needs a step that forces estimates and the reasons for them into the open and then re-elicits, so we can see whether a spread was private information or something else. Where a spread does not collapse, the procedure must diagnose the kind of divergence — different evidence, a different model of the same evidence, a different question being answered, or a difference in what the analysts care about — and treat each differently rather than defaulting to an average. It should also include a sharpening step for the well-documented tendency of a plain average to land closer to fifty-fifty than the analysts' combined information warrants, and say when sharpening is not warranted.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops, and where it hands the decision back to me because the question is one of values rather than probabilities. Then run it on three cases: estimates spread from 0.3 to 0.7 that narrow to 0.4–0.55 once reasons are exchanged; unanimous agreement near 0.05 that everyone justifies by saying it has never happened; and two analysts whose numbers differ because one is estimating the probability of the event and the other the probability of the event mattering. Tell me plainly where the procedure returns something other than the single number the director wants, and what it structurally cannot do — in particular, what the common-prior idea gives me no way to detect. I want the procedure itself, not a reading list.
