A colleague has asked me to comment on the draft of a handbook entry on Orwell before it goes to press, and I want to be sure my objections are well founded before I send them. The paragraph I am uneasy about reads:

'Orwell's distinctive contribution to the philosophy of language is the thesis that political thought is a function of political vocabulary. In his essay on politics and the English language he argues that slovenly prose breeds slovenly thinking, that bad writing is the seedbed of bad politics, and he prescribes his six rules for writing as the remedy. In Nineteen Eighty-Four he draws out the political consequence: Newspeak will eventually make dissent impossible, because a thought for which there are no words cannot be thought at all, and thoughtcrime will therefore be not merely illegal but literally unthinkable. Whoever controls the dictionary controls what can be believed. Orwell's claim is thus a linguistic determinism, and that is what licenses his conclusion that the decay of the language is itself a political catastrophe.'

I suspect the paragraph has the mood of Orwell right and the mechanics wrong, but I could not say precisely where the slippage occurs. Please audit it claim by claim: tell me what a careful reader of Orwell would accept as it stands, what is a defensible simplification of a more hedged position, and what is a misrepresentation. Where the paragraph moves between the essay and the novel, or between what a character or a Party institution asserts and what the author is committing himself to, identify exactly what is being conflated. Finally, say whether the closing conclusion, that Orwell holds a linguistic determinism, follows from anything he actually maintains; if it does not, state the strongest neighbouring thesis that the evidence would support.
