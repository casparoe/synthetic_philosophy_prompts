I'm running a reading group that's doing The Great Gatsby next month and I want to come at it from an angle nobody in the group expects. I've been reading a lot lately about biological signalling -- Zahavi's handicap principle, the idea that a peacock's tail is honest precisely because it's a burden a sickly bird couldn't afford to carry, plus the distinction people draw between a signal (evolved because it informs) and a mere cue (a byproduct that a receiver just happens to read off) -- and the economics version of the same idea, where education functions as a costly signal of productivity rather than as training, and the old Veblen point about conspicuous consumption as status display. Somewhere in the middle of all that it struck me that Gatsby is a textbook case of costly signalling and also a really strange edge case of it, and I want a proper critical reading instead of the usual thing where we all agree he's nouveau riche and the old money sneers at him.

Here's what's bugging me specifically. Gatsby's parties are enormously wasteful, which is exactly what the handicap principle says makes a display credible, except the guests don't know him, he doesn't care about them, and the one reciever he's signalling to isn't there -- so the signal is broadcast at full cost to an audience that can't decode it and a target that never receives it. Then the shirts scene, where Daisy finally breaks down over the pile of shirts: the signal lands, but it lands as an aesthetic and emotional experience rather than as the information about quality it was supposedly carrying. And then there's where the money comes from -- the bootlegging, Wolfsheim -- which is a real handicap, genuine legal risk, spent to advertise a quality that's partly fake. If the whole point of a handicap is that it can't be faked, because only a genuinely high-quality signaller can survive the cost, Gatsby looks like a counterexample: enormous cost buying credibility for something untrue. Is he a fake signal, or is the novel arguing that in human social life the mechanism comes unglued?

What I'd like from you is a reading of the novel through this lens that takes both the book and the theory seriously -- which scenes the group should look at closely, where the handicap framework really illuminates a choice or a character, and where it breaks down and I have to admit it. I'm especially curious about the ending: Gatsby takes the blame for the accident and ends up dead, which is about the most costly thing a signaller can pay, and he pays it for a signal there's no one left to receive. Is that the one honest signal in the whole book, or is it the reductio of the entire signalling economy the novel has been describing? Tell me where I'm overreading -- I've probably got a hammer and everything looks like a nail. Don't bother with citations or page numbers, I have the text in front of me.
