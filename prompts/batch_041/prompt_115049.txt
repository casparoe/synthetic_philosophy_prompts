I'm teaching a third-year seminar on free will and criminal justice and I want to end term with a film night, but I don't want it to turn into the usual 'but does Alex really have free will' pub chat. It's A Clockwork Orange — Kubrick's film, with the Burgess novel as set reading beforehand. (I'm fairly sure the novel's twenty-first chapter, where Alex outgrows the violence on his own without anyone's treatment, is missing from the film; correct me if I've got that wrong, because I think it matters a lot.) The pairing I want is Dennett against Caruso on just deserts: Dennett thinks desert survives in a deflationary, naturalistic form — it's about being responsive to reasons, not about some contra-causal power of the will — while Caruso thinks basic desert is a fiction and retributive punishment has no justification at all. What I want is a reading of film and novel through that argument, not a film-as-illustration-of-a-position. Where is Alex, at the start, the kind of agent Dennett needs? Where is he, after the Ludovico technique, exactly what Caruso says we all already are — a creature whose behaviour is caused by things it didn't choose — and does it matter that in his case we can watch the cause being installed? What do we do with the chaplain, who objects that Alex has been turned into something that can only react and can no longer choose: is he a compatibilist in spite of himself, a Kantian, or just a sentimentalist with a good line? And what on earth is going on with the Ninth Symphony — the treatment doesn't only take away his violence, it takes away the thing he loves — is that cruelty piled on cruelty, or the novel making a point about what gets lost when someone is engineered into goodness?

A mate of mine who's a hard determinist keeps saying that if nobody ever deserves anything in the basic sense then the Ludovico technique is only objectional because it's crude and involuntary — a gentler version, with consent and better councelling, would be perfectly fine, maybe even obligatory. I think he's wrong, but every time I try to say why I end up smuggling desert back in, which is the very thing he's denying. So I'd like you to tell me whether the story has an argument against him that Dennett and Caruso between them don't quite manage — whether the horror here is really about consent and cruelty, or about something the novel can show that the philosophical exchange can't state in argument form. Don't flatten Alex into a poster boy for either side, and be honest about where the reading strains, because my students will notice. End by telling me what the book and film add to the just-deserts debate that a straight exchange of arguments misses — or say flatly that they add nothing and merely dramatise it. No reading list needed, I've got the books.
