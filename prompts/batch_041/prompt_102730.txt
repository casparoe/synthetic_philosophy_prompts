So I had a kidney scare earlier this year (turned out fine), but for about three weeks I read everything I could about the organ shortage and fell into a rabbit hole on paying donors. The thing that stuck with me: every argument I could find against letting people sell a kidney, or against price gouging after a hurricane, or against letting people sell their votes, seemed to cash out in the end to some kind of harm. Someone gets exploited, someone's judgment gets warped, a third party suffers, the poor get squeezed by their circumstances. And I kept thinking, fine, but thats just harm. Why does everyone insist there's a seperate thing, commodification, doing the real work? I've basically convinced myself there isn't — that "this good shouldn't be for sale" always reduces, on inspection, to a story about who gets hurt and how. Markets are bad when and only when they harm; "degradation," "corruption of meaning," "the wrong kind of reason" are either disguised harm-talk or empty rhetoric.

The case that pushed me over, and I still can't tell if it's a counterexample or the best evidence for me: a friend floated a business idea — you pay strangers to attend your funeral so it doesn't look empty. Nobody's obviously harmed. The dead person isn't around to mind. The family, if anything, gets comfort. The hired mourners chose it and get paid. And yet everyone I describe it to has a viscerel "thats wrong" reaction, and not because anyone got hurt. Counterexample, or just squeamishness?

What I want from you: grant me the position for now and tell me what would count against it. Not "some people have intuitions" — the actual defeaters, ranked by how much they should move me. Which cases, if any, would a defender of this view have to concede are genuine counterexamples rather than harm stories in disguise? Are there structural or formal results about when putting a price on something changes what it is rather than just what it costs — results that would show a good can be corrupted with nobody harmed? What does my view predict that the rival "some goods are inherently not for sale" camp doesn't, and is there any observation that could actually tell us apart? And be honest with me: does my view risk going unfalsifiable, since I can always redescribe any corruption as a subtle harm to somebody? If so, is that fatal, or just the price of a view like this? What would move me most is a case where the exchange is fully voluntary, no third party is touched, everyone's better off ex ante, and it still feels like something went wrong — if such a case can be built, build it. And tell me which of these the other side would themselves admit counts against them, versus which they'd just wave off.
