I'm a junior at a high school with a small philosophy club, and we spent three weeks this fall on Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes and moral responsibility. Two club members, Priya and Owen — neither of them is me — got into an argument over a case that actually happened at our school last year, and the club never resolved it. I'd like you to adjudicate it point by point.

The case: a student, Ellis, turned in a history paper with several paragraphs copied from a source nobody else in the class had used. When the teacher asked him about it, he denied it twice before admitting it. Months later it came out that he had been on a high dose of a medication that his doctor said can cause disinhibition — impulsive behavior and poor judgment — and that his dose was lowered that spring, after which the problems stopped. His home situation that year was also bad.

Priya's line: Ellis is responsible and resentment is warranted. The concealment shows this was not a mere impulse: he picked an obscure source, he rewrote the opening, he lied twice, and he confessed only once the evidence was in front of him. If impairment of that kind excused everything, nobody could be blamed for anything done while exhausted, drunk, or furious, and we plainly do blame people in those states.

Owen's line: Strawson's whole point is that whether we should blame is not settled by facts about causes or capacities, and asking whether Ellis could have done otherwise is the wrong question. What matters is whether we take the participant stance toward him or slide into the objective stance, and the moment we say that this was the medication talking, we have slid, and the resentment does not survive the slide. Priya's evidence about planning does not rescue her, because planning is itself one of the capacities the medication degraded.

Priya's reply: the objective stance comes in more than one form. Excusing someone is different from exempting him, and exemption is for people who stand outside the moral community altogether — small children, the severely mentally ill. Ellis is plainly inside it. He apologized, he said he should have known better, and the school treated it as a lapse rather than a disqualification. If one bad semester on a medication exempts you, exemption swallows the whole practice.

Owen's reply: the apology cuts the other way — an apology is an attempt to rejoin the moral community, which suggests he was never fully outside it, but that only shows that neither of Priya's categories fits cleanly; it does not show that blame is warranted. And as for what the school does, that is a fact about our practices, not a justification of them. Strawson's point is that the practices come first and the justifications are stories told afterward.

Priya's counter: then the view cannot be argued against, which makes it useless as a position. If blaming him, excusing him, and exempting him are all just things we do, Owen is not telling me what to do — he is dressing up his own reaction as Strawson's theory.

There was a last twist that neither of them could handle. After the school held a restorative conference, the teacher who had been angriest said she no longer resented Ellis, but she could not say whether that was because she had learned something new or because the conference was built to produce exactly that feeling. Priya said that if a process can make you drop an attitude and you cannot tell whether it informed you or worked on you, the attitude was never tracking anything in the first place. Owen said that proves too much, since grief can also be eased by things that do not show it was misplaced.

Please give me a point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict on who won. For each move, say whether it is right, wrong, or right for the wrong reasons, and explain what each of them is missing. Be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on Strawson and moral responsibility and which parts are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus. Write in American English.
