I am planning a seventy-five-minute session for an adult debating society on the proposition that taxation is theft. The members are intelligent but untrained in philosophy, and they arrive with the question already settled in opposite directions, which means my real task is to make them see what the question is rather than to referee their answers. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the issue. I want the order in which the components have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it; my own hypothesis is that they first need the distinction between the legal sense of theft and the moral one, and then the thought that the slogan is not a claim about tax law at all but about where property rights come from — the state either takes what is antecedently yours or else is merely altering the rules by which anything counts as yours in the first place — and I want you to tell me whether that is the right order or whether something else has to come first. Give me the one example that should carry the session, and assess my candidate: a homeowners' association whose rules you accept on purchase and which can fine you and ultimately take your house for unpaid dues. It has a taking, rules, something like consent, and an entity that is not the state, but I cannot tell whether it illuminates the tax case or merely substitutes one puzzle for another; if it fails, say what the carrying example must have and what it must lack. Tell me also whether to use at all the case the room has already heard a hundred times, the robber who takes your money and calls it a fee, and if so where in the sequence it belongs and what work it is doing there. Then map the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface — legalism, the view that it cannot be theft because the law permits it; deflationism, the view that the whole dispute is about words; economism, the view that the only serious question is what taxes do to growth; and the slide I most want headed off, from classifying a taking as theft to condemning it as unjust, which lets them treat the slogan as either a proof or a refutation when it is neither — and say exactly where in the sequence each should be defused rather than merely mentioned. Close with one question I can put to the room that would reveal whether they have grasped the structure of the question instead of having picked a side, and tell me what to leave out at this level — including whether to raise the cases that are arguably harder than taxes, such as conscription or losing land to a road — and to announce that I am leaving it out.
