I'm taking a course on the philosophy of the social sciences this term, and because I needed a methods credit I'm also sitting in on a demography course, which has turned out to be an unexpectedly good combination: the two have given me a front-row seat at an argument that has been running, in one form or another, for about a century and shows no sign of stopping. The question is whether generations are real kinds or artefacts of how we talk about people. On one side are those who treat a generation as a genuine social formation: a cohort that came of age in a particular stretch of history, was stamped by the same formative events, and consequently carries a shared sensibility into its tastes, its politics, its expectations about work and family, and even its mental health. For them, the fact that a label like Gen Z or the boomers lines up with durable patterns in survey data — a stable tilt in party identification, a distinctive posture toward institutions, a difference in how people respond to the same wage or the same housing market — is evidence that the category tracks something causally real, and that its causal power is not exhausted by age, income, or education. On the other side are the sceptics, whom I also find convincing, usually on the day I have just read them: the cut-offs are arbitrary (why 1981 and not 1979?), the labels were largely manufactured by market research and magazine cover stories, and much of what gets attributed to a generation is a period effect or an age effect wearing a costume. Their complaint is not merely that the category is imprecise but that it does explanatory work that class, education, and period effects do better, and that a society which explains its politics as generational warfare is a society that has stopped asking who owns what. Each side knows the obvious reply from the other. The defenders know the boundaries are arbitrary and answer that all classificatory boundaries are; the sceptics know that cohort replacement is a real demographic process and answer that this shows only that people born at different times differ, not that they form a generation in the loaded sense. Nobody moves, and the argument is rediscovered by each new intake of students.

My provisional hypothesis about why it stalls has three parts, and I would like you to tell me whether it is right, whether it is complete, and where it is confused. The first part is that a chunk of the disagreement is not substantive but formal: age, period, and cohort are linearly dependent, since a cohort is just a period minus an age, so with ordinary data you cannot identify all three effects at once without an additional assumption — and the additional assumption is where the real argument lives. If that were the whole story, the dispute would be a disguised statistical one and would dissolve the moment everyone agreed on an identifying constraint. But it is not the whole story, because the disagreement survives among people who understand this perfectly well. So the second part of my hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one word: one side is asking a metaphysical question, whether there is a mind-independent joint here, a real kind in the way a species or a chemical element is a real kind; the other is asking a pragmatic or methodological question, whether the category earns its keep in explanation and intervention and what it costs us to use it. If that is right, then the impasse is at least partly a failure to notice that a metaphysical answer and a pragmatic answer can both be correct without conflict. The third part is the one that actually worries me, and it is where I most want your help. If a generational label helps produce the very cohesion it purports to describe — if being sorted into a cohort changes how people see themselves, what they buy, whom they vote for, whom they marry — then the category is partly self-fulfilling, and the question whether it is real stops having the shape it had at the start. I cannot tell whether that makes the category more real (it has causal power, after all), less real (there was no joint there until the label put one there), or whether it shows the metaphysical framing was the wrong framing all along. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, a hidden formal question, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for — using what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that the impasse is structural and I should stop expecting it to end? A comparison would help me if it clarifies matters: is a generation more like a species, more like adolescence or left-handedness (categories that became real partly because people were sorted by them), or more like a pattern with no causal unity at all, such as the supposed club of musicians who die at twenty-seven? I am not asking for a reading list; I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it.
