I'm giving the opening remarks at a small two-day symposium on type design and visual perception — about forty people, roughly half of them working type designers who will definately notice if I say something glib about "the essence of a letterform", the other half cognitive scientists who work on categorisation, plus a handful of design students who've come mostly for the free wine afterwards. I want the five-minute opener to actually do some work rather than just welcome everyone, and the thing I want it to be about is the question that got me into this whole area in the first place: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? The hook I keep coming back to is that the double-storey 'a' (the one with the bowl and the arch over the top, as in most text faces) and the single-storey 'a' (the round one with a tail, as in handwriting and a lot of geometric sans) share almost no strokes — there's no common part you can point at and say "that's the a-ness" — and yet nobody hesitates, we read both as the same letter instantly, even in a font neither of us has seen before.

Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit project is at the back of my mind here — the idea of a program that's given a few letters in some style and then has to invent the rest of the alphabet in that same style — because it seems to me the whole thing only makes sense if an 'a' is a region in a space of styles rather than a shape, and a font is just one point in that space where the letter-categories get intersected. But I'm not confident I can say that out loud in front of this room without a designer in the front row asking me where the space comes from, who's allowed to draw in it, and why some points in it are more 'a'-ish than others. There's also one person in the audience whose work I've admired for twenty years and who is famous for having no patience whatsoever with theory, so I don't want to stand up there and be all airy.

So: write me the opening remarks. The question of what holds the category together has to be doing real structural work in the speech — it should shape what I can and can't claim, and it should give the thing an arc — not just be a topic I mention and drop. It has to land for the students and the plus-ones as well as the specialists, so no jargon and no assuming everyone knows what "double-storey" means (though you can teach it in a sentence). If you're going to commit to a view about what makes an 'a' an 'a' — shared geometry, a role in a system, family resemblance across a style space, whatever — commit to it and tell me what it costs me, because I'd rather be disagreed with than vague. And I'd like the last line to leave the question open in a way that feels generous rather than evasive. British English please, and keep it to something I can actually deliver in five minutes without notes.
