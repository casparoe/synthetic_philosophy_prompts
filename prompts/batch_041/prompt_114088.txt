I'm in a social choice reading group that meets weekly, and this term we're assembling a satirical supplement for a small one-day workshop we run alongside the seminar. The audience is split roughly in half: theorists who can prove the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem on a napkin, and people from civic tech and election administration who have never opened a game theory textbook. I've been handed the flagship piece, and I'd like you to write it. The premise is a deadpan corporate launch announcement from a voting-technology startup that claims to have done what the theorem says cannot be done. The theorists should be laughing by the third paragraph, at the fine print rather than at any punchline, while the practitioners should get most of the way through believing they are reading a plausible press release from a company they might actually buy software from.

The form I have in mind: a press release of roughly 250–300 words, with a dateline, an invented company and product name, a canned quotation from an invented founder-CEO, and a closing boilerplate paragraph; then the company's own FAQ, six to eight entries, each answer kept to one to three sentences so that the concessions have somewhere to hide; then a single short customer testimonial. Around 800–1,000 words in total. The comedy has to be structural, not decorative: every reassurance in the marketing copy should be quietly true, and every concession should be buried in a subordinate clause, a footnote marker, or the forward-looking-statements boilerplate. Nobody in the document may acknowledge that anything is being conceded — no winking, no scare quotes, no narrator. Invent a euphemism for the company to coin (something on the order of "opinion-robust," "arbitrated fairness," or "chance-audited") and then use it with a straight face. Never use the words "dictatorship," "dictator," "impossibility," "manipulation," or "Arrow," never name Gibbard or Satterthwaite, and never refer to a real company, agency, or living person; the piece should not be a hit on any actual voting method or reform movement, and nothing in it should suggest that a particular real-world system is secretly dictatorial.

Here is the material to build the document out of, so that you don't have to go looking for it. The theorem: any voting rule over three or more possible outcomes that always produces a single winner and is never gainable by misreporting your preferences must be dictatorial — some one voter's top choice is always the outcome. It has escape hatches, and I want the FAQ to walk through all of them as though they were product tiers. (i) Two options only: a rule that can produce just two outcomes is untouched by the result, so let the flagship product be a binary referendum tool that really is immune, with multi-candidate races handled by running an engineered bracket of two-way contests, and let a "bracket architect" appear in the org chart. (ii) Domain restriction: if admissible preferences are single-peaked — everyone placed on one left-right axis — the problem dissolves, so include an onboarding step requiring users to declare their position on a single dimension before they may vote, and have the FAQ explain that other preference shapes are "not yet supported." (iii) Randomization: drawing one voter uniformly at random and letting that voter's top choice win is the one rule that is anonymous, ungameable, and efficient in a lottery sense, but it hands the election to chance, and the dashboard reporting each candidate's win probability is computationally intractable in the serial version (draw a random ordering of voters and let each in turn pick), so the displayed numbers can only be estimates. (iv) The catch that kills the rating-based fix: the theorem generalizes beyond ranked ballots to any mechanism at all, including cardinal, graded, and approval-style ballots, so a product that abandons ranking for rating does not escape it — the failure just reappears as the optimal rating depending on what everyone else is doing. Give that product a "voting coach" feature that recommends a different rating depending on early returns, and describe it as assistance. Finally, some readers of the theorem take its proper upshot to be a "none of the above" option, a vote permitted to return no result and send the question back to deliberation; let the company offer this as a premium feature. I'd like each FAQ answer to instantiate exactly one of these, with the last one, on who decides a three-way race, hidden under the boilerplate; and the testimonial should come from a satisfied user who has worked out that her honest ballot costs her and is grateful to be told what to submit. If an answer could be deleted without loss, cut it.
