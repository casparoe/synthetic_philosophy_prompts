My dear friend Marta is retiring after thirty-five years as a literary translator, and I'm giving the toast at her dinner. The room splits right down the middle: half translators who've spent their lives on this, half engineers from the company whose machine translation system her publisher just started using "for the first drafts" — and a few of them honestly think her job is basically solved now. I want it warm, I want the engineers to feel welcome rather than dunked on, but I also want it to land one real idea: that translating isn't matching strings, it's working out what someone meant, and a system can be perfectly fluent all night without ever doing the second thing. Don't cheat by defining "understand" so that no machine could ever qualify — give the engineers their best point (all we ever see is behavior anyway) and still make the case. And build it on one concrete example so it isn't just assertion. Take "she's impossible" — impossible to live with, impossible to beat, impossible to find, impossible to forget. Same words, four different thoughts, and which one you get depends on everything around it. Show the room what the machine does with that and what a person does with it, and let that carry the argument. Five minutes, alot of it should be the example.
