States now routinely apologize for wrongs committed long before anyone in office was born — for slavery, for massacres, for forced assimilation — and churches and universities have begun doing the same for their own historical complicity. The practice is strange once you look at how it actually works. The apology is spoken in the name of the state or institution and all its current members, none of whom did the deed; it is usually addressed to descendants rather than to the dead who suffered; it is sometimes paired with money, sometimes written into a legal settlement drafted by lawyers, and sometimes worded so as to avoid the word apology altogether; the same government that apologizes for one wrong refuses to apologize for another that seems no worse; and its courts will not hold anyone responsible for conduct that was lawful when it happened. I want to know what view of responsibility this practice assumes. Does it require the state to be a genuine person that acts, persists and can be guilty across centuries, so that the present generation really is the same wrongdoer, or can a thinner view carry the whole practice, on which only people now living have duties to repair and to remember? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and where is it simply underdetermined, so that a robust collective view, a repair-and-restitution view, and a promise-to-the-future view would each license the same ceremonies? Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits comfortably with, and what would have to change if we took the individualist line that no one alive owes an apology, only repairs.
