I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and something about my job has been bugging me that I can't quite put my finger on.

We do incident postmortems and the rule is they're "blameless." It's a whole thing. When checkout falls over for four hours, the writeup that comes out is about the system: the migration that could ship with no review, the integration test nobody wrote, the alert that fired into a muted channel. No names as causes. The reason, which I agree with, is that if you go hunting for the person who screwed up you learn less and everyone starts hiding things. So the failure belongs to the situation. Except we do write things like "the on-call should have escalated sooner," so it's not that nobody is responsible, it's that the responsible one is a role and not a person.

Meanwhile the same company hires on "culture fit" and "low ego, high ownership," gives me a rating in a performance review, and the promo packet is basically an argument about what kind of engineer I am. Thats all character language — the assumption that there's a stable thing in a person that shows up on every team, in every quarter. And our blameless rule isn't even fully blameless: if you turned the alert off on purpose to stop the pages, that's on you, and people have been managed out for it. Blameless unless you meant it.

I feel like there's some old argument about whether character is even a real thing or whether situations do all the work, but I dont know the names for it. So: what is each of these two practices actually assuming about people? Does the blameless postmortem need the situation view, or is it fine with the idea that people have fixed traits and the process just doesn't care? Does hiring on "ownership" need traits to travel with a person, or is it a useful fiction we tell ourselves? Are the two things quietly contradicting each other, or is the combination coherent and I'm the one who's confused? And that "on purpose" carve-out — is that the character view sneaking back in through the side door, and could you draw that line some other way?

Then the part I actually care about: if it turns out people don't have stable traits — if what someone does is mostly a function of the situation they're in, and "thats just who she is" is a story told after the fact — what has to change here? Does hiring and promotion get rebuilt from scratch, or is there a version that survives? Do the postmortems change at all, or were they the one thing that was right all along? And which parts of what we do are actually load-bearing on a theory of character versus which parts just happen to sit next to one?

No reading list, I wont read it. Just think it through.
