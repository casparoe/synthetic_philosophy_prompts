I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on normative ethics, and I would like your help auditing an analogy that keeps doing a lot of work in a reading group I sit in on. The subject is what I have seen called moral trade: two parties whose moral priorities diverge agree on an exchange, each doing something the other weights more heavily, so that the resulting arrangement is an improvement by both parties' own lights, even though neither has been argued out of their original view. The stock illustration is a person whose overriding concern is animals and a person whose overriding concern is global poverty, each of whom would prefer the other to shift a little; they strike a bargain and both come out ahead on their own standards.

An economist in the group leans hard on an analogy from trade theory: he says moral trade is just trade, and reaches for the whole apparatus, comparative advantage included. On his telling, this shows that moral trade is not a compromise or a sell-out but a mutual gain, and that the real work is institutional, building the escrow, the contracts, the reputation mechanisms and the matching markets, after which the gains follow. He uses it especially to defuse the objection that trading away moral commitments corrupts the trader.

What I want from you is an audit of that analogy rather than a general essay on trade and morality. Give me the correspondence first, in prose, one item at a time and in an order that makes the dependencies clear: what plays the role of the trading parties, of their production possibilities and opportunity costs, of the goods exchanged, of the terms of trade, of the contract and its enforcement, and of the currency. Then the places where the mapping fails, sorted by whether the failure matters for the conclusion he wants to draw. I am particularly interested in whether gains from trade are even well defined when there is no common unit of value and no guarantee that the parties' rankings are comparable; in what happens when the exchange changes the parties' preferences, given that trade theory treats preferences as fixed and here the goods on offer are precisely the things that shape them; in who enforces a bargain when there is no state; in whether goods such as one's integrity or one's own projects can be alienated at all; and in whether the whole picture presupposes that moral disagreement is a difference in taste rather than a disagreement about a fact, since if the parties are tracking a single truth there may be nothing to trade, only something to correct. Then tell me what the analogy quietly imports that its target lacks, and whether the conclusion, relaxed about corruption and focused on institutions, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole way. If a better analogy exists, name it and defend it.

Please take the role of someone who works comfortably on both sides of this, a philosopher-economist at home in welfare economics and in metaethics, and think it through carefully before you commit to a verdict; I would rather have your reasoning compressed into the final argument than a confident summary. Write it as a letter to me: continuous prose only, with no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italics, and no tables. A correspondence table would be the natural form, but I want the correspondences spelled out in sentences, because I want to see which ones you take to be load-bearing. No citations or reading lists, please; I want the analysis, not the bibliography.
