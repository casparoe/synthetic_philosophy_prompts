I teach a graduate seminar that pairs Keynes's early philosophical work on probability with his later economics, and every year the same impasse opens up. One group reads the early work as the foundation of everything interesting in Keynes: the logical relation between evidence and partial belief, the weight of evidence as distinct from probability itself, and the refusal to treat uncertainty as a merely psychological shortfall from precise credence. For them, the later economics is applied epistemology, and fundamental uncertainty is the central concept. Another group treats the early work as a youthful project in the logic of probability that Keynes himself largely set aside, and reads the economics as a theory of effective demand, conventions, and expectations that does not need the epistemology at all. A third group, more formal, insists that Keynes's early view is best understood as a version of subjective Bayesianism with unusual features, while a fourth insists it is a genuinely non-Bayesian, non-additive, or non-measurable account. The seminar has been stuck for three years. Nobody changes their mind, and the disagreement often seems to be about what counts as the same question.

I would like a diagnosis rather than another survey or reading list. Is this impasse substantive, verbal, or a case of different groups answering different questions under one heading? My own hypothesis is that at least two distinct disagreements are being run together: one about the correct formal epistemology of partial belief, and one about the ontology and epistemology of economic uncertainty, where the real issue is whether uncertainty is a feature of the world (open-ended, non-ergodic, not exhaustively describable by a probability distribution) or a feature of an agent's evidence and decision situation. A second hypothesis is that the logical-relation reading and the subjective-Bayesian reading are not rival theories of the same thing but answers to different questions: one about the structure of evidential support, one about the norms of credence. I want you to say what kind of disagreement this really is, using what each side actually claims, and to identify any hidden empirical, formal, or normative question that is doing the work. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the impasse, a formal result that rules out one reading, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. I am especially interested in whether the weight-of-evidence idea can be made precise without collapsing into subjective probability, and whether the economics can be reconstructed without any controversial claim about the measurability of uncertainty.
