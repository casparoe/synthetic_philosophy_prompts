In a seminar on collective decision-making we keep re-running the same argument about the doctrinal paradox and never land anywhere. We all accept the formal facts: in the standard three-judge case, majorities support each of two premises while a majority rejects the conclusion they jointly entail, so a premise-based procedure and a conclusion-based procedure return opposite verdicts, and List and Pettit's impossibility result shows that no aggregation rule can be at once fully majoritarian, responsive to the logical structure of the issues, and guaranteed to produce a consistent collective judgment set. What puzzles me is that the normative question — which procedure a court or committee ought to use — has not converged, even though nobody in the room disputes the theorem. My hypothesis is that the two camps are answering different questions under one name: one side reads the phrase the group's judgment reportively, as a faithful summary of what members think about each issue, while the other reads it constitutively, as a commitment the group itself must be able to own and defend; and that once this is seen the disagreement looks verbal rather than substantive. Is that right, or is there a genuine normative disagreement underneath — about whether majoritarian responsiveness or reason-responsiveness is the primary virtue of a procedure — or else a hidden empirical question about what actually happens when groups deliberate and whether consistency matters to them? Diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicating it: say what each side is actually committed to, whether the theorem settles anything normative, and what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, or a reasoned verdict that the choice is a design decision with no fact of the matter.
