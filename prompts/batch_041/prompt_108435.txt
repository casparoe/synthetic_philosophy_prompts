You are a philosopher working on personal identity and the ethics of medical decision-making, and I want you to audit my position rather than defend it. I'm preparing a position paper on advance directives and need to know whether my commitments hang together before I stake anything on them. Begin by spelling out, for each commitment below, what it entails and what it rules out; only then judge the package as a whole, rather than assessing the items one at a time.

Here is where I've landed. (1) A competent patient's advance refusal of life-sustaining treatment should be honored even if, once demented, she appears content and now asks for the treatment. (2) The authority of the directive rests on respect for autonomy: writing it is an exercise of the very capacity that makes a person's choices authoritative. (3) Autonomy is a present-tense achievement — one is self-governing to the extent that one now governs one's life in light of reasons one now endorses. (4) I am a reductionist about personal identity: what matters is not identity but psychological connectedness, which comes in degrees and can be very weak across the decades separating the directive from the dementia. (5) A person's good consists in the success of her own aims and values, not in an objective list of goods. (6) I want to insist that the demented patient's present interests are real and can be set back — honoring the directive must not amount to pretending that the later person does not exist.

Tell me which of these are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; order the genuine tensions by severity; for each, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost; and name the single revision that would buy the most consistency for the least. Then take a case I keep circling: a directive written soon after diagnosis that contains a clause saying she expects to seem happy and to ask for treatment, and that this apparent contentment will not be her and must not be trusted. Does anything in my set entitle me to honor that clause, or would honoring it require precisely the diachronic, non-reductionist picture of the self that (3) and (4) reject?
