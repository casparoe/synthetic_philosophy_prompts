I have spent the past few years working through the minute books, correspondence and stray memoranda of a regional planning board, trying to reconstruct how it reached a handful of consequential decisions, and I have noticed a pattern that I can describe but cannot name. Three things keep happening together, and I suspect they are one thing rather than three.

First, the record is lopsided in a particular direction. The decision is always documented; the alternatives that were seriously in play usually are not. A proposal that lost tends to survive only as a single line in a letter, or in someone's later recollection, so that when I write the episode up, a choice that I know from one stray document was genuinely open until the last week reads as a sequence of deliberate steps. Second, the shape of the filing system becomes the shape of my explanation: the papers are arranged by committee and by department, and without noticing it I find that my account of why things happened has committees and departments as its agents, because those are the units the record is divided into. Third, when I try to write the episode so that it stays faithful to the uncertainty, the result reads as weak — as though I had failed to establish anything — whereas when I write it as a story with a plot, readers (and I) find it more convincing, even though the evidence is identical in the two versions. The loose ends are the first thing anyone asks me to resolve, and the resolving is usually invention.

What I want to know is whether this has a name. I have been told it is just hindsight bias, and I can see the family resemblance, but hindsight bias as I understand it is a fact about a judge's probability estimates — you know the outcome, so you report that it was likely all along — and it seems to leave out the other two strands: that the surviving evidence is itself skewed towards the settled outcome, and that the demand for a coherent story converts a skewed record into a felt necessity. It also seems to leave out the way the archive's own categories do my explanatory work for me. Perhaps I am running several distinct things together under one heading, in which case I would rather be told that than handed a tidy label.

So: is there an established concept, or a small cluster of them, that covers this? If the nearest established ideas cover only parts of it, I should like to know which part each covers and what is left over; if nothing quite fits, I should like you to coin something, define it, and tell me where its edges are — whether it applies equally when the record is complete, when the writing is a bare chronicle with no plot, and when the historian is fully aware of the problem and writes anyway. Please be honest about how much of this is a real distinction rather than my preference for a new term, and about how confident you are that this is the right way to cut it up. If the carving I am asking for is not worth making, say so plainly and tell me which of the existing concepts I should simply be using instead. Please write in British English.
