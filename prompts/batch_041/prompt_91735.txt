I'm a high school student reading a popular book on the mind, and I want to get Daniel Dennett straight before I argue about him at philosophy club. The book summarizes his multiple drafts model roughly like this:

"Dennett's central claim is that consciousness is an illusion. Because there is no single place in the brain where everything comes together — no 'Cartesian theater' with an audience watching the show — there is no inner observer, no stream of experience, and no fact of the matter about what a person is conscious of at any moment. Mental life is just a swarm of parallel processes, and a content becomes conscious only when the brain writes it up as a finished draft and broadcasts it. This is why Dennett treats the question whether an animal or a fetus 'really' feels anything as a pseudo-question: once the theater is gone, there is nothing left to ask about."

How much of this is fair? Please go claim by claim and tell me what Dennett would accept, what he would reject as a misreading, and what is not his view at all. I especially want to know whether denying the Cartesian theater commits him to denying that consciousness is real, or that there are facts about what it is like to be something, and whether the book's closing sentence follows from anything true in the rest of the summary. My instinct is that the first half is basically right and the last sentence is too strong, but I can't see where the line falls — and if the model is a framework for interpreting evidence rather than a finding, I'd like to know what difference that makes.
