I am putting together a small collection of satirical pieces that double as stress tests for formal epistemology, and I would like you to write one. The form is a deadpan customer-support FAQ, around 700 words, for a fictional subscription service called Probability Kinematics Inc., whose flagship product, Jeffrey, promises to update your credences whenever your evidence is uncertain. The FAQ should be written in the voice of the company's support team, in numbered question-and-answer format, and should never break character to explain the joke. The target is the practice of marketing formal epistemology as a frictionless life hack, and more specifically the tendency to treat Jeffrey conditionalization as a general-purpose replacement for ordinary conditionalization whenever evidence feels less than certain. The humor should come from getting the epistemology right rather than from buzzwords: entries should turn on the rigidity condition, the need to specify a partition before any update can be defined, the difference between updating on a proposition and updating on a new distribution over a partition, order-dependence and failures of commutativity, the problem of old evidence, and the fact that much real-life uncertain evidence—a hesitant testimony, a blurry photograph, a doctor's hedged diagnosis—is not naturally a proposition with a sharp probability attached. I would like at least one entry to function as a quiet reductio, for example a refund policy for updates that fail to commute or a compliance audit for customers who have violated rigidity, and at least one entry to acknowledge, in corporate euphemism, that the user may have to re-describe their uncertain evidence as certain evidence in order to make the product work at all.

After the FAQ, please add a short editorial note, clearly separated from the parody, in which you identify where the piece takes liberties with the technical material or leans on a contested reading of Jeffrey conditionalization—for instance, whether order-dependence should be treated as a bug or a feature, whether rigidity is a harmless idealization, or whether the parody unfairly conflates Jeffrey conditionalization with a particular implementation of it. In that note, also say what would change your mind about the fairness of the satire: which feature of the actual view, if it turned out to be different, would make the parody misfire. If you think the satire is unfair, say so and sketch the fairer target. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific publications; the piece should stand on its own for a reader who knows only basic probability.
