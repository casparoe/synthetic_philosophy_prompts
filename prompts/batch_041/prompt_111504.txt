I coordinate the life-detection review panel for a national planetary-science program, and I need to turn our half-formed definition of life into a procedure my panel can actually run. The panel meets roughly every two months, usually with a week's notice, and its recommendations determine whether a result is announced as evidence of life, whether a sample is escalated to higher containment, and whether a mission team gets permission to characterize an anomaly as biological. We have chemists, geologists, a planetary scientist, and one philosopher of biology; the chair is usually a postdoc, and nobody agrees on whether life is a natural kind, a cluster concept, or a stipulation we make for regulatory convenience. Our current draft, which I am not attached to, has four criteria: A, bounded self-maintenance, where the system uses energy and matter to keep itself far from equilibrium and its boundary is produced and repaired by its own parts; B, heritable variation, where a material template is copied with variation that matters to the next generation's organization; C, open-ended evolution, where the variation-and-copying loop can generate novelty without a fixed ceiling; and D, functional integration, where the parts coordinate so that persistence is a consequence of their activity and the whole can be treated as a unit. We also have a stopgap we call the anomaly test: if two independent lines of evidence fail to explain a candidate by known nonbiological processes, it becomes a life candidate for further study rather than a confirmed instance of life. I suspect this draft conflates evidence that something is alive, was alive, is a product of life, and is merely life-like, and I want the procedure to keep those apart.

Please write the procedure itself: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is metaphysical or value-laden rather than empirical. It should be usable by a chair with no philosophy training, in a ninety-minute meeting, with evidence that is often incomplete and sometimes contradictory. It should tell us how to treat remote biosignatures, laboratory synthetic replicators, dormant or fossil-like structures, and digital or simulated organisms, and it should include a short record-keeping format so we can see later why we classified something as we did. Then run it on three cases: an exoplanet atmosphere showing a strong disequilibrium pair with no other evidence; a lab polymer that replicates and evolves but only within a narrow range and needs a technician to supply its template; and a Martian microscopic structure that resembles a cell but shows no detectable metabolism or heritable variation. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like, and you should say so plainly. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and which of its steps encode a philosophical choice rather than a discovery about life.
