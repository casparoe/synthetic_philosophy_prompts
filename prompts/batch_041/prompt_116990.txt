Two colleagues of mine, Nadia and Owen, have been deadlocked for months over a case one of them invented, and they have asked me to referee; I can't, so I am passing it to you. The case: an agent is deciding what credence to have in some proposition when a demon announces that it will read off the credence she actually holds and pay her according to a proper scoring rule — a payoff schedule under which her expected payment is greatest exactly when her credence matches her evidence. Nadia says this is a clean instance of the wrong kind of reasons: the demon's offer does give the agent a reason to hold a particular credence, but a prudential reason working through her desire for money, and prudential reasons for belief are the wrong kind. That the recommended credence happens to be the epistemically correct one is a coincidence of the case, and accepting it would make epistemic justification hostage to the demon's generosity — had the demon offered nothing, the same credence would have been equally justified, so the offer cannot be what justifies it. Owen says that is too quick. With a proper scoring rule the coincidence is not accidental: the payment is an increasing function of accuracy, so the reason is aimed at the very thing — getting the truth-value right — that makes a credence correct; and the fact that the credence would have been justified anyway shows only that the demon's offer is not necessary, not that it is of the wrong kind. Nadia replies that a reason whose force depends on what the agent wants cannot be of the right kind, and notes that the demon might instead have used an improper rule — paying a bonus, say, for full confidence in a falsehood — in which case everyone agrees the reason is of the wrong kind; since the structure of the reason is the same in both cases, its properness cannot be what fixes its kind. Owen replies that this conflates a reason's kind with its verdict: in the improper case the reason is of the wrong kind and also recommends the wrong credence, whereas here it recommends the right one, and one might hold that what makes a reason of the right kind is precisely that it can be answered by the considerations that bear on correctness.

Adjudicate this move by move — who is right about what, whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, and what both of them are missing. Assess the arguments on their merits; no literature review is needed. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the case.
