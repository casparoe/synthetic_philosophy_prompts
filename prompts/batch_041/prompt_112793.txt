An argument I constructed for a seminar on food ethics has survived every objection I have been able to devise, and I have begun to suspect that the fault lies somewhere I am not looking. I should be grateful if you would diagnose it.

The argument concerns what one ought to eat, given that both livestock farming and crop farming kill animals. It runs as follows.

(1) The wrongness of an action consists in the suffering it causes, and the suffering of any creature capable of suffering counts for exactly as much as the suffering of any other, irrespective of species.
(2) Whether a death is intended, or is a side-effect of something else that is intended, makes no difference to how bad that death is for the creature that dies.
(3) Hence the right diet is the one that causes the least suffering overall.
(4) The food on a plate is answerable for two sorts of death: the deaths of the animals reared for meat, and the deaths of the small creatures — voles, field mice, ground-nesting birds, and invertebrates — killed by tillage and harvest on the land where crops are grown, whether those crops are eaten by people or by livestock.
(5) Compare three ways of obtaining a million calories. Pasture-reared beef supplies them from one or two cattle, raised on land that could not have been cropped, and requires only a little supplementary feed, so the area tilled on its behalf is very small. Chicken supplies them from several hundred birds, each of which consumed several kilogrammes of feed grain, so the area tilled on its behalf is considerable. Pulses and grains eaten directly supply them from about a quarter of a hectare of tilled land, and involve no slaughter at all.
(6) The number of small animals killed by tillage and harvest is large, and the toll scales with the area tilled; grazing and mowing leave the great majority of them alive.
(7) Therefore, of the three, the beef diet is answerable for the fewest deaths and the chicken diet for the most; and the vegan diet, which involves no killing at all, is answerable for more deaths than the beef diet.
(8) The principle generalises. It favours eating the largest animals available, since each death then purchases the greatest number of calories; it treats the conversion of arable land to pasture as a humane measure; and it counts it as a merit in a food system that a single death should feed many people for a long time.

I can accept (7) only with reluctance, and (8) I regard as absurd. I should rather keep (1) and (4) than surrender anything else. I have tried the obvious rejoinder — that the death of a vole is a lesser evil than the death of a chicken — and I cannot make it do enough work, since the field toll is an order of magnitude larger than the number of cattle, so that even a fairly small weight per death would reverse the ranking again. The accuracy of the figures in (5) is not what troubles me; assume they are roughly right.

Please identify the step at which the argument should be rejected, explain why that step nevertheless looked compelling, and set out what is lost by rejecting it — in particular, whether the same difficulty undermines the more familiar argument that runs from the suffering of farmed animals to the conclusion that one ought not to eat them, and whether anything of my argument survives once it is repaired. Write your answer in British English.
