I'm a science journalist and I'm midway through a feature on consciousness, and I've managed to get myself in a bit of a tangle. A philosopher I interviewed — sharp bloke, generous with his time — handed me this arguement and told me it's the thing that finally sinks epiphenomenalism, the view that our conscious experiences are perfectly real but do no causal work at all: the brain causes them, they cause nothing, they're just along for the ride. Here's the argument as he gave it to me, written out roughly in his steps:

1. If epiphenomenalism is true, then an experience — the felt redness of a red patch, say — has no causal effects on anything whatsoever.
2. Yet I can form the judgement, and say out loud, "I'm having a reddish experience right now."
3. For a judgement like that to be *about* the experience, or to be warranted by it, the experience has to be at least part of what brings the judgement about — a judgement can only be a judgement about X, or justified by X, if X plays some causal role in producing it. (This is the step I keep staring at, but I can't tell whether that's because it's the weak link or just because it's the load-bearing one.)
4. So if epiphenomenalism is true, my judgement that I'm seeing red is not caused by my seeing red.
5. Then either my judgement is caused by something other than the experience — in which case it isn't really about the experience and doesn't count as knowing it — or it is about the experience but is a lucky guess, which isn't knowledge either.
6. Either way, the epiphenomenalist can't know, and arguably can't even coherently assert, that epiphenomenalism is true.
7. So epiphenomenalism is self-defeating, and therefore false.

He thinks that's a knockout blow. I'm suspicious, for two reasons. First, I half-remember a reply to the effect that the experience and the judgement are both caused by the same underlying brain state, so the judgement can reliably track and report the experience without the experience itself doing any causing — but I can't tell whether that actually rescues the position or just relocates the problem. Second, and this is what's really bothering me, if step 3 is doing the heavy lifting, it looks like it proves far too much: it would seem to sink any view on which my report of a feeling isn't caused by the feeling itself, which might be a lot more views than just epiphenomenalism.

So tell me which step I should give up, and what giving it up costs. If I keep it, does epiphenomenalism actually collapse — and is being self-defeating even the same thing as being false? I'd also like you to come clean on whether this is a valid argument whose price is just steep, or whether something has genuinely slipped between the steps; and say so plainly if part of the fight turns out to be about words rather than about the mind. Please write it up in British English, and don't pull your punches — I'd much rather know where the body's buried than get a polite summary.
