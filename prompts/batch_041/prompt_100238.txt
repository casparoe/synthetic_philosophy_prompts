I am not a philosopher by training, and until this year I would have said, without much embarrassment, that insects are beneath moral notice. That changed in a way I can date. I moved into a house with a garden, spent a summer paying attention to what I was doing to it, and found that I could no longer hold the old attitude comfortably. I now relocate spiders instead of killing them and feel faintly guilty about the lawn. I am not sure whether this is a genuine insight or just sentimentality with better manners, so I have tried to write out the argument that did the work, in a form I can inspect and, if necessary, attack.

1. Human activity — pesticide application, land conversion, the production of livestock feed, and so on — bears on an astronomically large number of individual insects. The number is finite, and to within a few orders of magnitude it is knowable.
2. It is not certain that insects are sentient, in the sense of having experiences that can go well or badly for them. Let my credence that they are be p. I take p to be small but not negligible; I would put it in the low single digits of a percent rather than at zero.
3. Conditional on their being sentient, the aggregate suffering bound up in those practices is proportional to the number in (1) and therefore enormous — plausibly dwarfing the aggregate suffering of all the humans and farmed vertebrates we ordinarily discuss.
4. The expected moral weight of insect suffering is thus p multiplied by something enormous, and even a small p leaves a quantity comparable to or larger than the quantities that drive my ordinary priorities.
5. So insect welfare belongs near the top of my list of concerns, above many things I presently treat as more pressing.

I find (5) uncomfortable but I can live with it. What I cannot live with is what happens when I run the same reasoning on a different input, because nothing in (4) used the fact that the number concerned insects, or that p was a few percent, or that the harm was bounded. So:

6. For any action whatsoever, if there is some nonzero probability that it prevents or produces a sufficiently large amount of suffering or benefit, its expected value can be made to exceed that of any ordinary act.
7. Concretely: some speculative donation, or some private ritual, carries a vanishing probability of averting an amount of suffering vastly larger than a neighbour's death; on the reasoning of (4) I should prefer it to saving the neighbour.
8. There is always some such act, and no stable way to rank them against one another, so the reasoning of (4) makes deliberation impossible.
9. Therefore (4) is false.

I would rather keep (4), since (5) now strikes me as genuinely correct, and give up (6) or (7) instead — but I cannot find a principled place to cut. I gather this shape of problem is sometimes called Pascal's mugging, and the obvious candidate for the flaw is the difference between a large-but-finite magnitude and an unbounded one, or between a small probability and an absurdly small one; I cannot tell whether that difference is doing real work or is merely where the discomfort sits.

Tell me which numbered step to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs. In particular I want to know whether the cost is confined to insect-sized cases, or whether it also erodes the ordinary expected-value reasoning I rely on for insurance, medicine, and climate policy — and whether there is any formulation that keeps the scale argument for insects while blocking the slide to (7).
