Bit of an odd one. I was in a long argument on a forum about dark patterns — you know the sort of thing, countdown timers, pre-ticked boxes, a subscription you can only cancel by phoning a number — and someone ended it by saying "manipulation is just persuasion you don't approve of". His implicaton was that trying to regulate any of it is just aesthetic distaste dressed up as ethics, and that there's no principled line between the two at all. I half think he's right and half think he's cheating, and I genuinely can't tell which.

Can you lay out all the different precise things that one sentence could actually be claiming? I want a menu, not a single verdict. Some of the readings I can already see lurking:

(a) "manipulation" is a purely emotive word, so the sentence is just a report that the speaker disapproves of the thing;

(b) there's a real distinction but it's one of degree — more deception, more covertness, more exploitation of a cognitive bias — so no actual case is ever purely one or the other;

(c) the distinction is real and categorical, but it lives somewhere other than in the influence itself: in the influencer's intention, say, or in whether the target would endorse what was done to them on reflection;

(d) the whole "rational persuasion" ideal is a myth, since all influence is causal and runs through the same psychological machinery, so "just persuasion" comes out true but trivially so;

(e) it's about authorship — persuasion leaves the decision genuinely yours, manipulation makes the influencer the real author of your choice;

(f) the wrong of manipulation is a wrong to your autonomy regardless of whether you end up worse off, whereas persuasion that merely harms you is a different complaint altogether;

(g) something I haven't thought of.

For each reading I'd like: what it commits you to, whether it's true or at least defensible, whether it's trivial or substantive, and — the bit I actually care about — whether it really gets him his conclusion that regulating dark patterns is just a matter of taste. Some readings might support him, some might be true but irrelevant to what he wanted, and some might be false in a way that makes the slogan look like a conversation-stopper rather than an argument. Where a genuine manipulation/persuasion line does survive, tell me which specific feature is doing the work, and what a clever manipulator would say to land their case on the "persuasion" side of whatever line you draw.

One more thing: keep the examples concrete and ordinary — a pushy salesperson, a mate talking you into a bad film, a charity advert with a sad piano, a casino comping you a free room — rather than sci-fi mind control or hypnotism, because I think those make the whole question too easy. Ta.
