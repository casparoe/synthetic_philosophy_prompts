I am a high school junior and the student chair of our school's philosophy club. This year, as a fundraiser for the library, we are putting on a Victorian séance evening in the old assembly hall. I have been asked to give the opening remarks in character as Henry Sidgwick, the Victorian utilitarian philosopher who was one of the founders and first president of the Society for Psychical Research. The audience will be mixed: students who came for the spooky atmosphere, parents who are skeptical, a few teachers who know Sidgwick's actual work, and some people who have lost someone recently and are not sure why they came. I want the speech to be honest, humane, and intellectually serious—not a parody, not a debunking, not a sentimental lie.

Please act as a professional speechwriter and an expert in Victorian intellectual history, the history of psychical research, and Sidgwick's philosophy. Before you write, brainstorm the central tension and outline how the speech will move. Then write the full speech, about five to seven minutes long, in Sidgwick's first-person voice, but in language a modern high school audience can follow. Do not include a bibliography, footnotes, or a reading list; I will not be citing anything from the stage. Do not quote from any real text. If you need to refer to an idea, paraphrase it.

The speech should be structured around Sidgwick's actual position, as I understand it: he spent years investigating mediums and survival claims; he hoped the evidence might show that consciousness survives death, partly because he thought the question of survival was bound up with whether the universe is morally ordered; but he never regarded the evidence as conclusive and remained an agnostic. So the speech has to hold together genuine hope and genuine doubt. It cannot resolve the tension by pretending the dead are certainly present, and it cannot resolve it by mocking the people who wish they were. That tension should shape what the speech can promise, what it must leave open, and how it addresses grief.

I want the speech to turn on one less familiar idea: Sidgwick's worry that the desire for survival can corrupt the evidence, and his corresponding insistence that the ethical stakes of the question are so high that wishful thinking is not a small epistemic sin but a betrayal of the very thing he hoped for. The speech should argue that intellectual honesty is itself a way of honoring the dead. You may develop this idea as you think Sidgwick would, even if he never put it exactly that way.

Make sure the speech does real work for the mixed audience. The students who came for atmosphere should not feel lectured at; the skeptics should not feel pandered to; the grieving should not feel used. The ending should be open but not empty—it should give the audience something to hold, without claiming more than the evidence allows. Think carefully about tone: Victorian in cadence but not archaic; warm but not sentimental; rigorous but not cold.
