My buddy in my complexity theory seminar has this argument and I honestly can't tell if it's a knockdown or a mess. He says:

(1) If consequentialism is true, then in any situation the right thing to do is whichever available action has the best consequences.
(2) Finding that action means searching over possible action-sequences and their downstream outcomes. In any realistic situation that search space blows up combinatorially, so the problem is NP-hard (he says people have even shown stripped-down planning formalisms come out PSPACE-complete).
(3) NP-hard means there's no efficient algorithm that solves it in general -- that's the whole point of the P vs NP business.
(4) A human brain is a finite physical system, so it can't brute-force an exponential search over a realistically sized problem.
(5) So consequentialism can't tell any actual human what to do in any realistic situation.
(6) A normative theory that can't tell any agent what to do is worthless.
(7) So consequentialism is false.

I want to keep (4) -- pretty sure I don't have a hypercomputer in my skull -- but (7) feels way too strong, and something nags me that this thing would eat alot of moral theories, not just consequentialism. Which step do I give up, why did it look fine in the first place, and what does giving it up cost me? Be specific about exactly where the slide happens.
