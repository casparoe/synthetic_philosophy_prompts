A friend of mine, a historian of science who has spent years writing about fringe claims, has over time pronounced verdicts on a long run of cases, and I have been trying to reverse-engineer the rule she is actually running. She calls astrology pseudoscience. She calls the literal-Atlantis reading of Plato's Timaeus pseudohistory. She calls Erich von Däniken's ancient-astronaut claims pseudoscience. She calls Anatoly Fomenko's New Chronology pseudoscience. She regards the ordinary scholarly wrangling over Egyptian chronology — the radiocarbon disputes over Old Kingdom dates, the arguments about which king reigned when — as entirely legitimate. She treats the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, the claim that a comet or airburst triggered the sudden cooling at the start of the Younger Dryas, as a legitimate if hotly contested scientific hypothesis rather than pseudoscience. And she calls the claim of a lost Ice Age civilization of the sort Graham Hancock popularizes pseudohistory.

The case she has not ruled on is this. A geologist argues that the heavy erosion on the Sphinx enclosure could only have been produced by prolonged rainfall, which would push the monument's construction thousands of years earlier than Egyptologists date it; he reads the profession's indifference to the argument as evidence of a cover-up, declines to submit it to the journals that would referee it, and does not engage the archaeological and epigraphic evidence bearing on the question. The geological core of his claim — what the weathering pattern implies about past rainfall, and hence about age — is in principle checkable against paleoclimate and weathering data. What will she call it: pseudohistory, or a legitimate if probably mistaken empirical claim?

I care less about the verdict than about the machinery. Set out the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well — a testability or falsifiability criterion, a Lakatos-style criterion about whether the program is progressive or degenerating, a social or institutional criterion about whether the claim is submitted to and answers the relevant expert community, or something else — say what each predicts about the Sphinx case, give me your best guess at which rule she is running and why, and name the further case that would best tell the candidate rules apart. Throughout, be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on demarcation and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
