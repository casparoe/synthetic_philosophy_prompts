Last night I spent two hours arguing about Hilbert's hotel with a friend, and I came away with the uncomfortable realization that I know how to perform the standard moves but not how to teach anyone to see why they are the right moves. He conceded the bijection every time and refused the hotel every time: the rooms, he said, are supposed to be physical, so "every guest moves down one" is an instruction that no guest ever finishes carrying out, and the story therefore trades on a process that never completes. He also insisted he has no quarrel with an infinity you can never finish, only with one you claim to have finished. I think he is running together the structure of an arrangement with the story we tell about rearranging it, but I could not locate the distinction cleanly enough to put it to him, and now I have to.

In three weeks I am running a seventy-five-minute session on actual infinity for a reading group of twelve graduate students in philosophy and history, none of whom have mathematics beyond high school, as the setup for a term on whether the past could be infinite. I do not need the content explained to me. I need the plan for putting it across.

Give me the order in which the ideas have to arrive and why each depends on the one before it. Tell me what should carry the session. I had assumed the hotel would, and I am no longer sure; if you keep it, say what the story has to be stripped of before it can do that work, and if you would rather something else carried it, say what that example must have and must lack. The only alternative I have come up with is to carry the session with something static — two infinite lists and a rule pairing their entries — and save the hotel for the end, which I suspect is either a good idea or a way of dodging the difficulty entirely.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface in the room, with the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. I am expecting "infinity is a very large number," "the hotel must be full, so there is nowhere to put the new guest," and "the hotel is a contradiction, hence actual infinities are impossible" — but I do not know which of those is load-bearing or when the overcorrection arrives, the student who, once convinced, concludes that "the naturals and the evens are the same size" means there is no sense at all in which one collection is bigger. I would also like to know where in the sequence, if anywhere, the finitist gets to speak, and how to present finitism as a live position rather than a confusion.

Finally, one question I can put to the group in the last five minutes that would show whether they have grasped the concept rather than the trick, and an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level — the diagonal argument, cardinal arithmetic, the axiom of choice — along with the sentence I should use to refuse it.

Please write the plan in American English.
