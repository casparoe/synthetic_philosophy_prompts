I am not a philosopher and I have no Greek; I have been reading a translation of Plato's Laws on my own, a few pages at a time in the evenings, mostly because a friend told me that Plato is a killjoy about pleasure and I wanted to see for myself. What I found in the first two books surprised me. The main speaker, the Athenian stranger, spends a long stretch of the argument defending the drinking party. Wine, on his account, is a drug that dissolves the guard a man normally keeps over himself, so that a party reveals what he is really like; if the party is well run, with a sober and experienced older man in charge keeping order, the drinkers get repeated practice at holding on to their self-control, and the whole thing becomes a kind of training ground for moderation — I think he even compares it to the way fire tests gold, though I am probably flattening a much more complicated argument. He also says the lawgivers of Crete and Sparta went wrong in simply banning the stuff, because a lawgiver who forbids it outright throws away a tool instead of learning to use it well. I would like you to work out, as carefully as you can, what this Plato would have thought about a set of practices and policies that came long after him and that he could never have imagined: the modern machinery of alcohol control (state-run liquor shops, minimum prices, licensing hours, heavy taxes, and outright prohibition, which some countries tried and later abandoned); harm reduction for drug users, meaning supervised places where people consume drugs under medical watch, the provision of a legal supply of opioids to people who cannot stop, testing services that tell people what is actually in the pills they have bought, and shelters for street drinkers where alcohol is handed out on a schedule — all of it aiming at keeping people alive and healthy rather than at making them sober or virtuous; and finally the modern idea that addiction is a disease and the addict a patient rather than a criminal, together with the treatment culture built on that idea, which usually insists on total abstinence, and the ordinary rules that keep alcohol away from everyone under a certain age, including on university campuses.

Please answer as a reasoned prediction rather than a summary of the dialogues. Tell me which of these three clusters he would support, which he would oppose, and on what grounds; and be explicit about where you are extrapolating, separating what follows fairly directly from the argument of the Laws itself, what follows from his other settled views — for instance the idea I associate with him that nobody does wrong on purpose, or that the vicious man is sick and to be pitied rather than blamed, though I may have picked that up from the wrong place — and what is genuinely underdetermined, where two different readings of him would yield two different answers. One thing I would especially like you to weigh: his drinking party aims at virtue and is run by the state for the sake of the drinkers' character, whereas the modern programs I have described aim at health and survival and are usually justified in medical language with no interest at all in anyone's character. Is that difference one of aim only, or does it break the analogy — would he see a supervised consumption site as a genuine descendant of his supervised symposium, or as a different and lesser thing that has quietly abandoned the point? I do not have the texts to hand and cannot check references, so please argue in plain prose, without long quotations or step-by-step citations; I would rather have your reasoning than your apparatus, and I would rather you told me plainly when Plato simply never said enough for us to know.
