ok weird request. i'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and i've got a problem at work that my brother (philosophy degree, now does something in insurance) says is a famous problem with a name. he called it radical interpretation and said the key bit is the principle of charity and that a guy named Davidson came up with it. i definately don't know the literature and i'm not asking you for a lecture on it. i want a short story.

The situation. My employer bought the wreckage of a small freight company that went under last year. They had one in-house system, an automated dispatcher, written in the 90s by two guys who are now dead or unreachable, source code mostly lost. What we do have is five years of its ops log — about two million lines of terse codes like FROST, DELTA7, ACK-3, plus a handful of odd English words — and the company's ground-truth records: which truck went where, when it arrived, when it broke down, weather, fuel. The system talked to human drivers over a terminal and also dumped status lines into the log.

My job is to build a replacement that downstream systems, and honestly three retired drivers who still call in, will accept. Which means i have to figure out what the old system's messages meant. There's no dictionary. There's no spec. There's me, the log, and the records.

Here's the thing my brother says is the famous problem, and it's exactly what i keep hitting. I can only work out what a message means by lining it up against what was going on in the world. But i only know what was going on in the world through the log and the records, and the records are patchy. So working out the meanings and working out what the system believed about the world are the same job done at the same time — i can't do words first and beliefs second. Every time i change what FROST means i change what i have to say the system believed, and every time i decide it believed something dumb that pushes back on what FROST can mean. One knot.

And the part that actually scares me: if i let the system be wrong too often, the whole thing dissolves. The only thing tethering FROST to anything is the assumption that it was mostly right about the world and mostly sensible. If i'm willing to say it was just wrong for months at a stretch, then FROST can mean anything, and if it can mean anything i can't read a single line of the log. So the assumption isn't me being sentimental about a dead program. It's the only way in. But i can't prove it was mostly right either, and that's the bit i can't get over.

The stretch i'm stuck on is about six weeks where the system keeps sending the same short message about a truck that the records say was running fine, right through a blizzard. Three readings all fit every line i've got: (a) the message didn't mean what i assumed — its word covered a range, or something else entirely, and it was right the whole time; (b) it meant what i assumed and the system was simply wrong for six weeks because a sensor or the weather feed was busted, and the sensor logs are gone; (c) it meant what i assumed and it knew, and it was feeding a driver something misleading on purpose. Nothing i've found kills any of the three. And it bugs me that i keep using my own vocabulary to describe its distinctions — i keep assuming it drew a line where my system draws one (planned vs assigned, say), and every time i drop that assumption the log gets a bit more readable.

So: write this as a short story, 1200 to 1800 words, first person or third person, whichever works better.

- The concept has to show up in what the character does — her notebook, an argument with the guy who owns the records, a phone call with her brother — not in a lecture. If someone names the philosophy thing, one throwaway line max.
- Let the reader actually feel the underdetermination. Spell out at least two readings of that six-week stretch in enough detail that the reader can see both fitting the same data, and show her hunting for something that breaks the tie and failing.
- The ending should be about her having to choose, and about the charity assumption being non-optional rather than virtuous. I'd rather it land on the choice than on a twist.
- No deus ex machina. Nobody finds a surviving developer, a spec, or a rosetta stone. She doesn't get certainty.
- Don't make it about whether the system was conscious or alive or had feelings. That's a seperate story and i don't want it.
- It shouldn't end with everything working out. She ships the manual, but the reader should know she shipped an interpretation, not a discovery.
