I am a novelist three weeks out from a copyedit, and the book I am finishing has a protagonist who has undergone a corpus callosotomy — the surgical severing of the fibres between the hemispheres — yet it is narrated throughout in the first person singular. The conceit I have committed to is a margin: on many pages her left hand writes, uninvited, in the white space beside the text, and the reader is left to wonder who is speaking there and whether that writer knows what the narrator knows. My working rule has been crude: where the two hands disagree, the book speaks as two; where they agree, as one. My editor says the result reads as an alternation I invented rather than one I discovered, and my continuity reader has found scenes in which the narrator claims knowledge that my own account of the character forbids her.

What I want is not a verdict on how many minds such a patient has, but a decision procedure I can run scene by scene — short enough to keep to one page, plain enough to hand to a first reader who knows no philosophy, so that the same scene gets the same treatment on every pass. I understand the dispute turns on what unity of consciousness is supposed to require: co-consciousness, joint access to a single store of information, one agent of action, integrated memory, the capacity to report. I am content to have you take a line on that, provided you tell me which line you have taken, why, and which of those requirements you kept and which you discarded to make the thing usable. I make this decision perhaps forty times a chapter, alone, at speed; my editor signs off on the whole, and there is no neuroscientist I can consult.

Each scene should yield three answers: how many subjects are present, which grammatical person and number the narration may therefore use, and whether the margin-hand may be privy to what the narrator knows. Give me the questions in the order I am to ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict, and where it hands the question back to me because the question is metaphysical or moral rather than empirical. It must survive at least these cases: the two hands reaching for different objects; a fluent verbal explanation of an act the speaking hemisphere did not initiate; a scene in which the halves agree, and another in which they agree by accident; both hands writing at once, one typing and one in longhand; a scene under anaesthetic or in sleep; a scene in which each half holds a memory the other lacks; a scene in which the two halves differ about a person they both love; and a scene in which the character's own testimony about herself is itself the product of one hemisphere's manufacture.

Then run it on two scenes I will sketch for you: one in which her signature on a contract is challenged, and one in which she is asked to forgive someone. I expect one of those applications to come out against me, and I want to be told plainly if the first person singular cannot survive the procedure, and where it breaks. Finish by naming what the procedure cannot decide, the cases in which it will misfire, and the sentence I should copy into my own working notes to remind myself that any question the procedure refers back to me is mine to answer and not a finding.
