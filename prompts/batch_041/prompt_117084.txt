The question was Q3, worth 20 of the 100 points:

Q3 (20 points). The accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism is often advertised as a purely epistemic vindication of the demand that our credences obey the probability calculus.
(a) Reconstruct the argument, saying explicitly what it assumes about (i) the scoring rule used to measure accuracy and (ii) what the agent herself cares about.
(b) Is the argument unconditional — does it apply to any agent, whatever her values? Explain.
(c) Does the same style of argument extend to conditionalization: does it show that an agent who fails to conditionalize on her evidence is accuracy-dominated? Explain.

Here's the answer:

(a) The argument starts with the idea of a scoring rule: a function that takes a credence function and a world and returns a number saying how accurate those credences are at that world. The central result is that any credence function that violates the probability axioms is dominated by some coherent, probabilistic credence function — in every possible world, the coherent one scores better. The scoring rule has to be strictly proper for this to go through, i.e. each probability function has to uniquely maximise its own expected score. What the argument delivers is an epistemic reason to be coherent: incoherent credences aren't only bad because they leave you open to a Dutch book, they're bad because there's a credence function you could have instead that is guaranteed to be more accurate.

(b) It's unconditional, and that's the main selling point. Dominance is a sure-thing argument: you do worse in every world, not merely on average, so no assumptions about the agent's goals or about what she's trying to do are needed. Even an agent who doesn't care about accuracy is irrational if her credences are incoherent, since she's then in a dominated position and rational choice theory forbids choosing dominated options. This is what makes it a genuine advance over the Dutch book argument, which only shows that you'd be exploitable if you were willing to bet at the implied odds.

(c) The same argument extends to conditionalization. Given a strictly proper scoring rule, an agent who fails to conditionalize on her evidence is dominated by one who does — conditionalizing gets her closer to the truth, whatever the world turns out to be. In the literature this is usually put in terms of expected accuracy instead, but I take that to be the same point restated, since dominance is just expected accuracy in the special case where one option wins everywhere. So the framework gives us both probabilism and conditionalization, and it does so for any strictly proper scoring rule, so neither result is an artifact of the Brier score.
