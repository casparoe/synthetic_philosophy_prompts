Our school's philosophy club has spent this term on decision problems — cases in which what you receive depends on something you cannot influence, so that the ordinary advice to do whatever brings about the best outcome stops being obviously right. A friend of mine in the club has answered every case we have raised, always without hedging, and I have begun to suspect that her answers are not separate hunches but the output of a single rule. I want to know what she will say about a case we have not discussed, and I would rather learn the rule than the answer. I will describe the earlier cases in full instead of using the club's shorthand, since the shorthand will mean nothing to you.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A being that has correctly predicted your choice in one hundred previous cases has already filled one of two boxes: if it predicted that you would take only that box, it has put a million dollars in it, and otherwise it has left it empty. The second box holds a thousand dollars either way. You may take both boxes or only the first. She takes only the first.

2. A coin has already been flipped. If it came up heads, a being will appear and ask you for a hundred dollars, and you may give it or refuse. If it came up tails, the same being will instead give you ten thousand dollars, but only on the condition that you would have handed over the hundred in the heads case. The being appears only in the heads case, and you know all of this in advance. She hands over the hundred.

3. You are stranded in a desert. A driver will take you to town only if you promise, before getting in, to pay him a hundred dollars on arrival. Nothing can hold you to the promise, and once you arrive you may refuse with no consequence. She pays him.

4. You play one round of the prisoner's dilemma against someone who, you are certain, decides exactly as you do — not a friend of yours, simply a person whose reasoning mirrors your own. She cooperates.

5. The same game against a stranger who will certainly defect. She defects.

6. A version of the first case in which the being's record is exactly fifty-fifty. She takes both boxes.

Now the case I want to put to her. A laboratory offers you a choice between two buttons. The red button pays you a hundred dollars and the blue button pays you nothing. Ten years ago, however, the laboratory ran a study on a randomly chosen half of your town. Everyone in that half received fifty thousand dollars, and was also given a drug that, in about nine people out of ten, produces a strong preference for pressing blue buttons. The drug does nothing else, and you do not know whether you were in that half. Sitting in front of the buttons, you notice that you feel a strong pull toward the blue one. She has not seen this case.

I would like three things. First, what will she say to do? Second, what rule or rules fit her six verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself, and what does each rule predict about the buttons? Third, if more than one rule fits and they disagree here, which one do you think she has actually been running, how confident should I be, and is the case I have described the cleanest way to find out, or is there a better one?
