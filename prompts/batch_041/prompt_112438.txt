I retired two years ago after a long career in experimental particle physics, mostly detector instrumentation, and I have been spending my retirement reading philosophy — with more difficulty, I confess, than I anticipated. I would like a careful explanation of one specific problem in the metaphysics of persistence, and I would rather have depth on that problem than a survey of the field.

The perdurantist picture — that a persisting thing is extended in time and has temporal parts much as it has spatial parts — is more or less what I already believe, since thirty years of drawing worldlines make it hard for me to hear the phrase "wholly present at a time" as anything other than a manner of speaking. But I distrust my own intuition and want to know what the argument is actually worth. The argument I keep encountering concerns what are called temporary intrinsics: an object that is spherical at one time and cubical at another appears to have two properties that cannot both be had, and if the object is wholly present at both times, the contradiction cannot be escaped by parceling the object into parts. The escape usually offered is to treat shape not as a property of the object but as a relation between the object and a time — whereupon, it is said, every property becomes relational and the distinction between intrinsic and relational properties collapses. I have seen this argument credited to David Lewis, though I cannot now recall where I read it.

What I would like is the following.

First, the argument stated as carefully as it can be, in a form that does not already presuppose that properties must be had simpliciter rather than at times. I want to know exactly which premise does the work, and what becomes of the argument if that premise is denied.

Second, an honest evaluation of the endurantist's options, above all the move of indexing shape to a time. Why is that supposed to be a cost? If the cost is only that it contradicts ordinary conviction, the objection seems weak; if there is a stronger reason, I want it. I would also like to know whether the remaining options — I have a vague sense that one of them is called the adverbial approach — are substantive positions or merely redescriptions, and what would distinguish the two.

Third, and here my own background makes me suspicious: in physics, no property is ever ascribed to anything except at a location in spacetime. Field values, densities, and temperatures are functions of coordinates; a rod's length is not had by an object in the abstract. Is the observation that all property ascriptions are already indexed a solution to the puzzle, a dissolution of it, or a confusion? The puzzle also appears to have a spatial analogue: a wall is red at one end and blue at the other, and nobody supposes this requires an exception to the law of non-contradiction. If spatial variation is handled by spatial parts without embarrassment, what exactly licenses the endurantist to refuse the analogous treatment of temporal variation? If the answer is the phrase "wholly present," I would like to know what work that phrase is doing that is not merely stipulative.

Fourth, as someone whose working life was governed by the relativity of simultaneity: does endurantism require a privileged notion of simultaneity — a fact about which parts of an object's history count as the present — and if so, how much weight should that carry in the debate?

Please finish with a verdict: whether the problem of temporary intrinsics gives perdurantism a genuine advantage, or whether it leaves the two views merely differently burdened.
