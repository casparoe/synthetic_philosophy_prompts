Write a dialogue between two philosophers, both fully competent and neither of them a strawman, who are trying to settle a concrete disagreement about self-locating reasoning. Call the first A, a defender of the self-sampling assumption -- the view that you should reason as if your position among observers were randomly selected, so that facts like your birth rank or your sequence number carry evidential weight. Call the second B, a defender of the self-indication assumption -- the view that the bare fact of your existing makes hypotheses that entail more observers proportionally more likely, so that sheer numbers of observers count in a hypothesis's favor.

Give them the following scenario to work through, and make them actually work it out with the numbers rather than gesturing at it. You come to self-awareness with no memories; a plaque informs you that you are Person number 50,000,000,000 in a sequence that has been assigned in order beginning from 1. Three rival theories about the sequence's total length are on the table, and stipulate for the sake of argument that they begin with equal prior probability: Short (the sequence ends at 10^11 persons), Long (it ends at 10^13), and Vast (it ends at 10^30). A should argue that your rank is genuine evidence -- that observing number 5×10^10 is a hundred times more likely if the sequence is Short than if it is Long, and again a hundred times more likely if Long than if Vast, so the observation should drag your credence sharply toward Short. B should reply that existence, not rank, is the datum: Vast posits enormously more observers, so your existing at all is correspondingly more likely under Vast, and this boost exactly cancels the rank evidence, leaving the priors where they started. B must then confront the consequence that, once the numbers are large enough, self-indication makes the theory with the most observers overwhelmingly probable, so that one can settle a large empirical question from an armchair. Do not let B escape that consequence by hand-waving: make B either defend it, bite the bullet, or locate a principled place to stop, and make A press the point until one of those happens.

The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: each character should respond to the other's actual moves, press the weakest joint in the other's position, and concede what has to be conceded rather than talking past the opponent. Have them distinguish the places where they substantively disagree from the places where they merely differ over what the relevant reference class is, and have them say what becomes of the Doomsday-style conclusion -- that your modest rank is evidence against a long future -- if B is right, and whether A can avoid a result just as unwelcome arising from the arbitrariness of the reference class. Neither should win by fiat. If they cannot reach agreement, end with each stating precisely what evidence or principle would change their mind.

After the dialogue, add a brief closing note, in your own voice, that separates what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on self-locating reasoning, marks which of the claims made are minority positions rather than the received view, and flags any point at which a character has been made to take a definite line on a question that is in fact open. Do not present any contested position as the consensus.
