Famine is now, in institutional practice, a technical classification rather than an event. An international partnership grades each crisis area on a five-point scale running from minimal to catastrophe and reserves the top category, famine, for places where a triad of thresholds is met at once: roughly a fifth of the population in extreme hunger, about a third of children acutely malnourished, and a death rate that has roughly doubled, past two deaths per ten thousand people a day. The judgement is made by analysts working from survey, market and satellite data — food prices and wages, terms of trade, the coping strategies households report, children's arm measurements — and is then verified by a committee of outside experts whose stated mandate is to reason from technical and scientific criteria alone, insulated from the interests of the governments concerned. A parallel machinery of anticipatory action commits money in advance and releases it automatically when a forecast crosses a pre-agreed trigger — a forecast rainfall deficit, a river level, a drought index — precisely so that no one has to decide, in the moment, whether things are bad enough.

What theory of famine does this arrangement presuppose? I am interested in how the practice settles, without ever asking, the question whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes. Reconstruct the implicit view: about what causes famine, about where human agency enters the picture, and about the relation between the analyst and the state. Notice that the classification's own vocabulary is hazards on one side and vulnerability on the other, and that its indicators describe what happens to the affected population — consumption, asset depletion, coping — so that political action, where it appears at all, appears as a property of the victims rather than as the act of an agent. Is that a presupposition of the practice or merely an artifact of its instruments? Say which rival views the arrangement quietly rules out, and where it is underdetermined: whether the same machinery could be operated by someone who held that famines are failures of entitlement rather than of supply, or that they are crimes. Then tell me where the parts sit awkwardly together. The triggers are meteorological in a period when the agencies themselves say the dominant driver is conflict. The relief that follows must be negotiated with governments that are frequently a party to the outcome. And the declaration of famine is itself consequential, which makes the technical insulation of the judgement look less like a fact about the world than an achievement maintained against pressure. Finally, suppose the political reading is right about the cases usually cited — Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, and the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward — where contemporaries and later historians have assigned much of the causation to procurement, export, requisitioning, denial of access and suppressed reporting. What would the practice have to become? Could a trigger be political? Could money be pre-committed against the will of a sovereign? And what would be lost, in authority, in access and in neutrality, if the threshold were openly an attribution as well as a measurement?
