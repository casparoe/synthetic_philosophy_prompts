I teach a type-design studio and have spent the past two years sitting in on a cognitive science seminar, and there is one question I cannot get either group to answer to my satisfaction: what exactly makes a shape an 'a'? Both my students and my colleagues tend to answer with a shrug and an appeal to intuition — you simply see when an 'a' has gone wrong — and I have come to suspect that the intuition is tracking something real for which neither discipline has a decent account. The obvious answers all fail on contact with the material. The 'a' of a text face has two storeys and an enclosed bowl; the 'a' of an italic, of most handwriting, and of a coarse pixel grid has one; the Greek alpha and the Cyrillic 'а' can be given outlines identical to a Latin 'a' without becoming Latin letters; 'b' and 'd' are reflections of one another and are plainly distinct letters; 'M' and 'W' are rotations of one another in most faces and are also distinct; and 'Q' is 'O' plus a tail that in some designs is little more than a flick of ink. Letter identity therefore survives the loss of topology, of orientation, and of almost any feature one might want to name, while being defeated by changes that look trivial from the outside.

There is a computational setup I find useful for stating the puzzle, because it forces the two pressures apart. Douglas Hofstadter's group at Indiana, working on computational models of analogy-making, once built a program that had to design an entire alphabet at once on a coarse grid, under two demands simultaneously: every shape had to be recognisably the letter it stood for, and all twenty-six had to look as though a single hand with a single set of habits had drawn them. Call the first demand the letter's identity and the second its style, and the interesting thing is that neither can be written down in advance as a list. I have been drafting the questions the setup seems to open up, and I would like your help in working out which of them deserve a serious effort. Roughly: (1) the metaphysics — is a letter an artefactual kind whose extension is fixed by convention and history, or does the human visual system supply the essence, and what sort of evidence could settle the matter? (2) the representational format — are letters held as features, prototypes, or templates plus transformations, or relationally, a letter being constituted by the contrasts it stands in with its neighbours in the alphabet, much as a phoneme is constituted by its contrasts within a phonological system? That last option would make the double-storey and single-storey 'a' a case of allographic variation, and would explain why 'Q' needs a tail only because 'O' exists. (3) style — is a style a vector of independent parameters such as weight, contrast, serif shape and x-height, or a second-order pattern, a consistent way of making the same decisions across all twenty-six letters, and how would one individuate styles, or decide whether a new face is a revival, a variation, or its own thing? (4) vagueness and degree — what is to be said about a shape that is seventy per cent of the way to being an 'a', or about the logotypes and brand marks that are read as letters without being any, and is letterhood a threshold, a family resemblance, or merely the reader's disposition to read? (5) the alphabet as a system — why alphabets have the inventories they do, whether there are sets of letterforms a human visual system simply cannot be taught to keep apart, and how much of the constraint is perceptual rather than historical, the awkward datum being that the Latin alphabet happily uses mirror pairs ('b' and 'd', 'p' and 'q') and rotation pairs ('n' and 'u', 'M' and 'W') as distinct letters, which suggests the constraints are not the obvious ones. (6) the ladder of abstractions — character, glyph, allograph, letter, case — with the awkward questions of whether 'A' and 'a' are the same letter, whether a small capital 'A' set in running text is the same letter as the 'a' beside it, and which rung of the ladder identity should live on. (7) the empirical question — how readers actually represent letters, and whether the answer constrains (1) and (2) or merely runs alongside them. And (8) the normative question — what makes a letterform well-formed and a face good, and whether that can be separated from identity at all, since a shape can presumably be a bad 'a' while remaining an 'a'.

The group I have in mind is small and fixed: three of us, one year, roughly a day a week each — myself, a psycholinguist who works on visual word recognition and has access to a modest participant pool, and a doctoral student who builds computational models of analogy-making and has a year of machine time but not much of it. We would like to finish the year with one or two pieces of work that a design school and a cognitive science department would both take seriously, and I would rather spend the year on the right question than on a merely interesting one. So: which two or three of these should we take up first, and why? I am far less interested in the shortlist than in the reasoning that produces it — for each candidate, what it is upstream of, how far three people with these particular skills could get within a year, whether it is genuinely neglected or merely unfashionable, and what we might learn in the first few months that would move it up or down the order. I should also like to be told which of my eight are in fact downstream of something else, or are not research questions at all but matters of taste dressed up as questions. And if there is a question I have not listed on which most of the others quietly depend, I would rather you put that first and explain why, even if it is not the one I would have chosen. I do not need a reading list or references — assume I can find the literature myself, and give me the argument instead.
