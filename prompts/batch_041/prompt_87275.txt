I teach a final-year seminar called Rationality and Choice — 16 students, mostly philosophy with a few from econ — and I get exactly one 90-minute session for money pumps and diachronic rationality. Every year I lecture at them about cycles and transitivity, their eyes glaze over, someone asks "but why can't I just have intransitive preferences?", and we go round in a circle of our own. I want the session to be an activity instead: they get pumped, and only afterwards do they find out what hit them. No lecture up front.

Here's my rough shape, take it or throw it out. Round 1: each student holds one item out of three, and the items (or their scoring rule) are set up so their own choices cycle. I've seen this done with bundles scored on three attributes where you just go with whichever bundle is better on more of them, which seems to reliably give A beats B beats C beats A — but you tell me if there's a cleaner mechanism, because I need the cycle to show up for everyone, not just the people who happen to have weird tastes. Then a couple of students act as brokers and post standing offers, with a small fee per swap. Let it run ten minutes and count how many times anyone comes back to where they started, minus fees. Round 2 has to be the genuinely diachronic half: rewards that pay off at different times, a break in the middle, and a broker quoting round-1 rates at the point where people's preferences have shifted. I also want them to have a real chance to defend themselves — a precommitment option they can buy, and the option to refuse to enter at all — so we can see who resolves, who plans backwards, and who just gets taken.

What I want from you: the full runnable thing — materials, rules, the payoff scheme (points, no real money, but there's a small prize at the end so they actually care), roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and the debrief. The debrief should map moments in the game onto the actual structure of a money pump — the cycle, the fee, the trader, the premise that every single step is an improvement by the agent's own lights — and then onto the escape routes: insisting on transitivity, resolute choice, sophisticated choice, and just denying that anything has gone wrong. For each escape, say what it costs, and say which one you'd take. Please don't hand me four variants and let me pick; commit to one design and defend the choices you made in it.

Two more things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm definately expecting at least one group that never gets a cycle, and someone who announces the broker is cheating — and how to handle it when it happens. And be straight with me about what the activity can't do. My suspicion is that it can't get at the version of the pump where the agent's preferences are static and it's the trader's information, or the agent's own uncertainty about their future choices, doing the work; and that by stipulating a broker who will trade at the posted rates we've given away the very thing the argument is supposed to establish. If that's right, say so plainly and tell me what I have to supply myself in the last ten minutes.

You're a philosopher who's taught decision theory for years and has run this sort of thing before — think it through properly, and feel free to brainstorm a couple of mechanisms before you settle on one.
