OK so I'm a novelist, I write historical fiction, and something's been nagging at me that I think is actually a philosophy question and not just a publishing question, so bear with me.

Here's the practice. Everywhere I turn, the same institutions slice the past into periods and then act like the slices are real. My publisher's historical-fiction list has categories — Medieval, Renaissance, Enlightenment, Victorian — and my editor genuinely couldn't tell me whether my book (set in 1490, in Bruges) belonged in the medieval slot or the renaissance one; she said "ask marketing." The museum two blocks from my flat has basically the same rooms and the same problem at the doors — the docent told me the Renaissance "starts" in a different year than the one in my old textbook, and shrugged like that was normal. The university where I teach a workshop has a Medieval Studies center and a Renaissance one, and I have friends who don't know which to join because their archive straddles the line. And everybody keeps telling me the boundaries were invented — the Renaissance was basically a nineteenth-century sales pitch, the Middle Ages got named by people who wanted to feel superior to it — and yet the same everybody keeps using the boundaries as if they carve nature at the joints.

So my question: what does this whole practice actually presuppose about historical change and about whether periods are real? I can see it needs something — you can't shelve a book or hang a gallery or hire a medievalist without pretending the line is somewhere — but I can't tell what. Does it need periods to be real kinds, or does it just need them to be useful fictions, or is there some middle thing (periods as real patterns with fuzzy edges, like a coastline)? Does it need historical change to be lumpy — long stretches of sameness interrupted by breaks — or is it compatible with everything changing all the time and us just drawing lines for convenience? And does it secretly need each period to have one unifying character, some spirit of the age that the painting and the politics and the plumbing all somehow share? Because honestly the reason my editor couldn't place my 1490 book is that the "Renaissance" as a thing seems to be about art and learning in Italy, and Bruges in 1490 is doing something else entirely, and the label is doing two different jobs at once.

Tell me which parts the practice strictly needs and which parts it just happens to sit on top of, and where it's actually underdetermined — where two totally different theories of historical time would both license the same shelving. Then: if we dropped the real-periods reading and went full these-are-just-our-labels, what in the practice would actually have to change? Would the museum have to be rearranged, would it turn out to run on the fictions just fine, or is there some third thing it's been quietly assuming all along that neither story captures?

I don't need a reading list, I just want to know what I've definately been signing up for every time I tick the Medieval box on a form.
