For several years I have taught a seminar that reads lives with an eye to what they do to the reader: a few of Plutarch's paired biographies, two or three Victorian lives, a modern memoir, and a selection of saints' lives. Plutarch opens the *Alexander* by saying that his design is not to write histories but lives, and that "a matter of less moment, an expression or a jest, informs us better of their characters and inclinations, than the most famous sieges, the greatest armaments, or the bloodiest battles whatsoever." My seminar keeps confirming him, and I keep meeting a pattern I cannot state cleanly. I would like either the established name for it or a carefully carved new one, and I care much more about the carving than the label.

The first half of the pattern: the lives that visibly change my students are the ones that yield no lesson. A student will tell me that the life of some minor figure undid something in her; I ask what she will do differently, and she either cannot answer or produces something that is a platitude ("be braver about small things") or a counsel of perfection ("give everything away"). Meanwhile the improving biographies — the ones that close with a chapter on what the subject's life teaches — leave the room flat. The force seems to live in particulars that do not generalize: the way a man took a snub at his own dinner table, the fact that a woman kept one particular letter and burned the rest. Say "courage" and nothing happens; describe the act and something does.

The second half: when I try to repair this by doing the generalizing myself, extracting the principle the life illustrates and setting it beside the story, the power dies in the extraction. The principle need not be false; it simply is not where the power was, and offering it seems to displace the life rather than condense it. My students are then left holding a principle with no motive, and I have taught them nothing.

I recognize the neighbourhood, but none of the landmarks is what I am after. Literary critics have a slogan to the effect that a poem's meaning does not survive being restated in prose. There is a recent moral theory that begins from admiration of exemplars rather than from principles. There is the long Aristotelian line about practical wisdom being knowledge of particulars. And there is something in the hagiographic tradition, which I only half recall, about the difference between copying a saint's deeds and acquiring the saint's disposition. Each is close in a different direction; each seems to leave out the specific thing I keep meeting.

So: is this one phenomenon or two — a fact about how narratives work plus a separate claim about what moral knowledge is? Is the ungeneralizable particular doing epistemic work, so that this is how we come to know the good at all, or only motivational work, on the assumption that we know the good already and the particular merely moves us to pursue it? Would a life that generalizes cleanly and still moves people falsify the pattern, or only show that it is a tendency rather than a law — and what about a stated principle that does move someone to act? I would also like to know how the pattern differs from (a) the plain fact that stories persuade where arguments do not, (b) moral particularism, which I take to be a thesis about the structure of reasons rather than about pedagogy, and (c) the ordinary thought that we learn by imitating role models. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and tell me what it rules out, including what a case of successful moral learning from a biography would have to look like for the term not to apply.
