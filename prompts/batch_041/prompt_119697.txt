I chair the pharmacy and therapeutics committee at a mid-size teaching hospital and I have to circulate a written opinion before Wednesday's vote. We're deciding whether to adopt a new one-off screening scan for a defined risk group. The benefit is real but modest -- a few hundred lives system-wide over ten years. The harm side is the mess: the chance of a serious complication from the follow-up biopsies comes out anywhere between about 1 in 1,300 and 1 in 300, and its not sampling noise -- it's three studies that disagree about the mechanism, and nobody can tell me which one is right. Our internal policy just says to weigh expected benefit against expected harm, which everyone apparently wrote assuming we'd have exactly one number on each side. And the studies that give the higher complication rate also tend to give the smaller benefit, so the bad end of one lines up with the bad end of the other, which makes the usual "just take the midpoint" move feel like a dodge. Two members are dug in: one says you can't average away a real disagreement about the mechanism and we should plan around the bad end; the other says if we only ever act on the worst case we'd never adopt anything and the status quo kills people too. We do have a middle option -- adopt for the highest-risk subgroup only, with a registry, so it's reversible. Draft the memo I'll read out: it should land on a concrete recommendation, give the reasoning, and treat the losing side honestly rather than just reciting the studies. Also tell me what would change your mind -- which finding or argument would flip the recommendation, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
