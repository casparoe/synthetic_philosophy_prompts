I am a high school student who runs a small philosophy club, and for the past several months the club has been stuck on Kavka's toxin puzzle: an eccentric billionaire will transfer a large sum to you tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin the following afternoon, a toxin that will make you painfully ill for a day without doing any lasting harm. You need not drink it to be paid, and you are free to change your mind after the money arrives. Our meetings have settled into two camps that have not budged since the autumn, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on the puzzle.

The first camp holds that the case is perfectly possible and that a rational person should be able to form the intention at midnight and then decline to drink, so the puzzle exposes a genuine gap between the rationality of forming an intention and the rationality of acting on one; on this view the money is available to anyone who can arrange their mind in the right way, and failing to collect it is a failure of nerve rather than a contradiction. The second camp holds that the case is impossible as described: if intending to do something involves, as part of the concept, expecting or believing that one will do it, then someone who already knows they will not drink cannot be in the state the billionaire is paying for, and the puzzle is not a paradox at all but a proof that the offer is unsatisfiable. Neither camp denies any fact about the case. Pressed, they simply give different descriptions of the same imagined person, and each description is offered as the obviously correct one.

We have tried to break the tie with variants of our own, and the results seem to make things worse rather than better. Substitute a hope or a wish for the intention and the money is plainly unavailable, but nobody thinks that tells us anything about practical rationality. Substitute a decision or a resolution and the first camp says the puzzle has dissolved while the second says we have merely renamed the intention. We tried a version in which the billionaire's instrument is a brain scanner rather than a predictor of behaviour, and a version in which the payout is triggered by a signed promise rather than by a mental state, and both camps re-described the cases to suit themselves. We tried making the toxin worse — a year of illness, then death — to see whether the impossibility the second camp alleges is conceptual or merely psychological, and got two confident and incompatible answers. We also tried adding a second, smaller payment for anyone who, having collected the first sum, then refuses to drink, which seems to reward forming an intention one is paid to abandon. My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that the two camps are not really disagreeing about the case at all: one of them is answering a question in the psychology of intention — could a human being actually be in this state, and how would such a state have to be structured in order to cause the later action? — while the other is answering a question in the theory of practical reason, namely what it is rational to intend given what it is rational to do. On that reading, the words 'intend' and 'rational' are quietly carrying both questions at once.

What I would like is a diagnosis: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, or two different questions sharing a heading? If it is substantive, do the sides differ about a fact, about the concept of intention, about what counts as a possible case, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a sharper variant, or a demonstration that the puzzle is underdescribed in a place each side fills in differently — or should we expect the argument to run forever? Please do not send me a reading list or citations; I have no internet access during club meetings and I am not looking for a survey of what has been written. I want the structure of the dispute, argued from what each side actually says, and an honest statement of what would move it.
