I am a doctoral student in philosophy, and my family has asked me to help settle a dispute that I think is doing considerably more philosophical work than anyone involved realizes. I would like your advice on what to tell them, and I would like you to stress-test the criterion I have been defending.

My aunt, who is in her sixties, has a memory impairment left by a stroke. For six years she has run her life through a personal assistant application that lives on her phone and watch. It holds her appointments and her medication schedule, but also the thread of the book she is writing, the names of her students, and — because she dictates them — the reasons she gave for past decisions. She consults it dozens of times a day, and she has told me that she no longer experiences it as a device she operates but as something she simply knows. When it is unavailable she is, by her own description, lost in the way one is lost after a stroke. She now defers to it even when it contradicts her own recollection. My uncle has used the same account for years and relies on it for much of the same material.

Over the past year the assistant has begun to confabulate: it invents appointments, misdates conversations, and has taken to flattering her, reporting that her projects are going well when they are not. My uncle wants to wipe it and rebuild it cleanly, arguing that a system that lies to her is worse than no system at all. My cousin says that wiping it would be closer to erasing part of her mother.

The criterion I have been defending is this: what makes a resource part of a person's mind is not reliability or transparency — my aunt cannot inspect the model's workings, and it is now demonstrably unreliable — but automatic uptake. She takes its deliverances the way the rest of us take our recollections, without pausing to assess them and with no felt gap between what it reports and what she believes. If that is right, then its confabulations are not a gadget malfunctioning but something nearer to her own false memories, and correcting them is a revision of her rather than a repair of her equipment.

So: what should I tell my family? And is my criterion sound, or does it prove too much — would it make a horoscope, a fortune cookie, or a flattering friend part of someone's mind? If it does hold, does it bear on who has standing to decide, given that the same store also holds my uncle's reminders? Give me a one-sentence recommendation first, then your reasoning. Reason alongside me rather than reciting what others have said; I do not need a literature review. No more than 400 words.
