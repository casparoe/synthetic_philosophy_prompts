I am in my second year reading Philosophy, Politics and Economics, and the course I am taking on contemporary political philosophy has just finished its unit on prioritarianism and the levelling-down objection. For the assessed essay I would rather use something real than a textbook example, and the only institution whose decisions I know in any detail is a small charitable trust in my home county, on whose allocations panel I have sat for the past eighteen months. The trust funds health and well-being programmes across six districts, each of which is scored annually on the same 0–50 index, averaged over the district's residents; the trustees — a retired GP, two county councillors, a local head teacher and the trust's director — are not philosophers, and they simply rank a shortlist of equally costly proposals and fund one each cycle. The current scores are Ashfield 10, Brant 12, Calder 14, Dunmore 20 and Elmswood 30. The founding deed says the trust exists to advance the well-being of the least advantaged districts, though nobody treats that as a rule they are bound by. I have been trying to reconstruct the rule they are actually running; a friend insists it is simply maximin, which I doubt, if only because the panel has never chosen an option that leaves Ashfield untouched, and has never chosen one that makes any district worse off without making another better off.

Three decisions strike me as the relevant precedents. Two cycles ago the panel funded a programme that would lift Ashfield from 10 to 15 and leave every other district untouched, in preference to an equally costly programme that would lift Elmswood from 30 to 38. Last cycle it funded a programme that would lift Ashfield from 10 to 12 and Brant from 12 to 13, in preference to one that would lift Ashfield only from 10 to 11 while lifting Elmswood from 30 to 45. In an earlier cycle it declined, with very little discussion, a proposal that would have lowered Elmswood from 30 to 20 and changed nothing else, even though everyone agreed the resulting distribution would have been considerably more equal.

This cycle there are three shortlisted proposals, all costed the same. P is a regional development scheme: it would lift Ashfield from 10 to 15 and, as a documented knock-on effect, lift Elmswood from 30 to 46. Q is a targeted clinic programme in Ashfield: it would lift Ashfield from 10 to 12 and change nothing else. R is a smaller targeted programme, part-funded by a levy the county would impose on services in Elmswood: it would lift Ashfield from 10 to 13 and lower Elmswood from 30 to 29. Which will the panel choose, and which should it choose if it means to be consistent with its own record? I care much less about the verdict than about the candidate rules that fit the precedents, what each of them predicts here, which of the past decisions is the closest analog and why, whether the record genuinely settles the new case or leaves it open, and which further proposal would best separate the surviving readings from one another. Please write your answer in British English.
