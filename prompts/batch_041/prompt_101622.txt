I'm helping put together a little collection of short fiction that's meant to dramatize philosophical problems, and I'm stuck on the one I said I'd write, so I'm handing you the brief. Here's the idea I keep circling: the whole business of learning how to live from somebody else's life. A friend of mine who works on medieval saints' lives told me that in a lot of those texts accuracy wasn't really the point — the writer was molding the subject into a type that a reader could pour themselves into, so the details got bent to fit the pattern, and a lot of people at the time apparently wouldn't have called that lying exactly. And Plutarch, way earlier, was pretty upfront that he was writing lives to be useful as mirrors rather than as straight history. So: if a biography is supposed to make the reader better, and the making-better happens through imitation, then does an edifying distortion help the medicine or poison it? I want a story that sits in that, not one that answers it.

Here's the setup, feel free to adjust the furniture but keep the bones. Nadia, mid-forties, a former student of a famous physician-activist who died a few years back, has been asked by the old woman's foundation to write the official life for its fiftieth anniversary. Two problems. First, the founding legend — that the mentor stood at a hospital gate in a war zone in the mid-nineties and talked a militia commander out of shooting his way in — turns out, on the evidence Nadia can actually find, to be mostly not true; the person who did it was a local nurse who was killed a week later and whose name nobody remembers. Second, in her last decade the mentor took a huge consulting fee from a drug company she'd spent her whole career attacking, and the board wants that out of the book. Nadia believes the mentor's life taught her how to live; she also thinks she owes the dead nurse something; and she's noticing that the version of the mentor that made her who she is was already partly a construction. I'd like at least one scene where a young volunteer tells Nadia, sincerely, that the gate story is the reason she became a doctor — so the myth is doing real work in the world — and maybe one where Nadia half-imagines the nurse as a presence she can't write into the book.

Take the voice of a literary novelist who's also read a decent amount of moral philosophy, and write me the story, something like 1000 to 1400 words, in American English. Start by brainstorming for yourself — jot the two or three directions this could go and what each would cost you — then pick one and commit to it. No tidy moral at the end; I want the reader to feel the pull of both keeping the myth and telling the truth, and to not be told which one wins. Definately no citations or footnotes, it's fiction. Since a story doesn't have a single answer, just give me one last line after the story in exactly this form, one sentence: Decision: <what Nadia decides to do with the manuscript>.
