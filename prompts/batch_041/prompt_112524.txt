For several years a reading group I'm in has kept circling back to the ethics of discounting the future, and the discussion has stalled in a way I now find more interesting than the original question. The time-neutralists hold that a good is worth no less for arriving later, so that pure time preference — caring about a benefit less merely because it lies further off — is a mistake; the discounters, mostly economists, reply that a positive rate is not a moral error but a condition of any coherent ranking of projects, and that the time-neutral position is either incoherent or quietly presupposes something it won't avow. Each side can state the other's case, and no one has moved in a long time. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

My hypothesis is that the parties are not really disagreeing about the value of future welfare. They may instead be (a) using "discounting" for two different things — an axiological claim that later goods are worth less, versus a decision rule for choosing among options — so that the word, rather than the substance, is doing the work; (b) answering different questions, one about what an impartial observer should prefer and one about what an agent who must commit resources should do under uncertainty, including uncertainty about whether the future exists at all; or (c) divided over whether future people stand to us in the relation that grounds prudential concern, which would make this a disagreement about personal identity and moral standing in disguise. Tell me which of these, or which combination, is actually load-bearing, and whether any substantive disagreement remains once the verbal and the merely contextual parts are set aside.

Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and why.
