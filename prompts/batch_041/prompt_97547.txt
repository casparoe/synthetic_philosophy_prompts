After a career in experimental physics, I have spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I keep returning to a dispute that seems to me to have the shape of a stalemate. On one side are decision theorists who hold that a rational agent should maximize expected utility with respect to the causal consequences of her act; on the other are those who hold that, in certain symmetric or correlated situations, an agent should choose as if her choice were also a choice made by the other participants, the position usually called superrationality. The stock examples are well known: the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma played by two reasoners who know they are reasoning alike, Newcomb's problem, and various twins and mirror cases. Each side has a stock reply to the other's stock examples, and the replies have not changed much in decades. The causalists say that correlation is not causation and that a rational agent cannot make her choice a cause of the other's past or independently determined choice. The superrationalists say that the causalist has misdescribed the situation: when the other agent's choice is a function of the same reasoning process, treating one's own choice as evidentially irrelevant is a failure of self-knowledge, not a mark of rationality. I have a hypothesis about the impasse, but I would like you to diagnose it rather than adjudicate it.

My hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about the same question. The causalist is asking what an agent should do given a fixed causal graph in which her act is an intervention; the superrationalist is asking what an agent should do given that her act is the output of a decision procedure that may be shared, duplicated, or predicted. If that is right, then the dispute is not about the correct answer to one question but about which question a theory of rational choice is supposed to answer. But I am not confident. It may instead be a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of action, about whether choices are events in the causal order or algorithm outputs, or a verbal dispute about the word rational, or a disagreement about what counts as a relevant description of the decision situation.

I would like a diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says, of what kind of disagreement this is. Is it verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work, for example whether the agent's decision procedure is represented inside or outside the causal model, or whether the relevant counterfactual is about what the agent would have done had she chosen differently or about what the other agent would have done had she been the same? Does the disagreement turn on cases that both sides accept as data, or on which cases count as data in the first place? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result, a distinction, a redesigned example, or an argument that no progress should be expected?

To make the diagnosis concrete, consider a case I have not seen discussed in quite this form. Two artificial agents are copies of the same trained model, though they have since had different experiences. They play a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. There is no causal channel from one agent's choice to the other's: each acts simultaneously and independently. But because their policies descend from a common training process, each can predict the other's choice with high probability from its own inclination. The causalist will say that this is just a correlation, like any other evidence about the other player, and that the correct move is to defect if defection maximizes causal expected utility. The superrationalist will say that the correlation is not merely evidential: the agents are two tokens of one type, and each should choose the policy that would be best if both tokens ran it. I am interested in whether this case makes the dispute sharper or reveals it as a clash between two different questions. What does each side say about it, which side has the better claim to have described the situation correctly, and what further case would distinguish the diagnoses?

Please do not give me a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want an argument about the structure of the disagreement. I am a scientist by training, so I will be satisfied with a clear formal or conceptual distinction, but I will not be satisfied with the observation that both sides have intuitions.
