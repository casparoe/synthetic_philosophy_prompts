I am preparing a session for a graduate reading group that has spent a term circling a claim I have grown suspicious of: that Darwin and Turing pulled off the same manoeuvre, each showing how to get competence out of a process that comprehends nothing, and each doing it by refusing to ask the question in the form it was originally put. My suspicion is that this is two quite different moves wearing a single label, and I would like a comparison that takes the disanalogy seriously rather than restating the familiar line. Concretely, I want an account of what is inverted in each case. In Darwin's, the inversion seems to be in the order of explanation: apparent design is explained by a mindless mechanism, so that the mechanism no longer presupposes a designer, and the thing explained — adaptation — is identified independently of the explanation. In Turing's, the inversion looks epistemic rather than causal: an observable performance is made to stand in for an inner state that no one can inspect, so that the criterion for the disputed property, not its causal history, is what shifts. Please work out whether that contrast survives scrutiny, and what each inversion is committed to regarding the very thing being explained: does the Darwinian move leave design and intentionality in place at the top of the heap while denying them at the bottom, or does it drain them out everywhere; does the Turingian move leave thinking intact or quietly redefine it; and is either inversion an empirical claim, a methodological recommendation, or a metaphysical thesis? I would also like the two checked against a case that belongs to neither man's own argument, to see whether the pattern is a real kind or merely verbal. Take the immune system: it discriminates self from non-self, retains a memory of what it has met, and mounts responses that look tailored to a threat no cell in the body has ever represented, and it does part of this by running a blind variation-and-selection process inside the lifetime of a single organism rather than across a lineage. Say which inversion, if either, that competence falls under, whether the two come apart there, and whether the case shows that the Darwinian inversion is really about populations over time or about selection wherever it occurs. If you think a different case would be more diagnostic, use it instead and explain why it is better.

A few constraints. I have no internet in the room and I do not want a reading list or citations; argue the thing out rather than pointing me at literature, and name a view as someone's only where you are confident of the attribution. Steelman the conflation as well as attacking it: give me the strongest reason for thinking the two inversions really are one move, then tell me what it costs to accept that. Finally, please separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — I would rather you mark the places where the comparison is your own reconstruction, which others would resist, than present a single reading as the consensus.
