I run the research program at a small foundation that funds conceptual work — philosophy, social ontology, history and philosophy of science, some interpretive social science — on how psychiatric classifications work and what they do to the people they classify. Our next funding cycle can support roughly ten researchers for three years, and I have to write the call for proposals. I want the call organized around a defended agenda rather than an open topic: a ranked set of the open questions about psychiatric classification and the looping effects of human kinds that are most worth three years of someone's working life, with the reasoning shown rather than merely asserted.

The work we can fund is conceptual, historical, or interpretive. No clinical trials, no new datasets, no imaging. Some grantees can collaborate with psychometricians or clinicians, but the core of every project has to be argument and interpretation.

Here are the candidate foci I have already sketched. Tell me where I am wrong, what I have misdescribed, and what I have left out.

- The identification problem: what would it take to establish that a loop is operating, as opposed to ordinary drift in diagnostic practice, shifting base rates, changing reporting norms, or clinician fashion? Is looping a hypothesis, a framework, or a heuristic?
- The moving-target problem for measurement: if the population a construct picks out is altered by the construct, what becomes of measurement invariance, longitudinal comparison, and the validity of the construct over time? Can a scale be stable in the relevant sense while its target moves, and how would we know?
- Looping and replication: could some failures to replicate in psychology and psychiatry be signs of a moving target rather than noise? Would that make the original findings more interesting or less credible, and how could the idea be tested at all?
- The boundary of the notion: critics of the interactive-versus-indifferent distinction argue that many biological and medical kinds are also modified by the concepts and interventions that pick them out. If so, does interactive kind still mark a real divide, and what work should the notion be asked to do?
- Algorithmic loops: actuarial risk scores, recommender systems, and now language models trained on how people describe themselves. These look like loops with new machinery — the classification changes behaviour, and the changed behaviour trains the classifier. Does the classical picture cover this, or does it need rebuilding?
- The normative question: Hacking himself was ambivalent about whether loops are vehicles of liberation or of control — compare the reshaping of autism and gender categories by the people classified with diagnostic expansion driven by reimbursement. Is the interesting question whether a loop is accurate, or who gets to run it?
- The design question: can a classification be built to be loop-aware — dimensional and transdiagnostic frameworks, practical-kinds approaches, harmful-dysfunction analyses, categories explicitly marked as revisable? Or is loop-resistance incoherent, a problem relocated rather than solved?

What I need from you:

1. A ranking. Which of these, and whatever I have omitted, deserve the three years, and which are overrated, premature, or would not change much downstream?
2. The dependency structure. Which questions are upstream of which? I care about this more than about the list itself: if settling X would make Y tractable, say so.
3. Criteria, stated plainly and then applied — importance, tractability for conceptual rather than empirical workers, neglectedness, and how many other questions wait on the answer. Where the criteria conflict, tell me how you break the tie.
4. What would move a question up or down the ranking over the next two years: a new case study, a conceptual distinction, a change in the DSM or ICD, a result from the empirical literature.
5. What progress would look like at the end of three years, so I can write it into the call. What would a finished project on your top two questions actually deliver?
6. Which item on this list is the one everyone in the field assumes is settled but is not? That is the one I most want on the agenda.

On the epistemics of your answer: keep separate what is settled among people who work on this material from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Where your ranking rests on a judgement call rather than on something established, say so, and say what it would take to change your mind. Do not give me a reading list or citations — I have read the literature and can find it, and what I want from you is the reasoning, not the references.
