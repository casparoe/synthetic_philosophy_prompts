I have been arguing with a friend about whether pleasure and pain sit on a single scale, and I would like to end the argument with a poem rather than another round of cases. The view I want to make vivid is this: below some level of suffering, pleasure and pain are commensurable — enough pleasure can offset a given pain, and a rate of exchange can be quoted — but there is a threshold of suffering such that anything at or above it is lexically worse than any amount of lesser pain or of pleasure, so that no quantity of good, however large, counts as an offset and the exchange rate stops being defined. Write a poem of roughly eighteen to twenty-four lines that performs this rather than asserts it. Let the opening stretch keep the reader inside the commensurable region, using a fixed meter and rhyme scheme in which two quantities — a pleasure and a pain, or two voices — repeatedly balance and can be traded for each other; then cross the threshold in a way the form itself registers, so that at the point where the offset is supposed to become impossible the meter, the rhyme, or the balance breaks. A reader who has never heard of the view should still feel the break, and a reader who holds it should be able to point to the line where the currency fails. Take care not to smuggle in the stronger claims that all pain is incommensurable with all pleasure or that pleasure is worthless; the view is about a threshold, not a blanket refusal. Use no philosophers' names and no citations — the argument should live in the poem. After the poem, add a short note, separate from the verse, saying how confident you are that the poem captures the view and naming the single point you are least sure of, whether that is where you placed the threshold or whether the formal break genuinely maps onto the claim. Write in American English.
