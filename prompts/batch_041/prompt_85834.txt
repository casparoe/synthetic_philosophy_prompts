I teach a unit on the Great Man theory of history, and this year I have decided to defend a position of my own rather than survey the field, so I need my verdicts on a set of counterfactuals turned into something I can actually state. Here they are.

Had Hitler never entered politics, I think there would have been an authoritarian German state in the 1930s, and probably an earlier one, but no Holocaust and no war of annihilation in the East: the specific content of the regime, not merely its brutality, was his. Had Hitler been killed in 1938, I go back and forth, and the oscillation is not idle — sometimes I think the apparatus had already acquired its own momentum and that the war and the camps arrive anyway under a military government, sometimes I think there is no war at all, because the timetable and the target were his alone. Had Gavrilo Princip's gun jammed in Sarajevo, I think the great-power war would have come within a decade and would not have been much different in kind, so the assassination looks to me like an occasion rather than a cause. Had Darwin died at thirty, natural selection would have been published within a generation by Wallace or someone like him, and yet the argument as it was actually made — the twenty years of accumulated cases, the chapters on the difficulties — was his, and I think it shaped how the theory was received and not merely when. Had Fleming not noticed the mould, penicillin would still have arrived, later and from another bench. Had Stalin died in 1924, the Soviet state would still have collectivized and still have run a terror, under someone else and with a different signature. Had Beethoven died at thirty, the late quartets would not exist, and I do not believe anyone else was going to write them. Had Newton and Leibniz both died in childhood, the calculus would have been found within a few decades, though perhaps in worse notation.

Two of these are hard for me to hold together. In the Stalin case I describe the outcome thinly, as a brutal dictatorship of a recognizable type, and conclude that the man was replaceable; in the Hitler case I describe it thickly, as this regime and this genocide, and conclude that he was not. That looks like my description doing the historical work rather than the history.

What I want is a principle, or a short list of candidates each stated precisely, that delivers as many of these verdicts as possible, along with an honest account of which ones it cannot have and why. Two ideas I have picked up and do not know what to do with: the notion of a critical juncture, in which structural constraints loosen and a contingent choice leaves a lasting trace, and the notion of overdetermination, in which an outcome has several sufficient causes and each individual cause therefore looks dispensable. I would also like to know whether the principle can escape the charge that any counterfactual verdict is really a verdict about the description of the outcome, and if so, how it escapes it. Please apply whichever candidate you favour to a case I have not listed — the printing press without Gutenberg — and tell me which single verdict of mine it forces me to give up.
