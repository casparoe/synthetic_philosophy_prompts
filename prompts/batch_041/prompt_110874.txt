I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have spent the past year shipping product features whose entire function is to shape what users choose: defaults, ordering, friction, reminders, pre-checked boxes. My team has been arguing about the ethics of this ever since, and two colleagues have dug into opposing trenches. One is a soft paternalist who thinks that because some choice architecture is unavoidable, the real question is only which architecture to use, and that steering people toward their own ends while leaving the option set intact is both effective and respectful. The other is an autonomy-first anti-paternalist who thinks that influencing someone's choice without their rational endorsement is a failure of respect whether or not you preserve their options, and that the language of "their own ends" is doing illicit work whenever those ends were themselves manufactured by the very environment being defended.

I would like the document these two could actually put their names to after a genuine adversarial collaboration, rather than another round of the argument. Concretely, I want:

1. A section of agreed claims — numbered, each one a full sentence both camps would sign. These should be substantive, not truisms: claims about what counts as a nudge, about the unavoidability of choice architecture, about what we do and do not know empirically about how nudges work and whether their effects last, and about the specific cases where both agree the intervention is clearly acceptable or clearly not.

2. A statement of where they still disagree, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would accept as its own — not a strawman, not a neutral formulation that smuggles in the other side's assumptions. I want the real fault lines, including at least: whether the means/ends distinction is stable (interfering with the means someone takes to their own ends versus overriding the ends themselves); whether transparency dissolves the worry about autonomy or is beside the point; whether a nudge that works by bypassing deliberation is normatively different from one that works by supplying reasons; and whether the "what they would choose for themselves" standard can even be applied to someone whose preferences were shaped by prior nudging.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give the concrete case, experiment, or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance — what each expects to observe or to be forced to concede. Flag any crux you think is really a dispute about words, and say which words.

Please write the disputed claims so that neither side is set up to lose, and keep the residual disagreement as sharp as you can make it. If the two positions turn out to overlap more than the argument suggests, say so explicitly and locate the overlap precisely.

Do not include any bibliography, reading list, citations, or references to named authors or works; where you need a position or principle, state it in your own words. Do not lean on jargon that only one camp would recognize — a smart outsider who has followed the debate but not read the literature should be able to follow every line.

Format: open directly with the document itself — no preface, no restating of my request, no commentary, and no closing remarks. Make the very first sentence the single claim both camps endorse that states, in plain terms, what their remaining disagreement ultimately comes down to. Then the numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table with columns for the issue, each side's own formulation, the crux, the evidence both accept, and each side's prediction.
