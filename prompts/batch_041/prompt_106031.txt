Alright, I need you to argue for a position, and its about the self-sampling assumption (SSA) vs the self-indication assumption (SIA). Quick gloss so we're aligned: SSA says reason as if you're a random observer drawn from the observers that actually exist in your reference class; SIA says reason as if you're a random observer drawn from all possible observers, so hypotheses containing more observers get a bigger boost. SIA's ugly consequence is the presumptuous philosopher: if hypothesis T1 says the universe has, say, 10^10 observers and T2 says it has 10^20, SIA boosts T2 by a factor of 10^10, so a philosopher can announce a priori -- no telescope required -- that T2 is about a trillion times more likely, just from armchair counting. That's supposed to be a reductio of SIA.

Here's the position I want you to argue for. I think the fix isn't to throw SIA out, it's to restrict it. Specifically, weight a hypothesis by the number of observers in it that lie inside your causal horizon -- the ones you could in principle have interacted with, or whose existence could have shaped your evidence -- rather than by the total observer count of the universe. Call it horizon-restricted self-indication (HRSI). I think HRSI is the right replacement for SIA: it should still cancel the doomsday argument (future observers are downstream of you, so they stay in the count) while defusing the presumptuous philosopher (the extra observers in T2 are causally sealed off from you). Argue that HRSI is correct and that it beats both plain SIA and SSA. If you spot an obvious objection, dont just flag it -- argue that it fails.

Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one a sentence or two. No intro, no conclusion.
