So I've got no philosophy training, I just read the Nietzsche essay about history and life a few months ago (the one with the three flavors of history in it) and it's been rattling around my head ever since. I started because of my grandad's diaries — I was trying to decide whether to keep reading them or just let them sit in the box, and it turned into this whole thing.

The three flavors, as I understand them: there's the one where you hold up great people from the past so you can believe greatness is possible again, there's the one where you root yourself in your own place and your own people and feel at home in them, and there's the one where you put the past on trial and walk away from it because it's crushing you. He likes all three and he's scared of all three, that's the part I like.

Anyway I tried to write out his test for when history is good, and it turned into this argument that I can't get out of. Here it is:

(1) A way of doing or using history is good exactly to the degree it serves life, and bad to the degree it hurts life. That's the whole test, no other test.
(2) Serving life means it strengthens you — makes you able to act, gives you a floor to stand on.
(3) The first flavor serves life by showing you greatness was real once so it can be real again, but to actually work on you it has to smooth the past out, make the great look closer and cleaner and more like you than they were.
(4) The second flavor serves life by rooting you, but to work it has to be fond and reverent, so it blinks at the rot and the shame in the family line.
(5) The third flavor serves life by letting you condemn the past and leave, but to work it has to be hostile, so it blinks at whatever was actually good in what it's throwing out.
(6) So all three only help you by leaving something out, and what gets left out is whatever would weaken the effect.
(7) A perfectly accurate, even-handed, everything-included history belongs to none of the three and helps nobody.
(8) By (1), what helps nobody is bad history.
(9) So the most selective history is the best history, and accuracy is irrelevant at best and a defect at worst.
(10) Which can't be right, because the whole point of the essay is that he's disgusted with people who bend the past to make themselves feel better.

I think something slips around (6) to (9) but I can't name it. My best guess is it's something to do with the difference between a history you're supposed to *believe* and a history you're supposed to *use as a model* — like a scale model of a bridge. The model isn't a lie about the bridge and it isn't a copy either, and nobody says it's a bad model because it's not accurate in every way. So maybe his three flavors aren't claims about the past at all, they're more like templates you think with. But then I don't know what happens to the truth, and he definately does seem to want people to face the real past, especially in the third flavor. Also I really don't want to give up (1) — the whole thing falls apart without it, that life is the test.

Which step do I drop, why did it look fine when it clearly isn't, and what does dropping it cost me? If the move is somewhere other than (6)-(9) tell me where. And is there a way to keep (1) through (6) and just fix the part where they get added up? I have a feeling I'm treating three seperate medicines as if they were one medicine with a dial on it, but I can't tell if that's the actual mistake or just a nicer way of saying the same mistake.
