I'm a biostatistician who works on psychiatric epidemiology — prevalence estimates out of registry and survey data, that kind of thing — so I spend my days on case definitions, case ascertainment, coding rules, sensitivity and specificity, misclassification, and the way a change in the diagnostic criteria can move a prevalence curve without anything changing in the world. A friend in a philosophy of science reading group keeps bringing up Ian Hacking and what she calls looping effects, and I keep half-following her. Her version, as best I can reconstruct it: when you classify people — say with a psychiatric diagnosis — the people classified can find out how they've been classified, take the category up, interpret themselves through it, and behave and report accordingly; then the people doing the classifying see the changed population and revise the category, which changes the population again. The target moves. She says Hacking thought this is why the human sciences can't be stable the way physics is, that categories of people get made up as much as discovered, and that there's a distinction between kinds that don't care how you classify them and kinds that do, human kinds being the second sort. Her favorite example is multiple personality, which supposedly multiplied and mutated once there was a diagnosis and a therapy for it; she also mentioned that homosexuality was in the DSM and then got taken out, which I think was in the seventies sometime.

What I want is that whole position restated as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. So: case definition, case ascertainment, registry coding, prevalence versus incidence, misclassification and measurement error, diagnostic substitution, secular trend, screening and case-finding, and the fact that a code can be a billing artifact. Push it hard — don't stop at the easy observation that the case definition is endogenous to the measurement, because I already believe that and I dont think thats the whole thing. Where my vocabulary has nothing to match an element of her account, say so explicitly rather than reaching for a nearby term. And be honest when one of my terms is doing violence to what she's saying: if I say the population is a moving target, what am I smuggling in?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of her claims translate cleanly, which translate only with a distortion I should be nervous about, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — with a verdict on whether the residue is a defect of my framework or a sign that her claims are vaguer than they sound. My own suspicion is that once I add endogeneity of the case definition to my models I've captured the substance, and the rest is rhetoric about labels being socially constructed, but I'd rather be told I'm wrong than be agreed with. Keep it under 800 words: lead with a two-sentence bottom line on whether the translation is basically faithful or basically lossy, then the translation itself, then the residue report.
