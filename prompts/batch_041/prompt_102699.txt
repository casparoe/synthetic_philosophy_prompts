Alright, I've talked myself into a position and I can't tell if it's deep or just a pun, so I want you to try to knock it down. It comes from a short paper David Lewis wrote in the 90s about penal substitution -- the Christian idea that Christ was punished in our place so we don't have to be. Lewis's move is roughly this: none of us, believer or atheist, thinks an innocent friend can serve your prison sentence for you, or take your flogging, or die in your chair. But almost all of us think the friend can pay your fine. And a fine is a penalty, so if we're happy to let someone else take that penalty off us, we already believe in penal substitution in at least one case, whether we'll admit it or not. His diagnosis is that the difference is what the penalty is for. If a fine is really about compensating the victim, restoring what was taken, then who pays is irrelevant and substitution is fine. If it's really about the offender suffering what he deserves, then substitution is nonsense. So we're all of two minds, and the two minds track a real split in what we think punishment is for.

I basically buy this. What would count against it? I want the defeaters sorted, from the ones that should make me drop it to the ones that are merely annoying. A few things I'm already half-aware of and want you to take seriously rather than wave off:

- someone once told me the fine case doesn't actually work, because a fine is still imposed on the offender -- the court still looks to him if it goes unpaid, the money just happens to come from somewhere else, and nobody ever thought the source of the funds was the point. If that's right, I've lost my one piece of evidence.
- the worry that guilt isn't a substance: maybe what we allow in the fine case isn't the substitution of punishment at all but a third party handing over a gift, while what the doctrine needs is the transfer of something -- guilt, desert, liability to suffer -- that can't be transferred even in principle.
- the disanalogy that's bugging me most: in the Christian story the substitute is supposed to be the same being as the one wronged and the one doing the judging. God pays God. The devoted-friend case has no analogue to that, and I suspect that's not a detail but the whole thing.

Also, is the position even saying anything? "We are of two minds" plus "both sides might be right" sounds like it could be trivially true, and I want you to tell me honestly whether it's been qualified into something that can't lose, and whether that's actually a problem for a claim of this kind. What would its rivals predict that it doesn't -- if punishment is essentially retributive, or essentially deterrent, or essentially expressive, what should my intutions about the fine case have been, and do they come out that way? And tell me which of these Lewis himself would have to concede and which he'd dig in on. What would actually move me here?
