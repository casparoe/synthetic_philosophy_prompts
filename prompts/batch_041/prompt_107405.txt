It is written for readers who already know the surrounding formalism, so please take familiarity with Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity for granted and do not spend space summarising them; I am interested in whether the argument holds up, not in a précis.

**The Razor Is a Unit**

Solomonoff induction assigns to each finite string a prior probability given by the sum, over every program that outputs the string on a fixed universal machine, of two to the power of minus the length of that program. Shorter programs therefore carry more weight, and this is usually presented as a proof of Occam's razor. The invariance theorem is then cited to show that the choice of machine does not matter: any two universal machines assign complexities that differ by at most an additive constant.

I want to argue that these two results, taken together, do not support the razor at all. They establish that a particular prior is well defined and machine-robust in the limit. They do not establish that the simpler of two hypotheses is more probable, in any sense independent of the machine one has chosen.

The reason is arithmetic. If the complexity gap between two hypotheses can shift by up to twice the translation constant when we change machines, then whenever the gap is smaller than that, the razor's preference between the two hypotheses is not fixed by the theory. It is fixed by a free parameter. The invariance theorem is usually read as saying that this free parameter is harmless, but harmlessness is an asymptotic claim: it says that enough data will eventually swamp the prior. That is precisely a concession that the prior's ranking is not what does the epistemic work.

The obvious reply is that the translation constant is just a constant, and constants wash out. I do not think this survives contact with finite agents. A constant in the exponent of a probability is a multiplicative factor in the prior, and for any finite body of evidence there is a choice of machine large enough to reverse any comparison we care about. So the constant is not a rounding error; it is the whole of the razor's finite-data content.

The conclusion I want to draw is that Occam's razor, in the only form in which it has a proof, is not a principle for selecting between hypotheses. It is a convention that fixes the scale on which hypotheses are compared, with about as much metaphysical content as the choice of a unit of length. The claim that simpler hypotheses are a priori more likely then either collapses into a tautology about program length, or asserts something the convergence theorem does not deliver.

I am aware that the last paragraph is where the essay is most exposed. My own view is that the step from machine-relativity to the absence of epistemic content is the weakest link, but I cannot see how to repair it without either abandoning the deflationary conclusion or giving an independent argument for why one encoding of the world is the privileged one.
