I am not a historian, only a serious reader of it, and there is a puzzle I have carried around for years without ever finding a name for it.

Often I read two books about the same events — the same war, the same decade, both authors careful, both drawing on the same letters and dispatches — and come away with two entirely different impressions. In one, the outcome feels inevitable from the first chapter. In the other, it feels like a near-run thing that might have gone the other way a dozen times. I can never find a sentence in either book that the other author would call false. The disagreement is real, and yet it seems to attach to nothing that can be checked: it lives in where the story begins and ends, in which events are placed beside which, in what is dwelt on and what is mentioned once and never returned to. One author opens with an assassination and treats the harvest as background; the other treats the harvest as the story and the assassination as an interruption. Both books are accurate, and they teach opposite lessons.

What interests me is that this is not mere taste either. Some arrangements are plainly better than others: one of them will oblige the reader to believe in coincidences, or leave a whole class of documents unexplained, or make the actors' choices unintelligible. But arguments of that kind rarely settle anything, because a rival arrangement can absorb the same evidence in a different order. Nor is it the old cynicism that history is written by the winners; I have seen the same archive yield a story of decline and a story of recovery from writers who agree about everything they can verify.

So I would like to know whether there is an established name for the thing that carries the disagreement in such cases. I do not mean the general doubt about whether history can be objective — I have read enough to know that argument, and it is not what I am after. I mean the specific item: the ordering and weighting and framing that seems answerable to the evidence without being checkable against it. If a term exists, I want to know how it differs from the nearest ones — from selection by itself, since choosing what to keep does not tell you how to arrange what you keep; from rhetoric or style; from the thought that the historian simply imports the preoccupations of her own day. And if no term exists, I would like one coined, with a definition that says what makes one arrangement better than another, and where the line falls between an arrangement that is defensible and one that is merely arbitrary.
