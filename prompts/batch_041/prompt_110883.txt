I teach an upper-level seminar on religion and the state and want one 90-minute session run as a constitutional convention rather than a lecture: sixteen students, no preparatory reading, nothing from me at the front of the hour except the brief itself. By the end I want them to have felt three things rather than been told them — that a funding rule for religion is a constitutional settlement and not an administrative detail; that each of the standard principles for such a settlement (even-handed support, non-recognition, opt-in allocation) has a characteristic failure mode that surfaces only in a concrete case; and that deciding who counts as a member of a religion forces the state into theological judgments that systematically favour organised, hierarchical, literate bodies over others. Design the activity, and keep the words establishment, laïcité and church tax out of my mouth until the debrief.

Build it from the following material rather than substituting a country of your own invention; the specificity is what stops the students retreating into generalities.

Setting. The Republic of Vantor, population 21 million, a parliamentary republic that emerged in 1978 from four decades of authoritarian rule in which the state had fused itself with the majority church. Under the 1978 Compromise the revenue agency collects a Confessional Levy of 1.1 per cent of taxable income from everyone entered on one of four registers: the Vantorian Catholic Church (44 per cent of the population), the Evangelical-Lutheran Church (7), the Jewish Community (1.2), the Old Rite Orthodox Church (0.6). Thirty-eight per cent are on no register; about nine per cent belong to unrecognised bodies, chiefly Muslim congregations and Pentecostal missions. Entry onto a register happens at baptism or on a parent's declaration; removal requires a civil declaration at a district office and also ends access to chaplaincy in church-run hospitals, burial in church cemeteries, and religious instruction in state schools. The Constitutional Court has held that the state may facilitate religious communities but may not identify itself with any of them. A convention has been summoned to replace the Levy with a settlement neutral between worldviews.

Palette. Three real arrangements are available and may be combined: the German one (a state-collected surcharge remitted to whichever body the taxpayer is registered with; departure requires a civil act); the Italian one (each taxpayer devolves eight per mille of income tax to a recognised body or to a state social fund, and undeclared shares are distributed in proportion to the declared ones, so that non-choosers' money follows the popular choice); and the French one (no religious body is recognised, paid or subsidised, but chaplaincies are permitted in schools, hospitals and prisons). These are accurate as stated; the students will be given them as facts.

The docket. Six cases, revealed only after the drafting is finished: the Ethical Fellowship, a secular assembly with Sunday meetings, a funeral rite and a building fund, claiming the Levy; the unrecognised Muslim congregations, with no single legal representative and with some congregations foreign-funded and under police attention; a Pentecostal mission with no clergy and no corporate property, whose funds sit in the founder's name; the Old Rite Orthodox Church after a synod split — which faction inherits the register, the buildings and the revenue; the Vantorian Catholic Church, which runs forty per cent of the country's hospices and is the only provider in two provinces, so that withdrawing the Levy closes the only hospice there; and the 1.4 million people on the registers because their parents entered them, who have never practised. Two further cases are held back until the end: a thirty-thousand-member new movement with a clean record, a legal person and a charismatic founder, applying for recognition; and an Anabaptist community whose doctrine forbids photographs of children, so that recognition would mean exemption from the child health registry.

Roles and stakes. Each student holds a private constituency card with an interest in the outcome — majority-church hospice administrator, imam of an unrecognised congregation, district tax assessor, patient in the last hospice in the province, district registrar, recent leaver from the register — and must argue publicly in clauses that name no community. One student is appointed Constitutional Court before drafting begins and may strike any clause that cannot be defended without reference to a particular body's doctrine. Two blocs each draft four clauses — funding, recognition, membership, services — and then have three amendment tokens, one per clause change, to spend in a repair round. No money changes hands. The stakes are that the winning bloc's settlement becomes the seminar's working model for the rest of term, and that every student whose constituency loses under the final text writes a 200-word letter to that constituency defending the settlement.

Timeline. 0–8 minutes, silent reading and drawing of cards, no framing from me. 8–35, bloc drafting. 35–50, I apply the two texts mechanically, clause by clause, to the six docket cases, reading the students' own words and refusing to interpret them charitably. 50–65, repair round with tokens and Court rulings. 65–72, the two withheld cases. 72–90, debrief. Tell me what to print, and make the design survive twelve to twenty participants by doubling roles.

Debrief. Map each moment onto the underlying problem: the clause that had to be repaired by a discretionary commission is the recognition dilemma, the one that leaves Italy unable to designate a Muslim body and that the French settlement escaped only by refusing recognition outright while still certifying chaplains; the register clause is the state deciding who is a member of a religion, which the German arrangement requires a civil act to leave; if they took the allocation mechanism, the moment they notice that non-choosers' money flows to the most popular body is the inertia effect of a de facto establishment; the hospice case is entanglement that cannot be undone without harming the people it serves; the exemption case is accommodation functioning as privilege, since every exemption is granted to someone in particular. Read one constituency letter aloud. Only now name the concepts.

Predictable failures. The blocs will horse-trade rather than seek a principle, and the Court veto is the remedy; someone will say that everyone should simply be treated equally and the group will declare victory in ten minutes, which is why the docket stays hidden; drafting will overrun, so hold the hard stop at thirty-five minutes; a bloc may reproduce the status quo under new names, which is fine — apply it without comment and let the docket do the work.

What the activity cannot reproduce, and I must supply. It cannot show the long-run causal question of whether state support crowds out or props up religious vitality, because ninety minutes contain no counterfactual trajectory of belief. It cannot produce the experience of establishment from the inside: a stake card simulates an interest, not the attachment of someone for whom the majority church is simply where their grandparents are buried. And it makes constitutional design look synoptic and deliberate, whereas real settlements accrete through tax codes, concordats and single rulings with nobody having designed them; that, and the fact that a revenue office cannot adjudicate doctrine, are mine to say.
