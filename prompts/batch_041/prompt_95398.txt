I run restorative justice for a mid-size school district — about a dozen cases a month, mostly fights, theft, and one ongoing mess where a group of kids has been making a Muslim girl's life hell. My facilitators are teachers and counselors who did a two-day training. We get 45 minutes and sometimes a follow-up. Anything that ends in suspension has to survive the principal and the district's lawyer, so I need something I can defend to people who are never going to read philosophy.

Here's my problem. We say we do this because of ubuntu — that a person is a person through other people, that you become a person rather than just starting out as one, that the community is what makes a self at all. I believe it, mostly. But when I actually have to decide something I've got nothing. Does the kid who got hurt get to say no to the conference and shut the whole thing down? Do we run it if the kid who did it won't admit anything? Do we let him back in class when the other parents are furious? I fall back on "what would the principal not get yelled at for," which isn't a philosophy, it's just cowardice with a talking stick.

So write me a procedure I can actually run on a case, in the order I'd run it: the questions to ask, what counts as an answer good enough to move on, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values question and not a factual one. Ground it in the relational view of persons, not generic "restorative practices." I want the parts that would be different if I didn't believe any of this, and I want you to flag the parts that are just good facilitation dressed up as metaphysics.

Some things it has to survive:

- The personhood-is-achieved part. If a 13-year-old isn't yet a full person in the relevant sense, that sounds like it could license treating kids as projects instead of as people with standing right now. Does the view actually say that, and should the procedure care?
- The community-defines-the-person part, which I've heard read two ways — one where the community is what makes you a person at all, another where it's necessary but doesn't get to swallow you. Tell me which reading the procedure is assuming and what breaks if I switch to the other one.
- The fact that this stuff has been used to demand conformity — "we're all one family here, so stop making trouble." Which is exactly what the kids tormenting my student could say, and what a school says to a gay kid who won't go along with the room.
- The accusation that it isn't a theory at all, just a warm feeling, and the counter-accusation that turning it into a flowchart is the real betrayal, since it's supposed to be about the person in front of you and not the rule. If a procedure is the wrong artifact, say so and tell me what to do instead — but I still have to decide something by Friday.

Then run it on three: the harassment case, where the victim's family wants it handled by the police and won't sit in a room with anyone; a one-time theft where the kid who took the phone is genuinely sorry and the victim wants him expelled anyway; and a case where the "community" — a pack of parents — is demanding that the kid who threw the punch be removed from school, and a conference would just be a nicer-looking way of doing what they already want.

Include at least one where the procedure tells me to do something I don't want to do, and one where it tells me I'm not allowed to do the thing everybody in the room wants. And tell me straight what the procedure can't do. I'd rather know the three places it will misfire than have it pretend to cover everything.
