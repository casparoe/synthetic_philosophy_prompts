Ok so I'm in this reading group — causal inference, mostly epi people plus one philosopher who keeps us honest — and we've hit an argument my friend Priya brought in that I can't get out of my head, and I genuinly can't tell if it's clever or broken. Here it is the way she lays it out. (1) Simpson's paradox is a real phenomenon: an association can hold within every stratum of a population and reverse when you pool the strata. (2) So the sign of an association between two variables isn't a fact about the data, it's a fact about which variables you happen to condition on. (3) And that's not an artifact of the cute textbook cases — for any association you find, there's some further variable you can condition on that flips the sign, including in data where no causal process is operating at all. (4) If a claim can be reversed by a legitimate reanalysis, it isn't a claim about the world, it's a claim about the analysis. (5) So a bare report that X is associated with Y doesn't yet say anything about the world — "ice cream sales are associated with drownings" is no more a candidate for truth than its reverse. (6) But that's absurd. Some associations are robust and worth betting on, and I'd stake my life on the smoking one. So one of (1)-(4) has to go, and I can't see which.

Our philosopher thinks the rot is in (4), the epidemiologist thinks it's in (3), and neither can convince the other.
