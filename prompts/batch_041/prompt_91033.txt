I am a novelist, some way into a book set partly in a transplant unit, and the research has left me with a puzzle I cannot settle on my own. When a patient is expected to die once treatment is withdrawn and is a possible organ donor — what the transplant service calls donation after circulatory death — the team waits for the heart to stop, waits a further interval of several minutes, and only then declares death and begins to take the organs. The length of that interval is fixed by protocol rather than by anything observed in the patient, and it is not the same everywhere: a couple of minutes in some places, five in others. The treatment must have been withdrawn on grounds that have nothing to do with donation, nothing may be done with the intention of hastening the death, and the surgeons who will take the organs keep out of the way until it is over. What I keep coming back to is that the same body, with no pulse for the same few minutes, would not be thought dead at all if it had been found in the street: there, someone would still be pressing on its chest. So the interval seems to be doing something other than reporting a fact about the body.

I should like the theory this practice commits its practitioners to, rather than a defence of it. What does it presuppose about when death happens — that death is an event and not a process, and if so, at which instant; that irreversibility is a fact about the body or a decision about what we are and are not prepared to attempt; that the rule against taking organs from the living is a real constraint or a piece of ceremonial furniture? Where does the protocol genuinely need those commitments, and where is it underdetermined, in the sense that someone who locates death in the whole brain, someone who locates it in the brainstem, and someone who thinks death is a status the law confers on a body could each run it unchanged? And what would have to change on the ward if the operative view were replaced by a rival — say, if we took seriously the thought that the patient in the street and the patient on the table are in the same state, and that the only difference between them is the intention with which the ventilator was switched off?

Please write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I am a novelist and not a philosopher, and I would rather be told plainly where the confusion lies than be given a survey of the literature.
