I spent thirty-seven years in experimental condensed-matter physics before retiring, and for the last four years I have been sitting in on graduate seminars in philosophy, chiefly formal epistemology and the philosophy of artificial intelligence. This term I am attending a seminar on self-reference and self-trust in artificial agents, and the instructor has asked me—as the resident outsider—to contribute one question to the final examination. The students are a mixed group from philosophy, computer science, and, in my case, physics. They can all recite the standard result about the Löbian obstacle: if an agent's reasoning is formalized in a consistent, recursively axiomatizable theory T that extends enough arithmetic, and the agent adopts the full reflection schema—for every sentence φ, from a proof of φ in T infer φ—then, by the theorem of Löb, the theory proves φ for every φ, and so is inconsistent. I do not want a question that merely asks them to reproduce that. I want a question that forces them to distinguish three things they currently run together: trusting one's current theory, trusting a syntactically restricted fragment of it, and trusting a successor theory that one has proved sound in the current theory. My physicist's intuition is that the third is just the first one level up, and that if you can prove the successor sound, you should be able to use it; but the diagonal lemma seems to make the proof of soundness eat its own tail, and I cannot get clear on where the bite is.

Please draft the exam question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme for a 75-minute closed-book final. I would like the question built around a concrete proposal of the sort a student might offer: for instance, that the agent avoid the obstacle by adopting a trust schema only for sentences of bounded quantifier complexity, or only for proofs shorter than some fixed length, or by proving in T that a successor theory S is sound—where S is either T itself, a finite extension of T by a sentence A that T does not prove, or a subtheory of T—and then using S's proofs. The question should have three parts, worth 6, 10, and 9 marks (25 total), and should require students to say which of these proposals are consistent (assuming T is consistent), which are blocked by Löb's theorem, and why the move from T proves that S proves φ to φ is not the same as T proves φ. It should also ask them to state a necessary condition for T to prove the soundness of S, and to say whether any non-trivial version of the trust proposal survives. Then give the model answer, making the key moves explicit, and a marking scheme that lists five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on the common error that costs marks at each point. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or names of papers; the exam is closed-book and the students have the standard results in their notes. The question must be self-contained: explain any technical terms you use inside the question itself, since not everyone in the room has a logic background.
