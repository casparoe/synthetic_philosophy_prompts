Thirty-five years of bench science and I retire into philosophy of mind, of all things — my wife thinks it's hilarous. Anyway, here's the thing that's been chewing on me.

In the reading group everyone keeps saying that consciousness is like the desktop on a computer. Icons, windows, the little arrow — you drag things around and it all behaves, but none of it is really in the machine; it's just the friendly face the hardware puts on so a user can get something done. The phrase they use is 'user illusion' — I want to say it comes from the GUI people (Alan Kay?) and Dennett ran with it. And from that they draw the big conclusion: consciousness is a user illusion, there's no little man watching the screen, no inner theater, the self is just a story the brain tells itself.

I don't want an argument about whether that conclusion is true — I want an audit of the analogy that's doing the work. Specifically:

1. Give me the correspondence table, as explicit as you can. What maps onto the desktop, the icons, the cursor, the click, the user, the programmer, the hardware, the electricity? I suspect a couple of these have no target at all and I want to know which ones.

2. Where does the mapping break, and what does the analogy quietly import that a brain might not have? Two things jump out at me. A desktop is a desktop for somebody — someone has to be sitting in front of it, outside the system, and that's exactly the inner viewer the picture was supposed to get rid of. And if you ever doubt the illusion, you can open a terminal and see the truth about the files; is there a terminal in the brain? I don't think there is. If both of those go, what's left of the analogy?

3. The part I keep tripping over: in computing a 'user illusion' isn't a mistake, it's a useful abstraction — nobody thinks the document is physically sitting inside the little folder on the screen. So when somebody says consciousness is a user illusion, are they saying experience is a useful fiction (fine, I can live with that) or that experience is an error (much bigger claim)? Those look like seperate claims and the desktop story seems to support only the first. Which one does the group actually need?

4. Related: is there a difference between 'experience isn't a window onto how things really are' and 'there is no experience'? The desktop picture is great for the first and everyone seems to use it for the second. Does the second even follow, or is that a slide?

5. Bottom line: does the analogy actually carry the conclusion, or was the conclusion riding on the analogy the whole time? And if there's a better picture out there — one that keeps what's right about the desktop without smuggling a user back in — what is it? I keep hearing the word 'transparent' thrown around for the interface, if that's a lead.

Sorry for the length. This one has been nagging at me for months.
