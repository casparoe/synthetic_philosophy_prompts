I recently sat on a citizens' assembly, having been selected by lot from the electoral register, and the experience changed my mind about how decisions of that kind ought to be made. I now think the assembly, rather than the council that convened it, should have held final authority over the question we were asked to settle — and I have agreed to put that case to the council's constitution and governance committee, which is currently reviewing how the authority takes its major decisions.

The committee is not a neutral audience. Most of its members are sitting councillors from every party, whose standing rests on election; a couple are party agents, and one is a former council leader. Two academics sit with them in an advisory capacity: one a deliberative-democracy researcher who is broadly sympathetic to citizens' assemblies, the other a political scientist who studies agenda-setting and who doubts that any randomly chosen body can resist the people who write its brief and choose its witnesses. The councillors care about accountability, mandate, and their personal responsibility to the voters who put them there; most of them will hear my proposal as a transfer of power away from themselves.

The bones of what I intend to argue are these. A stratified random sample of residents, paid for their time, briefed adversarially, and given access to expert testimony, should hold final decision authority over a defined class of decisions — the annual budget's major trade-offs and planning applications above a certain scale. The council would be reduced to implementing and administering what the assembly decides. The assembly would set neither its own remit nor its own timetable; those would be fixed in advance by a standing rule. Experts would appear as witnesses only, never as members. And assembly members would serve once, with no prospect of re-selection.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumbled them, each phrased the way a councillor or one of the two academics would actually put it, with the two-minute reply I should give, a note on whether I should concede or hold, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.

Give me this as structured data rather than prose: a JSON object with the following fields.

- "objections": an array, ordered from most to least damaging, each element an object with:
  - "rank": integer, 1 being the most damaging;
  - "objection": the challenge as this audience would phrase it, in their own vocabulary;
  - "likely_from": which part of the room it comes from (councillor of which stripe, party agent, which of the two academics);
  - "damage": one of "high", "medium", "low";
  - "two_minute_reply": the reply I should give, in the time available;
  - "concede_or_hold": what I should give up and what I should stand firm on;
  - "soft_spot": where that reply is weakest.
- "the_question_i_hope_nobody_asks": an object with "objection", "why_i_dread_it", and "what_i_would_say".
- "confidence": a short statement of how confident you are in this read of the room and in the replies you have drafted.
- "least_certain_claim": the single point in the whole prep sheet you are least sure of, and why.
