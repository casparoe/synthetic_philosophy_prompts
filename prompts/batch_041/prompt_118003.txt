Two of my colleagues argued through an entire lunch last week, and I have been turning it over ever since. I am a software engineer at a company that builds clinical workflow tools for hospitals, so I spend my days alongside people with clinical and hospital-legal backgrounds; I have no formal training in philosophy and none in medicine, and I was able to follow the argument without being able to settle it. I would like you to settle it for me, point by point rather than only at the end.

The two of them were N, a critical care nurse who has worked on organ donation cases for years, and J, a former hospital attorney now in compliance. Their dispute was over whether a patient who has been declared dead under the standard legal definition is really dead. That definition, as I understand it, offers two alternative routes: circulation and breathing have irreversibly stopped, or brain function has irreversibly stopped. The second route is worded as the loss of "all functions of the entire brain," which becomes relevant below.

(1) N began from the claim that death is a single biological event and that the two criteria are simply two ways of detecting it. On her view the brain is not privileged because it houses the mind but because it is the system that holds the rest of the body together; once it fails, the organism can no longer maintain itself. A patient who meets the neurological criteria and is on a ventilator, she said, is not a living being receiving support so much as a collection of organs being perfused from the outside.

(2) J said the biology does not bear this out. Patients who meet the neurological criteria are sometimes maintained for months, and women who meet them have carried pregnancies far enough to deliver; their wounds heal and their bodies still respond to infection. If the test is whether the body continues to do the work of maintaining itself, these bodies are doing it.

(3) N answered that these are subsystems running on external support, and that what has been lost is the regulation of the whole. She listed what stops: the drive to breathe, control of blood pressure, control of temperature, in many cases the hormone that governs fluid balance. That, she said, is what the disappearance of an organism looks like — organs that keep working as long as someone else is running the controls.

(4) J replied that the same description fits patients on a total artificial heart or on ECMO, and patients with a high spinal cord injury, who have no breathing drive and no autonomic regulation and depend on machines indefinitely, and whom nobody calls dead. So "depends on machines and has lost autonomic control" cannot be the line. He then pressed on what is supposed to make the brain essential. If it is consciousness, the criterion ought to be consciousness, and patients in a persistent vegetative state — who breathe and regulate their temperature — become a problem for the standard. If it is integration, then the empirical cases are the test, and he said they are mixed. Either way, the standard looks either over-inclusive or under-inclusive.

(5) N said that the vegetative patient retains brainstem and hypothalamic function, so the same standard keeps them alive, and that the neurological criterion has never required every neuron to be dead, only the loss of the functions of the brain as a whole. J replied that "all functions of the entire brain" is false as written — some patients who meet the criteria keep hypothalamic and pituitary function — and that reading the phrase as "functions of the brain as a whole" is a repair made after the fact, to keep the definition from failing its own test.

(6) J then said the clearest sign that the criteria are chosen rather than discovered is the circulatory one. In controlled donation after circulatory death, treatment is withdrawn, the heart stops, everyone waits a few minutes, death is declared — and then, in some protocols, the heart is taken and started again, either on a perfusion device or by re-establishing circulation in the body, and transplanted. A heart that can be restarted was not irreversibly stopped. N said the operative word is permanent, not irreversible: permanent means it will not be restored and would not have restarted on its own, and that if we insisted on irreversibility in the strict sense, nobody would be dead until they had decomposed. J said this makes the same few minutes count as death in one room and as a resuscitation in the next, and that what differs is the plan of care, not the patient's biology.

(7) N said that a determination of death is a judgment made under uncertainty, and that this does not make it arbitrary. J said the three levels should be kept apart — the concept of death, the criterion chosen to fit it, and the bedside tests — and that the tests look rigorous only because a contested criterion is doing the work behind them. He added that if the neurological criterion were abandoned, procuring organs from those patients would be killing them, and that he suspected the definition survives partly because of what depends on it. N said the reverse is also true: the criterion emerged from the ventilator, which could keep a body's heart going after the brain had died, and that if the conclusion were that such patients are alive, the practice would have to change rather than the definition.

Please go through this move by move. For each numbered move, say who has the better of it and whether the point as stated is right, half-right, or wrong, with a brief justification. Where either of them is leaning on an empirical claim that is less clean than they make it sound, or on a distinction that does less work than they think, say so explicitly. Then tell me what both of them are missing: in particular, whether there is a way to state the criteria that survives both of their objections, or whether the two criteria are really two different concepts sharing one word, and whether "permanent" is a legitimate repair or a fudge. Finally, say which of the two, if either, is closer to right overall, and whether the question of whether the patient is dead has an answer that does not depend on which criterion we adopt. Since I have no philosophy background, I would rather have the arguments assessed on their merits than by appeal to who has said what; I have no way to look anything up and do not need citations.
