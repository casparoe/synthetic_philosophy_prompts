I'm in a slow reading group working through Book I of Aristotle's Politics, and we've gotten bogged down in the stretch where he's talking about wealth-getting and money. Here's the bit we keep going round in circles on, from around 1257a-b (translation in front of me is old and a bit clunky, sorry for the ellipses):

"Take for example a shoe—there is its wear as a shoe and there is its use as an article of exchange; for both are ways of using a shoe, inasmuch as even he that barters a shoe for money or food with the customer that wants a shoe uses it as a shoe, though not for the use peculiar to a shoe, since shoes have not come into existence for the purpose of barter. ... Exchange on these lines therefore is not contrary to nature, nor is it any branch of the art of wealth-getting, for it existed for the replenishment of natural self-sufficiency; yet out of it the art of business in due course arose. ... But at other times, on the contrary, it is thought that money is nonsense, and nothing by nature but entirely a convention, because when those who use it have changed the currency it is worth nothing, and because it is of no use for any of the necessary needs of life and a man well supplied with money may often be destitute of the bare necessities of subsistence, yet it is anomalous that wealth should be of such a kind that a man may be well supplied with it and yet die of hunger, like the famous Midas in the legend, when owing to the insatiable covetousness of his prayer all the viands served up to him turned into gold. ... It is this art of wealth-getting that is thought to be concerned with money, for money is the first principle and limit of commerce. And these riches, that are derived from this art of wealth-getting, are truly unlimited."

What I can't get past is that the shoe case and the money case look like they're running on two seperate tests for "natural" and he never says which one wins. With the shoe, the test looks like telos: is this use the one the thing was made for? Barter fails that, but he still says barter isn't contrary to nature, because it answers to need — so telos isn't doing the whole job, need is. Then with money the test shifts again: money is nothing by nature, pure convention, so it has no telos to violate, and yet he still calls it the first principle and limit of commerce and treats the desire for it as unlimited. If money has no natural use, then a use of money can't be unnatural in the same way misusing a shoe would be — it's not a violation of anything, it's just unmoored. Which makes me suspect his real complaint about trade was never about the objects at all, and that the "contrary to nature" language is doing rhetorical work rather than arguementative work.

Don't just lay out the readings for me. Tell me which criterion you think is actually load-bearing for him — telos, need, or the fact that exchange has come loose from need — and defend that against the text, including any sentence you think kills it. And then there's one thing I keep half-seeing that I want you to either confirm or kill: when he says the barterer "uses it as a shoe," I think he means the barterer is handing the wearing-use over to someone else, so exchange is a use of a use, a second-order thing, with the first-order use still sitting there underneath it as what's really being transferred. Money, on that picture, is a thing whose only use is that second-order use — a use with nothing underneath it — which would explain why he thinks trade is unlimited: there's no first-order use left to set a limit. Is that in the text or am I importing it? Take a side on both questions and stick to it.
