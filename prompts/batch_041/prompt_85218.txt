Ok, one for you. I'm a retired marine biologist — thirty-odd years sorting polychaetes, so most of my career was arguments about where one species stops and the next begins — and since I retired I've been reading philosphy of science, which is how I ended up stuck on this.

Take Emil Kraepelin, who more or less invented the way we classify mental illness. My understanding is that he thought he was doing what I did: carving nature at the joints, and that the categories he drew (dementia praecox, manic-depressive insanity, the rest) would eventually be vindicated from the inside — by pathology, by cause — the way general paresis turned out to be an infection. Now here's what I can't get past. In the hundred years since he died, the categories have been settled by committee votes and rewritten every couple of decades; whole diagnoses have been deleted for what look like social reasons (the manual used to list homosexuality, then after a vote it didn't); and strangest of all, the categories seem to push back on the people they get applied to. I've read about clinics suddenly seeing waves of tic-like symptoms in teenage girls, many of them with the same phrases and movements, in the years after a certain video app took off. Sort people into a box and some of them start growing into it, which then changes what's in the box — I'm told there's a name for this, a "looping effect", but the name isn't what I'm after.

So put on the hat of a historian of psychiatry who also knows the philosophy of science, and think carefully before you write. Start by jotting down what you take Kraepelin's core commitments to have actually been, then tell me what he'd have made of all this. Would the looping thing have looked like a refutation of his entire program, or is there a version of his view that absorbs it? I want the parts that survive and the parts that don't, what he'd have to give up, and where he'd dig in and say we've misdescribed his position. Don't hand me a reading list, I've got no way to chase down papers, and don't just tell me he'd have been "surprised" — I want the argument.
