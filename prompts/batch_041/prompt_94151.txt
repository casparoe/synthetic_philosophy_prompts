I teach an upper-level political philosophy seminar, 16 students, one 90-minute session. I want an in-class game that makes them feel Simone Weil's point about political parties — the one she pressed near the end of her life, that the problem isn't this or that party's doctrine or its crooked leaders but the party form itself, so that any party, whatever good cause it serves, is a device for producing collective passion, and the price of belonging is that you stop being able to think the thought that would embarrass it. I don't want a mock election that lands on "groupthink is bad"; I want them to catch themselves doing it in real time and to notice that it felt like loyalty, not like lying.

So: split them into three parties, each of which must settle on a single platform across four or five issues. Score it so points go only to the party that carries the final vote, with a bonus for internal unanimity and a penalty for anyone who publicly breaks ranks. Before any party forms, everyone writes their own positions on the same issues on a sealed sheet — I want those sheets at the end. Then rig the arithmetic so that winning the last vote requires some party to endorse an amendment most of its own members privately reject. Include a rule that lets a person say what they actually think, at a personal cost, and note what happens when nobody takes it.

Give me rules, materials, the exact scoring, and a debrief that maps what happened onto Weil, including why she'd say a fairer version of the same game changes nothing. Then: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and which part of her view the activity can't reproduce at all — I assume something about how after enough years the doubt itself stops being formulable, but tell me.
