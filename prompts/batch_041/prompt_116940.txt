Ok so I've somehow ended up as the person presenting at next Thursday's neighborhood association meeting — 15 minutes on the agenda, then open Q&A, and then they vote on weather to formally support the project. The project: a 6-story, 52-unit building at the corner of Ellis and Grove, currently a 1920s fourplex (four rent-stabilized units, all occupied) plus a 14-space parking lot behind it. Eleven of the 52 units are below market, but at 80% of area median income, which in practice means studios for people who already have decent jobs. The developer is using the state density bonus to go two stories above what the parcel is zoned for, which is legal but everyone in that room knows it and hates it. The lot is also inside a survey-eligible historic district (the fourplex shows up as a contributing structure in a 2009 survey that was never adopted), and the 2019 neighborhood plan calls this stretch a conservation area, which is basically a legal way of saying no.

What I'm actually arguing is bigger than the building: that the right to the city is a right of access, not a right of control — that adding homes on a site is presumptively just, and the burden of proof sits with whoever wants to keep people out rather than with whoever wants to let them in. Which is not the same as saying the developer is a hero, and I know the room will smell that dodge immediately.

Who's in the room: the land use committee chair, a retired city planner who knows every code section and will bury me in procedure (expect "spot zoning," "inconsistent with the general plan," "you're asking us to pre-empt our own process"); about thirty homeowners, median age sixty, several of whom bought in the nineties and genuinely believe they're the last people who could ever afford it here; a preservationist who will call the fourplex irreplaceable and mean it; a tenant organizer who's coming specifically to say that market upzoning is a giveaway to whoever owned the parking lot, that the land value doubled the day the state bonus passed, and that the city should be buying sites like this for social housing instead; and one councilmember up for re-election who will not say anything controversial.

Give me the Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get, in their words, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, each with a two-minute answer and an honest note on where that answer is soft. Tell me what to concede up front — I'm planning to concede that the design is forgettable and that the tenants' relocation is the ugliest part of the whole thing, but tell me if that's a mistake. Tell me which single reply has to land for the vote not to be a rout. And tell me the one question I'm praying nobody asks, and what to say if they do.
