I am setting the final assessment for a final-year undergraduate seminar on minds, machines and the philosophical foundations of artificial intelligence. I need one essay question on Turing's treatment of extrasensory perception in his 1950 paper on computing machinery and intelligence, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the list of objections Turing canvasses, nor by a general summary of the imitation game. What I want to test is whether students can locate the precise philosophical work that the ESP objection does — and does not — perform. Turing, as you will recall, took the hypothesis of telepathy seriously enough to think the interrogation would have to be conducted in conditions screened against it, and he treated the matter as an empirical question rather than a conceptual one. The question should force candidates to say what follows from that stance.

Please build the question around the following crux. Suppose we grant, for the sake of argument, that telepathy occurs and can carry information between an interrogator and a respondent. Candidates should distinguish two ways this could damage the test: first, as leakage that corrupts a particular experimental run, which looks like ordinary experimental hygiene (compare the case of Clever Hans, or double-blind controls against placebo effects and experimenter expectancy); and second, as a threat to the criterion itself, on the grounds that the capacity being assessed is no longer conversational performance but some unidentified channel that the test cannot see. They should then say whether Turing's proposed insulation of the interrogation is a principled fix or a fudge, and whether his deflationary method — replacing the question of whether machines can think with a question about performance in a game — is committed to any view about which causal channels may legitimately carry the information.

To keep this from becoming a rehearsal of familiar material, add a structured set of sub-questions. Ask candidates to consider two variants and to say what each does to the validity of the test. In the first, telepathy holds only between human beings and not between a machine and an interrogator. In the second, it holds between an interrogator and a machine but not between two humans. Candidates should say, for each variant, whether the correct diagnosis is that the test has been invalidated, that it has been made harder or easier in a way that is irrelevant to intelligence, or that the question of validity has been confused with the question of reliability. They should also address whether a test that must be insulated from a real phenomenon is thereby shown to be measuring the wrong thing, or whether this is simply what it is to control for a nuisance variable.

The question should also ask candidates to say what the ESP objection reveals about the difference between objections that target the adequacy of a criterion and objections that target the conduct of an experiment, and to identify which of Turing's other objections belong on which side of that line. I want a candidate to be able to say why it matters that Turing treated ESP as an empirical possibility rather than as a conceptual impossibility, and whether that treatment is consistent with his general impatience about defining terms before proceeding.

Deliverables: the essay question itself, phrased so that it can be answered in sixty minutes; a model answer of roughly 800 words; and a marking scheme worth 20 marks, built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates typically lose that mark. The marking scheme should separate a first-class answer from a solid upper-second by naming the specific moves each must contain — in particular, the move that distinguishes leakage from criterion failure, and the move that explains why the second variant above is the more philosophically interesting of the two.

Write everything in British English. At the end of the model answer, include a short confidence note in which you state how confident you are in the claims you have made and identify the single point you are least sure of. Do not include reading lists, citations or references to secondary literature; the question and the model answer must stand on their own without them.
