Last night I got into a ridiculously long argument with my friend Nadia about epiphenomenalism -- the idea that conscious experience is a byproduct of what the brain does and doesn't cause anything itself -- and we kept circling the same weird corner. If my experiences don't cause anything, then the fact that I announce "I'm seeing red" is caused by whatever neural machinery churned out the announcement, not by the seeing-red, so what exactly licenses the epiphenomenalist to trust his own pronouncements about consciousness? He can't say "because I directly observe my experiences and then report them," since the reporting is supposed to be causally downstream of the brain, not of the experience. Nadia kept shrugging and saying it's just the old paradox of phenomenal judgment and you get past it, and honestly she's probably right that it's a known problem, but I want to build something that makes it land as a live problem rather than a one-line deflection. Thats the ask.

I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind -- 15 students, one 90-minute session -- and I want a runnable classroom activity: roles, rules, materials, a timeline, and a proper debrief. The idea should live in what happens, not in what I say afterward, so nobody should hear the word "epiphenomenalism" until the wrap-up. Here's the rough seed, and you should feel free to repair it or throw it out if it's broken: have students generate reports about a sealed private state they can't observe, where the reporting is driven entirely by an external signal and a fixed lookup rule, but calibrated so that the reports line up with the private states often enough to feel reliable. Then let them discover that their confident reports were never causally connected to the states they were supposedly about -- and ask what, if anything, they still knew. I want the debrief to map the specific moments in the game onto the paradox of phenomenal judgment, tell me what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be straight with me about which part of the real problem the activity simply can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.

Lead with the bottom line -- one sentence saying what the activity is -- then the reasoning, the full design, and the debrief. No preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
