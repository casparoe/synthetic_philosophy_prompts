So I host this little philosophy podcast with a friend — it's just the two of us in a basement room in the student union, radiator clanking, a few hundred listeners who mostly do the readings but aren't specialists — and next week's guest is a philosopher whose book defends origin essentialism: the thesis that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg, that your actual gametic origin is essential to you. She has a great stump speech (rigid designation, the necessity of identity, the intuition that this particular person could not have come from any other fusion event) and I don't want to just let her deliver it a third time. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that actually get into the machinery of the view, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? No citations or reading lists needed — she's the expert, I just need the questions.

Here's what I specifically want covered. First, the question of what the origin actually is: the pair of gametes, the particular sperm cell, the zygote, or the fusion event itself? And if the gametes count as the origin, and the gametes have their origins essentially too, does the view bottom out anywhere, or does it run back through her parents' meiosis and on to the beginning of the universe — and if it runs all the way back, is that a feature or a reductio? Second, the gap between "the same individual requires the same origin" and "the same origin delivers the same individual." I take it those are seperate claims and only the first one is the headline, so I want a question about whether the second is true, and the twinning case is where it seems to get nasty: if the zygote she's talking about had split into two, which one would be her? Please make this a sharp version rather than the lazy "so what about identical twins" version. Third, the charge that the whole thing is trivial — that "the sperm and egg you came from" just picks out that origin rigidly and everything else is the bare necessity of identity, so nothing substantive is being said about what's essential to a person. She tends to brush this off with exactly that identity point, so the follow-up should make the brush-off harder to use. Fourth, the everyday intuition that it very easily could have been a different sperm, the million-to-one sense of it — whether she's entitled to call that intuition simply confused, and what would even count as evidence that it's the intuition that's wrong rather than the theory.

Then, if you can fit it in without bloating the list: something using the messy cases from reproductive medicine — chimeras, embryos built with a third party's mitochondrial DNA, twinning and un-twinning — to ask whether the view is hostage to how the biology happens to work; and one closing question about whether origin essentialism is meant to hold for things other than people (my grandfather's pocket watch, a lake, a rock) and whether extending it that far makes it more or less plausible. Conversational is fine since the listeners aren't specialists, but don't water down the objections. Oh, and give me a rough running order, so the interview builds instead of peaking in the first ten minutes.
