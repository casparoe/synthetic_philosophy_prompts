A group of us have been arguing, half-seriously, about how far a visitor is bound by the ways of the place she is visiting — it started with a friend's complaints about being cold-shouldered abroad for tipping too much and for queuing the wrong way, and we ended up inventing a test case to try to isolate what we actually think. The case is this. On a fictional island I shall call Ondine, there is a firm and universally observed convention governing gratitude: when someone does you a substantial favour, you must not thank them in words at the moment it is done. You acknowledge it with a nod or a look, and the spoken thanks are given the next time you meet, a day or more later. To thank someone on the spot is understood to mean that the matter is now closed and the debt discharged, and locals regard it as mildly insulting — a way of treating a personal kindness as a transaction. The convention coordinates nothing and harms no one in any material sense; it is purely expressive. Now suppose a visitor, told all this in advance and quite capable of complying, thanks her host immediately anyway, either from force of habit or because she thinks the convention faintly absurd. My own credence that she has thereby committed a genuine moral wrong — not merely a breach of courtesy, not merely a faux pas, but something that gives the host a legitimate moral complaint — is about 0.65. A friend who suspects that appeals to local custom are mostly a cover for bullying outsiders puts it at about 0.15.

Give me your own credence in the same proposition, and — this is what I actually want — the decomposition that produces it. I should like a rough probability and one line of justification for each of the sub-questions you take it to turn on, which might include: whether there is any general moral duty to conform to the conventions of a place one is visiting, as opposed to a duty simply to be civil; whether a purely expressive convention can wrong someone in the way that a harmful or coordinating one can; whether the reasonableness of the offence taken bears on whether the conduct was wrong at all; whether a visitor who accepts local hospitality incurs a distinctive duty of reciprocity that a passing stranger would not; whether the distinction between a moral wrong and a breach of courtesy is a real distinction or a merely verbal one; and whether the visitor's own conviction that the convention is absurd is a morally relevant fact about her. Add any sub-question you think I have missed, and drop any you regard as idle. Then combine them into your figure, tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most, and finish by saying which of the two of us — the 0.65 or the 0.15 — your decomposition suggests is making the larger error, and precisely where. Please write your answer in British English.
