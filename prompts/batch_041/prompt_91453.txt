I have no academic training in this area, but I have spent a long while on one piece of reasoning that I use constantly and cannot justify. When two things are unconnected in everything I know, I treat them as independent, and I let that default do real work: I multiply their chances together, I decline to hedge one against the other, and I find it genuinely surprising when both happen. It is, I think, the same move people make when they say that a coincidence in the data is probably nothing, or that some mathematical statement is probably true because there is no reason for it to fail. It feels like more than a habit, but I cannot say what makes it legitimate, and I have watched it fail in ways that seem to matter.

Before I go further, I want the specification. Write me the desiderata that any account of the presumption of independence must satisfy in order to be adequate: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must respect, the results it cannot contradict, each with the consideration behind it, and a clear-eyed view of which requirements are genuine and which are the preferences of one camp. I am not asking for a new theory of my own; I am asking what a theory would have to deliver. The list is the deliverable.

At minimum I want the list to cover the following, though I would rather you tell me which of them are real requirements than simply accept my framing.

- The ordinary cases: two dice, a coin and a die, two thermometers standing in the same room. What must the account say about each, and what exactly must change when the thermometers agree not because they are reliable but because they are in the same room? I want that requirement stated precisely, not as a general instruction to be careful.
- The fact that independence is untouched when I change units or reparametrize each variable separately, whereas treating every possibility as equally likely is not, so that the same setup described differently yields a different answer, as in the familiar puzzle about choosing a point at random inside a circle. Is this invariance a requirement or a bonus? Can an account keep the ordinary cases while escaping those puzzles, or are the escape and the motivation for the presumption in tension?
- Mathematical rather than physical items: the ten-thousandth digit of one constant and the ten-thousandth digit of another, or two conjectures nobody has settled. These are fixed, not chancy; nothing is being drawn from an urn. Say what requirement, if any, covers reasoning about them as though independence were a sensible default, and whether meeting that requirement forces the account to give up the idea that probability is about frequencies.
- Cases where someone could have arranged the dependence: a lottery whose numbers the operator might have picked after seeing my ticket, a game of guess-my-number. I want a requirement that separates presuming independence about a mindless process from presuming it about a process that has read my guess.
- The gap between independence event by event and independence of whole variables: three events can be independent in every pair and not independent together. Must an account handle this, or may it stipulate it away?
- The infinite case: the chance that every member of an endless sequence of unrelated events turns out a particular way. The naive answer is zero, and I do not trust it.
- Whether the presumption is a claim about the world or only about my state of information, and whether the account is consistent with whichever answer it gives.

Then the parts I care about most. Which of these requirements pull against each other, and is any subset jointly unsatisfiable? If the list cannot be satisfied all at once, that is itself the result I am after, and I would rather have it than a tidy list. I am also suspicious of one thing in particular: an account that licenses the presumption only when there is already evidence of independence is safe and useless, while one that licenses it freely is useful and exposed to unmodeled common causes. Tell me whether that tension can be resolved, and which single requirement does the most to rule out otherwise attractive accounts. A short verdict on one or two well-known approaches is welcome where it does work against the list, but the list is the deliverable; describe any formal machinery in plain words, and do not send me to a reading list, since I cannot look anything up.
