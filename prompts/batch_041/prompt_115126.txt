A friend and I argued late into the night about whether good and evil add up at all in a world with infinitely many people, and neither of us moved the other. I would rather see the problem staged than restated, so: write me a short story.

The setting is a celestial audit office responsible for a realm containing countably many souls, each carrying a welfare score. Totals are always computed by adding the scores in the order the souls appear in the great ledger, which is the order of their creation. The plot should turn on the fact that this sum does not converge absolutely: rearranged, the very same infinite ledger can be made to total any finite amount, or none at all, so the office's verdict on whether a proposed reform improves the realm is an artifact of the ledger's ordering. One character, an old actuary, treats the creation order as privileged; a reformer proposes summing in order of need, or of who is causally affected by whom; a third argues that any order is arbitrary, that comparing these realms is meaningless, and that the office should stop aggregating and adopt some non-summing rule instead. Make the stakes visible — somebody's eternity turns on the answer — and resist ending in a lecture. If it helps, the realm also runs a lottery in which every citizen holds a ticket of zero chance and exactly one of them is always chosen.

Keep the story under 800 words. Then, in no more than 150 words, add a note outside the story saying where it takes liberties with the mathematics or philosophy it leans on — if the arithmetic in the decisive scene would not actually work, say so — and which contested reading of infinite value it commits to. Close that note with what would have led you to write the story with the opposite moral. Do not quote or cite any real text; the ideas should be carried by the fiction.
