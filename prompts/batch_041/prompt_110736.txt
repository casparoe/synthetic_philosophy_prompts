For thirty-five years I did experimental work in a university physics department, and since retiring I have spent my mornings reading philosophy of science — badly at first, less badly now. For the last two years I have also co-hosted a small interview podcast that a former colleague and I record in a converted study, mostly for an audience of graduate students and other late converts. Next week's guest is a philosopher of science who has just published a book defending a revisionist version of Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. Her thesis, as she states it in her opening chapter and has repeated in the two interviews of hers I have heard, is that the familiar distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts is unusable at the moment a working scientist actually has to choose, because a sufficiently ingenious community can always absorb an anomaly into the protective belt; and that the requirement of predicting a novel fact, being unavoidably retrospective, ought to be replaced by what she calls a ratio test. On her test, a programme counts as progressive at a given stage only if each successive modification of the belt adds consequences that can be tested independently faster than it adds auxiliary assumptions for which the programme's hard core gives us no independent reason to accept. She insists the test is meant to be applied in real time, by the people doing the work, and not by historians with the benefit of hindsight.

I would like you to draft ten to twelve interview questions for her. The last thing I want is a conversation in which she simply restates the book, so I would ask that at least one question address each of the following three matters, and that each of those questions be followed by a second question keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. First, whether the ratio test is really available in real time: I want to know how one counts the independently testable consequences of a modification before any of them has been tested, and whether that count is not itself a judgment only the future can settle. Second, how the test treats a programme that spends a long and undistinguished stretch adding epicycles and is then spectacularly vindicated — I would like her to work through the contraction and drag hypotheses invented to keep the aether alive, and then, closer to my own former field, the long failure to find supersymmetric partners at the LHC, where naturalness arguments were widely thought to make low-mass partners probable. Third, whether her amendment is itself a modification of Lakatos's belt rather than of his hard core, and what her own criterion says about the programme of defending it.

I should add that I am not looking for a gentle conversation. Where the standard objections to Lakatos have been pressed — that underdetermination makes it impossible to say which strand of the belt has failed, and that appraisal is retrospective in a way that leaves the methodology useless as advice — I would rather the question be phrased so that she has to take a side than so that she can agree with everyone. And if any of my three matters turns out to rest on a misunderstanding of her position, please say so in a note beneath the questions rather than softening the question itself.
