I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of dramatizing real people, and next month I have to give the opening remarks — five minutes, before the screening — at the premiere of a feature film for which I served as historical consultant. The film, which I'll refer to here as The Ledger, concerns a real nineteenth-century administrator of a provincial poorhouse; my job was to keep the script from contradicting the archive, and I did that job. The audience will be mixed: the director and producers; my department, including two historians who think the whole genre is kitsch; the funders; and the subject's descendants, who are arriving angry about one scene. In that scene the administrator turns away a woman at the gate on a freezing night and she dies. The woman is a composite and the incident appears in no source, but nothing in the record contradicts it either. The family's objection is not that the scene is false. It is that their ancestor would not have done it, and — worse — that it is now the only thing anyone will remember about him.

I want to avoid the two speeches that are available to me: the disclaimer that this is drama, not documentary, and so owes the dead nothing; and the reassurance that every date in the film has been checked. Instead I'd like the remarks to carry a distinction I've been working out. There is fidelity to the record, which means inventing nothing the evidence forbids, and there is fidelity to the person, which binds even where the evidence is silent, because the question is not whether a scene could have happened but whether the person it depicts is still that person. On that basis I want to claim that the characteristic injury of this kind of film is not falsehood but displacement: the invented scene that is more vivid than any documented one and quietly becomes the memory, so that the record is not contradicted but crowded out. And since the film's power derives entirely from the fact that these were real people with real names, I take it that the filmmakers cannot then fall back on the license of pure fiction.

Write the remarks in my voice. They must be honest with the family without collapsing into an apology for the film or into a lecture on the philosophy of posthumous harm; much of the room has read none of this, and the family is grieving something they cannot quite name. The distinction I've described has to do real structural work — it should determine what I can and cannot claim from that podium — and I would rather it lead me somewhere uncomfortable, as it does when I notice that on my own account the better the film, the graver the wrong it does, than sit in the speech as decoration. Keep the speech itself under 600 words. Then add a short note, under 150 words, on where this reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change my mind — in particular, whether the displacement argument proves too much, since every successful dramatization displaces something. Total response under 750 words.
