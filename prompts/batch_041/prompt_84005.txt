For about a year I have been arguing with friends about two puzzles that I have come to think are the same puzzle in different clothes, and I keep running into versions of it at work. I would rather have the specification than another attempt at a solution, so what I want from you is the list of things any adequate account of the matter would have to deliver.

The first version: I buy one ticket in a draw of a million. I am as sure as I am of anything that my ticket will lose, and equally sure that some ticket will win, and I cannot see which of those two certainties is the mistake. The second version: I read a long, carefully fact-checked book, and in the preface the author remarks that some errors surely remain. I take her to be honest and sensible rather than contradicting herself, even though she has asserted every claim in the book and now asserts that at least one of them is false. The third and fourth versions are mine, and come from the annual inspection I have to sign at the warehouse where I work. There are about eighty items on the sheet, I am confident of each one — call it ninety-eight times in a hundred — and I am asked to sign that the building is compliant. I also know that half of my judgments come down to a single moisture meter that is either in calibration or not, so that if it is off, a whole block of my eighty items is wrong together rather than one at a time. And I have noticed that I believe a fifteen-step plan of my own will work even though I would not put more than nine chances in ten on any single step.

What I want is the desiderata for a theory of when it is rational to accept a claim, or to accept a set of claims together: the cases it has to get right, each with the reason it is a case any theory must answer for; the distinctions it must not flatten, such as between believing a claim and being confident of it, between believing it and being willing to act on it, and between a set of claims that is merely unlikely and a set that is known to contain an error; and the constraints from outside, from the fact that we do in practice reason in long chains and that we hold people responsible for where those chains end up. Tell me which of the requirements you think are genuinely non-negotiable and which are artifacts of how one particular camp sets the problem up.

Then the part I care about most: which of the requirements cannot be satisfied together. I suspect the list contains a genuine conflict, and I want you to say which requirements collide, what has to be given up to break the tie, and which single requirement, if you insist on keeping it, forces out the largest number of the others. If you want to score the familiar moves against the list — that high probability is enough for acceptance; that consistency must be kept but acceptance need not be closed under conjunction; that my ticket is a special kind of claim and ordinary claims are not; that acceptance should be dropped in favour of degrees of confidence alone — do it in a line each. Please do not give me names, titles or dates; I have no way to check them, and I would rather have your reasoning than a citation I cannot verify.

Last, tell me what would change your mind about whichever verdict you reach on the conflict: which argument, or which outcome of one of my cases, would push you to a different answer, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to break down.
