ok so I just lost two hours arguing with my friend (she's finishing a PhD in archaeology, I'm the philosophy person at the table) about whether you can ever actually know what people believed, or how their institutions worked, from the objects they left behind. we didn't resolve it and now I want the spec on paper before we go again.

her position, roughly: you can infer behavior, maybe, if your formation processes are sorted -- you can never infer belief. if a thing is in a pit, all you know is that its in a pit. anything past that is storytime, and "ritual" is the word you reach for when you've given up.

mine: fine, but "behavior" is doing just as much smuggling as belief. telling me someone "deposited" an object is already telling me they did something on purpose, and now you owe me an account of how you know that, and it can't come from the objects, because the same smashed figurines turn up whether people were cursing someone, honoring them, or just cleaning out a workshop.

what I want from you: the desiderata. not a method, not a school. the list of things any adequate account of inferring beliefs and institutions from material remains has to be able to do -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things it can't contradict -- with the case or consideration behind each item, because I want to know which requirements are real and which are just one camp's prejudice (processualist, post-processualist, whatever label you want).

things I want handled, as requirements and not as opinions:
- granary vs temple: identical ground plan, no domestic debris, rows of big storage jars. what does the account owe me here -- a verdict, or an honest "can't tell"?
- deposition vs loss: the bog hoard vs the coin someone dropped. same object, two completely different social facts, and I want to know what licenses the difference.
- absence: no market building at a site that clearly moved alot of imported goods. when is "we didn't find it" evidence of anything, and when is it just sampling?
- the institution side: what's the material signature of something like taxation, or a priesthood, or a lineage -- and can a method distinguish a real institution from a pattern that merely looks like one from above?
- analogy: every route to meaning seems to run through some living society or some text. can that be a requirement, or does the requirement "don't impose present-day categories" kill it?
- testability: is there any version of this that lets evidence count against the belief-claim, or is the whole thing unfalsifiable by construction? she thinks it is and I think thats too strong.

then: which of these are non-negotiable and which are parochial, and -- this is the part I most want -- which pair or pairs you suspect can't be satisfied at the same time. if the list turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable in some corner, thats a result, and I'd rather know it now than keep arguing.

fix the purpose for me if you need to: I'm not asking for the standard a chemist would accept. I want the standard for a claim on a museum label that a few hundred thousand people a year will read and believe.

and if there's room at the end, tell me in a few lines how the "just read the symbols" hermeneutic camp and the "just the dirt and the bones" camp each do against the list. but keep that short, the list is the point.
