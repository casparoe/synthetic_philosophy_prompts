I got interested in Fernand Braudel after someone mentioned him to me, the French historian who split history into different speeds: the fast layer of events (battles, kings dying, elections), a middle layer of slower cycles (prices, economies, maybe the mood of a whole generation), and then the really slow layer he called the longue durée, geography, climate, coastlines, trade routes, the stuff that barely moves across centuries. I've thought alot about this and I wrote up a rough essay. I'd like your comments on it. Please answer in American English.

Here's the essay, more or less:

**Three speeds, or maybe just two**

The thing I can't stop noticing is that we explain things at the wrong speed all the time. It's like weather and climate. Nobody explains why it rained last Tuesday by talking about the climate, and nobody explains a thirty year average by pointing at one thunderstorm. But with history we do both constantly. Somebody writes a whole book about why one single war happened and treats it as the hinge of everything, and somebody else says no, it was all going to happen anyway because of where the mountains are. Both of those moves would look obviously confused if we were talking about rain.

My own addition, and I want to know if it's a real distinction or just a metaphor I happen to like: I want to call the slowest layer *sediment* instead of *structure*. Structure sounds like scaffolding, something that was put there, that holds a shape, that you could in principle take apart. Sediment is stuff that settled, in order, over a long time, and that can be dug into, stirred up, or buried deeper by something sudden. A landslide is an event, but it only matters because of how the layers were already stacked. I feel like sediment gets at something structure misses, namely that the slow layer is made of old fast layers that got compressed. Is that a real point or am I just relabeling?

Then there's the thought experiment that keeps tripping me up. Imagine an alien historian who only gets data about Earth at a hundred year resolution. What could they actually write? They couldn't write about people at all. But here's my worry, could they even see the slow structures? If you only ever get one run of a planet, one realization, you can't tell a real slow trend apart from a very long random walk. You've got an n of 1. So how do you ever know that the slow layer is doing causal work, rather than being the shadow that fast stuff casts when you squint? I think there's something here about the fact that history can't be re-run, but I dont know if the point is as strong as it feels to me or if I'm just rediscovering the problem of induction with extra steps.

The political thing bugs me too. Slow layer explanations are convenient in both directions. If it was geography and climate, then nobody is to blame, which is nice if you're the one who would otherwise be blamed. But it's also convenient if you want total transformation, because if the real causes are all structural then nothing short of remaking the whole structure counts as a fix and every incremental reform is a joke. Same idea, opposite politics. So the slow layer starts to look less like a finding and more like a tool that anybody can pick up.

And then there's the fact that you can always assign a cause after the fact. Something happens, and someone says, ah, that's the longue durée working itself out. But if every outcome confirms it, what would disconfirm it? I don't have an answer. I assume people have thought about this and I want to hear it in plain language.

Last thing. Braudel supposedly said events are just foam on the surface of a deep sea (paraphrasing from memory), but then he wrote gigantic books about the Mediterranean in which he clearly cared about the details. So is the three speed thing a real claim about causation, or is it more of a rhetorical move, a way of saying stop over-explaining everything with events, where the number three is basically arbitrary? I half suspect the middle layer is just where we put anything that isn't obviously fast or slow.

Push back hard, I'd rather be told I'm wrong than be complimented. American English please.
