Frank Ramsey died in 1930, decades before the accuracy-first program in epistemology turned the evaluation of credences into a branch of decision theory. Suppose you could show him a current proposal from that program, which we can call policy-scoring: the unit of epistemic evaluation is not the credence function an agent holds at a moment but her entire policy from evidence to credence, scored by the accuracy of the credences it would generate across every possible course of evidence, with probabilism and conditionalization then derived from a single dominance norm over policies. Its defenders present it as the natural heir to Ramsey's own pragmatism about belief, since it appraises a credal state by the guidance it offers across all contingencies rather than by its fit at a single world. Write Ramsey's reply. Say where he would agree and where he would object, addressing at least three points: whether he would accept a purely epistemic scoring rule at all, given his view that the value of a belief derives from the value of the actions it guides; whether the dominance argument for the policy-scoring norm is a genuine analogue of the consistency arguments he gave for the probability axioms, or whether moving from betting consistency to alethic accuracy changes the subject; and whether the trade-off between avoiding false belief and securing true belief is, for him, a fixed epistemic parameter or something that shifts with practical stakes. Keep the objections specific and internal to his own commitments rather than a generic pragmatist grumble, and finish by saying which parts of the accuracy-first program he would keep.
