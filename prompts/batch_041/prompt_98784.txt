I write about philosophy and the social sciences for a general readership, and I am preparing a piece on a striking result in the theory of rational belief: that two people who begin from the same assumptions and both reason impeccably cannot knowingly remain in disagreement about a factual question once each is aware of the other's view. The piece will land harder if it turns on a constructed case rather than a statement of the theorem, so I would like you to build me one. Specifications: two agents who are perfectly rational Bayesians, who share a common prior over a small finite set of states of the world, each of whom receives a private observation, and each of whom is genuinely in a position to know the other's posterior probability for a particular event, and yet whose posterior probabilities for that event differ. Constraints: the disagreement may not arise from irrationality, from priors that differ or are held privately, or from either agent holding a mistaken view of what the other believes; the state space must be small enough to set out in a table with explicit numbers; and the setting should be ordinary and human -- two analysts, two clinicians, two inspectors -- rather than abstract agents A and B. Once you have the case, do four things. First, walk through it state by state, giving each agent's posterior and showing why each is entitled to his or her belief about the other's posterior. Second, say precisely which structural feature of the case is doing the work; I suspect the point is that what each agent knows about the other is not itself something the other knows to be known, and so on up the hierarchy, and I want that diagnosis made explicit and the exact point where the chain breaks identified. Third, close the gap: make the relevant fact common knowledge between the two agents and show that their posteriors are then forced to coincide, noting which assumption of the theorem this isolates. Fourth, tell me candidly how a defender of the theorem would argue that my case, while formally correct, is not the kind of disagreement we meet in life, and whether you think that defence succeeds. The whole exercise is about isolating a single variable, so if any feature of your case could be varied without changing the verdict, say so.
