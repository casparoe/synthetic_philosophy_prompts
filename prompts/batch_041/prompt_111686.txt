A friend who TA'd an ethics course summarized the lying-vs-misleading debate for me like this: lying is when you assert something false with intent to deceive; misleading is when you say something true but let the other person infer something false. Morally, lying is worse because it breaks the norm of assertion and violates the other person's trust in your word. Misleading doesn't break that norm -- you haven't lied -- so it's only wrong if you had a duty to tell the whole truth. That's why it's okay to evade a nosy coworker's question about your salary by saying "I'm comfortable" even if they infer you're rich, but it's not okay for a doctor to tell a patient "the scan is clean" when it's ambiguous and the patient will think they're cancer-free. Kantians think lying is always wrong because it treats the other person as a means, but misleading is permissible because you're not using their rational agency, just declining to help it. So the law is right to punish perjury but not evasion.

I suspect this is mostly right but something feels off. And does the practical conclusion -- that misleading is generally the lesser wrong -- actually follow from the parts that are right?
