I serve on the historical advisory board of a museum that is finalizing a permanent gallery on a mass-atrocity campaign, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. I spent most of last night arguing about it with a friend, which is why I am asking you to draft it rather than writing it myself.

The case, briefly. Between 1971 and 1974 the government of a country I will call Vaskia killed roughly 180,000 people from a community I will call the Maren, a religious minority with its own language and liturgy. Villages and churches were burned, schools closed, clergy deported, thousands of children transferred into state homes, the region renamed. What complicates the label is the motive. The Maren had also become the organizational core of the political opposition, and many perpetrator documents speak of destroying the Maren's capacity to act politically, of settling the Maren question as a problem of public order, rather than of destroying the people as a people; other documents say something closer to the latter. No court has ever ruled on the campaign, and our charter requires gallery labels to be historically grounded and legally literate.

My friend defended a position I could not quite answer. Genocide, he said, is a legal verdict, not a moral compliment. The 1948 Convention protects national, ethnic, racial, and religious groups and requires an intent to destroy the group as such, in whole or in part; political groups were deliberately left out. On his view, a museum that calls the campaign a genocide is both preempting a judgment it has no authority to make and inflating a term whose value depends on its narrowness, which only makes genuine cases easier to deny. My instinct is that Lemkin's own idea was wider than the treaty he inspired: he was after the destruction of a group's way of life, its institutions, language, and religion, not only its bodies, and a museum is a historical institution rather than a tribunal. Two practical facts also weigh on me: the Maren survivors' association has said it will withdraw its loan of artifacts if we soften the label, and our foreign ministry has quietly asked us to avoid the word while a trade negotiation is pending with Vaskia's successor government.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate on Thursday. Recommend a concrete course of action, including which label the wall text should use, what qualification if any should accompany it, and by when we should act, and give my friend's argument the strongest hearing rather than brushing it aside. Keep it under 900 words; I do not need citations or a reading list, since the board wants an argument it can act on. Write in American English.

After the opinion, append a JSON object with exactly these fields: "recommendation" (one sentence stating the board's decision), "wall_text_label" (the exact wording the gallery should use), "definition_relied_on" (which conception of genocide the opinion applies, and in what order), "key_reasons" (array of three to five strings), "strongest_counterargument" (the best version of my friend's case), "reply_to_counterargument" (how the opinion answers it), "recorded_dissent" (the minority view to enter in the minutes), and "next_steps" (array of objects, each with "step", "owner", and "deadline").
