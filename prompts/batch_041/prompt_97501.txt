Some friends and I got into it at dinner the other night about whether everyday social conventions are real moral obligations or just etiquette with teeth, and since then I've been writing down my gut reactions to cases to see if they add up to any one principle. Here's my list. Tipping 18-20% at a sit-down restaurant in the US with normal service: obligatory, I'd feel I'd done something wrong by skipping it. Tapping the 'no tip' button on the tablet at a coffee counter where I ordered standing up: completely fine, zero guilt. Tipping the hotel housekeeper: I feel I ought to and forget half the time and don't feel like a criminal about it. Crossing an empty street at 3am against the light with no car within three blocks: fine. Slipping across mid-block on a four-lane road at rush hour, weaving between cars: not fine, I'd think less of someone who did it. Cutting to the front of a long airport security line because I'm about to miss a flight: wrong, but the kind of wrong I'd do anyway and apologize for. Holding a place in line for a friend who's still parking: fine at a food truck, not fine at the DMV, and I honestly can't say what the difference is supposed to be. Zipper-merging in traffic and refusing to let the next car in: you're a jerk, but I don't think you've violated an obligation to anyone. Splitting a restaurant bill evenly when I ordered the cheap thing: mildly annoying, not a wrong.

The cases that made me actually start writing this down are the visitor ones, because they're where my gut goes weird. A tourist from a country where nobody tips, not tipping at a US restaurant: I think they're genuinely in the wrong, even though nobody told them, and even though the norm is stupid and the employer should just pay the staff properly. But a tourist in a place where you're expected to haggle over every price, who pays the first asking price and walks out: I don't think they've done anything wrong at all, even though they've broken the local convention and the seller was expecting the game. Someone visiting a country where you push onto the bus rather than form a line, standing patiently and waiting their turn: fine, obviously fine, arguably better than fine. So it clearly can't just be that you do whatever the locals do, and it also can't just be that conventions never bind visitors, since I do think the non-tipping tourist owes something. The half-baked principle I've been trying is this: a convention is a real obligation when it's the mechanism by which somebody actually gets paid or gets their turn, so that quietly skipping it transfers something from them to you, and it's mere etiquette when the only cost of skipping it is a raised eyebrow. That maybe handles the tip screen and the queue-cutting, but it seems to say the non-tipping tourist is fine, since they didn't take anything, they just failed to give, which contradicts my gut.

So what I want from you is the principle that actually fits the verdicts I've given, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a case I haven't thought of, plus an honest note on which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I'd much rather learn that one or two of my reactions are just inconsistent than get a principle so padded with exceptions that it fits everything and predicts nothing, and if the best you can do is two competing principles that each save most of the list, say which verdicts each one sacrifices. Two further cases I'd like it to pronounce on: (a) a visitor who follows their home country's custom in a way that offends locals without harming anyone, say blowing their nose at the table or keeping their shoes on in a house where everyone takes them off, and (b) a local convention that's basically a racket, like a 'service charge' the manager pockets or a queue that exists only because the office is understaffed, where I'd like to know whether I still owe it or whether the obligation dies with the legitimacy of the practice.
