I teach a 90-minute seminar on the ethics of aggregation to about 18 upper-level undergraduates, and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the dust-specks problem from the inside rather than recognize it. The core I want them to run into: if you accept any positive exchange rate at all between one person's serious suffering and trivial annoyances — if there is any finite number of dust specks you would trade for a single hour of agony — then that rate can be multiplied until the specks outweigh the agony, and the only ways off that ladder are to say that below some point a "harm" stops being a harm at all, or that certain harms are protected from being outweighed by any quantity of trivial ones.

Design an activity in which the group sets its own exchange rate in public and in advance, and then has to live with it. It should have two stages: first, a structured way for the group to converge on a number themselves (no lecture up front — they produce the rate, and the rules should make them commit to it), and second, a moment where I take their own number and apply it to an extreme case so that they watch the conclusion arrive from their own premise. Include the rules, materials, and a timeline, plus a payoff or stake that does not require real money. Build in a contrast between two trivial harms — one the victim never notices at all and one the victim notices but finds utterly negligible — so the debrief can show whether their trading behavior is tracking magnitude or noticeability, and what that reveals about which escape they are actually reaching for. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the aggregation premise, the threshold escape, and the lexical escape, and should be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
