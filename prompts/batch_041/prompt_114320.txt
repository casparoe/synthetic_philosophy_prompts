I have no academic background in philosophy — I read it on my own, unsystematically but persistently — and I have lately become fixated on Stanisław Lem's *Solaris* and Tarkovsky's film of it. What I would like from you is a real critical reading of the novel, with the film kept alongside it, through Daniel Dennett's account of the three stances and of real patterns. I would much rather you tell me where the comparison breaks than force it to hold.

My reason for thinking there is something in this: the scientists on the station seem to me to work through Dennett's stances in order and to be defeated by each of them in turn. The physical stance gets them nowhere, since nothing about the ocean's composition allows its behaviour to be derived, and the enormous temporary formations that a century of Solaristics has catalogued and named remain entirely unaccounted for — a science that has accumulated descriptions without ever acquiring an explanation. The design stance looks more promising, because the ocean's behaviour seems purposive, but no one can say to what purpose, and what purpose there appears to be seems aimed at the observers. The intentional stance is then applied not to the ocean but to the visitors it produces: Kelvin's dead wife, who returns, believes herself to be herself, and is loved as herself.

Here is the knot I cannot untie. As I understand him, Dennett holds that a pattern is real when the description positing it earns its keep — when it compresses and predicts better than the alternatives — and he is content to say that the intentional stance, where it works, tracks something real rather than merely something useful. But this woman is generated out of Kelvin's own memory and returned to him, which makes her the most predictable creature on the station; her behaviour is legible to him in a way that no other person's ever quite is. On the criterion, that maximal predictability ought to make her mind as real as minds get, and yet it is precisely what makes the reader suspect that what Kelvin is seeing is a mirror. Does the novel therefore expose a weakness in the criterion, namely that it cannot distinguish a pattern genuinely in the world from one projected by an observer and handed back to him? Or does it dramatise a case the criterion handles correctly, with our feelings simply unable to accept the verdict? I want that argued rather than settled by intuition, and I want to know which scenes you would point to.

Two further matters. First, the ocean itself: if its behaviour is purposive without its having purposes, is it an instance of what Dennett calls free-floating rationales, and does that mean the design stance is where one should properly stop, with the intentional stance not refuted but simply unavailable? Second, the film's manner — its slowness, its lingering on the planet's surface, its refusal of thriller pacing — strikes me as a kind of stance-taking in its own right, and I would like to know whether that is a real observation or just an aesthetic preference dressed up as philosophy.

Please keep both directions honest: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a theory, and do not soften the theory to fit the novel. If the whole comparison is a category mistake — if the stances are a toolkit for prediction and Lem's ocean is a device for dramatising the limits of any such toolkit — say so plainly, and say what survives. End by telling me what the novel contributes to this debate that the arguments alone cannot.
