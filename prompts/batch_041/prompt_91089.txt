I am a fourth-year PhD student working on teleology and function in the philosophy of biology, and I would like to rehearse a defence of a taxonomy I have been developing against someone who thinks it is unnecessary. Please take the part of a philosopher of biology who holds what I will call the unrestricted view of free-floating rationales: the view that wherever a trait has a selection history, we may say what it is for and what it would be rational for the system to do, and that this attribution requires nothing further about the system's internal states. Play the position at full strength rather than as a foil — it has real resources, and I want them used: the distinction between a rationale and a mechanism, the thought that as-if locutions are legitimate wherever design is present, and the claim that the truthmaker for a rationale is the selective regime rather than any representation. Concede points where the argument forces you to; do not concede the main one. Speak as a working scientist-philosopher would: compact, a little impatient with terminological inflation, willing to use the design stance without embarrassment. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, stay in character until I say stop, and if I introduce a term you think is doing no work, say so and make me define it. You are free to attack the taxonomy itself rather than defend the case I am about to give you.

My taxonomy distinguishes three grades of teleological description. At the first grade we may say what a trait is for and what it would be rational for the system to do, with no claim whatever about internal states: a thin free-floating rationale. At the second grade the attribution is licensed only when some internal state of the system has a causal profile that covaries with the relevant feature of the environment, so that the state can be said to be about that feature without being a reason for anyone: a thick free-floating rationale. At the third grade we get ordinary reasons — states the system can be said to hold and to act on. My claim is that the unrestricted view collapses the first two grades into one, and that the cases which give the notion its appeal — kin recognition, mate choice, immune self/non-self discrimination, chemotaxis — are nearly all thick rather than thin.

Here is my opening move. Consider a hypothetical but physically unremarkable system: a lineage of mineral crystals in a natural thermal gradient, with no designers and no nervous systems anywhere in the picture. Suppose the crystals grow by templating their own lattice, so that lattice defects are heritable, and suppose the flow is such that crystals oriented one way against the gradient are preferentially dissolved while the others persist and go on seeding further growth. Over enough generations, orientation is selected for. Nothing in the crystal represents the gradient; no internal state covaries with it in any interesting sense; there is only a selection history. By your lights this is a thin free-floating rationale if anything is: it is rational for the crystal to hold that orientation, and nothing need entertain anything. So tell me what rationale is adding here that was selected for does not already say. If nothing, then the free-floating rationale is a redescription of selection and the normativity you wanted was never in it. If something, say what it is — and then tell me whether that something is present in the crystal or only in the describer, because I do not think the unrestricted view can have it both ways.
