I hold that an agent's preferences are, in principle, recoverable from its behavior — that when we fit a reward function to observed actions under an optimality assumption, we are identifying the mental state the behavior expresses, rather than constructing a convenient fiction. On this view, behavior is evidence of preference roughly the way a footprint is evidence of a foot.

Tell me what would count against it. I am most interested in the defeaters a committed defender would themselves concede, not the ones critics rehearse: whether the underdetermination of reward from behavior is a technical inconvenience to be managed with simplicity priors or a reason to abandon the identification altogether; whether demonstrations generated by a boundedly rational, habit-ridden, or inconsistent agent tell against the optimality assumption or merely complicate it; and whether the view retains any content once it has been qualified to accommodate preference reversals, framing effects, and preferences that appear to be constructed at the moment of choice rather than consulted.

Sort these from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which the position can absorb by amendment, and give me an honest verdict on whether it has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind.
