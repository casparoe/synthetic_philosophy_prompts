Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The subject is whether the simulation argument damps or amplifies fanaticism about the far future.

You may take the following as given, and should restate it in your own words in a sentence or two rather than citing anything. The simulation argument holds that one of three things must be the case: that almost no civilizations survive to the point of being able to run high-fidelity simulations of their ancestors, that those which do are almost never interested in running them, or that we are almost certainly living in one such simulation. Fanaticism about the far future is the disposition to let the astronomically large stakes of the very long run dominate practical reasoning, so that a minuscule probability of affecting the distant future outweighs a large and nearly certain near-term benefit. The usual thought is that the simulation argument cuts against fanaticism: if we are probably simulated, the future we take ourselves to be shaping is not ours to shape, since the simulators may truncate it or may determine it themselves, so our long-run leverage is discounted and the fanatical option loses ground.

Your task is to construct a case that runs the other way. Build a scenario in which accepting the simulation argument makes a specified far-future intervention look better rather than worse, so that an agent who takes the hypothesis seriously becomes more willing to gamble on the far future, while an otherwise identical agent who assigns the hypothesis no credence stays with the modest near-term option. The scenario must satisfy the following constraints.

1. The comparison is between the intervention's expected value conditional on our being simulated and its expected value conditional on our not being simulated, and the case must yield the former greater than the latter. The lever may not be the agent's credence in the simulation hypothesis itself, and it may not be a stipulation that the far future matters more, or is more responsive to us, than the base rate suggests. Whatever difference appears must be traced to a mechanism.

2. Axiology is held fixed. Both agents are total utilitarians who count the welfare of simulated beings, if there are any, exactly as they count the welfare of non-simulated beings. You may not get the amplification by valuing or discounting simulated minds differently, and you may not get it by assuming the simulators have interests that count for more.

3. No exotic devices. No omniscient or near-omniscient simulators; no simulators who evaluate our choices as such, reward or punish them, or arrange the world in anticipation of them; no reverse causation; no perfect predictors; no simulation physics that differs in a way that matters to the argument. The simulators must be ordinary agents with ordinary motives and ordinary constraints — curiosity, research, entertainment, pedagogy, budget — and their behaviour must be the sort of thing a proponent of the simulation argument would already concede.

4. The intervention must be something an ordinary person or a small organisation could actually do, not a planetary engineering project, and the scenario should stay at human scale and run to roughly a page. Name the agents, specify what the intervention costs and what it does, and give concrete numbers: the near-term benefit forgone, the far-future payoff at stake, and the probabilities of the intervention mattering under each hypothesis, so that the comparison can be checked arithmetically rather than asserted.

5. The scenario must not stipulate its own conclusion. The amplification has to fall out of the setup rather than be announced in it.

After the scenario, provide the demonstration.

(a) State the case cleanly.

(b) For each agent, give the expected value of the fanatical option and of the modest option, show the arithmetic, and say which option that agent takes.

(c) Identify the single structural feature doing the work, and say precisely what it alters — the probability that the intervention matters, the size of the far future at stake, the correlation between our situation and the simulators' purposes, or something else.

(d) Remove that feature and show what happens. If the case collapses back into the usual damping verdict, show the collapse; if it does not collapse cleanly, say so and say why.

(e) Say how a defender of the damping verdict would resist: what they would deny, whether denying it costs them anything they wanted elsewhere, and whether the case can be rebuilt on assumptions they are committed to.

The case is for an argument about whether the damping claim is a genuine consequence of the simulation argument or an artefact of one particular picture of what simulators do and why; that is what makes a scenario which simply stipulates the verdict a failure rather than a shortcut.
