Each spring our department runs a small recorded interview series for graduate students, and I have been asked to moderate the final session. The guest is a scholar of nineteenth-century political philosophy — I am deliberately leaving her unnamed here, since I am describing the position I want you to work against rather than any particular living person — who has been arguing, in a series of papers I admire but do not believe, that Mill's late socialist writings should be read not as a rupture with On Liberty but as the working-out of the same commitments. Her reading, as I understand it, runs roughly like this: Mill's distinction between the laws of production, which he took to be fixed by nature, and the laws of distribution, which he took to be of human institution and therefore revisable, makes the existing property regime contingent in a way that licenses a socialist conclusion; the stationary state, far from being a gloomy concession, is a destination he actually preferred to the endless trampling and elbowing of commercial society; and his own recorded objections to socialism — that it presupposes a level of mental culture and public spirit the working class had not yet attained, and that collective ownership would intensify the despotism of society over the individual — are claims about timing and risk rather than about principle. On her account Mill is a liberal socialist whose real quarrel with the Owenites and with state socialists is about means (cooperative association and partnership rather than central administration) and about sequence.

I want eight to eleven questions that put this reading under pressure, with a one-line follow-up attached to each, keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Please include at least one question on the production/distribution distinction — whether it really delivers a socialist conclusion or only a more aggressive reform of property rights — and press on what happens to the distinction once Mill concedes that the incentives of producers are themselves shaped by institutions. Include one on the precondition objection, whose sting is that it threatens to reduce her reading to the harmless claim that socialism is correct but premature; if she says the objection is about timing, the follow-up should ask what would count as the conditions having been met, and whether Mill gives any criterion at all. Include one on the liberty worry, where I expect her to say that Mill's anxiety about opinion under socialism is continuous with On Liberty rather than in tension with it; the follow-up should ask whether that continuity is purchased by reading the harm principle as narrower than most of its friends suppose. Include one on the wage relation and the partnership alternative — is Mill's target the wage form itself or merely its current distribution, and if the former, why does he stop at profit-sharing? — and one on his concrete proposals about inheritance and land, asking whether they require the very state capacity he distrusts. Close with a question that forces her to specify what she would accept as evidence against the continuity thesis. Two housekeeping requests: do not build questions whose interest lies in provenance (who anticipated whom, which edition changed what), and do not ask her to rehearse the secondary literature or cite editions — I want questions that make her defend the reading on the merits. Give me a committed set: where the evidence underdetermines the interpretation, take a side and frame the question so that it presupposes that side, rather than writing a question that surveys the options and leaves the answer open. Add a short note at the top, in a sentence or two, saying which single question you think is most likely to unsettle her, and where in the sequence you would place it.
