I host a small philosophy-of-language reading group that runs a public interview series, and next week's guest is a philosopher who's spent basically his whole career defending Russell's theory of descriptions with no hedging: his line is that a sentence of the form the F is G just is the existence claim plus the at-most-one claim plus the predication, that descriptions are quantifiers and not referring devices, and that every supposed counterexample of the last sixty years is either pragmatics or a misreading of what Russell was up to. He's also got this pet line that the theory was built for the definite article of mathematics and logic — the empty set, the identity element — where uniqueness is handed to you by the axioms, and that people who complain about ordinary-language 'the' are just noticing that ordinary language is sloppy.

I'm moderating, and I want questions that actually corner him rather than let him recite the 1905 paper. Can you write me 9 or 10 of them, in the order I should ask, with a follow-up attached to each one that's keyed to the answer he's most likely to give? He has two escape hatches he uses constantly — thats pragmatics, and you're confusing meaning with use — so I want the later follow-ups to make those harder and harder to reach for, and the last question to be about what evidence or argument would actually make him give the theory up.

Please make sure we hit at least these, roughly:

- kind-level definites: 'the electron', 'the tiger'. There's no unique individual there, so what happened to the uniqueness clause — and if you need a separate generic operator to save it, isn't that giving up on a uniform account of 'the'?
- incomplete descriptions: 'the table' when there are fifty tables in the room. If the fix is that context restricts the domain, then the uniqueness clause never does any work on its own, and I want to know how that isn't just relabelling the problem.
- anaphoric 'the': a man walked in, the man sat down. Does he really want to say the second sentence asserts that there's exactly one man?
- predicative uses: 'John is the mayor'. Nothing there is claiming that there's exactly one mayor in the world.
- the scope stuff: negation, as in the king of France is not bald, and belief reports, where the theory predicts a genuine ambiguity. Do ordinary speakers actually hear two readings, or is the theory inventing an ambiguity to keep its truth conditions intact?
- the presupposition question: is the uniqueness claim asserted or presupposed? I want to know which he says, and then I want to be ready for both answers.
- the epistemic motivation — the man in the iron mask kind of case, where we can know alot about whoever he was without knowing who he was. Is that an argument about meaning or an argument about knowledge? This is the one I'm least sure how to press.
- the rigidity thing: descriptions don't pick out the same thing across possible worlds the way names do, so if it turned out that someone else did the thing the description mentions, the theory has to say the description picks out that other person.
- and one nonstandard one: a friend of mine is building a question-answering system and keeps asking me what a Russellian thinks the system should do when a user's query contains a definite description that fails uniqueness — return false, return nothing, or throw a presupposition error? I'd like a question that gets him to commit to something there, since it's a place where the semantics has to cash out.

One more thing: some of my bullets might be built on a bad reading of the theory, and if you think one of them is, tell me that instead of writing a question around it. And for each question, say how confident you are that it lands on his actual position rather than just sounding clever, and single out the one question you're least sure about.
