I'm in a jurisprudence reading group that has now spent three sessions on the Hart–Fuller exchange — Hart's 1958 Harvard Law Review article and Fuller's reply in the same volume — and the frustrating thing is that nobody has moved an inch. Both sides seem to know the other's arguments by heart and neither is persuaded, and at this point I'd rather have a diagnosis of the impasse than another round of it. As I understand the two positions: Hart holds that whether something is law and whether it is just are two seperate questions, so a rule can be valid law and still be so wicked that we have decisive reason not to obey it; faced with the post-war German informer case, where a wife denounced her husband under a Nazi statute and the courts afterwards had to decide what to do with her, he thinks the honest route is to punish her under a frankly retrospective statute rather than pretend the Nazi rule was never law at all. Fuller thinks that picture treats law as a one-way projection of authority onto passive subjects, and that law is really an enterprise — a sustained, reciprocal effort to subject conduct to the governance of rules — and an enterprise can fail so badly at being itself that it stops being the thing it is trying to be; his eight requirements (generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, and the rest) are internal to that activity rather than moral ornaments stuck on the outside, so a regime that hides its rules, rules retroactively and ignores its own pronouncements isn't law that happens to be bad but at best a borderline case. Both are answering the question 'what is law?', which is why I can't see how they manage to miss each other so completely for sixty-odd years.

My hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about law at all, but something closer to a negotiation over what the word should be used for — Hart defending a concept built for descriptive social science, where 'this is law, and it's monstrous' needs to be a coherent thing to say, and Fuller defending one built for the question a citizen or judge actually faces, where calling something law is supposed to settle something about what you owe it. If that's right then neither is refuting the other, they're offering differnt tools and arguing about which tool we need, which is a question about our purposes rather than about the nature of law. But I can see at least two rival diagnoses and can't tell which is right. One: it's a disagreement about which cases are the data — Hart takes the clear statute of a functioning state as the central case and the Nazi mess as peripheral, Fuller takes the pathology as revealing what the thing is for, and there may be no neutral way to decide which case gets to be central. Two: it's a covert normative disagreement about what judges and citizens should do, dressed up as conceptual analysis, so that 'is this law?' is quietly doing the work of 'may I disobey it?'. There's also a guy in the group who keeps insisting the whole thing turns on whether 'law' is a functional kind like 'knife' — defined by what it's for, so that a thing that can't cut isn't a knife — or a natural kind like 'water', defined by what it's made of whatever we use it for; he thinks Fuller wins by default if it's functional and Hart wins if it's natural, and I think that's too quick but I can't quite say why.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really — verbal, a metalinguistic negotiation over a term, two people answering different questions under one heading, a fight over which cases count, or bedrock normative disagreement that no amount of analysis will shift? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a stipulation, a change of question, or nothing, with the stall being permanent for a reason you can state? Please don't smooth over the parts that are genuinely contested among people who work on this: I don't want to be told that everyone now agrees Hart was obviously right that the two questions can be prised apart, or that Fuller's inner morality was long ago shown to be question-begging, if in fact the field is split — keep what's settled clearly apart from what's up for grabs, and flag it when you're giving me your own view rather than the consensus. Finish with a last line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the diagnosis.
