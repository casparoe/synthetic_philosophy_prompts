My sister is finishing a psychiatry residency and I write software, and we have spent roughly two years re-arguing Thomas Szasz whenever we are in the same house. She thinks his claim that mental illness is a myth is a cruel and empirically dead slogan. I keep finding myself half-persuaded by the thought that a lot of what gets called mental illness is really a set of problems in living, and that the diagnosis is doing something other than describing a disease. Dinner is not going to settle this. What I want instead is the document we should have written: the kind of thing my team puts together when two engineers have been arguing past each other for months — a joint statement both of us could sign.

Treat the signatories as a committed Szaszian — someone who holds that without a demonstrable disease of the brain a psychiatric diagnosis is not a medical finding but a judgment, and that the diagnosis is what licenses the coercion — and a committed mainstream clinician who holds that psychiatric disorders are real illnesses, evidenced by genetics, course, and treatment response.

Please give me: a numbered list of claims both could sign, about the facts as well as the concepts; a table of the disagreements that remain, each written in wording its own side would accept; and the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody, with, for each, a case or piece of evidence both sides agree is relevant and each side's prediction about it recorded in advance.

Three things I would rather you press on than smooth over. One, diagnoses that include, as part of what defines them, the patient's failure to recognize that she is ill — eating disorders are the usual example — together with the fact that treatment sometimes proceeds by declaring the patient incapable and feeding her against her refusal: does that pattern confirm the illness, or show that the category is a way of overruling her? Two, the hypothetical that a reliable brain lesion or biomarker for schizophrenia is found: would that vindicate psychiatry, or dissolve the diagnosis, on the grounds that the condition would then be a neurological disease rather than a mental one? Three, whether general medicine's categories are really any less value-laden than psychiatry's; I suspect the mainstream side's strongest reply is that Szasz's critique proves too much, and I want to know whether that reply works.

If part of the disagreement turns out to be about what the word disease is being made to mean, say which part, and say what is left over once that is set aside.
