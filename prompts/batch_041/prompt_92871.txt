I am working through the seventeenth discussion — headed "Problem XVII," on the impossibility of a departure from the natural course of events — of al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, in Sabih Ahmad Kamali's English rendering. I can follow the broad shape of the argument, but two of its moves seem to pull against each other, and I would like help working out what the passage is actually doing. The excerpt I am stuck on runs as follows:

"In our view, the connection between what are believed to be the cause and the effect is not necessary. Take any two things. This is not That; nor can That be This. The affirmation of one does not imply the affirmation of the other; nor does its denial imply the denial of the other. … Fire, which is an inanimate thing, has no action. How can one prove that it is an agent? The only argument is from the observation of the fact of burning at the time of contact with fire. But observation only shows that one is with the other, not that it is by it and has no other cause than it. … So it is clear that existence with a thing does not prove being by it. Let us illustrate. Suppose there is a blind man whose eyes are diseased, and who has not heard from anyone of the difference between night and day. If one day his disease is cured, and he can consequently see colours, he will guess that the agent of the perception of the forms of colours which has now been acquired by his eyes is the opening of the eyes. This will not be absolutely untrue, inasmuch as given the soundness of the eyes: and their being open: and the obstruction being removed: and the object before him having colour, it follows that he must be able to see, and it would be unintelligible if he were not. But when the Sun is set, and the atmosphere is dark, he will acquire the knowledge that the light of the Sun was the cause of the impression of colours upon his sight. Therefore, how can our opponent safely ignore the possibility that: (a) among the Principles of Being there should be causes from which flow the temporal events which are observed to be connected with each other; (b) unlike bodies in motion, such temporal events should not be liable to destruction or elimination by us, and (c) that if they were so liable, only then could we apprehend their separability from each other, and would consequently understand that their cause lies beyond what we observe?"

My difficulty is this. Up to the blind man, the argument looks purely epistemological: observation discloses only that one event is with another, never that it is by the other, and al-Ghazālī draws the modest-sounding conclusion that existence with a thing does not prove being by it. That is a claim about what we can know, not about what causes what. Yet he evidently takes himself to have established something stronger — that fire has no action, and that God is the agent of the burning.

The blind man is plainly meant to bridge that gap, and this is where I lose the thread. The blind man's error is inferring agency from observed conjunction, and he is corrected not by being told that nothing causes the impression of colour but by learning that the sun does. The analogy therefore seems to establish that a further cause lies beyond the observed conjunction — but the further cause it produces is a perfectly ordinary natural one. Why doesn't the same reasoning license a picture on which fire really is a cause of burning, with God as a yet further cause behind it? And if it does, what in the passage rules that picture out? I notice that he even allows for "causes from which flow the temporal events" among the "Principles of Being," which reads to me like a concession to secondary causes rather than a denial of them.

I am also defeated by the compressed counterfactual in (c): "if they were so liable, only then could we apprehend their separability from each other, and would consequently understand that their cause lies beyond what we observe." I cannot see what work the clause about our being able to destroy or eliminate the events is doing, or why our incapacity to pry cause from effect should tell us anything about whether they are separable in fact.

What I would like is an exposition that takes the passage in order: what each step is supposed to establish, which steps are epistemological and which metaphysical, and whether the conclusion he needs — that fire is not an agent at all — actually follows, or whether he has secured only the weaker claim that we cannot know fire to be one. On the blind man, I want the strongest reading on which the analogy supports him rather than the philosophers, if such a reading exists, together with an honest statement of where the analogy strains. On (c), a reconstruction of the reasoning in plain prose would help, including what the destruction-and-elimination clause is there for. Please treat the excerpt as the whole of the evidence; I am after what can be extracted from it, not a general survey of occasionalism.
