Write a pantoum about the decision problem known as Death in Damascus. The scenario, for reference: a traveler in Damascus meets Death in the market, and Death looks startled; the traveler flees to Aleppo, and only later learns that Death's appointment with him had been set for that night in Aleppo. In the decision-theoretic version, a perfectly reliable predictor has already fixed where the traveler will be, and Death will be waiting at that place. The traveler knows the setup. Staying in Damascus therefore means Death is in Damascus; fleeing to Aleppo means Death is in Aleppo. Each act is close to a death sentence, and each act is also evidence about the prediction, and so about where Death stands. Resolving to stay makes fleeing look better; resolving to flee makes staying look better. Evidential reasoning tells him to choose the act that would be the best news; causal reasoning tells him that his present choice cannot move Death, who has already been placed. Neither settles the deliberation, because the credence he assigns to Death's location is tied to the credence he assigns to his own act.

Requirements for the pantoum:

- Five quatrains.
- Strict pantoum repetition: the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear as the first and third lines of the next, and in the final stanza the second and fourth lines are the first and third lines of the first stanza, so the poem closes on itself. Rhyme is optional; the repetition is not.
- The form should do the philosophical work. The same line, returning in a new position, should shift its meaning or its recommendation — a line that counsels staying should, on its return, counsel fleeing, or the identical words should come to name the opposite conclusion. The closed loop should leave the reader where the deliberation leaves the agent: no stable resting point.
- Do not resolve the problem. No third option, no acceptance, no serene resignation, no discovery that the traveler was never really in danger. The tension should remain open.
- Render both the evidential and the causal readings of the case, and make clear that even the causal reading fails to ratify any act. A decision theorist should recognize the instability rather than a vague fatalism; a reader who does not know the problem should still find the poem coherent and worth reading.
- No citations, no named theorists, no footnotes. The poem should stand on its own.
