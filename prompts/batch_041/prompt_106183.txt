Hey, I'm stuck on a bit of Plato's Republic and could really use some help. It's Book II, where Glaucon lays out his challenge to Socrates — the ring of Gyges bit. Here's the chunk I keep re-reading:

"They say that to do injustice is, by nature, good; to suffer injustice, evil; but that the evil is greater than the good. And so when men have both done and suffered injustice and have had experience of both, not being able to avoid the one and obtain the other, they think that they had better agree among themselves to have neither; hence there arise laws and mutual covenants; and that which is ordained by law is termed by them lawful and just. This they affirm to be the origin and nature of justice; --it is a mean or compromise, between the best of all, which is to do injustice and not be punished, and the worst of all, which is to suffer injustice without the power of retaliation; and justice, being at a middle point between the two, is tolerated not as a good, but as the lesser evil, and honoured by reason of the inability of men to do injustice. For no man who is worthy to be called a man would ever submit to such an agreement if he were able to resist; he would be mad if he did. Such is the received account, Socrates, of the nature and origin of justice."

Then he goes on to tell the story of Gyges' ancestor, the shepherd who finds a ring in a bronze horse down in a cave opened up by an earthquake, figures out it turns him invisible, and uses it to seduce the queen and murder the king — and Glaucon's punchline is that if you gave that ring to a just man and an unjust man, they'd both end up doing the same thing. For context, earlier he'd asked Socrates which of three kinds of goods justice is — the sort you want for its own sake, the sort you want for its results, or the sort you only put up with for the results — and Socrates says the first, so the whole challenge is Glaucon trying to make that answer impossible.

What I can't get my head around is the structure of the arguement. The "received account" he gives already assumes that injustice is naturally good and that justice is just the compromise you settle for when you're too weak to get away with anything. But then he turns around and says he'll *show* that people are only just against their will — as if that's a conclusion he's establishing rather than the premise he started from. Is that circular, or am I missing a step somewhere? And where does the ring actually fit — is it a separate argument, or just a vivid illustration of the same claim?

Heres the part thats really bugging me, though. The ring takes away the punishment for injustice, sure. But it also takes away the reward for being just — the reputation, the praise, the fact that people trust you. So the just man who keeps the ring and still behaves justly isn't being tested on whether he loves justice for its own sake; he's also been stripped of every ordinary social reason to be just. So of course the thought experiment "fails" to turn up anyone who stays just. Does that objection land, or is Glaucon entitled to say the only reason that could matter is the intrinsic one? And if the ring isn't a fair test, what would a fair test even look like?

Last thing: is Glaucon making a claim about how people actually are, or about what justice *is*? Those seem to pull apart and I can never tell which one Socrates is supposed to be answering. Would love a patient walk-through — what the passage is actually doing, which hidden premise is doing the real work, and what Socrates would have to show for the challenge to be genuinely met rather than dodged.
