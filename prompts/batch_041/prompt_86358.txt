I'm a novelist — no philosophy training past a undergrad minor — and next month I'm the outside speaker at a university's action theory reading group. I read a chapter from my novel in progress, then give a short paper built out of it, then they get me for an hour of questions, which is the part I'm scared of.

The chapter: a man stops between two doors that are, as far as he or anyone can tell, identical, and stays there for three days. I wrote him as neither paralyzed by anxiety nor stupid, just genuinely indifferent, and I want to defend that as a real state and not just a very small preference. My thesis is that the standard fix — there's always some tie-breaker, even an arbitrary one, so the case never really arises — is a cheat, because it hands the agent the resolution and calls that an explanation. What I actually want to say is that the move from indifference to action isn't a rational transition at all; it's closer to an event than a conclusion. And I want to claim that picking, where you have no reason, is a different act from choosing, where you do, and that action theory only ever built a model for the second one.

The room: mostly decision theorists and philosophers of action, one Kantian who'll think a will without a ground isn't a will, and three or four grad students. Nobody there works on fiction. I expect one of them to tell me the picking/choosing distinction is already in the literature and I've reinvented it worse, and someone else to say the starving-ass fable isn't actually in Buridan, it just got hung on him later — I don't know if that's true, or whether it matters, so tell me.

Give me the prep sheet: the eight objections I'll actually get from this room, in their vocabulary rather than mine, ordered by how much damage they do, each with a two-minute answer and a note on where the answer is soft, plus what I should concede early and what I should hold. And the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Skip the reading list, I won't read it.
