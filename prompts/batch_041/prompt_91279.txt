I am an undergraduate in a 200-level epistemology seminar — 18 students, 75 minutes per session — and I, along with two classmates, have been assigned to run the session on the epistemology of conspiracy theories. I need a runnable activity with all of the materials written out, and it has to make the group feel the force of one specific difficulty before anyone names it: that the difference between a warranted suspicion and a conspiracy theory does not seem to lie in the evidence, since the same body of evidence can be arranged to support either story; and it does not seem to lie in the form of the belief either, since real conspiracies occur and are often confirmed only after the fact. Whatever is left has to be located in the epistemic procedure the believer runs, and I want the group to discover that from the inside rather than hear it from us. So no lecture up front, and the phrase "conspiracy theory" should not appear in anything participants read or hear until the debrief.

Here is the fiction my classmates and I have sketched. Please use it and fill it out. In a mid-sized town, the Clearfield Water Authority has announced that routine testing found elevated levels of an industrial solvent in the reservoir, and that the cause was a late-reported accidental spill by a contractor. Two months later, a mid-level employee who had raised concerns about the plant's record-keeping dies by suicide; the coroner rules suicide. A local blog then claims the man was about to go public and that the utility, the coroner, and the state regulator are covering up a program of deliberate dumping.

The materials should be: a public dossier of 16 short evidence cards — six that fit the official account, five that raise suspicion, three that are genuinely ambiguous, and two that are simply irrelevant — plus six private role cards, one each for the utility's spokesperson, a state regulator, the dead employee's sister, a local reporter, a business owner whose shop depends on the reservoir, and an independent hydrologist. Every private card must be something a reasonable person would take seriously, and they must be distributed so that no single participant has enough to settle anything: some should cut against the cover-up story, some for it. With 18 students you can decide whether roles are shared three ways or whether some participants stay unassigned; tell me which you chose and why.

Keep the shape of the procedure unless you can improve it: silent reading; then an anonymous written credence — the probability that the utility deliberately dumped and covered it up — together with one sentence stating what evidence would move that number, which the facilitator collects and seals; then about 25 minutes of deliberation in which the group must produce a single shared account of what happened, under the rule that any card not fitting the account is either explained or set aside, with setting a card aside costing nothing; then the facilitator reveals one new card that cuts strongly for the official story; then a second credence vote, identical in form, unsealed and compared with the first; then a sealed envelope giving the actual facts, which must contain both a genuine exoneration and a genuine misconduct that neither the official account nor the cover-up story predicted.

Give me: the full text of all 16 public cards, the six private cards, and the role sheets; the exact wording of both vote forms; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; whatever small stakes the group is playing for — I would rather not use candy or points, so tell me what is at stake and why it is enough — and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the ideas, says what will predictably go wrong the first time we run it and how to handle it, and is frank about which feature of the real phenomenon the fiction structurally cannot deliver. I suspect the gaps include the years-long investment and social identity of an actual believer, and the on-demand supply of fresh defeaters from an information environment; for each gap, tell me whether the facilitator can patch it by saying something or whether it simply has to be conceded.

In the debrief, without citing any literature — no names, no reading list, I have the syllabus — cover the possibility that "conspiracy theory" does not name a single epistemic kind; the idea that a hypothesis can be made unfalsifiable by collective interpretation rather than by any individual's decision; why "you cannot prove it" is not by itself a defect; and why the outcome of our exercise must not be read as "the suspicion was false." Participants should not come away thinking that conspiracy believers are a type of person. Write the answer in American English.
