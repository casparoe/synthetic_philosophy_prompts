I'm a journalist who covers AI and ideas, and there's a design habit in safety circles I cant get past. A lot of teams want their systems to be "low impact": they build in a penalty, or write it into the spec, for changing the world more than you have to — don't knock over the vase, don't rearrange the furniture, don't burn the house down on the way to the fridge. The picture is that a very capable agent should behave like a good houseguest. One researcher told me they just score the agent on how far it moves the world's future from where it would otherwise have gone, and penalize the difference.

My problem is that every version of this seems to need two things nobody quite states: a baseline (low compared to what?) and a line between the changes that are the point and the changes that are just collateral. So what does the practice actually presuppose? Is "avoid side effects" secretly a full theory of value in a trenchcoat, or can it be cashed out thin — a default trajectory, a preference for changes that can be undone, a rule about what counts as "the task"? Which views about value would license the practice, and which does it quietly rule out? Is it even internally consistent, given that a system that avoids every side effect usually fails to do anything at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, and be honest about how much of it is strictly required by the practice and how much is just one convenient way of implementing it. Then tell me what the practice would have to give up if the baseline idea turned out to be incoherent.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
