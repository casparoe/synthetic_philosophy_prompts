**Question 2 (10 marks).** On Monday a teacher tells her class: there will be an exam next week, on one of Monday to Friday, and you won't be able to know the night before which day it will be — it will be a genuine surprise. The students reason: it can't be Friday, because if we get to Thursday night with no exam we'll know it has to be Friday, and then it won't be a surprise. But then it can't be Thursday either, because if we get to Wednesday night with no exam we'll know it has to be Thursday, Friday being already ruled out, and again it won't be a surprise. The same move rules out Wednesday, Tuesday and Monday, so no exam can be given, and the teacher's announcement was false. As it happens, the exam is given on Wednesday and the class is surprised. (a) Set out the students' reasoning as clearly as you can. (b) Say where, if anywhere, that reasoning fails, and say whether the teacher's announcement is false, inconsistent, or neither. Justify your answer. *Rubric: 4 marks for (a), 4 for (b), 2 for clarity and justification.*

**Student's answer.** "The students are using backwards induction, and the induction itself is fine. Suppose the exam hasn't happened by Thursday night. The only day left in the week is Friday, so the students know the exam is on Friday, so it isn't a surprise, so the teacher can't have meant Friday. But once Friday is ruled out, the same argument rules out Thursday: if Wednesday night arrives with no exam, the only remaining days are Thursday and Friday, and Friday is already excluded, so the students know it's Thursday, so it isn't a surprise. Repeat for Wednesday, Tuesday and Monday, and no day survives. So the students conclude there will be no exam, which contradicts the teacher's announcement. The reasoning is valid at every step — the students only ever use the announcement plus the fact that the exam hasn't happened yet — so the fault must be in what they're entitled to assume at the start. Specifically, they're entitled to infer that if Thursday night passes with no exam then the exam is on Friday, but they aren't entitled to assume the teacher will actually go through with it. If the teacher might change her mind, or might have been bluffing, the students can't rule Friday out, and the whole chain collapses. The announcement therefore can't be treated as a promise; it is inconsistent, in the way the liar sentence is inconsistent: it says something which, if the students accept it, makes it impossible for what it says to come true. So this is really a self-referential paradox like the liar, and a teacher who genuinely means what she says can't set a surprise exam at all."
