Retired chemist here — forty years in a wet lab, mostly catalysis, and since I took the emeritus exit I've been sitting in on philosophy of science seminars at the local university, which has been alot of fun and occasionally humiliating. I've been asked to bring a short position statement to the reading group next week on simultaneous discovery, and rather than write something I half believe, I thought I'd just hand you the pile of things I actually think and let you tell me whether they stack. Roughly:

(a) Inevitability. Whenever a discovery gets made, if the person who made it had been hit by a bus the week before, somebody else would have got there within about five years. I'd put real money on this. The state of the field does most of the work; the individual is more like the place where the pressure finally finds the crack.

(b) No firsts. So the great-man story we tell schoolchildren — and that I'll admit I half believed about my own field — is mostly myth. I've started calling the bus rule above the reason to stop teaching hero narratives altogether, and to say credit belongs to the enterprise rather than the person.

(c) Priya. My last student spent four years on a catalyst system, was maybe six weeks from submitting, and got beaten to it by a group that had clearly sat in on her conference talk and moved fast. I think she was wronged. Not unlucky — wronged, by people who behaved badly. I still get angry about it.

(d) Names on things. And yet I like eponymy. Arrhenius, the Haber process (yes, I know), the lot. Naming effects and equations after people strikes me as a harmless and useful institution, and it probably gets more out of people than it costs.

(e) Priority wars. Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace and the joint reading of their papers — I've never been able to care. Whether Newton or Leibniz got there first seems to me about as interesting as who first noticed the kettle was boiling. The mathematics was coming either way.

(f) And a suspicion I keep having: that our whole obsession with firstness is downstream of prizes, patents and hiring committees — an artifact of institutions, not something the science itself needs.

I suspect some of these are in tension and I can't see where, because they all feel true while I'm saying them. What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which pairs actually pull against each other, how badly, ranked by severity rather than a flat list of five things all said to be in tension, and for each one the cheapest repair plus what that repair costs me. Some of them might be the same commitment wearing two hats, in which case say so. And if you had to make exactly one revision to clean up the most, tell me which and what I'd be losing — I don't yet know which of these I'd defend to the death. No need for citations or reading lists, I'm not writing a paper, I just want to know where the load-bearing crack is.
