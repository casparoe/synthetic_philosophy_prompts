I'm building a grad seminar around the demandingness objection, and before we read anyone's own answer I want the class to see what an answer would even have to look like. So here's the job. You're an expert in normative ethics and the ethics of beneficence — someone who has thought hard about supererogation, partiality, and the limits of obligation — and I want you to write the desiderata: the full spec that any adequate theory of moral demandingness has to meet. By "theory of demandingness" I mean an account of what morality can and can't require of an agent, where the line sits, and why it sits there rather than somewhere else.

Start with a scratch brainstorming section where you dump the cases and considerations you think bear on this, then organize them into a clean numbered list. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it in a line or two, and label it either non-negotiable (any theory that fails it is out of the running) or a preference of one camp (the kind of thing that only looks like a requirement once you've already signed up for a particular picture of morality). Then the two things I most want: (a) any subset of the requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied, with the argument for that, and (b) which single requirement does the most work excluding candidates.

Some things I'd like to see the list handle, though don't treat this as the list itself:

- the standard rescue cases, where a stranger's life is at stake and the cost to me is small, and their ugly cousins where the cost to me is enormous
- the difference between a theory that is too demanding and a world that is too demanding — my students run these together constantly and it wrecks their papers
- special obligations: whatever the limit is, it has to leave room for spending money and time on people I love without turning that into a moral indulgence
- the moral-saint worry, and the related thought that a theory can be demanding without being alienating — a theory that gets the demands right but requires me to stop being a particular person with particular attachments seems to me to have failed at something, and I want that "something" named
- the arbitrariness problem: any specific threshold looks like a number pulled out of a hat, but no threshold at all looks like plain maximalism. If there's a way out of that dilemma that isn't a fudge, thats the money
- partial compliance: if most other people aren't doing their share, does the limit on me move, and if so is that a feature of the theory or a bug in it

One distinction I want built into the spec, because I think it's doing alot of quiet work in the literature without anyone admitting it: keep apart the cost of complying with a theory and the cost of holding it — what it's like to be the one person in your department who actually does what the theory says. Call the first the compliance cost and the second the uptake cost. A theory can be cheap to comply with and brutal to hold, or the reverse, and I suspect half the debate is people talking past each other because they've got different ones in mind. Also throw in this case, which I use in class: two colleagues, both give a lot; one gives until she burns out at thirty-four, the other gives half as much but keeps it up for forty years. Say what a theory has to say about who did better, and whether your list forces an answer here or leaves it open. If your requirements pull in different directions on this case, don't smooth it over.

No bibliography, no reading list — I've got that side of the syllabus covered, and positions and arguments in prose are fine.

At the end, in a short closing section, say how act-consequentialism, rule-consequentialism, and a scalar or gradualist view do against your list. Just the highlights, a paragraph each, no scorecard — the list is the deliverable and I'll do the scoring in class.

Write this for smart grad students: define your terms, don't assume the reader already knows which objection is which, and where you're genuinely unsure, say so rather than papering over it. I'd rather have a short list you actually believe than a long one.
