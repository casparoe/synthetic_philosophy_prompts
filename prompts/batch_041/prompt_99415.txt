For a philosophy of language seminar I have to hand in a specification before I evaluate any particular account: what would a theory of slurs have to get right to count as an adequate account of how these terms mean and what they do? I want the desiderata themselves, not a tour of the literature. Give me the phenomena an adequate account must accommodate, the cases it must adjudicate, and the distinctions it must not collapse, each with the consideration that motivates it; mark each item as either a genuine constraint that any account owes us or a demand that one camp has smuggled in; flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, since an inconsistent list would be the most important result the exercise could yield; and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive accounts.

Treat adequacy as relative to a purpose I am fixing here: the account should be good enough to underwrite judgments about what may be said -- whether a term belongs in a classroom, whether a speaker may deploy a reclaimed form, whether a target is entitled to object -- not good enough to settle every question in the metaphysics of meaning.

I care about at least the following axes, and I want you to argue each one in or out rather than take it as given:

- Co-extensionality: a slur and its neutral counterpart appear to pick out the same class, so whatever divides them cannot be reference or descriptive content. Must the account treat the pair as one lexical item with two uses, as two items, or as something else?
- Gradation: offensiveness runs from mild to extreme and shifts with context and audience. Must the account predict the degree of derogation, or only its presence?
- Projection: how the derogation behaves under negation, in conditionals, in questions, and in reported speech. What pattern must the account capture, and can it do so without simply filing the derogation under presupposition, entailment, or conversational implicature?
- The neutral-use problem: the derogation often survives even when the speaker plainly lacks the attitude -- quoting, reporting, or speaking as a member of the targeted group. What must be true of the account for this not to be a contradiction?
- Speaker-independence: contrast a slur with a plain insult such as idiot. Why can the slur's force attach to the group rather than to the individual, and must the account explain that asymmetry?
- Non-derogatory and reclamatory uses, including a term that is affectionate inside the group and hostile outside it, and the later case in which an outsider adopts the reclaimed form. Is reclamation the same word, a new word, or a distinct use of the same word, and what does the account owe here?
- Slurs without neutral counterparts, and terms whose slur status is contested -- coined by opponents to name a group that rejects the label. Can a theory stay neutral about whether such a term is a slur, or must it decide, and on what basis?

Then build the account's hardest test out of two cases you construct yourself: (i) a term that is derogatory only in one dialect or region and innocuous elsewhere, and (ii) a term whose target is defined by ideology rather than by an inherited identity. State explicitly whether a single account can pass both along with the constraints above; if the demands conflict, say which you would give up and why. Only after the list is complete, and briefly, note how the main families of account -- semantic derogation, conventional-implicature or pragmatic views, expressivist views, and deflationary views that deny slurs are theoretically special -- fare against it.
