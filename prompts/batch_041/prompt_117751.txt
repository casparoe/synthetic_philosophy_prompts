Imagine a person whose neurons are replaced one at a time by silicon devices functionally equivalent to the tissue they displace, so that her memories, dispositions and behaviour are undisturbed throughout. The familiar argument from this case runs as follows: no single replacement could plausibly make the difference between surviving and not surviving, identity is transitive, so the wholly silicon system at the end of the process is the very person who began it — and therefore at least one form of mind uploading preserves the person who uploads.

Argue that this reasoning cannot establish that conclusion. Read the pivotal premise — that one replacement cannot make the difference — as an instance of a tolerance principle, that is, a principle licensing the inference from a difference too small to matter in the relevant respect to no difference in status, of exactly the sort that generates sorites paradoxes, and show that the argument therefore either begs the question or commits its proponent to a criterion of survival on which survival comes in degrees rather than being all or nothing. Then take up the most promising rejoinder, that the gradual process, unlike a one-step scan and copy, keeps the person inside a single continuous causal process, and argue that this rejoinder does not do the work required of it. Either it treats continuity as a qualitative difference, in which case it owes an account of why a gap in the process is fatal while a wholesale change of material is not; or it locates the difference in the size of the steps, in which case it inherits the sorites, since nothing in a discretized microphysics distinguishes one large step in kind from many small ones. Close by saying what a proponent of uploading can still legitimately claim once the replacement argument is set aside, and what she must give up.
