I'm putting together an application for a one-year junior research fellowship in philosophy, and the research proposal has to be short — about 1,200 words — and pitched at readers who are philosophers but not specialists in social choice. My topic is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I've been circling an idea for months in a reading group without ever getting it into a shape I'd be willing to defend on paper. The standard discussion of the theorem, the one I keep being told is the interesting one, turns on whether it is legitimate to impose the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms on the social or moral ordering and not merely on individual preferences, so that the argument appears to presuppose the very ex ante standpoint it is supposed to justify. I don't want to write that proposal; I know that terrain and I would only be restating it. What actually strikes me is something upstream. The impartial-observer version of the argument asks me to imagine being each person in turn, taking on that person's preferences over her own circumstances, and then to choose among the positions under an equal-probability assumption. That construction seems to presuppose a metaphysics of the self — that there is a 'me' who can be slotted into any life and who survives the slotting with her evaluative standpoint intact, and who can coherently rank being one particular person above being another. My suspicion is that this transferable-subject assumption, rather than the Pareto condition or the additivity of the aggregated function, is what does the real work in the argument, and that if one took seriously a view on which a person's preferences are partly constitutive of who she is, the observer's extended preferences would be either ill-defined or would smuggle in a substantive ethical claim. I genuinely don't know whether that is a philosophical problem or just a confusion of mine about what the construction is for.

What I want from you is help turning this into a proposal. Act as a philosopher of economics with a strong background in the foundations of welfare economics and in the metaphysics of personal identity, and begin by brainstorming in a way I can see: state in your own words what the aggregation theorem and the impartial-observer theorem each establish and what each assumes, then tell me candidly whether my suspicion is well-formed, what its strongest version is, and whether it survives the most obvious reply — that the extended-preference construction is a formal device which need not be read metaphysically at all. Please think carefully about this before drafting, and don't flatter the idea; if the honest answer is that the suspicion collapses into the standard objection, say so and propose the nearest thing that does not.

If it survives, or can be repaired into something better, draft the proposal itself. It should contain a precise statement of the question, sharp enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the existing positions leave it open — including the standard ex ante/ex post objection, the welfarism objection, and the bargaining-theoretic reading of the theorem, each of which I take to be distinct from my question; the method, meaning what I would actually do and in what order over a year of part-time work, and why conceptual analysis rather than a formal characterization theorem or an intuition-survey; the possible outcomes and which considerations would decide between them; an honest statement of what would count as the project failing, or as it succeeding in a way that is too thin to be worth reporting; and a realistic scope, including what I should cut if this turns out to be two projects rather than one. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list — I will supply the citations myself; where you need to refer to a particular argument or position, describe it well enough that I can track it down on my own. Write it in a register suitable for a fellowship panel, and keep it within the word limit.
