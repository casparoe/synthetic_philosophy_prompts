You are an expert in the comparative history of famine, at home in demography and agrarian economics as well as in moral and political philosophy, and you reason from causal mechanisms rather than from stock phrases. Please think carefully before you answer, and begin by spending a short paragraph setting out the dimensions along which one can sensibly attribute a famine to nature as opposed to politics. I want to see the rubric you will actually apply, not one reverse-engineered to fit conclusions you have already reached.

Some context on why I am asking. I spent thirty-five years as a soil and climate scientist, and for most of that time I treated famines as the far tail of a weather-and-yield distribution: a drought here, a blight there, a bad monsoon, and the rest was arithmetic. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I have been shaken by Amartya Sen's argument that major famines have occurred without any appreciable fall in the total food available in the affected region, which would make access and entitlement, rather than nature, the thing to explain. I am now unsure whether I spent a career studying the cause of famines or merely one of their enabling conditions. I want a definite verdict, not a survey of what scholars have said.

Here is what I would like you to do. Take the following famines and rate each one on a single dimension: the degree to which it should be classified as a political crime rather than a natural disaster. Give me a number from 0 to 10 for each, where 0 means it was essentially a natural event that no competent and honest authority could have averted, and 10 means it was essentially an act or omission of political will with nature playing at most a minor role. Then group the entries into tiers on the basis of those scores (for instance, tiers such as mostly natural, mixed, mostly political) and say in one sentence why each entry lands where it does. The entries are: the Irish famine of the 1840s; the Bengal famine of 1943; the Chinese famine associated with the Great Leap Forward, roughly 1959 to 1961; the Dutch Hongerwinter of 1944 to 1945; the Ethiopian famine of 1984 to 1985; and the Soviet famine of 1932 to 1933. Do not bother with precise mortality figures; I care about causal attribution, not body counts, and I would rather you say a number is contested than invent one.

For each famine, apply a test I have been turning over in my head, which I will call the counterfactual-harvest test: hold the weather, the pests, and the harvest fixed, and replace only the political and administrative response with that of a competent, honest government that cared about the affected population. Estimate, as a fraction between 0 and 1, how much of the excess mortality would have remained. State the fraction for each entry and justify it briefly.

Finally, I want you to commit. Tell me plainly which of the six you regard as most nearly a pure natural disaster and which as most nearly a pure political crime, and defend both choices against the strongest objection you can construct. If, after constructing that objection, you would revise a score, revise it and say so; if you would not, say that just as plainly. Do not close by telling me the question is contested and leaving the judgment to me — I am asking for your judgment, with your reasons.
