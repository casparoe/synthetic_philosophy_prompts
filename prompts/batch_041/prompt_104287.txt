I direct grantmaking at a foundation whose board has just voted to reorient our portfolio around the reduction of severe suffering, and I need to turn the moral position behind that vote into a procedure my two analysts and I can actually run on the roughly two hundred proposals we receive each funding cycle.

The board's position — our chair calls it lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — has three parts as I understand them. Suffering and enjoyment both count, but not on one scale: the disvalue of suffering is not simply the mirror image of the value of enjoyment. There is a threshold of suffering, understood as intensity sustained over time, above which suffering is lexically prior to everything else, so that no quantity of enjoyment, for any number of beings, can justify or outweigh even a small additional amount of suffering past that threshold. Below the threshold, suffering and enjoyment are commensurable and can be traded off in a common currency, so that relieving a very large amount of mild distress can outrank producing a smaller amount of mild enjoyment.

I am not certain I have stated the position correctly, and I am fairly sure the board has not thought through the cases that will decide the hard calls. So please give me the procedure rather than a defense of the view.

What the procedure has to fit: about two hundred proposals a year, forty or so funded; two analysts, no in-house expertise beyond public health and economics; we can never measure anyone's experience directly and must infer severity from clinical, behavioral, and epidemiological proxies; anything above $250,000 requires board approval; and every decision has to be defensible in one page to a board whose members do not share the chair's philosophical commitments.

Please give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot do.

It also has to handle the points where the position is ambiguous, and say which way it resolves them and what that costs. Does a large number of sub-threshold sufferings aggregate into something that crosses the threshold, or does the threshold apply only to individual episodes? Is the threshold a property of a single experience, of a life, or of a population? And if enjoyment and suffering turn out not to be commensurable below the threshold at all, the procedure needs a fallback that does not quietly reintroduce a common currency.

Finally, run the procedure on three proposals: a cheap intervention that measurably reduces mild chronic pain across several hundred thousand people; an expensive surgical and palliative program for a rare, agonizing condition affecting a few hundred people a year at a cost per patient hundreds of times higher; and an intervention aimed at the welfare of a farmed invertebrate species whose sentience is genuinely disputed. If the procedure forces an answer I will not like — and I suspect it will on the second case — say so plainly instead of softening it.
