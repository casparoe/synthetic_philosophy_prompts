I'm a philosophy postdoc and I somehow got invted to give a talk at our university's sustainable pest management seminar series -- the room is mostly applied entomologists who do integrated pest management work with growers, plus an ecotoxicologist, a guy from the state organic growers' association, a beekeeper who's extremely online, and one animal-welfare philosopher who is probably the only person there who'll be sympathetic to my framing. My thesis is that the insecticide debate is being run on the wrong moral ledger: both sides fight about human health, pollinators, ecosystem services, residues, and whether something is "chemical" or "natural," and basically nobody counts the pests themselves, even though an aphid or caterpillar outbreak on one acre is plausibly a much bigger pile of suffering than anything anyone is worried about in the residue data. So I'm proposing what I call a per-hectare sentient cost -- for each control option, the sentient-animal suffering caused directly and indirectly, divided by yield saved -- and arguing that the organic/synthetic line doesn't track that metric at all, and that some narrow-spectrum synthetic options may come out ahead of some organic-approved ones once you run the ledger honestly. I'm not claiming insects are conscious the way mammals are; I'm claiming we can't rule it out, and the asymmetry of the stakes means they belong on the ledger. I'm also openly biting the bullet that my metric is totalist, which I know is going to be a fight with the one philosopher in the room. (This all started as an argument with my roommate, who works on bee health, and I have never taken a biology class past high school, so you can guess where my flank is.)

I've got thirty minutes and then maybe twenty minutes of questions. Prep me for the Q&A, not the talk: the objections I'll actually recieve from this specific room rather than the ones I'd get at an animal ethics conference, phrased the way these people would phrase them, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does to me. For each one give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and then tell me where that reply is soft or where I should just concede. Flag which objection has real force as opposed to just rhetorical force, tell me what I should give away in the first two minutes of the talk to defuse the worst of them, and give me the one question I'm praying nobody asks. Keep it in American English, please.
