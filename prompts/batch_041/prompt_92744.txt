Two of my colleagues, Ana and Bruno, spent most of a seminar dinner arguing about a case, and I have not been able to decide between them. I was a bystander throughout and report their exchange as neutrally as I can.

The case. Elena spent three days at a conference in Vienna with her friend Ravi: she sat beside him through the sessions, dined with him each evening, and saw him off at the airport on Friday. She knows, in the fullest sense, that Ravi was in Vienna all week. On the following Monday she receives what would ordinarily be strong evidence to the contrary — a timestamped photograph of a man resembling Ravi in a Lisbon café, a card record showing a purchase in Lisbon on the Wednesday, and two acquaintances who say independently that they spoke with Ravi in Lisbon on the Thursday. Elena declines to look into any of it. The photograph must be of someone else or doctored, the card record an error or a fraud, the acquaintances mistaken or lying; she knows Ravi was in Vienna, so she knows the whole file is misleading, and she treats the matter as settled.

Ana's position.

(1) Elena is entitled to reason as she does. Because she knows Ravi was in Vienna, and knowledge cannot be of what is false, she knows that nothing in that file could be genuine evidence that he was elsewhere; whatever it contains, it cannot establish what is not so. She need not know how each item is misleading in order to know that it is.

(2) Bruno says this makes knowledge a charter for closed-mindedness, and that some step must be illegitimate. But the only step the argument needs is the closure of knowledge under known entailment — if one knows that P, and knows that P entails Q, one knows that Q — and closure is among the least dispensable principles in epistemology. Rejecting it because one dislikes the conclusion is ad hoc.

Bruno's position.

(3) The step that fails is the claim that Elena knows this evidence to be misleading. She may know that Ravi was in Vienna, and know that if he was, the evidence is misleading; what she cannot have is knowledge of the misleadingness of these particular items that does not already presuppose the belief they threaten. To reach that knowledge she must first exempt the belief from revision, which is the very point at issue. The argument is circular.

(4) Even granting the inference, the entitlement Ana describes cannot be exercised, because it is not available from the inside. Someone who merely believes, with unshakable confidence, that Ravi was in Vienna is in precisely Elena's position so far as her own mind goes: equally certain, equally unable to find the counter-evidence credible, equally unable to point to any mark distinguishing knowledge from unwavering belief. If knowledge licenses dismissal and mere confidence does not, the license is real but undetectable, and the merely confident will dismiss the evidence exactly as Elena does. A permission whose holder cannot tell she has it, and which yields the same conduct whether or not it is in force, is no norm of inquiry at all.

Ana's reply.

(5) That confuses a permission with a way of detecting that one has it. Knowledge may be factive and its possession external; what one is entitled to do can turn on facts one cannot inspect. A lucky guesser and a knower are indistinguishable from the inside, yet only one knows. Were Bruno's objection sound it would show that knowledge itself is idle, which is absurd. And the permission is not idle: it tells Elena what she may do — decline to treat the reports as reasons — whether or not she can certify that she may.

(6) Bruno's rejoinder: the question is what Elena is to do given that she cannot certify it. If she merely believes, her dismissal is a vice; if she knows, it is not; and she cannot tell which situation she is in. So either the permission must be supplemented with a rule she can apply — in which case it collapses into ordinary weighing of evidence — or it commends the same conduct to the dogmatist and the knower alike.

I would like an adjudication, move by move: for each of (1) through (6), whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then say, on the whole, whether Elena may do as she does, and identify the premise or distinction that both Ana and Bruno are missing.
