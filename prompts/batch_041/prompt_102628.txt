I teach a 90-minute session in a graduate seminar in metaphysics and philosophy of science, with twelve students who are comfortable with Bayesian reasoning and thought experiments but who have not read anything on anthropic reasoning. I want to run an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the doomsday argument, rather than hear it described, and I do not want them to hear the name doomsday argument until the debrief. The target is the moment when they see that their own position in the sequence of observers is evidence about how many observers there will be, and that this evidence comes from a selection effect rather than from any prediction about the future.

The activity should have two stages that pull in opposite directions. In the first, participants should be randomly assigned a rank within an unknown total and then have to bet on whether the total is small or large; the scoring should reward the correct inference so that the selection effect has teeth. In the second, I want them to encounter the self-indication response from the inside: the setup should make it vivid that hypotheses with more observers also make their own existence more likely, and that this can offset or cancel the first-stage shift. I also want the reference class problem to be felt rather than described. One way to do this would be to give different teams different counting rules for who belongs to the reference class, or to make the total number of observers depend on choices the participants themselves make in an earlier round, so that groups with apparently the same evidence reach different conclusions. The two stages together should leave them genuinely unsure whether the doomsday reasoning is compelling or self-undermining.

Please give me the full runnable design: setting and materials, rules for both stages, a payoff or scoring scheme that does not need real money, any roles, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the components of the doomsday argument and the self-indication response. The debrief should explain where each stage could be resisted and what it costs to resist it there. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Finally, be frank about which feature of the doomsday argument the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief. The activity should not require prior reading and should not depend on citations or the names of published works.
