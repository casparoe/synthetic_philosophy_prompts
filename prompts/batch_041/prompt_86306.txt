Ok so I've somehow ended up as head judge for our district science fair, and I need help before February. It's about 220 projects over two days across life science, physical science, engineering/applied, computer science, and math; my twenty volunteer judges are parents and local engineers, none of whom have read any philosophy of science, and every year the panel nearly comes to blows over what even counts as a science project. One guy, a retired chemist, says flat out that if there's no falsifiable prediction it's not science and shouldn't be eligible for anything. A woman from the ed school says Popper's criterion has been dead since Laudan wrote its obituary back in the early eighties — that it either throws out good science or waves through any crank claim that happens to be false, and that we're all wasting our time. Meanwhile the district's own entry rules require every project to state a hypothesis, which is a disaster for the kid who spent three months optimizing a solar water heater or a trash-sorting classifier. Winners go to regionals and the seniors put this on college apps, so the stakes are real but nobody's getting paid for it, and I have maybe ten minutes per project to write a rationale.

What I want from you is a rubric I can hand to twenty volunteers with no philosophy background: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand the call back to a human because it's really a values question wearing a rule's clothing. It has to keep two things apart that I suspect everyone on my panel is going to mash together — whether a project is a test of a falsifiable claim at all, versus whether it was a decent test of anything — so that "falsifiable" never gets to stand in for "good." Somewhere in there it needs to handle claims that are statistical or vague enough that no single result refutes them ("this supplement helps memory a little"), the problem that any test leans on a pile of background assumptions so a failed prediction doesn't tell you which piece was wrong, and the move where a student explains away a null result with a new excuse invented after the fact (the effect only shows up in people who already believe it, the crystals needed more time, etc.). I'd also like it to reward what some people call a severe test — one that had a genuine chance of coming out the other way — and be honest about the places where the rubric is just me dressing up a judgment call, plus what the whole procedure flatly can't do.

Then run it on three entries I'm already dreading. First, two eighth graders tested whether "positive intentions" aimed at seeds change germination; 20 pots each, blinded by nobody, and when the numbers came out flat they told me the effect only appears when the person doing the intending really believes it, so there is no result that could ever count against them. Second, a pair of juniors built and iteratively improved a solar water heater, no hypothesis about the world at all, they just want the best design, and the entry form forced them to write something in the hypothesis box anyway. Third, a sophomore did a genuinely clean test of whether sun signs predict personality scores in a sample of 60 classmates, found nothing, and wrote it up honestly. That third one is the case where I want you to tell me the rubric says something I don't like, and explain why I'm wrong to flinch — the panel is going to want it thrown out as pseudoscience, and I have a feeling the philosophy here cuts the other way.
