Not a philosopher, so forgive me if this is a dumb question with an obvious answer.

I write compliance-ish copy for a living, and I keep running into one move that I don't have a word for.

Instance one: a supplier's blurb says their ingredients are approved for use in food. True — every single ingredient is approved, individually. But a reader walks away thinking the *product* is approved, and it isn't; there's no product-level approval to speak of. Nobody wrote a false sentence. Ask them and they'll say, correctly, that they never claimed the product was approved.

Instance two: a colleague who doesn't want to come to my thing tells me he already has something that night. Which is true in the sense that he could always find something. The sentence does the job of "I'm not coming" without him having said it, and if I press him on whether he was blowing me off, he has a true sentence to stand on.

Instance three, the one I actually care about: a press release the whole team signed off on. Every sentence true. Every individual could honestly say they weren't trying to put one over on anyone. And the document as a whole still leaves the audiance with a picture that's wrong. Nobody did it, exactly.

Is there a name for this? I don't think "lying" is it — lying seems to need the false sentence. And "misleading" is too big; it covers accidents, and it covers just keeping your mouth shut, and this feels like neither. What I'm circling is the delibrate part: picking the true words *because* they'll make the other person believe the false thing, and keeping the true sentence around as a place to hide.

So: does it have a name, and where are the edges? Does it require the speaker to have *intended* the wrong belief, or is it enough that they saw it coming and let it happen? What do you call the third case, where the effect is real but spread across a group so that no single person has the intention? And how does it relate to vagueness or to changing the subject, which feel like different tools that sometimes do the same job?

One more and then I'll stop. In instance two I'm fairly sure we both know what's happening. Part of why neither of us says it out loud is that we'd both rather not. Is there a term for that setup — where the unspoken thing is common knowledge, and that's exactly why it stays unspoken?

Please don't just hand me a label, if there is one. I want to know what the thing is and what it isn't.
