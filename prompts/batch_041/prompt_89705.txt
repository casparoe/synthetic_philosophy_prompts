I sit on the stratigraphy committee at my country's geological survey — we're the ones who maintain the national time scale, which sounds ceremonial until you notice the mapping division, the universities, and the environment ministry all print it on things. In three weeks we vote on whether to add an entry for the Anthropocene to the national chart, and I have to circulate a written opinion before then. The situation: the international body has just voted the formal epoch down after fifteen years of work, so on the global chart we are still, officially, in the Holocene. But the ministry is already writing 'Anthropocene' into guidance documents, both big universities here teach it as a period, and a couple of my collegues figure that if we don't put it on the chart, someone downstream will put it there badly. The other half says a national committee has no business inventing a unit that the international commission has explicitly declined to define, and that the fastest way to turn a time scale into a political football is to start voting on it.

What I keep tripping over, and what I want the memo to actually engage rather than gesture at: when somebody says the Anthropocene started in 1610, or 1784, or 1945, or 1950, are they discovering a boundary or stipulating one? The formal route the working group went down was a golden spike — a physical marker you can point at in a section — and they settled on a plutonium signal from the bomb tests in a single small lake in Ontario, which is a spike in one lake's mud rather than a point in a continuous sequence, and which is presumably legible in lake sediment and anybodies guess in a deep-sea core or a soil profile. Meanwhile the earlier candidates people like (industrialization, the spread of farming) are diachronous, which is precisely what a boundary marker isn't supposed to be. There's also a faction saying the golden-spike framing is a category error and the Anthropocene is an event like the Great Oxidation Event, not a unit with a base — and you don't vote on when an event starts, you argue about it. And there's the older fallback convention for units where no marker works: you just fix a date by committee. That option exists here, and it makes the discovery-versus-stipulation question uncomfortably literal.

So: draft the memo. The bottom line has to be add it, don't add it, or add it in some qualified form — and if it's qualified, I need to know exactly what a map legend or a permit form would actually print, and what we say when the ministry asks why our chart disagrees with theirs. Give me a timeline (vote in three weeks; the next revision of the national scale goes to press in about 18 months). State the losing side's best case as well as they'd state it themselves — I'd rather my colleagues read it from me than hear it for the first time in the room. No references needed, I've read the papers, I just want the reasoning. And tell me how confident you actually are in what you're claiming, and which single point you're least sure about, because I'll be defending this in front of two people who know the stratigraphic code better than I do.
