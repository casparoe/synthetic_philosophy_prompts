Write a short dialogue between two philosophers arguing over whether pleasure and pain are commensurable. The first is a lexical-threshold negative utilitarian: below some intensity of suffering, pains and pleasures may be traded off against one another, but above that intensity suffering takes absolute priority, so no quantity of pleasure, however vast, can compensate for a single sufficiently intense pain. The second holds that all hedonic states lie on one cardinal scale, so any talk of a "threshold" is either an arbitrary cutoff or a disguised weighting.

Ground the exchange in a concrete choice: a policy that would secure lives of mild contentment for an astronomically large number of beings, at the price of one being's intense agony. The commensurabilist argues by straightforward multiplication; the lexicalist refuses to multiply. Then let each press the other at the weakest point. The commensurabilist should demand what fixes the threshold without already presupposing a shared unit of intensity, and whether the threshold view has its own fanaticism on the other flank — whether, below the threshold, it can really let a great heap of trivial pleasures outweigh a moderate pain. The lexicalist should ask whether the single scale can explain why a mild pain is not swamped by any finite heap of trivial pleasures, or whether positing one scale simply legislates that answer. Each should concede at least one point that genuinely troubles their own position, and the two should answer each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another. Neither is a strawman. A page or so.
