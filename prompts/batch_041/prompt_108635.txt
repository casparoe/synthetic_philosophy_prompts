I'm a novelist, not an academic, so be gentle with me. I'm applying for a one-year fellowship and need a short research proposal (two pages) for a nonfiction essay project that's grown out of the novel I'm writing. I'd like you to take the role of a political philosopher who knows the democratic-theory literature cold, and help me turn my half-formed idea into something real. Think it through carefully before you write.

The novel is about a small coastal town that holds a referendum on whether to let a neighboring community vote in town elections — these are people who work in the town, pay its taxes, send kids to its schools, but live just over the line. So the whole book is basically the boundry problem, who belongs to the demos, except none of the characters calls it that. What bugs me is this: every character has a principled-sounding answer, and every answer turns out to depend on a prior decision about who gets to decide, which is the very thing in dispute. I suspect the usual fixes — the demos is everyone affected by a decision, or everyone subject to it — don't escape that circle, and that the way out isn't a better criterion for drawing the line but something about the line being permanently revisable and contestable rather than correctly drawn once and for all. I might be wrong about that.

Can you help? Start by brainstorming three or four ways to sharpen my question so it has a determinate answer, tell me which one is most tractable and why, then write the proposal itself: the precise question, why the existing positions (all-affected, all-subjected, and whatever else is in play) leave it open, the method — what I'd actually do month by month, given that I'm a writer and not running surveys — what a successful result would look like versus what would count as the project failing, and a scope that honestly fits one year. Don't bother with citations or a lit review, I'll fill those in myself. And please flag it if my idea is actually a settled question that I just haven't read enough to know about.
