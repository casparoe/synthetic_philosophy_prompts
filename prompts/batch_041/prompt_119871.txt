I work in ops at a mid-size company that runs a few big automated decision systems — a claims triage model, a content-moderation classifier, an ad delivery optimiser. I've now sat through maybe five incident reviews, and there's a shape to them that I can't name, and honestly its starting to bother me more than the incidents do.

Here's what happens. Something goes badly wrong at scale — the moderation model starts nuking a whole class of accounts it had previously left alone, or the pricing system quietly starts charging one subgroup more. We do the review. And the review always lands in the same place: the engineer who shipped the change didn't write the rule that did the damage, the rule came out of two subsystems interacting in a way nobody predicted; the ops person who signed off was looking at a dashboard that was, by the standards of that week, complete; the model was behaving inside its spec; the agent on the other end followed the policy they were given. Everyone did something, nobody did the thing. So the finding is 'systemic' and we add a line to the checklist. But here's the part I can't get past. That finding — the sentence 'no individual is responsible here' — is itself a decision, and there's nobody whose job it is to make it. The verdict that responsibility has nowhere to land is itself unlanded. It's not that the buck stops nowhere; it's that nobody is even holding the buck. And the same holds in the other direction, which I think is the actually expensive half: when the system does something genuinely brilliant, there's also nobody who can take credit for it, so nobody has standing to defend it, so the thing that made it work has no constituency and gets slowly optimised away by whatever happens to be locally measurable.

I've read enough to know about the 'problem of many hands' and about the 'responsibility gap' for autonomous systems, and neither is quite it — the first is about blame being spread thin across people who each contributed a bit, the second is about a machine being unable to bear what a person would have borne, and neither is about the missing owner of the verdict, and neither says anything about the praise half. So: is there an established concept for exactly this, and if there isn't, coin one and define it properly? I want the carving, not the label — what counts as an instance and what doesn't (does an ordinary committee decision count? does a market? does a genuinely unforeseeable accident with no automation in it at all?), where the line is between this and ordinary diffusion of responsibility, and whether the praise symmetry I'm describing is part of the same phenomenon or something separate I've bundled in by mistake. If the closest established thing fits only partly, tell me exactly where it stops fitting.
