I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade — the Sequences, the long decision-theory threads, the recurring arguments about whether Bayesianism is a complete normative theory of belief or only a fragment of one — and I've decided to stop lurking and spend roughly two years doing serious independent work on one corner of formal epistemology: the theory of uncertain evidence, meaning the cluster of questions surrounding Jeffrey conditionalization and its rivals. I have a decent grounding in probability and a lot of patience, but no lab, no dataset, and no interest in producing yet another survey that re-derives the rule P'(A) = Σᵢ P'(Eᵢ)·P(A | Eᵢ) and declares victory. What I want from you is an agenda: since I can realistically pursue only a handful of these questions, which are most worth the years, and on what grounds?

To make this concrete, here are the candidates I keep circling, stated roughly as I understand them: (a) the fact that Jeffrey conditioning is not commutative — receiving uncertain evidence in different orders can leave you with different posteriors — and what that implies about whether the rule is even well-defined over a stream of observations; (b) the rigidity assumption, that conditional probabilities within the partition stay fixed through the update, and whether it is a substantive commitment or a harmless bookkeeping convention; (c) whether genuinely uncertain evidence exists at all, or whether every such case can be reconstructed as certain evidence about one's own experiences, and what that reconstruction costs; (d) how uncertain evidence connects to the problem of old evidence and to the reflection principle; (e) whether probability kinematics is the right framework in the first place, or whether constraint-based updating — maximum entropy, superconditioning-style treatments, or keeping a set of distributions rather than a single one — does the job better, and what would adjudicate between them; and (f) the suspicion that the whole problem is an artifact of representing evidence as propositions, so that a proper theory would traffic in constraints on credences instead. My real motivation is downstream: I care about how an ideal reasoner, or a machine, should absorb a noisy stream of observations that never arrives as a clean proposition, and I suspect the standard formalisms quietly presuppose the very thing they are meant to explain.

Please don't hand me a reading list or a neutral survey; I want a defended agenda. Which of these — or something I'm not seeing — is upstream of the others, which are tractable for one person with two years, which are neglected, and which only look important and would not actually change much downstream? Commit to an ordering and tell me what would move an item up or down. Structure the response as follows: a Summary of a few sentences stating the ranking; then The Candidates, a short paragraph on each open question in your own terms, including any I've missed that deserves a place; then The Ranking, the bulk of the response, one subsection per item in order, each defending its position by appeal to importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other questions depend on it; then What Would Change the Ranking, naming the specific results, arguments, or counterexamples that would reorder things; and finally What to Skip, listing the lines of work here that you take to be overrated or a dead end, with reasons.
