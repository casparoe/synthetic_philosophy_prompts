I'm a science-and-ideas journalist -- fortnightly column, a newsletter, mostly biology and philosophy with an economics habit -- and I've fallen into a hole I need someone to pull me out of. It started for a completly unrelated reason: I was reading up on Berkeley (the idealist, the to-be-is-to-be-perceived guy, God as the perceiver of last resort) and discovered that in the 1730s, living in Ireland, he published this strange little book of questions about Irish poverty, and that his answer was essentially monetary. Money, on his account, isn't a thing with value in it; it's a ticket or a counter, a claim on goods and labour. The real wealth of a country is the labour it can command. What makes the whole thing go is the circulation of credit, not the quantity of coin sitting in a vault. And he wanted a national bank, and wanted the poor put to work rather than left idle in the dirt. I'd somehow never read any of it and I'm now half convinced it's the most interesting thing he ever did, though I can't tell how much of it is economics and how much is just a humane, impatient man trying to feed people.

Which brings me to my problem. Over Christmas I watched It's a Wonderful Life with my kids for the tenth time, and the run on the Building and Loan stopped me cold -- George behind the counter with the crowd wanting their cash, telling them their money isn't in the safe, it's in their neighbours' houses, and then him and Mary handing over the honeymoon money to keep the doors open -- and I thought, that's the ticket theory of money, that's Berkeley, better than any textbook. My editor wants me to just write that piece: Capra as Berkeleyan. I'm not convinced, and I'd rather you settle it than me. Read the film through Berkeley's monetary thinking and commit to a verdict. Where does it genuinely dramatize his view -- the run itself, the credit and the trust, Bailey Park set against Potter's slums, the suggestion that a community's real wealth is the labour it can put to use, the way the film treats a bank as a moral institution and not just a clearing house? And where does it push back, or get him wrong? Does the ending, where the whole town bails George out with a basket of cash, quietly restore the very thing the run scene denied, that money is a pile of stuff that has to physically turn up? Is Potter a commodity man, and does the film need him to be a villain because it can't picture a credit system without one? And is there something in those Irish queries about idleness, employment and the poor that the film sentimentalises away, or that the film sees better than he did? Don't quote Berkeley or the screenplay at me -- I've got both to hand, and what I want is the reading, not the citations. Take a definite side even if it's the one I'll end up disagreeing with. Just write it as an email back to me, continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, nothing that looks like a report.
