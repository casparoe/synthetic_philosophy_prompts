So I'm recently retired -- forty-odd years in a biology department, mostly nerve and muscle work -- and I've been trying to teach myself philosophy in my spare time, which is going about as well as you'd expect. Anyway, I picked up one of those popular brain books at the airport (I won't name it, I don't want to be unfair to the author) and there's a chapter on pain I can't stop chewing on. I'd like you to tell me how much of it I should beleive.

Here's the gist of the chapter. There's a real condition called pain asymbolia. People who have it -- it usually comes on after a stroke, sometimes after certain kinds of brain surgery -- will tell you, honestly, that they feel the pain when you prick them. They can point to exactly where it is. If you ask them to rate the intensity on a scale of one to ten, they give you an ordinary-looking number, same as you or me. What they don't do is seem to mind. They don't pull the arm back. Some of them smile or laugh when it happens. The author says the damage is usually in a spot called the insula, and that what's happened is basically a cut wire between the part of the brain that registers the pain and the part that attaches the misery to it. So the signal gets through, but the suffering never gets stapled on. He calls these the sensory part and the affective part, and he says asymbolia is the clean case where you can see them come apart.

Then he takes the next step, and this is the part I want checked. He says this shows pain isn't bad in itself. The hurting, he says, is just an emotional reaction we've learned to hang on the signal -- the signal itself is neutral, like a smoke alarm going off. He says it's the clearest proof that pain is all in the brain, and that it shows we could in principle abolish the suffering without touching the sensation, which is what the ideal painkiller would do. He also lumps asymbolia in with people born unable to feel pain, and with what morphine does, as though those were all the same story of pain-minus-the-bad-part. And he says asymbolics are the lucky ones -- they get the warning light without the misery, whereas the folks born without pain hurt themselves and die young.

Here's my problem. My gut agrees with him. I spent my whole career around physiology, and I've always half-believed that the suffering is something the brain adds on top of the signal, so when I read this I thought, yes, obviously, and this proves it. But I've also figured out by now that the obvious reading is usually the one the philosophers have already taken apart, and I dont want to be the retired scientist who read one airport book and thinks he's got it settled.

So: go through the chapter claim by claim. What's solid -- what would a neurologist or a pain researcher actually sign off on? What is the author overselling, or quietly assuming, or getting plain wrong? Where would somebody who works on the philosophy of pain push back -- on the leap from "these patients don't seem to mind" to "pain isn't bad," or on the "learned reaction" bit, or on the insula-as-a-cut-wire story, or on treating asymbolia and born-without-pain and morphine as one phenomenon? And does any version of his conclusion survive once you've fixed the parts that need fixing, or is the whole thing hanging on a misreading? Be blunt, I can take it.
