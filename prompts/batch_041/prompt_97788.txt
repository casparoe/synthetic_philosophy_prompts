Write the joint statement that a counterfactual theorist of causation and an interventionist could both put their names to at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The first camp treats causation as counterfactual dependence (or the ancestral of it) and holds that the hard cases are problems about the right similarity ordering among worlds; the second treats causation as what is invariant under an ideal intervention within a model of the system's variables and holds that the hard cases are problems about choosing the right variable set and specifying the intervention. Assume both parties are competent, neither is a strawman, and neither is permitted to win by definitional fiat. Do not supply citations, reading lists, or a bibliography; argue from the positions and from the cases given below.

Structure the document as follows.

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. Include at least: what each side takes causation to be a relation between (events, variables, or something else); whether the interventionist's counterfactuals are a special case of the counterfactual theorist's or vice versa; what both accept about the difference between singular (token) and general (type) causal claims; and what both concede are genuine, unresolved problems for their own side. Each claim must be substantive — no formulations so weak that no one would deny them.

2. A disagreements section, in which each surviving disagreement is stated twice: once in wording the counterfactual theorist would accept as a fair statement of the interventionist's view, and once in wording the interventionist would accept as a fair statement of the counterfactual theorist's view. For each disagreement, say plainly whether you judge it to be a genuine disagreement or merely verbal, and if verbal, what the two sides are actually doing with the word "cause."

3. A cruxes section. For each crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — record what both parties agree would count as bearing on it, and each side's prediction about how it comes out. Where a crux cannot be settled by any case either side would accept, say so and explain why.

Address at least these contested matters along the way: preemption and its variants; trumping by a minor miracle; double prevention; omissions and causation by absence; whether the interventionist framework genuinely earns its keep on the cases that embarrass the counterfactual analysis, or merely relocates the difficulty into the choice of variables and the specification of the intervention; whether the notion of an intervention smuggles in causal notions it was meant to analyse; and whether transitivity should be a constraint on the correct analysis at all.

Then bring both parties to a concrete case, given here, and have each side commit in advance to its verdicts. Call it the Relay. A machine can be driven by either of two motors, M1 and M2, wired in parallel so that either alone is sufficient to drive it; a switch transfers the load from M1 to M2 the instant M1 stops. Variant (a): a fault disables M1, the switch transfers the load, and the machine runs. Variant (b): the same fault disables M1, but an independent fault has also disabled the switch, so no transfer occurs and the machine stops. Variant (c): an operator deliberately disabled the switch an hour earlier, M1 then fails, and the machine stops. For each variant, state whether the fault in M1 is a cause of the machine's running or stopping, whether the operator's act in (c) is a cause, and whether the difference between the fault-disabled and act-disabled switch in (b) and (c) is a real causal difference or only a difference in our interest in blaming someone. Say which single variant is the sharpest discriminator between the two camps, and why.

If, at the end, you think a shared formal apparatus would dissolve part of the dispute, say which part, what residue would remain after the translation, and why that residue is or is not a substantive disagreement.

Aim for roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words. Write the statement in British English — "analyse," "behaviour," "centre," "modelling," and so on.
