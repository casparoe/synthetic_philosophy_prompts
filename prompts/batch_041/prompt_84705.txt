A historian I work with sorts famines into three categories — natural disaster, mixed, and political crime — and has been consistent across four cases. Bengal, 1943: political crime, because for him the deaths ran through wartime inflation, the collapse of rural purchasing power, the diversion of supply to Calcutta and to the military, and a provincial administration that never declared a famine, rather than through any large shortfall in the province's grain; the cyclone and the rice-crop disease are background. Ireland, 1845–49: mixed, because the blight was the trigger and no government could have saved the potato, though he concedes that relief was withdrawn too early, that food continued to leave the country throughout, and that the burden was shifted onto the poor law and its ratepayers. The Dutch Hunger Winter, 1944–45: political crime, because the German embargo on food transport to the western provinces, imposed in retaliation for the railway strike, was deliberate and was the operative cause. China, 1959–61: political crime, because procurement quotas were set from falsified harvest reports, grain continued to be exported, and no free press existed to force a correction.

The case I now have to file is a composite, so please take my description as the whole of the evidence. A semi-arid pastoralist region suffers two consecutive years of severe drought. National grain reserves remain close to normal and the harvest elsewhere is adequate. The deaths, however, come overwhelmingly from disease among people weakened by hunger, and the mechanism is a collapse in the terms of trade between livestock and cereals: as animals weaken and are dumped on the market, their price falls by three-quarters against grain, so herders who own hundreds of head of cattle can no longer buy a subsistence quantity of cereal. The government responds to urban pressure by banning cereal exports, then by capping the grain price, which drives grain into black markets, and then by banning livestock exports, which destroys the one asset the herders still hold. In one province, an insurgency has led the army to restrict aid convoys and to relocate rural populations into camps. The country is an electoral democracy with a vigorous press and courts that do in fact order the executive around; the deaths are reported, debated and litigated. The courts order the release of reserve stocks, and the government complies, four months late. The affected pastoralists are a marginalised minority of little electoral weight. Deaths are in the tens of thousands.

Tell me: (1) the verdict his record predicts, and the reasoning behind it; (2) the rules that fit all four of his earlier verdicts — I can see at least a rule treating culpable policy as a necessary condition of the deaths, a rule turning on whether an accountability mechanism existed that could force a correction, and a rule turning on whether the exogenous shock would have killed comparably many on its own; (3) whether the record rules any of these out, and specifically whether his mixed verdict on Ireland can survive a rule that calls a famine political whenever the deaths run through a collapse in exchange entitlements rather than through a fall in aggregate supply, or whether he must be reading Ireland as a case in which the blight rather than the market was the binding constraint; (4) what each surviving rule predicts about the new case, including whether the accountability rule applies to the country as a whole or only to the affected group; (5) your own best guess at his verdict, with your reasons for preferring it, and a plain statement of what he would be committing himself to if he went the other way; and (6) the single further case that would best separate the surviving rules, with the verdicts on it that would tell them apart.

Argue from the cases as I have set them out; there is no need to cite any literature. Please write your answer in British English.
