So I've been staring at something in my environmental policy seminar and it's bugging me. Regulators do cost-benefit analysis on new rules — they tally up the benefits and harms, then discount the future ones back to present value at some positive rate, so a life saved in fifty years counts for a fraction of one saved today. Fine. But the same people doing this will cheerfully admit we have almost no real idea what the long-run consequences of any policy are, that the causal chains run off into the far future and swamp everything we can actually measure. That smells like a tension to me, or at least a hidden commitment. What I want from you is this: tell me what the practice is actually presupposing — what view of value and of our knowledge of consequences it needs in order to make sense, which rival views it quietly rules out, and where it's just underdetermined so that several theories would license the same spreadsheet. And say what would have to change if you dropped the presupposition. Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion.
