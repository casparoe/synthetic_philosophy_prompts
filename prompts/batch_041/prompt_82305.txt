You're a philosopher who works on animal minds, and also someone who can write a speech people will actually sit through — I need both halves, so don't just give me an essay with a toast bolted on. Before you write, take a minute and brainstorm: what's actually at stake here, what are the tensions, what can and can't be said in this room, what's the strongest version of the objections I'm about to hand you. Then write the speech itself, the real thing, roughly six or seven minutes read aloud, no bullet points in the deliverable.

The situation. I run a shrimp farm — 180 acres of ponds, family place, my dad started it in the seventies. In two weeks I hand over the gavel at our growers' association's annual dinner. It's a hall with bad lighting and a cash bar: maybe 120 people, mostly farmers, a few processors, a couple folks from extension, my wife, my brother, and a table of men I've worked next to for thirty years who will be polite to my face and merciless in the parking lot.

Five years ago I stopped killing our shrimp the way everybody does. I had a fella wire up a rig — they go into a current first and they're out cold before they hit the ice. It's slower, it costs me money every single week, and I can't prove it makes one bit of difference to the shrimp. What got me going was reading around on whether crustaceans might actually experience something, and I want you to be straight about the state of that question rather than fudging it in either direction: nobody can tell me yes and nobody can tell me no, and the people who act like it's settled are settled in both directions.

So the speech isn't "be nice to shrimp." What I actually want to say out loud, to a room that's mostly going to hate it, is something like: I don't have to believe a shrimp feels pain in order to stop betting that it doesn't. And I want to say it without calling anybody in that room a bad man, because they aren't, and because I was one of them for thirty years, and because my own crew thinks I've gone soft.

Three things you have to deal with, because they're what I can't answer:

1) A guy at the co-op told me my whole argument proves too much. If "you can't prove it doesn't feel anything" is enough, it's enough for the bugs in the field, the yeast in the tank, the laptop on my desk. If I can't draw a line somewhere, he says, my precaution is just a feeling I'm dressing up as a principle.

2) My brother says it's theater. We kill them either way, thousands a day, and one farm doing it gentler changes nothing except my conscience — and he says the conscience might be the real reason I do it.

3) My father will be in the room. He built that place with his hands. I'm not going to stand up there and imply his whole working life was a moral error, and I'm not going to lie about him either.

Take a position. Don't hedge into both-sides mush, and let whatever you decide actually control the speech — if you think my brother's right, that's a different speech than if you think he's wrong, and I'll be able to tell which one you wrote. It has to end with me toasting the incoming president, and I need to still have friends in that hall when the lights come up. One more thing: don't use the word sentient more than once, it's a word that makes farmers stop listening.
