I'm a novelist about a third of the way into a book that's essentially a standoff — two people who could each end the other, neither of whom wants to, and the whole thing hangs on whether either can make the other believe they'd really do it. I keep bumping into things I suspect have names: that a threat is more convincing if you've handed part of the decision over to chance instead of keeping it all in your own hands; that seeming slightly unhinged can itself be a strategy; that total stability at the top somehow makes small provocations more tempting rather than less. Could you restate the main paradoxes of deterrence and brinkmanship in the vocabulary I actually think in — scene, suspense, dramatic irony, the character who mustnt know their own mind, the gap between what the audience believes and what the character believes — as faithfully as you can, without tidying them up into something more sensible than they are? Then the residue report: what translates cleanly, what I've had to fudge, and what has no counterpart at all in how a novelist builds a person. And tell me whether the leftover is my craft being too thin, or the strategists' picture of a person being too thin.
