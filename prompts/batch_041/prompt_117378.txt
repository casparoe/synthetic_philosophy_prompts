I spent an embarrassingly long evening arguing with a friend about mechanistic anomaly detection, and I would like an outside judgement more careful than either of ours. The idea, as we both understand it, is this: you have a model whose behaviour you have come to trust across some distribution, and you want to flag those inputs on which it arrives at its answer through a mechanism you did not expect to be operating — an anomaly — even where the answer itself is unobjectionable. That much we agreed on. The argument was about what makes two episodes of computation the same mechanism. My friend's criterion is invariance: two runs count as the same mechanism just in case the same set of interventions leaves the input–output relation intact. Mine is computational role: two runs count as the same mechanism just in case they occupy the same position in the algorithm that generates the behaviour, whether or not they survive the same interventions. A third view, which neither of us defended but which kept resurfacing, is that mechanisms are individuated by the grain of the explanation one is giving, so that sameness of mechanism is always relative to a questioner's interests and there is no further fact to appeal to. We manufactured case after case in which the criteria came apart, and each of us could always redescribe the case so that his own criterion was the one doing the work. I came away suspecting that at least some of these cases are underdetermined in principle rather than merely under-evidenced, but I could not construct the case that proves it.

So: give me an example of a conceptual issue, in this vicinity, that shows why the dispute cannot be settled by collecting more data or building better tooling — a single concrete case in which two observers who agree on every behavioural and structural fact nevertheless reach opposite verdicts about whether the same mechanism is running, and in which the disagreement is not merely terminological. Make the case one that a person auditing a real model could plausibly encounter, rather than a philosopher's thought experiment. Then say which criterion you would adopt, and what a practitioner should do about the residual underdetermination — declare an anomaly whenever any admissible individuation says so, refuse to declare one unless all of them do, or something else again. I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a case sharp enough to force a choice.
