I am a decision-theory PhD student trying to pin down what I actually believe about the value of information, because the standard result — that for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer a free, private piece of evidence can never lower expected utility, since she can always ignore it — keeps coming apart from my case-by-case reactions. Here is where my intuitions land.

A free, private, reliable forecast of tomorrow's weather, when I am deciding whether to carry an umbrella: taking it is obviously right, and I cannot see how it could hurt.

A free, private test telling me whether I carry a gene for a condition I can do nothing about: I would rather not know, and I do not think that preference is confused, even though there is no decision for the information to improve.

A mechanic offers, free and in confidence, to tell me what my used car is really worth before I advertise it: I take the information; it can only help me price.

A village whose members insure one another against crop failure: a free harvest forecast circulated before contracts are signed is good for each villager to have, yet I think it leaves the village worse off, because those who learn the harvest will fail stop offering cover.

A defendant offered, free and in confidence, the single fact that would establish her own liability, in a jurisdiction where acquiring it triggers a duty to disclose: I think she should decline, and not merely because knowing would distress her.

A case in which I know I am a poor updater — I will overreact to a mildly diagnostic result and act against my own interests: I think the information is bad for me, and the badness is genuinely about the decision, not about how I feel.

My tentative view is that the standard result is true but far narrower than it sounds, and that each negative verdict above is a distinct failure of one of its presuppositions: that the information is free, that acquiring it is unobserved, that the acts available to me do not depend on what I learn, that what I care about does not depend on the state except through my choice, and that I update as a Bayesian. But two of my verdicts I cannot fit. The gene test and the mechanic's offer look alike in every formal respect — free, private, nobody watching — and I still want opposite verdicts on them. And the village forecast is good for every individual yet bad in aggregate, which makes me suspect the standard result is not even the right object for the question I care about.

Please give me the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely: for each clause, the case that forces it and its verdict on a case I have not listed; an account of what the gene-test verdict is a verdict about, if not the value of information; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the best principle cannot keep.
