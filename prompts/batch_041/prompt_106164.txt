Im a retired biologist — thirty years at the bench and then a decade of departmental committees — and since I quit doing science I've been trying to read philosophy, which mostly makes me feel dumb in a good way. The idea I can't shake is Goodhart's law. A friend broke it into four flavors for me and I think he had it right: the regressional kind, where the proxy is only correlated with what you actually care about, so optimizing it also optimizes the gap between the two; the extremal kind, where the correlation holds fine in the normal range but you go and push out into the tails where it doesnt; the causal kind, where the proxy is a symptom of the goal rather than a lever on it, so pulling the lever does nothing or does damage; and the adversarial kind, where people simply start lying to the number on purpose. I watched all four of those happen to the same set of committee-approved metrics in my own field, which is why I want a poem about it rather than another memo.

Here's what I'm after. A pantoum, five quatrains, numbered 1 through 5 — the repeated lines are the metric, and each time a line comes back around it should have been quietly captured by a different one of those four failure modes, so that by the fifth quatrain the same words are saying something the first quatrain would have laughed at. Let the last stanza loop back into the first the way pantoums are supposed to, because thats the whole point: the measure comes around again and it isn't the measure anymore. It should hold up as a poem for someone who's never heard of Goodhart, but anyone who knows the literature should be able to point at which quatrain is which flavor.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered quatrains, no preamble and no notes at the end.
