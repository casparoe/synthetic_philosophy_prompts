My best friend Rosa is about as close to a living saint as I've ever met — gives away most of what she earns, weekends at a hospice, never once in twenty years done the selfish thing when the unselfish one was available. I'm writing a novel and one of my characters is loosly based on her, so I need to be able to predict her, and that means I need to know what rule she's actually running.

Here's how she judges other people who've gone all-in on being good. A lawyer who quit corporate work for death row appeals — still has a partner and two kids she adores, still paints on Sundays, seems genuinely happy — Rosa says that's the best life a person can have, she'd swap into it tomorrow. A guy who lives on beans and gives away most of his income to malaria charities, but has a loud circle of friends, plays in a terrible band, is fun to be around — Rosa says yes, that's it, that's what a life is for. A hospice nurse we knew, no partner, no kids, no hobbies at all, double shifts until her body quit — Rosa says she'd take that life too, it's beautiful. But then there's a woman we both know who volunteers constantly, is quietly awful to everyone around her, and clearly does it so people will call her good — Rosa says that isn't goodness, that's vanity with a charity logo, and it doesn't count.

Now the character. She's given up everything the way Rosa has — no partner, no kids, no close friends left, no music, no books for pleasure, no ambition that isn't moral. She sleeps four hours and works the rest for other people, and by any measure she does a huge amount of good. But she isn't happy. She isn't sad either. She's just empty — there's nothing left in her that isn't the work.

Will Rosa say that's a life worth living — want it, recommend it, call it desirable? And tell me what rule she's actually running that fits everything above, because I suspect more than one does, what each of those rules predicts about my character, which one you'd bet on and why, and what case I should write next that would finally tell the rules apart. I don't need it tidy, I need it right.
