I spent 38 years doing experimental physics and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so go easy on me but please don't dumb it down. I've been chewing on the philosophy of time and I want a consistency check before I start boring my old colleagues with it.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

(a) Special relativity is right, and there's no privileged frame — simultaneity is relative to a frame, full stop.

(b) Which is why I think the Rietdijk–Putnam arguement goes through: if simultaneity is relative then there's no objective 'now', so every event that ever happens is equally real, and the present is indexical, like 'here'. Block universe. I'm quite confident about this one.

(c) All that said, I'm definately not ready to say the passage of time is a pure illusion. I don't want a spooky moving spotlight, but I think there's something real about becoming, even if I can't yet say what.

(d) I think causation is a real, mind-independent, asymmetric relation — effects depend on causes and not the other way round — and not just a habit we project.

(e) I think the fundamental laws, setting aside the small CP/T-violating effects, don't single out a direction of time; the arrow is entirely a thermodynamic thing, coming from the low-entropy initial condition.

(f) I think whether the future is 'open' is an empirical question, and I lean towards thinking quantum indeterminacy makes it genuinely open rather than just unknown to us.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? My gut says the trouble is (b) against (c). But I've heard seperately that the argument for (b) only goes through if you assume that 'being real for' is transitive and is not itself frame-relative — which sounds like exactly the sort of thing a relativist like me shouldn't grant. So maybe I'm in better shape on (c) than I fear, or maybe in worse shape somewhere I haven't looked. I also don't know whether (d) survives giving up absolute simultaneity.

Give it back to me in these four sections, in this order:

1. 'Where the package holds' — one short paragraph, just the bits that are plainly fine together.
2. 'The real tensions' — worst first, one paragraph each, with a severity tag (mild / serious / fatal) and exactly what would have to give in each case.
3. 'Tensions that aren't' — the pairs that look bad at first glance but dissolve, one paragraph each, saying what does the dissolving.
4. 'Cheapest fix and the bill' — the single revision that buys me the most consistency, what it costs me, and whether I could pay it without abandoning (a).

Keep the whole thing under about 1000 words, and don't hedge everything into mush — if something has to go, say so and say why.
