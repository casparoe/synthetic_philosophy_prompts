One of my friends is writing a seminar paper on the honor / dignity / victimhood typology and she's pulled me into it, so now I can't stop chewing on it. Quick version of the three, in case it matters: honor culture is the one where a slight is something you answer yourself, because your standing is yours to maintain and letting an insult pass is itself the injury; dignity culture is the one where you're supposed to absorb the insult, since your worth isn't the kind of thing other people's opinions can really touch, and genuine offenses go to law; victimhood culture is the one where the offense is a harm you're owed recognition for, and the community, or some authority standing in for it, is supposed to do the answering on your behalf. Her twist — and I think she's right — is that these aren't three separate moralities so much as one morality with three different answers to a single question: who is licensed to respond to a wrong. Which is exactly why the argument she builds on top of it bugs me.

(1) A moral culture is a public system of norms that says what counts as a wrong and who is licensed to answer it.
(2) All three cultures agree on what a wrong is — an injury to your standing as an equal — and differ only over who gets to answer it.
(3) License the wronged party to answer (honor) and you get duels, feuds, retaliation chains, dead teenagers over a parking space.
(4) License third parties to answer on his behalf (victimhood) and you get an arms race of grievance, everyone performing their wounds for an audience that keeps score, and a bureaucracy of sensitivity.
(5) License no one (dignity) and you get wrongs that simply go unanswered, with "rise above it" as advice that only works if you can afford to rise above it.
(6) Any system has to assign the license somewhere: to the wronged party, to third parties, or to no one.
(7) So every culture is defective, and asking which of them is morally superior is malformed. She even claims it generalizes — any fourth option you dream up will have its own costs, and so will having no norms about any of this at all, so "superior" comes out empty wherever you point it.

I can't accept (7). If its true then there couldn't be a decent moral culture, and that seems obviously wrong — but each premise looks fine to me on its own and I can't find the exit. So where does it go wrong, and what does giving up that step actually cost me? I'd rather keep (2) and lose whatever else has to go, though I have a feeling that's the expensive option and I want to know the price. And if the argument is definately fine as it stands and I'm just being sentimental about (7), say so and tell me why.
