I chair the building-code advisory committee in a mid-sized city. Our terms of reference confine us to life safety and construction feasibility, and we must send the council a written recommendation before its March meeting on a proposed amendment to the residential code.

Under the rule now in force, apartment buildings above three storeys require two independent means of egress. The practical effect is that nearly every such building is a double-loaded corridor block: a long central hallway with units strung along both sides and most units lit from a single face. The amendment would permit, up to six storeys, what planners call a point access block — every unit on a floor opening onto one enclosed stair, on a small floor plate that lets each unit have windows on two or three sides. Buildings of this kind are ordinary in many European cities and rare here.

Two colleagues have dug in. One, a fire engineer, says this is a technical question with a technical answer: what matters is egress capacity, travel distance and suppression, and the social consequences of the resulting buildings are not our business. The other says the two-stair rule is no neutral standard but an artifact that has quietly settled what kind of city we get — large units, family-only households, car-dependent sites — and that a committee treating it as pure engineering is laundering a value choice as physics. A third proposes a middle path: allow the single stair only where the building also meets a package of compensating measures (full sprinklers, compartmentation, a hard cap on units per floor and on travel distance, and a required share of family-sized units plus a further share deed-restricted below market), with a mandatory review after five years.

Draft the memo I will circulate. Recommend one of the three courses concretely, with reasons, and give the council a timeline it can act on. Engage the strongest case against your recommendation rather than caricaturing it — in particular the argument that the households most likely to occupy the cheapest new units are the ones most exposed if a safety gamble goes wrong. Say plainly whether the committee is entitled to weigh the housing-type effects at all under our terms of reference; whether the political character of a design like this is intrinsic to the design or merely a product of the market and code around it; and how much causal weight the floor plan itself carries — whether a building type can decide who lives somewhere, or only bias a market that would have produced similar exclusions regardless. Argue from the case in front of us rather than from authorities.
