So I've ended up on this little commission that's reviewing how our appellate courts write their opinions, and I keep tripping over the same thing. Here's the setup. A three-judge panel hears an appeal. The judges vote on each issue seperately -- was the contract valid, was it breached, did the limitation period run -- and the majority carries each issue on its own. Then the court files one document, "the opinion of the court," written as if there were a single mind behind it that believes everything in it. But it's trivially easy to build a case where the majority on issue one is a different pair of judges than the majority on issue two. Say two judges think the contract was valid, two think it was breached, but the overlap between those two pairs is one judge. Vote issue by issue and the court holds that the contract was both valid and breached, which only one of the three judges actually thinks -- and yet the loser is bound, because the judgment just falls out of the issue-by-issue tally. There's even a whole clerkly art to figuring out what "the holding" is when nobody agreed on the reasoning; you go hunting for the narrowest thing a majority could be read as having signed onto.

And here's the bit that bugs me. The same system insists the court give reasons the losing party can follow, that the opinion be internally consistent, that it not contradict itself two paragraphs later, that like cases come out alike. Some courts do it the other way round -- every judge writes their own opinion, in their own voice, and nobody pretends there's one court mind at all. So the practice isn't forced on us by anything.

What I want to know is what our version presupposes. Does it presuppose that the court is a single rational agent with beliefs of its own -- one that could be, in some sense, inconsistent with itself? Or does it only presuppose a machine for turning votes into a binding outcome, with the opinion as a kind of press release? Which rival pictures of what a court is does the practice quietly rule out? Is it internally consistent, or does wanting one opinion of the court and majority-per-issue at the same time pull in two directions? And where is it just underdetermined -- what range of views about groups, reasons, and rationality would all license exactly these same rules, so that the practice can't tell them apart? Last thing: if you really took seriously that the court is not an agent, just three people whose votes get tallied, what would actually have to change -- the writing of opinions, the doctrine of precedent, the requirement of reasons -- and what would turn out to be seperable from all that?
