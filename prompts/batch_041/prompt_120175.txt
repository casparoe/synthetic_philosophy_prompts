I've drafted the following as notes toward a short paper in the philosophy of biology, and I'd like detailed comments before I write it up. The topic is the long-standing disagreement between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function.

**Thesis.** The two accounts are usually presented as rival analyses of a single concept. I want to argue that they are better read as answers to two different questions: the etiological account answers why a trait is present rather than absent, and the causal-role account answers what a part contributes to the working of the system it belongs to. The appearance of rivalry comes from the fact that ordinary biological usage fuses the two into one word. I claim that fusion is not a confusion to be cleaned up but the source of the concept's usefulness: the causal-role half supplies explanatory grip in physiology, while the etiological half supplies the normativity — the difference between a heart that fails to pump and a heart that merely makes noise.

**Two cases.** First, fever. On both accounts the elevated set point comes out as functional, and they agree because the same trait was plausibly selected for its contribution to pathogen clearance. Second, nausea and vomiting in early pregnancy, for which an adaptationist story has been proposed (avoidance of foodborne toxins during the window when the embryo is most vulnerable), and which the causal-role account would be inclined to classify as a malfunction, since the immediate contribution to the maternal system is negative. I am aware the adaptationist story is itself contested; tell me if I am leaning on it too hard. The point of the pair is that the accounts diverge exactly where history and current contribution come apart.

**An extension I want to press.** I take seriously a third family that grounds function in the self-maintaining organization of a system rather than in selection history, and also the more recent proposal to generalize selected-effects accounts so that selection at any level counts — clonal selection in the immune response, reinforcement in trial-and-error learning, and the like. The generalization is attractive because it handles traits that are functional without having been selected for over evolutionary time. My worry is that if selection can occur within a single lifetime, the account becomes so permissive that it no longer distinguishes function from purpose and loses the normative edge that made the etiological account attractive in the first place — a rat's lever-press would count as functioning to obtain food.

**The payoff.** Dysfunction talk in medicine and psychiatry, including the harmful-dysfunction analysis of disorder associated with Wakefield, is etiological in disguise. If that is right, disputes over whether a condition is a disorder — a pathology, or a costly but functional response — cannot be settled by any amount of mechanistic detail, because the two sides are asking different questions while using one word. This is the part I most suspect is overreach.

So, three claims: (1) the accounts answer different questions rather than competing over one; (2) their fusion in ordinary usage is what gives function talk both its explanatory grip and its normativity; (3) consequently, medical and psychiatric dysfunction disputes are not resolvable by mechanism alone.

What I want from you:

1. Accuracy first. Is my reading of the causal-role account fair — in particular, am I right that it cannot by itself distinguish a function from an incidental effect? If some version of it can, my thesis needs revising, and I would rather find out now than after drafting.
2. Is the two-questions claim substantive, or is it a verbal truce that dodges the disagreement? What would make it more than that? And if I could keep only two of my three claims, which would you cut?
3. Is the psychiatric payoff a genuine consequence of the analysis or a rhetorical flourish?
4. What is the strongest objection to the thesis, and what specific evidence or argument would move you most against it?
5. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Where you are reconstructing a position rather than reporting what its defenders actually say, mark it as such. I don't need a reading list or citations — I want the argument and its weak points, not the bibliography.

Write in American English.
