I'm a retired physical chemist — 30-some years of statistical mechanics before I hung up the lab coat — and I've spent the last few years reading philosophy of physics because it turns out I never really understood the foundations of my own field. Anyway, here's what I want.

Everybody's favourite story about Maxwell's demon is that the demon gets caught by the cost of measurement: that you have to spend energy to find out which side of the box the molecule is on, and that's what rescues the second law. I've never belived it. If the demon's memory starts blank, the measurement can be done reversibly, so nothing has to get paid there. So here's the position I want you to argue, as hard as you can: the demon is defeated only because it is a finite physical system whose memory sits on the same thermodynamic ledger as the gas. Either it keeps the record, and then the entropy it thought it was stealing is sitting right there in the memory it had to write, or it reuses the memory and pays the erasure cost. And I want you to argue those two stories are the same story told twice, not two competing fixes — that the demon's memory, and not some special price of knowing, is the thing that saves the law. If you think the slogan that information is physical actually earns its keep here, say so; if it's just a metaphor doing no work, say that too.

Then be honest with me about where this argument is weakest and what would change your mind: what a second-law-violating demon would actually have to do, and whether there's a consistent design my story rules out too fast. I'd rather you tell me the argument is shaky than pad it.
