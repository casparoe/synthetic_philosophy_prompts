For the past several years I have run a small interview series out of my department — no real audience, but the guests are serious and the conversations are allowed to get technical — and I could use help preparing for next month's. The guest is a philosopher who defends a distinctive account of the standing to blame, and my sense is that the position is both attractive and unstable, which is the mood I want the interview to have. Her view, briefly stated: standing is not a function of the blamer's overall moral record but of her place in a practice of mutual answerability with the person she blames, so hypocrisy costs you standing only when you have exempted yourself from that practice — that is, only when you would not accept the same criticism in return. She adds that standing is graded rather than all-or-nothing, running along two dimensions she insists on distinguishing, the authority to hold someone accountable and the standing to be heard, and she holds the mildly counterintuitive thesis that a reformed wrongdoer, someone who has done the very thing she now condemns and has genuinely reckoned with having done it, typically has more standing to blame for that wrong than a blameless outsider does, since having done it from the inside is part of what makes the address apt.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each paired with a short follow-up of one or two sentences that is keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I can keep pressing no matter how she plays it. The questions should aim at the joints of the position rather than at the book jacket. I want at least one question on each of the following: whether her account can explain why hypocritical blame is wrong rather than merely ineffective, or whether it can only explain why we are permitted to ignore it; what exactly the hypocrite lacks, if not knowledge — whether standing here is a permission, a competence, or something else entirely, and what her answer implies about blame issuing from the morally blind or the badly informed; the relation between the standing to blame and the standing to forgive, including whether her relational machinery makes the two cases come out differently for the right reason, and what it entails for third parties who purport to forgive wrongs done to others; and the suspicion that graded standing plus a reciprocity condition collapses into the claim that standing is just whatever the blamed party is willing to entertain. Add at least one question aimed squarely at the thesis about the reformed wrongdoer: I want to see whether she will accept that her view makes standing hostage to autobiography, and what she says when the wrongdoer's inside knowledge is itself distorted, partial, or self-serving.

Order the questions so that the pressure accumulates across the hour rather than arriving all at once, keep the register pitched for graduate students and faculty who already know the terrain, and leave me a closing question that gives her a genuine chance to repair the position if the earlier questions have landed. I would rather come away with two questions too sharp than with a transcript of softballs, so please avoid anything that invites her to begin with how she came to the topic or to summarize what the debate has been about.
