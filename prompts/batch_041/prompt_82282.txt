I spent thirty-one years pricing catastrophe reinsurance and pension liabilities, and I am now, in retirement, a trustee of a small family foundation with about $400,000 a year to give away and a standing interest in what one might call the long view. Over the past two years my own reading has drifted from actuarial material into imperial history — Rome, the Ottomans, the Spanish, the British, the Soviet Union, and a shelf of books promising to explain why empires fall — and I have come to distrust almost all of it in a very specific way. The books argue from the inside: they tell me which rot set in, which elite faction lost its nerve, which fiscal or demographic indicator turned. I keep wanting to ask the question an underwriter would ask, which is what the base rate is, and whether the indicators would have looked any different in the empires that did not fall. I have a board meeting in the spring and I would rather spend the foundation's next three years on the right conceptual problems than on another round of narrative history.

So here is what I would like from you. Assume the effort at stake is small and specific: roughly three years, perhaps two part-time researchers plus me, and money enough for a few commissioned essays but not for building a database. Given that, which open questions or lines of work in the outside-view forecasting of empires and large states matter most, and in what order would you put them? I am less interested in a list than in the reasoning — which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable for people who are careful and numerate but not professional historians, and what you would expect to see over three years that would move a question up or down the ranking. Please do not give me a reading list; I am after the agenda and the case for it.

To be concrete about the sort of thing I mean, here are four worries I have not been able to settle on my own, and I would like you to tell me whether they are the crux or a distraction. First, every empire in the sample has already ended, while the ones still standing are, so to speak, still on the books; I cannot tell whether that makes the base-rate arithmetic useless or merely in need of care. Second, several polities seem to have died more than once, or to have been reclassified by later historians as having died earlier or later than contemporaries believed, so I cannot tell whether I am counting lives, deaths, or the historians' convenience. Third, the candidate causes of decline are almost always identified by studying states that did decline, which is precisely the error I would have been fired for in my old job; I do not know what an honest control group would even look like. And fourth, I suspect that "the empire fell," "the regime changed," and "the country stopped being a great power" are three different outcomes with three different base rates, and that much of the literature muddles them together. If one of those is the real bottleneck, say so and say why; if the real bottleneck is something I have not thought of, I would rather hear that.
