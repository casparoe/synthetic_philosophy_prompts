I am giving the inaugural address to a newly constituted citizens' assembly: a body of randomly selected residents, most of them unaccustomed to public speaking, who will spend a month deliberating on a single contested question before sending a recommendation to the legislature. Two features of the occasion should shape the speech. First, the charter gives the assembly a mandatory second vote, held a fixed interval after the first, at which the entire question is reopened; second, the inaugural speaker is barred from advocating on the substance of the question before them, so everything I say must be about how they deliberate rather than what they should decide. Write the address, drawing it entirely from the three canonical failures of Athenian democracy — the Mytilene debate and its reversal, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates — and use them to be honest about what the second vote can and cannot buy: that the reversal at Mytilene came a day late and hung on a second ship, and was won not by an appeal to mercy but by an argument about what was useful; that Sicily was a failure of appetite rather than of deliberative machinery; and that the same city which could be argued out of a massacre, and which had only just sworn to forget its own civil war, could nonetheless condemn an old man for asking questions. Quote nothing and deliver no history lecture; the political scientists in the room should hear that the cases are understood, while the members hear counsel addressed to nervous neighbours. Do not end on the comfortable note that deliberation is good. About 800 words.
