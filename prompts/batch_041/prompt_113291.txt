I spent my professional life in experimental physics, and since retiring I have been reading my way through the British empiricists, slowly and with more pleasure than I anticipated. A former colleague who now works alongside machine-learning researchers has been pressing me on the contemporary debate about whether machine intelligence might at some point undergo a runaway, self-improving takeoff — a sudden rather than gradual transition to something vastly more capable than us. He insists the question is a purely empirical one on which philosophers have nothing useful to say. It seems to me, on the contrary, that David Hume would have had a great deal to say about it, and I would like you to help me reconstruct his likely position. I have just finished the Treatise of Human Nature and the first Enquiry, so I am not asking for a reading list or a survey of secondary literature; what I want is an argument, built strictly out of the commitments Hume actually took on, about what he would have made of the modern intelligence-explosion and takeoff-speed dispute.

Please work through the following, and be explicit wherever Hume's texts leave the matter genuinely open rather than forcing a verdict. First, the fast-takeoff case rests on a self-reinforcing causal loop — more capable systems improve the methods by which further systems are built — and I want to know whether Hume's analysis of causation as constant conjunction, together with the rules he sets out for judging causes and effects, can even accommodate a causal process of that reflexive kind, or whether it must treat the extrapolation as unlicensed. Second, his sceptical treatment of induction and his account of custom as the great guide of life: does his own "sceptical solution" tell us anything about how much weight to give a single unprecedented discontinuity against a long run of gradual improvement, or does it leave us, as he seems to admit, still obliged to form expectations in practice? Third, his essay on miracles sets a demanding evidential standard for testimony to the extraordinary; would he apply a comparable balancing of evidence to the claims made by laboratories and company directors that a threshold has been crossed or that takeoff is imminent, and would he think such testimony could ever clear the bar? Fourth, his associationism — ideas as copies of impressions, associated by resemblance, contiguity, and causation, with his well-known puzzle about a missing shade of blue — bears on whether a machine could originate genuinely novel concepts or only recombine received ones; does that place any ceiling on the process, or is the exception he grants fatal to such a ceiling? Fifth, his political essays on the balance of power and on commercial jealousy among nations suggest how he thought rivalrous states behave under technological competition; would he expect a race dynamic to accelerate a takeoff, retard it, or turn it into a tragedy of the commons? Sixth, given that he insisted reason is the servant of the passions and drew a sharp line between descriptions of what is and prescriptions of what ought to be, would he regard the contemporary preoccupation with "alignment" as a coherent target, or as a category confusion about whether we are predicting a system's behaviour or prescribing its aims?

Finally, I would like a defended verdict rather than a hedge: on balance, would Hume have been a fast-takeoff thinker, a slow-takeoff thinker, or someone who regarded the entire framing as ill-posed, and which of his commitments does the most work in settling that? Where his position is underdetermined by what he wrote, say so plainly and tell me what further evidence or argument would tip the reconstruction one way or the other. Assume throughout that you cannot look anything up; reason from the texts and doctrines you already know, and flag any point at which you are unsure of what Hume actually held rather than papering over the gap.
