You are grading a midterm examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Kant's ethics. The unit on the universalizability tests used the following stipulated vocabulary, and students were instructed to use it precisely:

- A maxim is the subjective principle on which an agent acts, expressed in the form: in circumstances C, I will do A in order to achieve end E.
- A contradiction in conception occurs when a maxim, universalized into a law for all rational agents, cannot be thought as a coherent system of action; it is supposed to ground a perfect duty.
- A contradiction in will occurs when a maxim can be thought as a universal law but cannot be willed by a rational agent without defeating a necessary end of rational agency; it is supposed to ground an imperfect duty.
- The universalization test asks whether the maxim itself can be willed as universal law. It is distinct from a generalization test, which asks what would probably happen if everyone acted that way.

The exam question was:

Explain how the two contradiction tests are supposed to work. Then consider these two maxims:
(M1) When I need money, I will make a promise to repay that I know I cannot keep, because it will get me the money.
(M2) When others are in need and I could help at little cost to myself, I will not help, because their needs are not my concern.
For each maxim, state whether it fails the contradiction in conception test, the contradiction in will test, both, or neither, and explain why. Finally, assess the objection that the universalization test is merely a consistency check on maxims and therefore cannot generate substantive moral duties.

A student submitted the following answer:

The universalization test asks whether the maxim of an action could be adopted by every rational agent. Kant distinguishes two ways a maxim can fail. A contradiction in conception arises when the universalized maxim cannot be coherently thought; a contradiction in will arises when it can be thought but cannot be willed because it defeats a necessary end of rational agency. The first yields perfect duties, the second imperfect duties.

M1 fails the contradiction in will test. If everyone in need of money made promises they knew they could not keep, then promising would become useless: no one would believe such promises, and the agent could not achieve the end of getting money by promising. The maxim can still be thought as a universal law in a thin sense—people could keep making false promises—but it cannot be willed, because the practice on which the agent depends would collapse. So M1 violates a perfect duty not to make false promises.

M2 fails the contradiction in conception test. A world in which no one ever helps anyone else is not a coherent system of rational agency, since rational agents necessarily have ends that require the assistance of others. If everyone adopted M2, the very conditions of rational agency would be undermined. So M2 violates an imperfect duty of beneficence.

The objection that the test is merely a consistency check is partly right. The test shows that a maxim cannot be universalized; it does not by itself show that the opposite maxim is required. But Kant thinks the contradiction in conception is more than logical consistency: it is a practical contradiction, because the universalized maxim makes the action's purpose impossible. So the test does generate duties. However, because the contradiction in will test relies on what ends the agent happens to have, it cannot by itself yield substantive duties for all agents. That is why Kant needs the formula of humanity as an end in itself to supply the missing content.
