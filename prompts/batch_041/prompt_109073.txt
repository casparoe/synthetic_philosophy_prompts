I am in the upper sixth at a school that still does the old-fashioned thing of having an honour pledge: at the start of each year the new prefects and the heads of houses process into the chapel and sign a book, promising not to lie, cheat or steal and not to stand by while others do. Our head teacher wants to abolish the book at the end of this year. Her argument is that a promise nobody can enforce is worse than no promise at all, and that the word honour belongs to duelling, to boarding-school bullying and to men who cared more about their reputations than about anyone else. I have been asked to give the closing address at Founder's Day, which is the occasion on which the book is signed, and I have roughly five minutes to say something that might actually matter before she makes her decision. I want to defend keeping the book, but I do not want to do it by pretending that the objections are stupid, and I do not want to end up saying that everyone has a point.

In sociology we spent a fortnight on something I keep coming back to: the suggestion that moral life can be organised around three quite different pictures of where a person's worth comes from. On the first picture, worth is held publicly and can be lost, so an insult is a real injury that has to be answered, and a person who lets it pass is diminished. On the second, worth is intrinsic and equal in everyone, so slights can be absorbed, and the dignified response to an insult is often to ignore it entirely. On the third, moral standing attaches above all to the recognition of harm, so a small insult matters chiefly for what it reveals about what a group has been made to endure, and the wrong is addressed by making the injury visible. I would like the speech to argue that one of these three really is morally better than the others, and to say which, and why the other two are not merely different but worse — or, if that cannot be defended, to argue honestly that they are equal, though I suspect that will be harder to make moving.

Please make those three pictures do structural work rather than decorate the speech. The book is an honour device being defended inside an institution that is in every other respect a dignity institution: we have a well-being hub, a counselling service, a bullying policy and a great deal of talk about kindness, and the speaker therefore has to explain how the two can sit together, or why they cannot. I would also like the speech to face the hardest objection to my side, which is that a dignity culture can only work if somebody else is doing the enforcing, and that a school without the book will simply replace self-policing with surveillance by teachers. The room will be mixed. The head teacher read Classics at university and will notice any sloppy use of the word honour; the chaplain has views about forgiveness; most of the parents would like it over in five minutes; and there is a large block of Year 9s at the back for whom none of this is familiar. It should sound like a sixth-former speaking aloud rather than an essay being read, and I would be grateful if it were written in British English. One further thing, which I would like to be the hinge of the speech rather than a set piece: last term a Year 11 boy was filmed being mocked on the bus and the video went round the school; the school ran a restorative meeting and a well-being assembly, and what struck me was that the pupils who thought he should have hit back and the pupils who thought the school had handled it beautifully each regarded the other lot as morally backward.
