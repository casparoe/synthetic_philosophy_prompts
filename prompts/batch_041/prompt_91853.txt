I am preparing a term-paper proposal (roughly 6,000 words, one semester) and want help turning a half-formed idea into a proper plan. The topic is the Platonia Dilemma and the Luring Lottery, the pair of thought experiments Douglas Hofstadter used to introduce superrationality.

A quick reminder of the setups, so the proposal does not depend on the reader already knowing them. In the Platonia Dilemma, twenty people are each invited to send in a telegram bearing their name; if exactly one sends, that person wins a large prize; if two or more send, nobody wins; if none send, nothing is awarded. There is no communication, no way to learn who the others are, and the winner may not share the prize. In the Luring Lottery, a large prize is divided equally among everyone who enters, so a single entrant takes the whole prize while each additional entrant dilutes it, and entering is cheap.

My half-formed idea is this. The standard story is that superrationality — the rule that says to reason as though every agent in the same situation will reach the same answer, then maximize the common payoff — solves the Prisoner's Dilemma by making cooperation rational, and that the Platonia Dilemma and the Luring Lottery are either further applications of it or embarrassing counterexamples, since Hofstadter's readers flooded the Luring Lottery with postcards and that is usually read as a failure of superrationality. I think both readings are too quick, because they run together two assumptions that happen to coincide in the Prisoner's Dilemma but come apart in these cases:

(a) the symmetry assumption: all agents reason by the same procedure, so their choices are identical; and
(b) the correlation assumption: my choosing some option is evidence that, or is functionally linked to, others choosing it.

In the Prisoner's Dilemma, (a) and (b) point the same way and both favor cooperating. In the Platonia Dilemma they point in opposite directions. If (a) is doing the work, the only symmetric outcomes are everyone enters and everyone abstains, and both yield zero, so superrationality has no common payoff to maximize and simply has no verdict. If (b) is doing the work, then my entering is evidence that others enter, which means two or more entries and no prize, so evidential reasoning says to abstain; but if every agent abstains for that reason, the prize is never awarded, and the reasoning is self-undermining in a way it is not in the Prisoner's Dilemma.

What I want the proposal to do is state a precise question that turns this into something with a determinate answer. Something like: does the Platonia Dilemma fall outside the domain of superrationality, or does it reveal that superrationality needs to be reformulated, for instance as a mixed strategy or with a notion of roles and positions rather than strict symmetry? And does the Luring Lottery give independent evidence, or is it confounded?

I would like the plan to:

1. State the question so a reader can tell what would count as an answer, and say which of the two readings, boundary condition versus reformulation, the paper is trying to adjudicate.
2. Reconstruct the standard superrationality argument and identify exactly where the symmetry and correlation assumptions enter. The paper should not survey the literature by citation; it should lay out the arguments as positions and let me add references later.
3. Do the formal work: write the Platonia Dilemma down as a game, show that its only symmetric pure profiles give zero, and then work out what happens under a mixed-strategy version in which each of the twenty enters with some probability and the agent maximizes the common expected payoff. I want to know whether a well-defined mixed superrational strategy exists here, whether it is unique, and whether it depends on the prize size and the number of players. I suspect there is a threshold effect: with a large enough prize relative to the entry cost, entering with small probability may beat abstaining with certainty, and the superrational answer may be to enter with positive probability rather than zero. I want the proposal to test that suspicion rather than assume it.
4. Address the asymmetric-solution problem directly: the good outcome in these games is asymmetric, since it requires exactly one entrant, so any rule that forces identical choices across agents cannot reach it. Consider whether superrationality can be extended with roles or positions, that is, agents identical in reasoning capacity but occupying different slots in a solution, and whether that extension is a genuine fix or a change of subject.
5. Say what the Luring Lottery does and does not show. My view is that the postcard flood is usually read as evidence that people are not superrational, but it is equally consistent with people entering with a small positive probability because each thinks they might be the unique entrant, which is exactly what a mixed-strategy version of superrationality might recommend. The proposal should say what would be needed to distinguish these, and whether the original setup can, given that the participants' beliefs about the number of other entrants were never measured.
6. Give a month-by-month plan for a semester, an honest assessment of what could make this too big, since the mixed-strategy analysis could balloon and the role-based extension may be a research program rather than a paper, and what would count as the project failing, for instance if the mixed-strategy version turns out to be either trivially degenerate or to depend entirely on assumptions I cannot justify.

Do not give me a bibliography; I will fill in citations. Please also flag any step where the argument depends on a factual claim about what Hofstadter or his readers actually did, so I know what I would need to check.
