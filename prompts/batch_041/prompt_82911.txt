I have been reading the rationalist blogosphere's long-running arguments about population ethics for years, and this term I volunteered to TA an upper-level seminar on the subject — mostly philosophy and economics majors, plus a handful of students who drifted in from our local reading group after we spent a season on Parfit. I have just collected the take-home question for the unit on the nonidentity problem, and I would like you to grade one student's answer.

Here is the question I set.

A provincial government must choose between two long-run development policies. Policy A continues the present pattern: it is cheap, it holds taxes down, and it delivers high welfare for the next few decades, but it degrades the province's soils and aquifers, so that the people living there a century from now will be poorer, sicker, and less well educated than they would otherwise have been. Policy B is an expensive program of soil and water restoration: it means somewhat lower welfare in the near term, but the people living in the province a century from now will be substantially better off. Assume that either policy would so reshape settlement, employment, migration, and marriage over the intervening century that the people alive a century from now under A would be almost entirely different individuals from those who would be alive under B. Assume also that the lives of the future people under A are clearly worth living: they are worse off than the people under B would be, but not so badly off that it would have been better for them never to have existed.

(i) Does choosing A wrong anyone? Explain, using the nonidentity problem.
(ii) What does the case show about the person-affecting view — the claim that an act can be wrong only if it is bad for some particular person?
(iii) Is this a same-number or a different-number case, and does that distinction bear on your answer?

Here is the answer I received.

(i) The nonidentity problem is the problem that arises when a choice determines not only how well off future people will be but which people they will be. If choosing A makes the future worse, it does not make things worse for any particular person, because the people who would have been better off under B are not the people who exist under A. The future people under A have no complaint: had B been chosen, they would never have existed, and since their lives are worth living, existing under A is better for them than never existing at all. So A wrongs no one.

This is the nonidentity problem at its sharpest. Because the number of people is the same under A and B, the only thing that changes is which people exist, and that is exactly what makes the harm invisible to a person-by-person accounting. Nor is this a wrongful-life case: if the future people's lives were not worth living, they would be wronged by being brought into existence, and the nonidentity shield would not apply. What makes the case hard is that their lives are worth living, so each of them is better off existing than not.

(ii) The case bears directly on the person-affecting view. Under A, no particular person is worse off than they would have been under the alternative, since for each future person under A the alternative is never existing. So the person-affecting view cannot condemn A. This is the same structure that generates the repugnant conclusion. Parfit's mere addition paradox shows that if we assess outcomes only by how they are for the people in them, we are driven to accept that a vastly larger population with lives barely worth living can be better than a smaller, flourishing one. The nonidentity problem is really the first stage of that same argument: once we grant that we cannot compare outcomes person by person, we are pushed to compare them by totals, and the mere addition paradox does the rest.

(iii) This is a same-number case. A and B produce the same number of people, but different people, and that is what makes it a clean instance of the nonidentity problem.

The upshot is that the person-affecting view leaves us unable to say that A is wrong; and since no one is wronged by A, A is not wrong. To condemn A we would have to abandon the person-affecting view for an impersonal criterion, and accept the repugnant conclusion as the price.
