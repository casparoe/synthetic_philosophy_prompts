ok, weird one for you. Kant died well over a century before anyone was cutting the corpus callosum, so he never had to say anything about split-brain patients. but suppose you could sit him down with the modern data — patients whose hemispheres are disconnected, where the left one talks and the right one can only point at cards, and now and then the two give oposite answers to the same question.

what does he say? no hedging, i want an actual prediction. does the unity of apperception commit him to exactly one transcendental subject per body, so the patient counts as one person no matter what the hemispheres get up to? or does he have to bite the bullet and admit two centres of experience, which would be pretty awkward given how much of his whole project leans on there being a single self-conscious subject? and if he tries to wriggle out by saying there's one noumenal subject and two merely empirical selves, is that actually coherent or is it just a patch?

the part i care about most: picture a patient whose left hemisphere, when it can talk, will happily lie to save a friend from a would-be killer, while the right hemisphere, answering by pointing, refuses the lie no matter what — it'll take the awful outcome instead. so which one is the person, for Kant? the one that's self-conscious, or the one that's the locus of the moral law? can he even let the moral law come apart in half like that, when he thinks it's one and the same in every rational being?

be honest about where the prediction breaks down and needs patching. no bibliography, just argue it out.
