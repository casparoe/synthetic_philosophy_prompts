I'm TAing an upper-level seminar on evolution and the mind, and I have to write one of the essay questions for the final. The topic is free-floating rationales -- the Dennett idea that there can be reasons nobody actually has that still explain why some trait or behaviour is the way it is. I want a question students can't pass by writing "Dennett: reasons without reasoners, intentional stance, moving on." It has to make them decide where the concept actually bites and where it's being over-applied.

So build the question around these four cases and make them sort them, then defend a general claim:

(a) a spider's web -- the geometry is well explained as a solution to catching prey of a certain size across a certain gap, even though the spider computes nothing;
(b) a neural net trained by gradient descent that ends up implementing a policy that's provably optimal for its training objective, though no engineer ever wrote that policy down or understood it;
(c) some bit of bird plumage that's a byproduct of a developmental constraint and does nothing for survival or reproduction, but that we find ourselves telling a tidy just-so story about anyway;
(d) a ritual hand-washing custom that persists in a community, where the practice really does cut disease, but the reason it exists is that somebody once worked out the germ connection and the practice then got passed down as custom.

Then the general part: does the free-floating rationale idea do real explanatory work, or is it just the intentional stance dressed up? And is there a fact of the matter about which rationale a system has, or can several rationales fit the same behaviour equally well?

Please give me: the question itself (it's a 90-minute essay, so aim it at something answerable in 700-900 words), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves separating an A from a B -- especially what each must say about (i) whether (c) and (d) have free-floating rationales and why, and (ii) whether the free-floating rationale idea is committed to a selection history or only to current causal role. And a one-line note on how students typically get each must-hit point wrong.
