My friend and I got into a stupidly long argument last night about unions and I'm still chewing on it. Here's the thing that's stuck with me. Where I live, when a union wins the vote it becomes the exclusive bargaining rep for the whole unit — it negotiates the contract for everyone, files the grievances for everyone, and the people who voted no, or who never joined, are still bound by whatever it signs. You can't cut your own seperate deal on the side. Fine, I get majority rule, one voice for the shop. But then there are the right-to-work places, where that same union — the one that speaks for everybody whether they like it or not — is not allowed to collect any fee from the people who refuse to join. So you get the representation, you just don't pay for it. And the people who like that setup say it's about freedom: nobody should have to fund an organization they didn't choose. Which sounds reasonable right up until you notice it cuts against the first thing. Because if the union is the voice of the unit whether you picked it or not, that's starting to look like a government, and maybe you owe it support the way you owe taxes. If instead it's just a voluntary club you're free to fund or skip, then why does it get to speak for you and bind you?

That's what we were yelling about. My friend thinks the whole thing is actually consistent and I'm just mixing up two different questions — who negotiates for you vs. who pays for the negotiating. I said no, those aren't seperate, the second one is the whole point. The old line people always trot out is no taxation without representation; this arrangement feels like the photo negative of that, representation without taxation, and I genuinely can't tell if that's a bug or if it's secretly the only fair way to do it.

So here's what I actually want from you. Exclusive representation seems to presuppose some picture of what a union is — whose agent it is, and what kind of thing is happening when you're being represented by it. Reconstruct that picture for me. Does it strictly require that the union be a monopoly voice for the unit, or is exclusivity a separate add-on that could be peeled off? What does it presuppose about obligation — that the union's bargaining is a burden you're stuck with, a benefit you can decline, or a public good like clean air that gets free-ridden? Then tell me whether the right-to-work half is genuinely inconsistent with the exclusive-representation half, or whether there's a reading where both fall out of one coherent theory. And separate for me what the practice actually presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with — I suspect a few different theories of representation would all license this arrangement, and I want to know which ones and how you'd tell them apart. Finally, if you swapped in the rival view — that representation is a voluntary contract you opt into, or that it's a public utility you pay a regulated fee for whether or not you like the service — what concretely would have to change about how the whole thing works?
