I am writing a short paper on the moral standing of ordinary local conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking — and I keep meeting a proverb that is treated as settling the matter: "When in Rome, do as the Romans do." Four cases from my notes pull it in different directions. (i) At a bus stop where everyone crowds the door, a local friend told me to abandon the orderly queue I had formed: the queue, he said, was a foreign habit, and when in Rome one does as the Romans do. (ii) Jaywalking is illegal in my city and practised by everyone, including in front of the police; I have heard the proverb used to tell me that I must obey the law here, and also to tell me that I must cross with the crowd — which suggests that "the Romans" can mean either the rule-makers or the crowd. (iii) In the United States, where I now live, the local practice is a twenty per cent tip, and the practice is what makes a server's wage; here the proverb gives the answer I would have given anyway, but for a reason I distrust, since what seems to bind me is not the custom but the person whose income depends on it. (iv) I can imagine a town whose only Romans are visitors — where the visible local practice is performed by visitors for visitors — and I do not know what the proverb asks of me there.

What I want is an inventory of the distinct claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, rather than a defence of any one of them. For each reading: a precise statement of the thesis; a verdict on whether it is a platitude, a substantive claim that can be defended, or false; the kind of obligation it would generate and to whom it would be owed; and whether it actually supports the use to which the proverb was put in the cases above. I have already noticed that it can mean at least a piece of prudence, a claim that local conventions are genuine obligations attaching to anyone present in a jurisdiction, a claim about what an action means rather than whether it is permitted, a claim about coordination, and a claim about respect owed to a community rather than to a practice; do not restrict yourself to these, and tell me which readings are inconsistent with one another. I would also like to know whether any single reading covers all four cases or whether the proverb quietly splits into different claims for different conventions, and how much work the familiar contrast between a transgression that is wrong whatever the local rule says and one that is wrong only because of the local rule can do here — including whether that contrast survives being applied across societies.

Throughout, distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study norms, conventions, and their moral standing, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. I would rather have the disagreement marked than resolved.
