I'm doing a statutory interpretation module and theres one thing I cant get past. The courts keep telling us they're just applying what Parliament enacted, and then in the same breath they'll read an Act down so that it doesn't operate retrospectively, or doesn't put us in breach of international law, or doesn't produce some absurd result, all on the basis that Parliament can't have intended any of that -- unless the words are clear enough, in which case they shrug and say well, Parliament is sovereign, thats what it enacted. They'll also refuse to read general words as letting a minister do something drastic to people's rights, on the same Parliament-can't-have-meant-that basis. So what does the practice actually presuppose about legislative intention? Is it a claim about what the people in the chamber really had in mind, or a norm about what we're entitled to attribute to them? Does it presuppose that an interpreter has to assume the legislature is reasonable, and if so is that a moral constraint on interpretation or just a sensible rule of thumb? Is any of it consistent with being a textualist, or does the practice quietly presuppose something closer to purposivism? And if you swapped the presupposed view for a strictly textualist one, what would actually change in court? Ta.
