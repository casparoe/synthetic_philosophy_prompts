Two colleagues of mine spent most of a workshop discussion arguing about whether the Darwinian framework genuinely applies to culture. I was in the audience and took no side, and I would like the exchange adjudicated rather than settled by fiat.

The first, a selectionist, argued that Darwinian explanation requires only variation, heritability, and differential transmission. Culture supplies all three: songs, sayings, rituals, and beliefs differ from one another; some spread far more widely than others; and much of what spreads is learned from other people, so differences are carried down lineages. On that basis she was willing to call cultural items replicators and to treat their frequencies as shaped by selection. She conceded that transmission is noisy, but held that the conditions usually demanded of a replicator — copying fidelity, fecundity, longevity — are matters of degree, and that where fidelity is low the remedy is to look for larger and more robust units of transmission rather than to abandon the framework.

The second, an attraction theorist, replied that the analogy fails at heritability rather than at fidelity. On his account, a person who learns a tune or retells a story does not copy it but reconstructs it out of her own expectations, and the errors are not random with respect to content: they are pulled repeatedly toward the same few forms, which is why the same variants turn up in lineages that have had no contact with one another. Where variation is directed toward attractors, he said, the observed distribution of cultural forms is explained by the attractors themselves, and differential reproduction does no work. He added a second objection: no one has produced a principled way of individuating a meme, and without items whose frequencies can be tracked, talk of selection lacks a well-defined subject.

She answered that directed variation does not abolish selection; it constrains the variation on which selection operates, much as mutational bias constrains genetic evolution without replacing it. She also said the individuation objection proves too much, since species are equally contested as units and population biology proceeds regardless. He answered that the two cases are not parallel: genetic evolution is variational, with variation arising first and being sorted afterwards, whereas cultural transmission is transformational, the mutation being produced by the very process that explains the endpoint — and where a law-like transformer is available, selection is not merely bounded but unnecessary. She replied that a selection coefficient for a cultural variant is measurable in principle once a transmission channel is specified, so his objection marks an empirical gap rather than a conceptual one. Neither of them budged.

Adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each move, rate it on a scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification, and say who bears the burden of proof at that point. Then say what the disagreement is really about: whether it is a substantive disagreement about how culture changes, a disagreement about what a Darwinian explanation is required to contain, or a terminological dispute about how loosely the word selection may be used. Finally, identify what both parties are missing — including what would count as decisive evidence for either position, and whether either side's position can be maintained in the form in which it was actually stated.
