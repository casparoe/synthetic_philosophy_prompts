I am preparing a seminar on decision theory and want to pair Sophocles' Oedipus Rex with Good's theorem on the value of information. Good's theorem says that, under standard Bayesian assumptions, the expected value of information is non-negative: an agent who can costlessly ignore new evidence cannot be made worse off, in expectation, by receiving it. Oedipus looks like a counterexample. He seeks information about the murder of Laius and about his own parentage, and what he learns destroys him. I want a reading that takes both the play and the theorem seriously. Does the play dramatize a genuine limit of the theorem, or does it instead illustrate its scope conditions? Is Oedipus's failure that he cannot commit to ignoring the information, that his preferences change once he knows, that the information is not merely evidence but a cause of the outcome, or that the oracle's pronouncements are not information in the Bayesian sense at all? Where does the theorem illuminate a scene -- the consultation with Tiresias, the Corinthian messenger, the shepherd, Jocasta's realization -- and where does the play resist the reading and the honest critic has to say so? Finally, what does the tragedy add to the philosophical debate that decision theory alone does not: does it show that some knowledge is valuable only if it can be acted on, or that the value of information can be negative when the information is about who one is?
