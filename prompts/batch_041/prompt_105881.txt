I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have volunteered to run next month's lunchtime discussion for my team: eight or nine engineers, mostly backend and infrastructure, none of whom have read any philosophy since university, and two or three of whom will arrive assuming that the whole exercise is word games. I have forty-five minutes and a whiteboard. The topic I proposed is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, which as I understand it runs roughly like this: you do what you do because of the kind of person you are; so to be genuinely responsible for what you do you would have to be responsible for the kind of person you are; and to be responsible for that you would have to have made yourself, which nothing can do; therefore nothing is ever genuinely responsible. I do not want you to explain the argument to me. I want you to plan how I should explain it to them.

Give me the order the pieces have to go in, and why it has to be that order rather than another — which distinctions must already be in place before the first step can be heard as a substantive claim rather than as an obvious truth or an obvious falsehood, and what specifically goes wrong if I introduce each piece too early. I am not sure whether the word responsible is doing the same work throughout the argument as it does in ordinary talk, and I would like the plan to tell me whether that has to be sorted out, and where.

Then tell me the single example that should carry the session. It has to be drawn from software engineering, since that is the only shared vocabulary in the room, and I would rather not fall back on the usual case of not having chosen one's genes or upbringing. Tell me what the example must have in order to bear the weight, what it must conspicuously lack, and precisely where it breaks down; and tell me what I should do in the moment when somebody spots the break.

Then give me the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface, and for each one the exact point in the sequence at which I should head it off. I expect at least these: that the argument is just the familiar worry about determinism; that its cousin, the thought that the conclusion is avoided if some choices are undetermined or partly random, is a way out; that the argument shows we should stop holding people accountable; and that a person can always remake themselves by an act of will, so the regress never gets going. Tell me which of these is most likely to survive the session intact, and why.

Finally, give me one question to put to the room at the end that would separate someone who has understood the argument from someone who has merely caught a mood of fatalism; and tell me what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level, naming the things I am leaving out and saying why, so that nobody leaves thinking the argument is settled.
