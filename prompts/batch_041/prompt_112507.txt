I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have ended up in a long, unresolved argument with a colleague about the phrase "the will of the people". He treats a referendum result as the thing itself; I am not convinced that any count of votes can be identified with something of that kind. To get some footing I have started reading Rousseau's *The Social Contract* in G. D. H. Cole's translation, and I have been brought to a halt by the first chapter of Book IV, the one titled "That the General Will is Indestructible". Here is the passage as it stands in the edition I have:

But when the social bond begins to be relaxed and the State to grow weak, when particular interests begin to make themselves felt and the smaller societies to exercise an influence over the larger, the common interest changes and finds opponents: opinion is no longer unanimous; the general will ceases to be the will of all; contradictory views and debates arise; and the best advice is not taken without question.

Does it follow from this that the general will is exterminated or corrupted? Not at all: it is always constant, unalterable and pure; but it is subordinated to other wills which encroach upon its sphere. Each man, in detaching his interest from the common interest, sees clearly that he cannot entirely separate them; but his share in the public mishaps seems to him negligible beside the exclusive good he aims at making his own. Apart from this particular good, he wills the general good in his own interest, as strongly as any one else. Even in selling his vote for money, he does not extinguish in himself the general will, but only eludes it. The fault he commits is that of changing the state of the question, and answering something different from what he is asked. Instead of saying, by his vote, "It is to the advantage of the State," he says, "It is of advantage to this or that man or party that this or that view should prevail." Thus the law of public order in assemblies is not so much to maintain in them the general will as to secure that the question be always put to it, and the answer always given by it.

What I cannot get straight is this. First, in what sense can a will be "constant, unalterable and pure" at a time when, on Rousseau's own account, everyone in the assembly is pursuing private advantage and the general will has fallen silent? Is it that they still will the general good but are outweighed, or that the general will is not the sort of thing that can be present or absent in anyone's mind at all? Second, the sentence about the man who sells his vote puzzles me most: he "does not extinguish in himself the general will, but only eludes it". What does eluding consist in? As written it looks like a rule that no evidence could ever count against, and I would like to know whether there is a reading on which it says something substantive rather than simply protecting the theory. Third, the clause "the general will ceases to be the will of all" troubles me, since I understood the general will and the will of all to be two different things throughout; if they are different, how can one cease to be the other — has the general will changed, or has the vote merely stopped tracking it? Fourth, the closing sentence about the law of public order in assemblies seems to claim that procedure exists to get the question right rather than to aggregate preferences; if so, what does that commit him to about how an assembly is meant to work?

I should also confess my own working analogy, so that you can tell me where it breaks down. I keep reading the general will as being to the citizens' actual votes roughly what a specification is to a buggy implementation: something the system is supposed to satisfy whether or not anyone's behaviour conforms to it, and something whose content does not depend on what the implementers happen to want. That makes the passage sound almost trivial to me, which is probably a sign that it is wrong.

Please help me understand the passage. I would like a plain-language explanation pitched at someone with no philosophy training, with any technical term you use defined when you introduce it; an account of what the passage does and does not settle; a verdict on whether it is coherent as it stands and, if it is strained, exactly where the strain is; an assessment of my specification analogy, including what it distorts and what a sympathetic reader of Rousseau would say I have smuggled in; and finally, some indication of what all this implies for the modern use of "the will of the people" — in particular whether it leaves room for a majority to be wrong about the people's will, and whether that makes the slogan empty or merely dangerous. If any of my four questions rests on a misreading, please say so directly rather than answering it as posed. I do not need a reading list, citations or references; I would rather have your explanation. Please write in British English.
