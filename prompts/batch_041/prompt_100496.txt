I am a retired physicist — four decades in quantum field theory — who has spent the last several years reading moral philosophy, and I have a half-formed idea I would like turned into a short research proposal. In field theory one is not frightened by a divergent integral. One regularizes, subtracts a reference contribution, checks that the residue does not depend on the regulator, and only then trusts the finite answer. It has struck me that the standard puzzles of infinite ethics — a world with infinitely many people at finite positive welfare, so that adding or removing any finite number of them appears to change nothing; two such worlds that seem to tie on total value even when one is plainly better — look like divergences that no one has tried to tame in this way. My idea is that a renormalization-style procedure might do for infinite aggregation what it did for the integrals: impose a cutoff over a finite region of the world, compute the aggregate, subtract a background or reference aggregate, then ask whether the result has a well-defined limit as the cutoff is removed and whether that limit is independent of how the cutoff was imposed. I would like a proposal of about two pages for a one-year visiting fellowship, addressed to a selection committee of philosophers who will be sceptical that a physicist's reflex is anything more than a metaphor.

Give me, in this order: a precise statement of the question, narrowed to something that can be answered rather than merely pondered; an account of why the three familiar responses to these puzzles — accept the paralysis, deny that the world is infinite, or reject aggregation or the comparability of infinite values — each leave my question open, and in what sense the renormalization angle is doing something none of them does rather than restating the density or rate-of-value approach in borrowed vocabulary; the method, set out month by month for a single year, saying concretely what would be regularized, what subtracted, and what would count as the analogue of regulator-independence; what a genuine result would look like and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the procedure works formally but yields verdicts no one could accept; and an honest assessment of whether a year is enough or whether this is a decade-long programme in disguise. Do not supply citations or a literature review; I will add references myself. At the end, state what would change your mind about whether the project is worth doing — which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict on its promise — and say where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
