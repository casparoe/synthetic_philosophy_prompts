I help run a small podcast/reading-group thing for software engineers who are into game theory and decision theory (no philosophy degrees in the room, so I always tell guests to define their terms). Next month's guest is a researcher who has spent, like, a decade defending program equilibrium as the right way to think about rational cooperation, and I'm half sold and half suspicious, so I want to interview him properly instead of letting him do a TED talk. Here's the view as I understand it, so you know what he'll be defending: instead of picking an action, each player writes a *program*; both programs get run against each other; your payoff is whatever the two programs output. Supposedly that gets you mutual cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, because a program can prove something like "if I output cooperate, then the other program — which is reasoning about me the same way — will also output cooperate," and that proof is what licenses the cooperation, via some self-reference result about provability (Löb's theorem, maybe?). The classic bot cooperates with a copy of itself, defects against a program that always defects, and I'm told it does get suckered by a program that always cooperates, which someone later patched. Also, my understanding is the original formal result was roughly that any correlated-equilibrium payoff can be achieved this way, which to me sounds less like "cooperation is uniquely rational" and more like "cooperation is now one more equilibrium among many" — I want that pressed hard. And a friend of mine who knows more decision theory than me says the whole thing is just evidential decision theory with extra steps, which I'd also like you to make him answer.

So: draft me eight to ten interview questions, ordered so the pressure builds rather than being a random grab bag, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give — he's a bit of a deflector, and his standard move on objections is to say the critic is asking the theory to do something it was never meant to do, so have the follow-up ready for that move specifically. I want at least one question on the transparency assumption (the two agents have to actually read each other's source code and reason about it — fine for two submitted programs, but what does that have to do with humans, or with any agent whose code you can't see?), at least one on fragility (what happens when the two agents use different logics or different proof systems, or when the proof search is time-limited like it always is in practice — does cooperation survive or is the whole thing knife-edge?), at least one on the meta-game and the equilibrium-selection regress (you choose your program, so there's a game one level up, and that game has its own bad equilibria), at least one on the EDT/superrationality collapse, and end with one about what evidence or result would count against the view. Don't ask him to define program equilibrium again, no "what got you into this" fluff, and don't worry about citing papers — I'll pull references myself if a listener asks. Please label them Q1, Q2, ... with the follow-up marked as Follow-up, and as the very last line put a line starting with "Sharpest question:" followed by, in under twenty words, which one you'd bet he can't answer cleanly.
