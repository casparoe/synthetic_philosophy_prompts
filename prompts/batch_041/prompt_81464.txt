I'm putting together the final for a senior seminar I co-teach (half CS, half philosophy) on oversight and training signals for AI systems, and I need one essay question with a model answer and a marking scheme I can hand to the TA's.

The whole term has been process vs outcome supervision -- reward the steps, reward the final answer, that debate. Trouble is every question I've drafted can be answered by someone who's read three blog posts and memorized the line about intermediate steps being easier to verify. I want something that makes them actually think.

So here's a distinction I've been kicking around in lecture and I want the question built on it. I've been calling the two sides local-validity supervision and path-consistency supervision. Local validity: a step gets credit for being acceptable on its own terms given what came before it -- the inference is valid, the algebra is fine, no competent reasoner would object to the step, regardless of where the trajectory ends up. Path consistency: a step gets credit for sitting on a route that actually arrives at the right answer, i.e. the supervisor is scoring it against solutions known to work. Both get called process supervision in the wild, and I dont think they're the same thing at all. I want the exam to make students work out what the difference amounts to.

The question should force three things. First, whether the process/outcome distinction survives this split -- specifically whether one of the two collapses into outcome supervision with extra bookkeeping, which one, and what turns on that. Second, the fact that we only ever see the model's written steps and not its computation: say what has to be true for supervising a transcript to count as supervising a process, and whether that assumption is checkable in principle or only assumable. Third, they have to take a side on what step-level credit should target when a step is well-justified but leads nowhere, or unjustified but lucky -- should the reward track warrantedness or expediency, and does the answer change depending on whether we care about the model's competence or just its behaviour on the training distribution.

To stop them reciting, put a concrete case inside the question. Something like: a model solves a linear system by eliminating variables in an unusual order; every step is valid; human raters check against a reference solution and mark two of the steps as mistakes; the trained model afterwards avoids that order even when its the cheaper route. They have to say what the case shows about what the step-level reward was actually measuring, and whether it's a bug in the reward or a feature of the task. Keep the case self-contained -- it shouldn't lean on any particular paper or result.

Level: senior undergrad / first-year grad, 75 minutes, 30 marks, roughly 900-1000 words. The model answer should be about that long and should actually take positions rather than survey the field.

For the marking scheme, give me a table with these columns: Point (the thing the student has to do), Marks, A_move (the specific move that gets full marks on that point), B_move (the move that gets partial credit), Typical_failure (the specific error that costs marks here). The marks should add to 30, and no point should be skippable without losing real ground.

Then, after the model answer, give me a JSON object with these fields: question_text (the exam question, verbatim), time_allowance_minutes, total_marks, model_answer_word_count, one_line_summary (the single sentence a top answer is defending), hardest_move (the move separating an A from a B, one sentence), biggest_common_error (one sentence), and marking_points (an array with one object per row of the table, fields point, marks, a_move, b_move, typical_failure).

Don't quote or cite any papers anywhere in this -- I'm handing it out with no reading list attached, so it has to stand on its own.
