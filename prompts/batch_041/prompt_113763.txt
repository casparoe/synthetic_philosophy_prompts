So I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science and I got voluntold into TAing the big cross-listed "collapse" course this term — half archaeology students, half philosophers — and the prof wants me to write the take-home essay question for the final becuase she's decided I'm the only one who "gets the conceptual stuff." Great. Problem is every question I draft can be answered by paraphrasing the diminishing-returns story or the ecocide story, and honestly so can I, and that's the entire problem.

What I want from you: one essay question, 60-minute take-home, 25 marks, plus a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme. It has to be answerable by someone who's read nothing beyond the lecture slides, since most of them haven't — so build the machinery into the question itself rather than pointing at reading.

Here's the framing I've been using in section and I want the question to turn on. Call it the two-thresholds picture:

- Thin collapse: a particular institutional complex (a state, a dynasty, a network of cities) stops working. Population may well persist, the region may reorganize, but the thing itself is gone.
- Thick collapse: the region's people lose the ability to sustain any society above some organizational ceiling. Not "this polity ended" but "the achievable level of complexity here dropped, and stayed dropped."

And to make thin vs. thick operational, a resumption test: you may only call a case thick if there's a stretch of time during which no polity of comparable scale or complexity exists anywhere in the region, and the missing institutions and techniques aren't just sitting next door waiting to be reimported.

I want the question to force them to work out whether that distinction does real explanatory work or is just a fancy redescription of "the polity ended but people kept living" — and I'm fairly sure there's at least one spot where the framework eats itself. I want the marking scheme to reward whoever finds it and dock whoever just narrates cases.

Things the question should make them do: apply the framework to at least two of the usual suspects (Bronze Age, Maya, Rapa Nui — whatever they've got), state the thin/thick verdict for each and defend it, then turn the framework on itself. It should be unanswerable by reciting the two standard stories.

Model answer: written as an actual answer, not an outline — I'm handing it out as a sample.

Marking scheme: 25 marks split into the moves a top answer has to make, with the mark value for each, plus a short list of the specific mistakes that should cost marks and how much. And give me a clear line between an A and a B — name the one or two moves that are the actual difference, so I can defend grades when people complain. They will.

One thing: don't lean on specific dates or numbers, I don't want to be correcting factual errors in the sample all week.
