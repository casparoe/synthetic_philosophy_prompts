Two members of a working group on probability have spent a year arguing past each other about the reference class problem and have agreed to write the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them would produce. The first, whom we may call the single-class realist, holds that for any singular probability claim — the chance that this particular patient carries the disease, that this particular atom decays within the hour, that this particular toss lands heads — there is one correct reference class, fixed by the causal structure of the situation, so that a disagreement about which class to use is a disagreement about a fact. The second, the question-relativist, holds that a probability is always relative to a question: a class is correct only relative to a decision problem, an evidential situation, or an interest, and the appearance of a single right answer is manufactured by an unacknowledged default class. Neither will abandon their position, and each regards the other's as not merely false but evasive. Write the joint statement they could both sign.

Treat the following as shared background. Probability is standardly modeled as a measure on a space of possibilities, and the probability of an event given information as a conditional expectation with respect to a sub-sigma-algebra; neither party takes the dispute to be mathematical. Both accept that two desiderata on the choice of a class can pull apart: specificity, which favors the narrowest class with stable statistics, and statistical relevance, which favors excluding factors that leave the probability unchanged once the others are fixed. Both also accept that a factor can be statistically relevant without being causally relevant, as when a marker is a mere proxy for a cause. And both agree on the numbers whenever the candidate classes do not disagree.

Produce the document in three parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both sides would sign, stated so that neither smuggles in its own view. Cover: what the formalism does and does not settle; which desiderata both accept and what makes each attractive; what the dispute is not (not about the axioms, not about whether classes can be ranked by informativeness or specificity, not about whether some class choices are practically indefensible); and any cases in which the two positions deliver the same verdict.

2. Disagreements. A table, one row per remaining disagreement, with columns for: the question at issue; the realist's statement of its own position; the relativist's statement of its own position; the crux; a case, argument, or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on the crux; the realist's prediction about it; the relativist's prediction about it. Each side's statement must be in wording that side would accept as fair, not wording only the other side would find plausible. The table must include at least these rows: whether the correctness of a singular probability claim presupposes a decision context; whether objective chance in a physical setup supplies a distinguished class or only a distinguished protocol; and whether the conflict between specificity and relevance is governed by a rule or resolved only by a choice that reflects an interest. Use at least these stipulated cases: a person for whom the actuarial class gives a risk of one in a hundred while a narrower class, defined by a marker that screens off the actuarial variables, gives eight in a hundred; and a single atom with an exactly known decay constant, where its chance of decaying in the next hour is asked about with no decision attached. Where a case cannot discriminate between the positions, say so and say why.

3. Verbal residue. Identify any part of the dispute that is merely verbal, name the word doing the work, and state what the two sides would agree on once it is disambiguated.

No bibliography, no citations, no appeals to who has argued what. Reason only from the formalism and the stipulated cases, do not resolve the dispute in favor of either side, and where the two positions turn out to coincide, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a difference.
