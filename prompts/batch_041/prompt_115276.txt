I have spent a decade reporting on drug policy, and the argument has stopped moving in a way I can no longer account for. Both camps now cite the same material: Portugal's decision to treat personal possession as an administrative rather than a criminal matter (supply, notably, stayed criminal, so it is not a test of legalisation at all, though both sides argue as if it were), and the jurisdictions that have legalised retail cannabis. One side reads prevalence figures that barely moved, and rose in some age groups; the other reads the fall in HIV infections, overdose deaths and prison populations. Each treats the other's headline number as an artifact of the bundle — no jurisdiction has ever tested a pure policy, since legalisation always arrives with taxes, age limits and a retail model attached.

I don't want another survey of the evidence. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is. Is it a factual dispute that better research could settle? A dispute about which outcomes count as success — use rates against deaths against arrests against the sheer fact of a criminal record — such that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading? A dispute about the counterfactual, where what would otherwise have happened is not merely unmeasured but unmeasurable? Or a moral disagreement about the status of drug use that both sides have learned to conduct in the idiom of evidence — my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis only? Give me reasons drawn from what each side actually claims, then tell me what would count as progress given your answer: a study design, a distinction, a change in the question. Or tell me the impasse is structural and I should stop waiting for it to break.
