I'm putting together the reduction-and-emergence lecture for my philosophy of science course, and the more I prep the more I worry I'm about to hand my students a tidy but wrong picture — so I want to stress-test it on you before I stand up in front of them. Here's the picture. Take any special science: cell biology, ecology, economics, take your pick. Grant that the microphysical facts fix all the higher-level facts (I'm happy to grant that much). Now either you can in principle derive the higher-level facts from the micro-story, or you can't. If you can, then the higher-level talk is just convenient shorthand for something already fully present down below — useful, fine, but not describing anything over and above the micro-facts. If you can't, then you're either ignorent of the derivation, or you're secretly smuggling in downward causation, some macro-feature shoving the micro-parts around. Either way the honest lesson seems to be that "emergent" is a promissory note for a reduction we haven't finished yet, or else a bit of mysticism, and there's no respectable third thing.

My colleague keeps complicating it, though. She points out that physicists call the arrow of time "emergent" from time-symmetric laws, and biologists call flocking "emergent" from local interaction rules, and those really don't look like the same claim at all — but I figure that just shows people are sloppy with the word, which is exactly the sort of thing I'd tell my students to ignore. So: am I right that the dichotomy holds and that "emergence" has no defensable middle? If not, tell me precisely where it breaks, and whether my colleague's two examples are actually the same move or two different ones wearing the same word. I don't want the whole zoo of -isms, just the crux, and keep it to a single paragraph.
