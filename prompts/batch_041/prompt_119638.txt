I run the exposure-investigation unit at a state health department — four of us, and we get two or three "cancer cluster near the plant" petitions a year, plus alot of smaller complaints, each one with a family attached and usually a lawyer on the other side. Right now we decide whether the exposure caused it by instinct, and we get accused of both rubber-stamping and crying wolf. I want a written proceedure we can actually run on a petition: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the process stops with a no, and where it goes to the director because it's a values call about how much evidence is enough rather than a fact we can settle.

Build it out of the philosophy of causation, specifically the disagreement between the but-for / counterfactual line (X caused Y if Y wouldn't have happened without X) and the interventionist line (ask what would happen if you could intervene on X surgically, setting aside everything that normally travels with X). I want to know which of those two questions to reach for when, because they come apart on exactly the cases that land on my desk: two sources each of which was sufficient on its own; a factor that only mattered because it made things happen sooner; an exposure that made things worse only because somebody else had already switched off the thing that would have stopped it; and the ordinary case where the exposure is one factor among many and the real question is which factor to blame.

Run it on three: (1) a cluster of a rare birth defect downstream of a plant with a documented release the year before and no other local source anyone can find; (2) a neighborhood where the water utility and an old industrial site both plausibly put the same solvent into wells, and the wells are contaminated; (3) a complaint where the exposure made an existing condition worse but the condition would have progressed anyway.

And be blunt if this is a bad idea — if a checklist honestly can't do this, or if I'm quietly mashing together two different questions (did this exposure cause this person's illness, vs. does this exposure cause this illness in general), say so instead of handing me a tidy flowchart. Flag the steps that are judgment calls in a lab coat.
