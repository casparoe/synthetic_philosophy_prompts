Analyze the 1977 execution of Gary Gilmore in Utah through the argument Albert Camus makes in his 1957 essay "Reflections on the Guillotine." The salient facts, which you should treat as given: Gilmore murdered two men in Utah in July 1976, was sentenced to death that October, dismissed his appeals and demanded that the sentence be carried out, and was shot by a firing squad at Utah State Prison on January 17, 1977 — the first execution in the United States in nearly a decade, and the first after the Supreme Court upheld new death-penalty statutes in 1976. Utah law at the time let the condemned choose the manner of death, and Gilmore chose the firing squad. He was, in the ordinary sense, a volunteer, and the case was publicly understood that way.

Camus's case for abolition in that essay does not run primarily through sympathy for the condemned, and it is not one argument but several that need to be kept apart. Reconstruct them separately before doing anything else: first, the deterrence claim, on which capital punishment has lost whatever deterrent force it once had, especially now that executions are performed privately rather than as public spectacle; second, the claim that the condemned suffers a uniquely premeditated cruelty, held for months at the mercy of officials who have already fixed the date of his death, so that the state's act exceeds in deliberation anything a private murderer could contrive; third, the claim that in a secular world the execution strips the condemned of the chance for atonement that a pious civilization's death penalty at least left open, and that no human being possesses the absolute innocence required to judge another absolutely; fourth, the claim that life imprisonment at hard labor is the appropriate maximum, both because judicial error remains reversible and because Camus takes such a sentence to be harsher than death; and fifth, the claim that the practice coarsens the society that maintains it.

Now run the analysis in both directions. For each of those claims, say what Gilmore's case does to it: confirms it, leaves it untouched, or exposes it as resting on assumptions about the condemned's state of mind that Camus's rhetoric needs but his argument may not. The willing condemned man is the pressure point. If the prisoner wants to die, the anticipatory-cruelty claim loses its grip on this case and the deterrence claim becomes irrelevant, while the claims about judicial error, about the state's assumption of absolute judgment, and about atonement do not obviously depend on consent at all. Say which of Camus's arguments survive a volunteer and which do not, and whether the result shows his abolitionism to be intrinsic — the practice is wrong whatever the condemned wants — or contingent, resting on what the practice does to people who fear it. Press his own concession that a prisoner serving life labor would retain the option of suicide: if the state need not prevent a prisoner from choosing death, what precisely is wrong with the state killing a prisoner who chooses it? Treat the execution, finally, as a communicative act, and ask what this one communicated to the public that consumed it.

Then say what Camus's framework cannot see about the episode — the American moratorium and its abrupt end, the religious and political setting in Utah, the media spectacle surrounding Gilmore's refusal to appeal, the fact that his death was widely read as self-willed. Where the essay is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak. Conclude by saying what this case adds to the debate over capital punishment that Camus's essay does not already contain, and whether it is best read as an anomaly for abolitionism, a distraction from it, or a test that clarifies which of its arguments are actually carrying the weight.
