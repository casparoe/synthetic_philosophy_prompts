I have recently been appointed to the review board that oversees the preference-data pipeline behind the assistant my company ships, and the further I get into the documentation the less clear I am about what the arrangement actually commits us to. The pipeline asks a pool of paid contract annotators, recruited in two countries where almost none of our users live, to rank pairs of model outputs; their rankings are averaged into a single reward signal; and a short written list of principles, drafted by a handful of staff, decides which outputs are even eligible to be ranked. We then describe the finished model as one that respects the values of its users, and we hold as a matter of policy that we will not impose any particular conception of the good life on anyone.

Reconstruct the theory of value this arrangement presupposes. Whose values does the reward signal in fact encode, and does averaging pairwise rankings commit us to interpersonal comparisons and to a specific aggregation rule rather than leaving that open? Is the neutrality we claim even coherent when a single averaged model is served to everyone, or does it survive only as a description of our intentions? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to do for convenience, and tell me which rival positions it quietly rules out — deference to each user's own values at inference time, a deliberative rather than aggregative procedure, a menu of separately aligned models. Answer in no more than 500 words.
