I'm in a seminar on American philosophy and I've gotten a bit obsessed with Ben Franklins moral perfection project — the 13 virtues, the little book where he marked his faults each day, the whole idea that a person could, with enough discipline and a good chart, get to a life with no faults at all. I want to use it as a case study for a paper on what a practice presupposes, but I keep going back and forth. On one hand the chart looks like it assumes moral perfection is basically a matter of habit, record-keeping, and incremental self-improvement, with no grace, no tragedy, and no deep conflict between the virtues. On the other hand Franklin himself admitted he never got there, that order gave him the most trouble, and that pride was the one he could not cure — and he seems to treat that failure as almost useful or at least unavoidable. I've also been reading about the quantified-self movement, and it struck me that Franklin's chart is basically an early self-tracking app, which makes me wonder whether the practice imports a whole theory of the self as a manageable, measurable project.

So what I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of moral perfection, agency, and self-knowledge that the practice actually presupposes — not just what Franklin said he believed. Be concrete about which parts are strictly presupposed and which are merely compatible with the practice, and if the practice really does commit to a particular view, say what would have to change in the practice if that view were dropped. If the practice is philosophically underdetermined — if a Stoic, a Christian perfectionist, and a secular habit-formation theorist could all run the same chart — say where. I'd like exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no intro or conclusion. In each item, also tell me what would change your mind about that reading, or where that part of your reasoning is most likely to fail.
