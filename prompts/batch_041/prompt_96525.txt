For a history-of-ideas project, I am preparing an imagined interview with Keynes in which he advises a modern government panel on using machine-learning forecasting systems and prediction markets to guide policy on climate tipping points and advanced AI risk. These tools are entirely after his time, but his early work on probability seems directly relevant: he argued that some uncertainties cannot be assigned numerical probabilities and that evidence has a weight independent of the probability it supports. How would he most likely respond to a panel that treats a model’s output of, say, a 3 percent chance of a climate tipping point or an AI catastrophe as a precise, decision-ready probability? Would he regard this as a legitimate extension of his approach, as dangerous false precision, or as something more complicated? Please reconstruct his probable arguments in his own spirit, identifying which of his older concerns would survive unchanged, which he might revise after seeing modern computation, and where he might surprise us by endorsing the new tools. I am less interested in a verdict like cautious or optimistic than in the specific reasoning he would give.
