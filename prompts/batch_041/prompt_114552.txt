I run a small discussion group at the library on Wednesday nights — this term it's a kind of DIY philosophy-of-maths thing, everyone's a hobbyist and I'm definately not a philosopher of maths, I just pick the topic and keep the conversation moving. Last week was Gödel, and since everyone there has already absorbed the usual popular-science version of the theorems (the "there are truths maths can never reach" line), I wrote a short exercise to get them off the script. I made up a blog post and gave it to them cold, then asked them to say which of its claims the theorems actually support, which ones they don't, and where the reasoning goes wrong. The made-up post said: "Any AI worth worrying about runs on rules, and Gödel showed that no rule-based system can capture all mathematical truth. So there will always be facts about numbers that the machine can neither prove nor refute, facts that a human observer can simply see are true. This means (a) no machine will ever match a human mathematician, (b) an AI can never establish its own trustworthiness, and neither can a team of engineers standing outside it, since any check they run is just another rule-based system with the same blind spot, and (c) there are mathematical facts that no possible thinking, human or machine, can ever reach. Anyone who thinks some future system is going to prove itself safe hasn't understood the second theorem."

Now I have a stack of answers to mark, and the arrangement with the group is a mark out of 10 with comments that point at particular sentences rather than just vibes, 6 being a pass. The one below is the one I'm stuck on — it reads well and I can't tell whether it's as solid as it sounds, so I'd rather you be picky. If there's something subtly off, I want to know which sentence it's in and how much it should cost, even if it's the kind of thing a tired marker would wave through. And if some part I'd be tempted to mark down is actually fine, tell me that as well, because I don't want to dock marks for my own confusion.

What they wrote:

"Gödel's first theorem: take any consistent formal system that's strong enough to talk about the natural numbers and whose axioms can be mechanically checked. You can build a sentence G which says, in effect, 'this sentence cannot be proved in this system'. If the system proved G, then it would also prove that G is provable, which contradicts what G says, so the system would be inconsistent. So a consistent system can't prove G. And since G says it isn't provable, and it isn't, G is true — so there's a truth the system can't reach. The second theorem: the same system can't prove its own consistency either, so it can't certify itself.

The first claim in the post is the safest one. A system meeting those conditions will have unprovable truths, and an observer who can reason about the system from outside it can see that G is true. But the conditions do real work and the post just says 'runs on rules': a rules-based system that doesn't contain enough arithmetic, or whose axioms can't be mechanically checked, or that's inconsistent, isn't covered at all. A chess engine runs on rules and the theorem says nothing about it.

The second claim is the classic Lucas–Penrose move — I can see G is true, the machine can't, so I've got something the machine hasn't got. The standard objection is that this only goes through if my own mathematical reasoning is consistent and could in principle be written down as a formal system; and if it could, the same theorem applies to me, so I shouldn't be so confident about my ability to 'see' G either. I think that's a serious objection and the post doesn't answer it.

The third claim, about self-certification, is where I think the post is strongest. The second theorem says a consistent system can't prove its own consistency, and that generalises: you can't establish the trustworthiness of a system from inside it, and you can't do it from outside either, because whatever checker you build is itself just another formal system, which by the same theorem can't vouch for itself. So a company claiming their system is 'provably safe' is saying something empty, and any safety argument has to bottom out in something unproven. Going further, I'd say the theorems show there are mathematical truths that no possible reasoning — human or machine, now or ever — will ever reach, truths that simply aren't reachable by any finite chain of steps. That's the part of the post people underrate, and it's why I'd give it alot of credit."

So: the mark out of 10, whether it's a pass, and comments keyed to particular sentences — which lines are right, which are wrong, and what each error should cost. Don't just tell me the overall impression.
