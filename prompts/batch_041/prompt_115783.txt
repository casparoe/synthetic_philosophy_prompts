I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, loosely attached to my department's technology-and-ethics group, and I'm assembling an application for a six-month seed grant from an institute that funds early-stage work on the ethics of computing. The deliverable at the application stage is a two-page proposal (call it a thousand words) that a review panel of mixed philosophers and engineers can evaluate; if funded, the six months produce a working paper of roughly six thousand words. I have a half-formed idea and I'd like you to turn it into a proposal. It started at a reading group last term, where we spent three sessions on moral uncertainty and I came away with a nagging asymmetry. Every decision rule anyone offers for an agent who is unsure which moral theory is correct is an ex ante rule: it tells the agent what to do given its credences, whether by maximizing expected choiceworthiness across the theories, by acting on the single theory it finds most credible, or by some variance-sensitive scheme for making the theories' verdicts comparable in the first place. But almost everything the world actually asks of a deployed artificial agent is ex post. A hospital, a regulator, or a person who was harmed wants to know, after the fact, why the system did that, on what basis, and whether that basis is one they could have contested. My suspicion is that the two demands pull apart, and that this is not a complaint about present-day architectures: aggregating across theories that may not be commensurable is precisely what makes a rule rational before the fact, and it is also what makes the resulting choice unanswerable after it. I want the proposal to state that suspicion precisely enough that it could turn out to be false.

Here is what I think the proposal has to contain, and where I need you to do the framing work. First, a statement of the question in a form that has a determinate answer, so that yes, no, and yes-only-if-you-weaken-one-of-the-requirements are all live and distinguishable. My own attempt: given a machine that must choose among actions while holding credences over a set of moral theories, does there exist a decision procedure that is both ex ante rational under some defensible set of constraints on acting under moral uncertainty, and ex post answerable in the sense that for any action taken, the considerations that actually drove the choice are recoverable by an affected party and are of a kind that party could in principle dispute? Second, why the existing positions leave it open. My reading is that the moral-uncertainty discussion is written for a single agent reasoning in private, where the only constraint is decision-theoretic rationality, and so never asks what the decision must look like from outside; while the accountability discussion is written about outcomes and their causes, takes the decision rule for granted, and asks only whether an output can be explained. The gap sits between the two, and the proposal should say so without pretending to survey the field. Third, the method, which I want to be analytic and formal rather than empirical, since I am not going to run experiments or survey practitioners: characterize the two requirements as constraints on a procedure, look for a representation theorem or an impossibility result, and where neither is available, work through the candidate rules one at a time and locate exactly where each one forfeits contestability. Fourth, what counts as success and what counts as failure, and I want failure named in advance, because I think a panel will respect it: success is either an explicit construction meeting both requirements or a sharp statement of the trade-off together with what has to be surrendered; failure is if the tension dissolves once the requirements are stated carefully, for instance because a system that simply publishes its credences and the decision-relevant comparisons it made is already fully answerable, in which case the honest result is a short negative paper saying exactly that.

Make the schedule realistic for six months at roughly one day a week, and put the month-by-month plan in the proposal itself, along with a candid paragraph on why this is a philosophical project rather than an engineering one and what could make it too large for the time. Please do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation; I will supply the references myself. Name the positions and arguments you rely on in plain prose instead, so that a reader can tell what I am responding to, and keep the prose free of attributions I would then have to check. Finally, lead the framing with the ex ante/ex post asymmetry rather than with the usual contrast between rule-based and learning-based approaches to machine ethics, since I think that contrast is exhausted and any panel will have read it many times before.
