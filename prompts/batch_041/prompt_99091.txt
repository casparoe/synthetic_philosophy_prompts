I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political ethics with eighteen students, and I have one seventy-five-minute session to spend on corruption, clientelism, gift-giving, and the question of whether anti-corruption standards are universal or parochial. I do not want a lecture or a reading list. I want a runnable activity that makes students feel the pull of two positions at once: that an official who diverts public resources to kin, party allies, or a home village is corrupt in a way any adequate ethics must condemn, and that the same official may be acting under obligations of reciprocity and social survival that a purely impartialist code cannot see. The activity should let them encounter the conflict in their own decisions before anyone names it, and they should come away seeing that the question of what counts as corruption is not settled by a dictionary but by what obligations we think public office creates, and that both universalism and relativism have costs.

Please design a game or structured role-play set in a fictional municipality, say Santa Clara, which has received a one-time development fund. Participants are council members who must allocate grants among applicants. Each participant has a public role and private ties: a cousin who needs a loan, a party that expects loyalty, a village that supported them, and a genuinely needy stranger. The payoff scheme should make impartiality costly, so that if they ignore their network they lose support and may face social sanction, while also making universalist evaluation present through an outside monitor or a citizen assembly that scores them against a published ethics code. The rules should force at least one round of public justification and one round of secret allocation, so participants can feel the difference between what they can say and what they can do. Materials can be cards, tokens, or a simple score sheet; no real money. I want the activity to run in about forty-five minutes, leaving thirty for debrief.

The debrief should map moments in the game onto the conceptual terrain: the distinction between corruption as abuse of entrusted power and gift exchange as a system of mutual obligation; the difference between what is illegal, what is morally wrong, and what is socially expected; the charge that Western anti-corruption standards are a form of cultural imperialism and the countercharge that relativism can license exploitation. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, for example students treating it as a simple morality play and condemning all favoritism, or collapsing into relativism and saying anything goes, and how to handle each failure. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce, such as the structural conditions under which clientelism becomes a rational survival strategy for the poor, or the way real corruption networks extend beyond a single decision. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the rules, roles, timeline, payoff scheme, debrief, and failure modes.
