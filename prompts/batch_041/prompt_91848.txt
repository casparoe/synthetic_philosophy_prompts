Hey — I'm a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy training, and I've somehow been voluntered into running a session at our company's monthly engineering forum (basically a lunch-and-learn: pizza, half the room on Slack). About 14 people, mix of engineers, PMs and one designer, 75 minutes, one conference room, no laptops, and I can spend maybe $20 on props. Topic is manipulation vs. legitimate persuasion. This came out of an argument after we shipped an onboarding flow where someone said we were being manipulative and my manager said no, that's just good product design, and honeslty none of us could say where the line actually is. A friend who did a philosophy degree keeps saying the difference is whether you're changing the inputs to someone's deliberation or messing with the deliberation machinery itself, which sounds smart but I can't operationalize it, and I'd rather the session do that work than me standing up and lecturing.

What I want is a runnable activity where people feel the force of the distinction in their bodies before anyone names it. My rough sketch — treat it as a starting point you're free to throw out — is pairs, where one person has to get the other to agree to something mildly costly: take a Friday on-call shift, adopt a lint rule they hate, review a 900-line PR by Monday. Then three rounds with tightening constraints: round one, anything goes except outright false statements; round two, after the agreement you read out a card with your real motive written on it and the target can revoke; round three, only true statements, but you choose which true ones to surface and when. Each pair scores three things: did it work, did the agreement survive disclosure, and — the bit I actually care about — whether the reason you gave is one you'd still accept as a reason if you were on the receiving end. Then a debrief. Give me the real rules, the materials (cards, timers, whatever), a minute-by-minute timeline, what the facilitator says and pointedly does not say at each stage, and a debrief that maps specific moments people just lived through onto the parts of the distinction: bypassing vs engaging someone's practical reasoning, selection and framing of true information, exploiting timing or fatigue, social proof, defaults, reciprocity, and the awkward fact that honesty and disclosure can themselves be used as tools. I want that moment where someone realises their own winning move in round one is exactly the thing they'd call manipulation if a company did it.

Then the honest parts. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I'm already worried half the room refuses to play round one, or someone genuinely gets annoyed at a coworker, or it collapses into generic dark-patterns complaining, or everyone succeeds because the ask is too easy — and how do I handle each of those in the moment. Also which feature of the real distinction the activity simply cannot reproduce; my guess is anything about slow, diffuse, non-agential influence, like recommendation feeds or an office culture where nobody is in the room persuading anyone, but tell me what else gets lost and what I have to supply verbally. And if there's a version of the round-three scoring that actually tests the thing I said I cared about rather than just measuring whether people feel bad afterwards, swap it in. No citations or reading lists please, I won't read them.
