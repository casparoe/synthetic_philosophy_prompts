I am a retired field biologist — thirty-five years on the foraging ecology of shorebirds, mostly standing in estuaries counting things — and for the past four years I have served as the organismal-biology member on the scientific advisory committee of the Calder Foundation, which makes roughly forty grants a year in behavioural research. Our next meeting is on the eleventh, and the docket includes a three-year proposal, about half a million dollars, from a well-regarded group that wants to test whether the human capacity to move in time with other people — tapping, marching, clapping, dancing in unison — is an adaptation: a special-purpose mechanism that evolved because synchronised action advertised commitment and thereby built the coalitions on which our ancestors depended. Their case rests on three lines of evidence: that the behaviour appears in every society anyone has documented; that infants already entrain to a beat before they can speak; and that adults who move in time with a stranger afterwards cooperate with that stranger more, and report liking them more. They conclude that the capacity is an adaptation for coalition solidarity, and throughout the proposal they call the behaviour affiliation signalling. I find the individual studies competent and the conclusion much too fast, but I have not been able to say to my own satisfaction why, and the standard we settle on here will govern the other two proposals on the docket as well.

Part of the trouble is that I no longer trust my instincts. My whole career consisted of asking what a behaviour was for, and I was usually right in the way a good guess is right — because I could return to the estuary the following season and watch. Here there is no following season: what I want to know is what happened in the Pleistocene, and what the team has measured is what happens in a laboratory now. I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of science with more enthusiasm than method, which has made me more articulate about my doubts without bringing me any closer to settling them. My two most vocal colleagues are no help. One holds that the entire adaptationist programme is unfalsifiable and that we should decline anything with adaptation in its title; the other holds that the evidence is as strong as anything in our portfolio and that a foundation which funds behavioural science and then flinches at adaptation claims has no business existing. I believe both are wrong in ways I cannot yet articulate.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete disposition — approve, decline, or approve with conditions — and if the recommendation is conditional, it should state the conditions as requirements the team could actually meet in a revised protocol, with a deadline before our next quarterly meeting. It should set out, in terms my colleagues can apply to the other proposals on the docket, the standard by which an adaptationist claim ought to be judged: what this team has established, what it has not, and what further evidence would close the gap. I would like the strongest version of each of my colleagues' positions stated and answered, and a clear account of what would change the recommendation. Finally, there is a worry I have been unable to put into words, and I want the memo to try. The team's three findings seem to establish that the capacity is useful and reliably developing, which is not the same as establishing that it was selected for the use they name; and the label they use, affiliation signalling, appears to have the function built into it, so that describing the behaviour and explaining it threaten to be the same act performed twice. If that gap cannot be closed by any study we could realistically fund, say so plainly, and say what a funding body should then do with a proposal whose central claim is, for all practical purposes, unclosable.
