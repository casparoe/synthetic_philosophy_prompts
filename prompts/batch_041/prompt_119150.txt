I have spent an unreasonable amount of time reading about precommitment — the trick of arranging, while you are still in a position to do so, to be prevented from doing what you will later want to do — and I have finally written down my verdicts on a set of cases. What I want is the principle, or the small set of principles, that they add up to.

I am deliberately skipping the canonical case (the man who has himself lashed to the mast and then begs to be untied). Everybody agrees about it, so it tells us nothing. Here is what I actually think.

1. A woman with a cyclical psychiatric condition signs, during a lucid period, an advance document consenting to be held and treated if she later becomes manic and refuses help. In the episode she demands release. She should be held. This is not a close call for me.
2. A friend of mine gives his partner the password to his website blocker and asks her never to hand it back on request. Weeks later he asks for it, citing a work deadline — a reason he would have recognized as legitimate when he set the arrangement up. She should hand it over.
3. The same arrangement, but his stated reason is that he is bored and wants to scroll. She should refuse.
4. A man stakes a sum with a third party, payable to a cause he despises if he has not finished his thesis by a date. He misses the date and asks to be let off. He should pay, and the third party should not be merciful on his behalf.
5. At twenty-two he accepted a large sum in exchange for ten years of work at a fixed wage; at twenty-six he wants out. He should be allowed to leave, modulo restitution.
6. A constitutional provision entrenches certain rights behind a supermajority so demanding that they are effectively unrepealable. A later generation, with an ordinary majority of unprecedented size, wants them gone. The entrenchment holds; the majority is bound.

Two things puzzle me. First, 4 and 5 are structurally identical — a competent adult voluntarily accepted a cost for changing his mind, and then changed his mind — yet I enforce one and release the other. Second, 1 and 6 both have the shape of an earlier settlement defeating a later, apparently sincere preference that the settlement was built to defeat, and I am comfortable with 1 in a way I am not with 6, though I cannot yet say why the demos is not simply the manic patient writ large.

My first candidate principle is what I have been calling counterfactual ratification: an arrangement binds the later self just when the earlier self, fully informed of the later self's reasons for wanting out, would still want it to hold. That gets 2, 3, and 4. It is circular in 1, where the point is precisely that the later self is not in a position to weigh her own reasons. My second candidate is that a precommitment may bind the later self against a failure of nerve but never against a change of mind. That gets 1 through 5 in a way I find satisfying — it treats the thesis stake as an akrasia problem and the labour contract as a change in what he values — but it seems to cost me 6, and it makes me worry that I am simply redrawing the line wherever my sympathies happen to fall. I also cannot tell whether it matters that some of these arrangements work by removing an option while others work by attaching a penalty.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of principles, my verdicts actually support, stated precisely and with the case that motivates each clause. Then apply it to three cases I have not listed: a patient who precommits to refuse analgesia and then, in pain, asks for it; a person who precommits to donate a kidney and then withdraws; and a person who arranges, while calm, to be physically prevented from abandoning a project he knows he will want to abandon. Finally, tell me which of my six verdicts the principle forces me to give up. I would rather have a simple principle that costs me one verdict than an elaborate one that saves all six.
