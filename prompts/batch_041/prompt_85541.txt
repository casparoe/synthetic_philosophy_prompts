Okay, this is a wierd ask, but hear me out. I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and last month we did Dennett's four grades of creature: the Darwinian ones that are just built some way and find out whether they were built right by dying; the Skinnerian ones that get to learn from consequences, so being wrong doesn't have to kill them; the Popperian ones that can run the trial inside their own heads, so what gets destroyed is a hypothesis instead of the animal; and then the Gregorian ones — us, or us plus our stuff — who think with things that live outside the skull, words and tally marks and maps and diagrams and other people. The bit I can't stop chewing on is that it isn't a replacement story. Nothing gets thrown away, we're all four at once, the older ones just get quieter, and every time I scratch out a bad sentence I'm basically a rat in a box that learned to outsource. I want that in a poem.

So: one refrain line, coming back four times, once per rung. It has to be literally true every single time and true in a different way each time — the way "something died instead of me" is true of a mouse in a Skinner box and true of me crossing out a sentence, except those aren't the same death at all. The refrain has to be word-for-word identical all four times; the shift has to come entirely from what's around it. Free verse, however long it needs to be, but no lecture in verse, and definately none of the four labels showing up in the poem itself — if someone who's never heard of Dennett can't tell which stanza is which from the images alone, it hasn't worked. I'd also like the last section to feel like it's standing on the other three rather than graduating past them, if you can do that without getting mushy about it.

Two last things. Start with the poem, no throat-clearing, no title unless you really want one. Then a short note afterwards: where the poem takes liberties with the idea, or where it leans on a reading of Dennett that people who know the material would fight about — and what would change your mind. If I told you the fourth turn of the refrain was too cute, or that the Gregorian section smuggles in a claim about culture he wouldn't sign off on, would you fix it, and would the fix amount to a different poem?
