My mate Priya is finishing a PhD in democratic theory and she's got very firm views about who gets to vote. Her rulings so far: resident non-citizens who've been here ten years, working and paying tax — yes, give them the vote. Tourists — obviously not. Prisoners serving their sentences — yes. Citizens who emigrated twenty years ago and have no property, family or business left here — no. And last month she was adamant that the villagers over the border whose homes our new dam is going to flood should get no say in our referendum about it, even though it'll wreck their lives, because they're not part of us. She also reckons sixteen-year-olds should vote and twelve-year-olds shouldn't, and insists that falls out of the same principle as everything else, which I dont follow — it looks like a seperate question about competence to me.

Here's the case I actually want your read on. There's a big factory town on our border, and a large chunk of its workforce lives across the river in the neighbouring country and commutes in every day. They pay our income tax, they're covered by our employment and health-and-safety law, they can sue and be sued in our courts and be prosecuted here for anything they do on shift, and the bill everyone's arguing about at the moment would change thier tax and their working conditions directly.

So what's she going to say about whether they get a vote? I care less about the yes/no than about: the rules that fit everything she's said so far, what each of those rules predicts for the commuters, which one she's most likely actually running and why you'd back it over the others, and which single further case would best tell the candidates apart. And if her record genuinely doesn't settle it, say what she'd be committing herself to either way. British English please.
