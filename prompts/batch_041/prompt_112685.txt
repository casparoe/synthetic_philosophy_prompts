My reading group's been chewing on this argument for two weeks and it's driving me up the wall, because I'm sure it proves far too much but I can't say precisely where it breaks. My mate wrote it out like this:

(1) Knowledge is factive: if I know P, then P is true.
(2) So if I know P, then anything that entails not-P entails something false.
(3) Evidence that entails something false is misleading evidence.
(4) So if I know P, any evidence that entails not-P is misleading evidence.
(5) If I know that a bit of evidence is misleading, I'm entitled to set it aside; it can't rationally lower my confidence in P.
(6) And I don't have to wait to actually see the evidence to know this. By (4), whatever would overturn P is misleading, and I know P right now.
(7) So if I know P, I already know that whatever counter-evidence shows up is misleading, and by (5) I'm entitled to ignore it.
(8) So knowing something licenses total closed-mindedness about it.

I can't swallow (8). It'd mean a scientist who's genuinely certain can bin every failed replication, and that the better your grounds for P are, the less you ever have to listen to anyone. That can't be right — it makes inquiry eat itself. But I'd rather not give up (1), that's just what knowing means, and (5) feels like it's only spelling out what 'entitled' could possibly mean here. So which step do I actually reject, and what does it cost me? My gut says the sleight of hand is at (6), but every time I try to say what's wrong with it I sound like I'm just refusing to follow the logic. Name the step, tell me why it looked fine, and price up what each of the other plausible repairs would cost me instead.
