I teach a seminar on human rights law and moral philosophy -- 18 students, mostly third and fourth years, one 90-minute session, and I want an activity that makes them feel in their gut why the definition of genocide is a political artifact and not a neutral description: that naming an atrocity is itself a move in a power game, and that the boundary of the category was bargained over by people with things to lose. Put on the hat of someone who has designed simulations for law and philosophy classrooms for years. Think carefully about the mechanics before you commit -- sketch two or three possible framings for yourself first, then give me only the one you think will actually land.

Here's my instinct, scrap it if you have something better. Don't tell them the word. Set it up as a 1947 drafting committee whose job is to write the operative definition of a new international crime covering the deliberate destruction of human communities. Each small group is a delegation with a secret brief -- one represents a state with a restive minority inside its own borders, one a small state that fears invasion, one a state that mostly jails political dissidents, one a bloc that needs the category broad enough to cover what was recently done to its own people, and so on. They have to (a) name the crime and (b) write the definition: which groups are protected, whether intent must be proven or can be inferred, whether forced assimilation and cultural destruction count, whether the intent has to be to destroy the group entire or only a part of it and if a part then whether there's a size threshold, and whether non-state actors can commit it. Then the whole room has to agree on one text, and you can make the agreement rule whatever is most brutal -- unanimity, or a veto for the big powers.

Second half: hand every delegation the same four or five short anonymised case vignettes, deliberately built so each one sits on a different fault line of their own definition (a case where the death toll is small but the group is tiny; forced assimilation with no killings; victims targeted as a political movement; intent plain from speeches but no orders on paper; a militia rather than a state as perpetrator). They apply their own adopted text and watch the verdicts split. Then swap: give each group a different group's definition and let them see the same case come out the other way.

Then the reveal -- the real 1948 text, the four protected groups, the categories that got dropped, the intent requirement -- and a debrief that maps each moment of the game back onto the history: the naming as an act, the exclusions as bargains, the ambiguity about how much of a group has to be targeted as a deferral that later courts had to clean up.

Include the rules and materials, the secret briefs written out, a timeline to the minute for 90 minutes, the vignettes themselves, a debrief built as a list of questions rather than a lecture, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time (I'm betting one group refuses to be cynical and drafts the morally correct definition, and another just imports the real convention from memory -- tell me how to handle both). End with a frank paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.

No citations and no reading list -- I can't hand those out and I don't want the answer padded with them. Keep it under 900 words, headers and bullets so I can scan it at a glance.
