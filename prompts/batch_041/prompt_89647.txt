I chair the case-handling committee of our national competition authority, and in three weeks the committee votes on whether to open proceedings in a matter that has divided the staff. Six firms supply a standard industrial input in a small and transparent market. Their prices have moved in near-lockstep for four years — no two of them have been more than one percent apart — and industry margins have risen by a third. We have no evidence that any employee of any firm ever discussed prices, output or territories with a competitor. What we have is this. All six license the same third-party pricing engine, which each morning recommends a price computed from that firm's own cost and inventory data. The engine's objective function was chosen by the vendor, not by the firms; the firms follow its recommendation about nine times in ten; and each firm knows that its rivals use the same engine. Our statute prohibits agreements and concerted practices between undertakings, and our tribunal has read that language to require some form of contact or meeting of minds. The chief economist argues that a common cost shock, together with the transparency of the market, fully explains the parallelism, and that we would be punishing firms for an outcome they neither intended nor controlled.

I want the opinion to engage two arguments that invert the ordinary order of explanation. The first is Darwin's: design-like adaptation is produced by a mindless process of variation and selection, so that looking for a designer is not merely unnecessary but a mistake once the process is understood. The second is Turing's: rather than ask whether a machine really thinks, ask whether it can do the things we take thinking to enable — a move that treats comprehension as something attributable on the evidence of competent performance rather than as a precondition of it. Both bear on what our statute asks for when it asks for an agreement, and I want that connection made explicitly rather than gestured at.

Please draft the committee's written opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action — proceedings against the firms, proceedings against the vendor as well, closing the file, or declining to prosecute while referring a rule change to the board — with a timetable the staff can work to. It should state the test we should apply in future cases of this kind, in terms an investigator could apply without having read the philosophy. It should say what work the agreement requirement is doing: whether it is epistemic, a proxy for the coordination we actually care about; constitutive, so that no minds means no wrong; or attributive, the thing that makes it fair to blame and fine these particular firms. It should say what it would even mean for a firm, as distinct from the individuals inside it, to have a meeting of minds. And it should give the chief economist's position its strongest form before saying why it does or does not carry the day. No citations — I will add references before it circulates.
