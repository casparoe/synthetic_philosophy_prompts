I am drafting the methods section of a paper on subgroup analysis, and I keep meeting reversals in which a treatment looks better overall but worse in every subgroup, or the reverse. Before I settle on a rule for handling them, give me the full specification: what must any adequate account of this phenomenon deliver? I want the desiderata list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, not a survey of who has said what.

The list should cover the cases the account must get right — including a reversal that persists under finer and finer stratification, one that appears only after conditioning on a variable that is a common effect of the treatment and the outcome, and one that turns out to be an artifact of the effect measure rather than of confounding; the distinctions it must not collapse (association from causation in the statement of the result; a common cause from an intermediate variable from a common effect; what to condition on from which population to average over; a pooled number being misleading from the treatment working differently in different people); and the mathematical facts it cannot contradict.

Two things I especially want. First, whether any pair of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — my suspicion is that a demand for full generality and a demand for a verdict derivable from the data alone cannot both be met, and if so I want that stated as a result of the list rather than as a caveat. Second, which single requirement does the most to exclude the accounts people actually offer. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp.

Assume throughout that the account has to be usable by someone who must commit to an analysis plan before seeing the data, and who will have to defend the resulting decision to a committee that does not share their statistical framework. Close with a brief note on which families of positions the list eliminates and which survive it.
