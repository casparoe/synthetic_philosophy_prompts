I have been reading Tolstoy's What Is Art? slowly, with a dictionary in one hand and a translation in the other, and there is a stretch early in the book — the part where he finally says what he takes art to be — that I can follow sentence by sentence but not as a single argument. I will not try to reproduce his wording, since my Russian is not good enough to risk that; what follows is my own close paraphrase of the passage, and where I have flattened or sharpened something, I would rather you say so than assume I have it right.

As I render it: Tolstoy says that to call something art is not to say that it is beautiful or that it pleases, and that those notions belong to a corrupted aesthetics he wants to set aside. Art, he says, is a human activity in which one person, having once experienced a feeling, deliberately calls that feeling up again in himself and expresses it by certain external signs, so that other people are infected by those signs and come to experience the same feeling. He distinguishes this from speech, which transmits thoughts; art transmits feelings. Then he says something I find both clear and troubling: that the mark of art is this infection, and that the degree of infection is the measure of a work's excellence as art, whatever the feeling may be. Fear, contempt, tenderness, grief — if the work infects, it is art, and the more strongly it infects, the better it is as art. Only afterwards does he turn to which feelings are worth transmitting, and there he becomes severe, holding that the feelings which further the union of human beings are what good art transmits, and that much of what his age called art transmits only the bored feelings of a privileged class. He also gives conditions that seem to make infection more likely — that the feeling be the artist's own rather than a stale one borrowed from others, that the expression be clear, and that the artist sincerely feel what he conveys — and at that point my grip slips, because I cannot tell whether these are conditions of a work's being art at all or only of its being good art.

What I want help with is the shape of the whole thing, not the translation of any one sentence. My own guess is that the sincerity condition is doing the work of a definition while the infection condition is doing the work of an evaluation, and that Tolstoy never quite admits this; but I would rather be told that the guess is wrong than have it confirmed out of politeness.

First, how are the two halves supposed to fit together? If the criterion of art is infection and the criterion of good art is the worth of the feeling transmitted, then the first looks like a descriptive definition and the second like a moral one, and he seems to want both at once: he will not let you say that a work which infects contempt is bad art, in the same breath as he says that the strength of the infection is the measure of its excellence as art. Is there a reading on which these are one criterion rather than two, or does he hold two criteria and pay a price for it?

Second, how strong is the infection relation supposed to be? When he says the audience comes to experience the same feeling, does the account require sameness — one feeling, as it were, transferred — or only a feeling of the same kind, reached by the audience's own route? The answer changes what the view can say about several cases. A lullaby sung by a mother to a child: is that art on this account, and must the child feel what the mother feels? A horror film that reliably produces dread in an audience that knows perfectly well it is safe: is that dread the same feeling the makers felt, and if not, has the film failed as art? And a joke that is uproarious in one dialect community and inert outside it: does the infection criterion make art-status relative to a community, which I do not think Tolstoy wants?

Third, and this is the case that made me write, what does the view say about a work produced by a system that has no feelings at all — say a program trained on a large body of existing art which, given a brief, produces a piece that reliably and powerfully moves audiences, though nothing in the program ever felt anything? By the infection criterion it seems to be art, and excellent art; by the condition about the artist's own feeling it seems to be excluded from art altogether, or at best to be a counterfeit. I suspect Tolstoy would say without hesitation that such a piece is not art, but I want to know whether his own criterion forces that verdict or whether it is an extra commitment he brings in — and whether, if the sincerity condition is dropped, anything is left of the distinction between art and the counterfeits he is so concerned to mark.

Fourth, a smaller matter. Does the account have room for works that infect only on a second or third encounter — a novel that leaves me cold at twenty and devastates me at forty, a piece whose feeling I can receive only after I have learned its conventions? If infection is the mark of art and infection is a fact about what happens when a work meets an audience, then art-status looks less like a property of the work than like a history of encounters, which would make the whole aesthetics more unstable than the tone of the book suggests.

Please treat my paraphrase as fallible throughout. Where my rendering has made his position look more paradoxical than it is, say so; where the paradox is really in the view, tell me which of these four questions it can answer and which it cannot.
