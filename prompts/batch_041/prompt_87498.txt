In a mock-trial debrief last week, a classmate defended a witness who had given literally true but plainly evasive answers from the stand. His argument was that the witness had not lied, and since the oath forbids false statements, the witness had not broken it. Our instructor disagreed, pointing to the fuller formula the witness had recited — the promise to tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth — and said the second and third clauses forbid precisely what the witness did. I suspect both of them are collapsing several distinct claims into one sentence, and I would like the menu of readings before I take a side.

Treat the formula as three separable clauses and set out, for each, the precise claims it could be making. For the first clause: does it require correspondence between each assertion and the facts, sincerity in asserting, or something weaker like the absence of intentional false assertion? Does it reach presuppositions and conversational implicatures, or only what is explicitly asserted? For the second: completeness relative to what — the question asked, the transaction under dispute, everything material to the case, or everything the witness knows? Is it an obligation on the witness at all, or does an adversarial system assign the burden of elicitation to the examiner, so that a witness who answers the questions put to him has done all that can be demanded? For the third: is it a ban on additional falsehoods, a ban on misleading by implication, a relevance or non-argumentation rule, or a prohibition on advocacy in the witness box? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible requirement, or false or incoherent; and say which readings make the three clauses redundant with one another and which make them genuinely independent.

Two pieces of material should be folded into the analysis. First, the casuistical tradition distinguished two forms of mental reservation. In the wide form, the qualification comes from the ambiguity of the words themselves or from the circumstances of utterance, so a reasonable hearer could recover the restricted sense; in the strict form, the speaker silently adds a qualification that makes the words-plus-qualification true but that the hearer cannot recover from anything available to him. Which readings of the oath's clauses would permit wide reservation, which would permit strict, and which would rule out both?

Second, a legal test case. A witness was asked whether he had ever held a Swiss bank account and answered by describing a company account in Zurich; he had in fact held a personal account in Geneva, but every word he uttered was true and the answer was designed to leave a false impression. The Supreme Court held that this was not perjury, because the answer was literally true and the remedy lay in a better follow-up question from counsel. Which readings of the oath would count that witness as having kept it, and which would count him as having broken it while committing no crime? Is the resulting gap a defect in the oath, a defect in the perjury statute, or a feature of an adversarial system that deliberately places the burden of elicitation on the examiner?

Finally, connect this to the underlying moral question. If a witness violates the third clause by misleading without asserting anything false, is his wrong the same kind of wrong as lying, a lesser instance of it, or a different wrong altogether — say, an offense against the tribunal's epistemic process rather than against truth? Which reading of the slogan does someone need in order to insist that the misleading witness did something the oath forbids, and is that reading defensible? End by saying which reading my classmate needed, which the instructor needed, and whether the formula on its best reading is one promise or three.
