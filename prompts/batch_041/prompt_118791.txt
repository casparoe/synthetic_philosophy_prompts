I teach a ninety-minute session at a general-audience philosophy meetup — adults, no formal training, plenty of opinions — on whether an enormous number of tiny harms can outweigh one enormous harm. Don't give me the argument; plan how to teach it. I want the session to hang on one carrying case, and I want a dial rather than the usual fixed speck-versus-torture telling: one arm is fixed at a single person tortured for fifty years, the other is set by N, the number of people who each get a one-second itch, and the group watches where each person's dial stops and then has to say what principle stops it there. Build in three variants, because they do different work: the tiny harm made imperceptible to the person who suffers it; the tiny harms all landing on one person instead of spread across many; and the case reversed so the many small items are benefits weighed against one large benefit. The sequence has to move them from a case they already agree about to the contested one through small steps each of which looks like an improvement, so that the chain rather than any arithmetic carries the conclusion. Expect them to reach for: that the itches don't count because they are too small to matter; that this is just what utilitarianism says, and they aren't utilitarians; that it is the same puzzle as the utility monster, and the same as the population puzzle about many barely-worth-living lives; and that a threshold below which small harms count for nothing is a costless escape. Tell me the order the pieces have to come in and why each depends on the one before, where in the sequence each of those reactions should be met, the one question to put at the end that shows whether they have seen that the real choice is between denying aggregation, denying continuity, and accepting the verdict, and what I should say out loud that I am not going to cover. Answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion.
