A colleague in my metaphysics reading group has a habit of settling disputes about borderline cases with a single line: that vagueness is a feature of our language rather than of the world. He deploys it most recently to wave away two questions — whether a particular drifting mass of water vapour is one cloud or several, and whether the boundary between two lineages that we call separate species corresponds to anything in nature or is merely a classificatory artefact. I would like to treat that line as a family of distinct claims rather than as a single settled doctrine, so that I can see exactly which of them he is entitled to and which he is smuggling in. Please lay out the candidate readings as a menu; for each, state the thesis precisely, give a verdict on whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and note what it would take to establish it. I am especially interested in how the readings distribute over three axes: whether the indeterminacy is located in the predicate, in the proposition expressed, or in the world; whether the relevant exclusion is meant to target vague objects, vague properties, or merely sharp boundaries; and how each reading fares against two pressure points — the problem of the many, and higher-order vagueness, in which the question whether something is a borderline case is itself borderline. A further wrinkle to fold in: suppose cognitive science discovered that human categorisation at the borderline is underwritten by a sharp internal threshold. Would that vindicate the epistemicist reading, refute the slogan outright, or leave it untouched? Finally, identify the single reading the line is most likely doing the work of when someone reaches for it in an argument of this kind, and say whether that reading actually supports the dismissive conclusion my colleague draws from it.
