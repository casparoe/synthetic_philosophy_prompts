I am a retired experimental biologist. After forty years at the bench I have spent the last several years reading philosophy of medicine, and I have reached the point where I can no longer reliably tell whether I am reasoning or merely repeating positions I have absorbed. I would like help translating the debate between naturalist and normativist accounts of disease into something a non-philosopher can actually run.

Concretely: I sit on the treatment-eligibility committee of a small health charity. Roughly once a month a member petitions us to fund treatment for a condition, and the committee must decide whether the condition counts as a disease or disorder for our purposes, because that determination decides eligibility and the money is finite. The committee is seven people; two are clinicians, the rest are lay members; I am the one expected to write the paragraph of justification that goes into the minutes. We have about an hour per case, any one dissenting member can force an appeal, and I sign off on the final wording. So I need a procedure, not an essay: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and — what I care about most — the exact point at which it stops being a factual question and becomes a value judgment that the committee must own rather than conceal.

I have drafted a scaffold and I suspect it is naive. It has two axes. Axis A is factual: does the person have a state of some biological part that is statistically abnormal, and is that state plausibly a failure of a naturally selected function of that part? Axis B is normative: does the condition cause harm or incapacity to this person, and is medicalization — treating it as the province of medicine — the appropriate social response? I also want your view on whether to add a third test, tractability: is there something medicine could actually do? Please tell me whether to adopt this scaffold, repair it, or replace it, and where you repair it, say what you are keeping and why.

Two problems I cannot solve on my own, and any usable procedure has to handle them. First, the reference-class problem: statistically abnormal relative to what population? Age, sex, pregnancy, altitude, genotype and culture all move the baseline, and I can manufacture a dysfunction or a normality for almost any condition by choosing the class. Second, the distinction I have seen drawn between disease, illness and sickness — roughly, the biological dysfunction, the person's experience of being unwell, and the social role of being a patient. Tell me whether the procedure should track all three, and if so, which of the three our eligibility decision is actually about.

Then run the procedure, start to finish, on three petitions: (a) a member with community-acquired bacterial pneumonia; (b) a member seeking funding for treatment of severe obesity; (c) a member seeking funding for a clinic that markets itself as treating age-related decline, the sort of thing sold as anti-aging medicine. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like, and I would rather you tell me so than spare me.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will misfire on, the distinctions it will flatten, and the places where a seven-person committee with an hour to spare will predictably misuse it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of what I have asked. End by saying what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would lead you to endorse a different scaffold, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
