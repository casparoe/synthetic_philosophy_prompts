I retired four years ago from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, and I have spent most of my time since reading philosophy. Next month I am to give a paper to a philosophy of science reading group, and what I want from you is a preparation sheet for the question period rather than a summary of my argument.

My thesis is this. Wigner's surprise at the applicability of mathematics rests on a bookkeeping error, and the genuine puzzle is far smaller than the one he advertised. I distinguish three claims that are usually run together: (i) that physical regularities admit compact formal description at all; (ii) that some mathematics developed by mathematicians for reasons internal to mathematics was later and independently found to fit a physical phenomenon; (iii) that the mathematics doing the work in physics is the same mathematics pure mathematicians care about. I grant (i) as a real but modest fact about the world. I argue that (ii) is much rarer than the stock anecdotes suggest, for three reasons: we count the hits and never the misses, since nobody keeps a ledger of the mathematics that never applied; a great deal of what is presented as a lucky fit is retro-fitting, a known physical result re-expressed in whatever formalism was current; and much of the remainder is a two-way trade within a single small community that supplies problems to mathematics and mathematics to physics, so the coincidence is partly manufactured. I argue that (iii) is false outright, since the mathematics physics uses is a thin and idiosyncratic slice of the mathematical universe. What is left over, on my account, is a selection artifact plus a question about base rates that cannot be answered, because there is no measure on the space of mathematical structures and therefore no non-metaphorical sense in which the applicable ones are few or improbable.

The room holds about a dozen people. Most are philosophers of mathematics and physics. One is a platonist who treats applicability as the strongest evidence for mathematical realism; two are structuralists; one is a historian of modern mathematics; one is a working theoretical physicist; two are graduate students, one of whom works on confirmation theory. None of them knows my old field, and several are disposed to suspect that a retired experimentalist believes having done physics qualifies him to do philosophy of mathematics.

Draft my Q&A sheet. I want the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones physicists would raise, and not the ones I have already answered in the paper — ordered by the product of how likely each is and how much damage it does. Phrase each in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in two minutes, an honest note on where that reply is soft, and a judgment on whether conceding the point would narrow my thesis or destroy it. Tell me which two or three I should concede in my opening remarks before anyone else raises them, and where I should simply refuse to be drawn. Include separately the question I am most hoping nobody asks. Assume twenty minutes of discussion and no slides. I do not need references or a reading list; I need the arguments as they will come at me.

Finally, tell me what would change my mind: name the single objection or consideration that, if I could not answer it, should make me abandon the thesis rather than restate it in a new form, and say where you are least confident in this sheet — that is, which of your predicted objections is most likely to be an artifact of your model of this audience rather than a real feature of it.
