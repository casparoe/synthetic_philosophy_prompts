Ok so I fell down a rabbit hole on P vs NP and now I can't stop thinking about Leibniz. The guy had this grand vision of a universal formal language plus a mechanical method — you'd feed in any disagreement and out would pop the right answer, argument over. Complexity theory seems to punch a hole in that: we now know tons of problems are solvable in principle but would take longer than the age of the universe to actually solve, and for some of them the hardness even infects approximation, so you can't get a decent ballpark answer cheaply either. The 'calculus' would technically exist and still be useless. Here's what I want: how would Leibniz actually take this? And I don't mean the lazy "eh, he'd be optimistic" answer. Would intractability be a real threat to the whole program, or would he just absorbe it into his metaphysics — like, maybe God choosing the best of all possible worlds is itself an intractable optimization, and the gap between 'possible in principle' and 'feasible for us' is just another face of the finite/infinite divide he was so into? Would he even have the vocabulary to keep "can't be solved at all" separate from "can't be solved fast enough to matter," or does that distinction only make sense once you've already got complexity theory? Second thing I'm curious about: suppose it turned out P = NP, but the only known algorithm runs in n to the hundredth power with a monstrous constant attached — polynomial on paper, useless in practice. Does that vindicate Leibniz or make a mockery of him? Reason from his actual commitments — the principle of sufficient reason, pre-established harmony, the whole apparatus — not just "he liked logic and formal systems."
