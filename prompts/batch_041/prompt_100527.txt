Okay, this has been bugging me since a long argument after a meetup, and I want to actually pin down what I believe rather than what I think I'm supposed to believe. It's the old thing about side effects and whether we call them intentional. I'm not a philosopher, I just read alot of blogs, so forgive the framing. Here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is generating them:

1. A CEO hears that a new program will boost profits but will also pollute a river. He says he doesn't care about the river at all, he just wants profit, and greenlights it. The river gets polluted. I say he polluted it intentionally.
2. Same setup, but the program also cleans up the river. He says he doesn't care about the river. I say he did not clean it up intentionally.
3. A pilot bombs a factory, knowing the raid will also kill civilians nearby. He hates that it'll kill them and would avoid it if he could, but the factory matters, so he goes. I say he did not intentionally kill the civilians.
4. A surgeon does a lifesaving operation knowing the patient will lose their hearing as a side effect. She genuinely doesn't care one way or the other about the hearing. I say she intentionally caused the deafness.
5. A company launches an ad campaign that will mildly irritate one competitor. The CEO doesn't care. I say he did not intentionally irritate the competitor — even though he foresaw it and shrugged.
6. A CEO who thinks polluting rivers is positively good — he's some kind of eco-nihilist — runs a program that pollutes. I say he intentionally polluted.
7. One program pollutes a river (bad) and, as a separate consequence, makes a different downstream town's water cleaner (good). The CEO is indifferent to both. I say he intentionally polluted and did not intentionally clean.
8. A CEO says he'd rather not pollute, spends real money trying to reduce it, but the program still pollutes a lot and he proceeds for the profit. Did he intentionally pollute? I lean yes but with way less confidence than in case 1, and I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just me being a coward.

My tentative theory, which I fully expect you to demolish: I suspect that when we attach intentional to a *side effect*, we're not tracking anything about the agent's mind at all — we're just thinly disguising a blame judgment. We say intentional when we'd hold the agent responsible for that outcome, and we withhold it when the outcome is good because nobody blames anyone for a good outcome. That would explain 1 vs 2 and 6 vs 7, and maybe 3 (we don't blame the pilot much, or we blame the war, not him) and 5 (trivial, no blame). What I can't square is 3 vs 4, which look identical in foresight-and-proceed but come out opposite, and case 8, where he actively tried to prevent the harm and I still want to say intentional. So: is there a coherent principle that gets all eight, or close, and does it force me to give one up? Is my blame theory right, and if so does that make intentional redundant — is there any work left for the word to do? And whatever principle you land on, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed.
