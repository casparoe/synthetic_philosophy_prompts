Ok so I've been in a seminar on theories of truth and I've basically talked myself into deflationism, in a fairly strong sense: the truth predicate exists so we can generalise and endorse (everything she said is true; if the theory is true so is its consequence), and there's no substantive property of truth with a nature to be investigated — calling a belief true doesn't add a further fact about it beyond what the belief already claims. I'm pretty convinced, but I also suspect I'm convinced partly because the view is so good at absorbing hits, so I want you to play devil's advocate properly. What would actually count against this? Not the pop objection that truth is obviously correspondence; I want the real pressure points, sorted by how decisive they'd be if they held up, and I want to know which of them the deflationist themselves would concede as a cost versus wave away as a misunderstanding.

The things I keep half-worrying about: the liar and its revenge versions, since the view wants truth to be a transparent device you can always substitue in and out, and the paradox looks like the substitution breaking somewhere — does that cost just the transparency thesis or the whole position? Then the point a friend raised, that adding a truth predicate to a formal theory lets you prove new things in the base language (reflection-type principles), which smells like truth doing real explanatory work rather than mere bookkeeping. Then whether truth's role as the norm or aim of assertion and inquiry — that we criticise beliefs as true or false and that this actually guides revision — needs a more substantive notion than deflationism allows. And whether a truth predicate can earn its keep in compositional semantics, or really is just a device for genealising.
