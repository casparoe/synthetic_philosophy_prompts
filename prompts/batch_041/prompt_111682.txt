I'm a retired limnologist — 31 years counting fish and measuring phosphorus in lakes for a state agency — and since I stopped doing that I've been sitting in on a third-year ethics course at the university here. First philosophy since a survey course in about 1975, and honestly I'm having a wonderful time, though I'm pretty sure I'm the only one in the room taking notes in pencil and drawing little diagrams in the margin. Anyway, the professor has taken to asking the few of us who linger after class to draft bits of the final, and I've been handed one essay question on Williams. Here's my trouble. I can recite both of the readings backwords now: the one where the visitor is offered the choice of shooting one prisoner himself so that the rest go free, and the chemist who could take a job making rather unpleasant gas; and then the second piece, where a man can only pull one person out of the water and it's his wife. Everyone gets the wife one by week three — the man shouldn't have to think about whether he's *allowed* to save her. What I can't work out, and nor can the four other people I've cornered about it, is whether that second complaint is even the same complaint as the first one.

My friend Priya, who works on consequentialism and has the patience of a saint with me, says the wife business is purely a point about what goes through the man's head in the moment, and that any sensible consequentialist will cheerfully agree: the man needn't have that thought at all, the justification can sit quietly in the background where he never has to look at it. Which would make the whole thing a claim about psychology rather than about ethics, and finished in a paragraph. But it doesn't feel finished to me. My suspicion — and I may well be making this up — is that the real bite isn't about how many thoughts he has but about the direction of the justification: on that theory, the reason his wife matters is that the theory permits him to care, rather than the theory permitting it because she matters; and that the deeper complaint is that he would have to be willing to drop her, or drop his life's work, if the sums went the wrong way. Which is a different complaint from the one about morality asking too much of us — I'm fairly sure half the class thinks Williams is just the demandingness people with better prose, and I want the question to smoke that out. My suspicion may be bunk, of course. That's fine, I'd rather have a question that makes them argue about it than one they can answer by nodding.

So: could you write me the exam question itself, plus a model answer of about 600 words, plus a marking scheme? Constraints — third-year undergrads, 50 minutes, no notes, 20 marks; they've read the two pieces and had one lecture on the sophisticated/indirect consequentialist reply, so the question has to be answerable from that plus thinking, not from who published what when. No citations please, and nothing that depends on them knowing titles or dates. I'd like the question to make them (a) say whether the two objections stand or fall together, and what if anything they share; (b) seperate the complaint about how much morality demands from the complaint about the agent being alienated from his own projects; (c) take the best shot at the reply that the justification need only be available in the background, and say what is left of the objection if that reply works; and (d) finish with a part asking what a moral theory would have to be like in order for the objection not to touch it at all — I want to see whether they can say what would have to be true of the theory rather than just praising Williams for being humane. For the marking scheme, give me five must-hit points with the marks attached to each, a line per point on how candidates typically lose marks there, and a clear statement of what separates an A from a B. I'll be marking a stack of these myself and I need to know what I'm looking for.
