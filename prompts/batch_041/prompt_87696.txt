I teach philosophy at a mid-sized state school and I've somehow ended up chairing the review panel for a small regional foundation that hands out project grants. We meet three times a year, forty-odd proposals a round, twelve of us round a table, ten minutes of real discussion per proposal before we vote, and the board rubber-stamps whatever we send up, so the reasons have to survive being read by someone who wasn't in the room. I'm the token humanist, which mostly means I'm the one who says hang on and gets ignored.

Here's what's eating me. The panel decides on the basis of stuff that isn't the proposal. Matching funds pledged by a local bank. A letter from someone famous. An evaluation plan that clearly took three weeks and a consultant to write. A partner with a big name on the letterhead. My collegues treat all of it as evidence that the project is serious. Some of it is. Some of it is money and staff time being set on fire in front of us, and I think we're rewarding the fire. My rough idea, which I don't think I can state properly, is something like: a thing is only evidence about a project if it would be harder for a bluffer to produce than for the real thing, and the person paying for it has to be the person whose quality is actually in question, and if we start weighting it then everybody will start producing it and it'll stop meaning anything. I also suspect that some of what convinces me isn't expensive at all, it's just unfakeable, and I don't have a good grip on the difference between those two.

Can you write me the one-page procedure? I want the questions in order, for any credential someone raises in discussion; a step that sorts them into ones that are just expensive, ones that can't really be faked, and ones that are neither; what to do when we can't tell which we're looking at; how much weight such a thing is allowed to carry in the final score, and whether it's ever allowed to be decisive on its own; and what we have to do to our own rubric, given that applicants will adapt to whatever we start rewarding. Where the procedure runs out and somebody just has to make a values call, say so in the procedure rather than hiding it behind a number. It has to be runnable by panelists who've never read anything about any of this, inside the ten minutes we have. And please don't tell me to go read someone — I don't have time and the panel definitely doesnt.

Then run it on three of ours. (a) A well known regional theatre with $40k in matching funds from a bank and a letter from a state senator, for a rural storytelling project that on paper is a website and six workshops. (b) Four retired librarians with no matching funds, no famous names, $12k, who have run the same bookmobile thing in three counties for eleven years and whose past reports are boring and complete. (c) A university centre asking for three years, with a logic model, an external evaluator at 15% of budget, and a partner with a famous name — the evidence apparatus alone is a third of the ask. I'd like to know which of my instincts the procedure contradicts, because I'm fairly sure at least one of them it will, and I want to find out before I hand this out to people I have to sit next to.
