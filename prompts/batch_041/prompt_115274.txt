Write a sustained piece of deadpan satire in the form of an internal memorandum from an invented office. The conceit: the firm is a biological lineage, its ledger is a variance decomposition of fitness, its shareholders are replicators, its workforce is a population of organisms, and its divisions are groups, demes, and trait groups. The memorandum — issued by a body with a plausible corporate name, such as an Office of Fitness Accounting or a Committee on Levels of Booking — announces a change in the level at which fitness is henceforth recognized as revenue, sets out the new policy in numbered clauses, and reports the disputes that the change has provoked. Keep the register that of a real, faintly defensive corporate memo, and never let the author concede that anything in the document is funny.

The piece should run 700–900 words and include headings, numbered clauses, a rationale, a closing signature from an invented official with an invented title, and one appendix (a dispute form, a glossary of internal terms, or a schedule of exemptions).

Requirements:

- The humour must come from the biology and the philosophy, not from whimsy. Every joke should be recognizably an instance of a real position, argument, or objection in the levels-of-selection debate. A specialist should find the piece fair rather than loose. Do not caricature any side: the memo's author is a partisan, and that partisanship is the joke, while the objections of the other side are stated at their strongest.
- The memo must earn its laughs from the following disputes at least: whether a group-level term in a decomposition of fitness names a distinct causal process or merely re-partitions the same total; whether insisting that one level is the real level commits a fallacy of averaging; how a trait that is altruistic at one level can be selfish at another, and what that does to the description of the trait; what free-riders and cheaters do to the possibility of group-level adaptation, and which conditions — population structure, relatedness, heritable between-group variance — would have to hold for group-level selection to matter; how to classify genetic elements whose interests diverge from the rest of the genome; and whether kin selection and group selection are rival explanations or two bookkeepings of a single process.
- You may use the field's ordinary vocabulary (replicator, vehicle, interactor, trait group, contextual analysis, and the like) without explaining it, but the piece must not read as a glossary in disguise: the terms have to be doing work in the memo's own bureaucratic logic.
- Carry at least one concrete case through the document — a rogue shareholder who declines to abide by the bylaws of fair meiosis, a subsidised worker in a colony, a poultry division whose pilot programme produced awkward results — invented, but biologically coherent, and consistent from first mention to last.
- Include one clause that turns against the memo's own author: applied consistently, the new policy implies something unwelcome about the status of the office issuing it, or of the firm itself. Let that implication stand; do not soften it.
- The irony must stay legible to a reader who knows no biology: such a reader should be able to tell that the document is a joke, and roughly what is being sent up, without any sentence winking at them.
- Cite no scholar, paper, date, or study, and quote no text. Assume the reader's library is empty and the reader's concepts are not.
- Do not resolve the underlying dispute. The joke lies in the office's attempt to legislate the dispute away and in its failure to do so.

The target is a habit of argument rather than a person: settling a substantive question about causation — at what level selection acts, and whether higher-level terms do explanatory work — by choosing an accounting convention, and the mirror-image move of treating the chosen convention as though it had settled the biology.
