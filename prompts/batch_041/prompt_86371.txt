Me and a friend got into a stupid-long argument last night about Leibniz and the whole best-of-all-possible-worlds thing, and I'm definately not over it yet. Here's where I've landed, and I want you to try to take it apart.

I basically buy the Leibnizian package. God had to pick one world to actualize (principle of sufficient reason -- nothing happens without a reason), the pick had to be guided by what's best, so the actual world is the best of all the possible ones. Not best as in most pleasure or least pain; best as in maximum perfection, which for him means the most variety together with the most order, the most harmony. And the crucial move is that you can't grade a world piece by piece. Take out a plague, a war, some kid's toothache, and you don't get a slightly better world, you get a different world whose whole structure might be worse. Evil is the shadow that makes the light read as light.

My friend's counter is what's bugging me. He said the view is untouchable -- that anything I'd call an evil just gets folded into the harmony, so nothing could ever count against it. I don't think that's right but I couldn't say precisely why not.

So what I want is this: what would actually count against the view? Not the usual "but there's suffering" complaint. I mean the specific things -- an argument, a case, a formal result, an intuition -- that a Leibnizian ought to feel as a real threat, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. A few things I'm wondering whether they bite:

(1) What if there's no best possible world at all -- if for every world there's a slightly better one, an infinite ascending chain with no top? Does the whole thing collapse, or can he just say the series has a limit God sees?

(2) Does "best" even mean anything if there's no single scale to compare worlds on -- if one world is better in one respect and worse in another and the respects don't trade off against each other?

(3) Is there anything the view predicts that a rival doesn't, that we could actually check -- say, anything about how bad the worst parts of the world are allowed to be?

And be honest about the insulation charge. If the view really can absorb any evil as part of the harmony, is that a fatal flaw, or is it just what any holistic theory of value looks like? Would Leibniz himself concede some things as counting against him, or does he have a standing reply to everything?

Also tell me how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
