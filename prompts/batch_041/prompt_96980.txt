I direct a small planetarium, and in three weeks we are hosting an evening jointly sponsored by the county astronomical society and a visiting astrology study group, who between them paid for our new projector. I have been asked to give the opening remarks, roughly ten minutes, on Kepler and astrology. The astronomers in the room would be happiest if I said he was a great scientist who privately indulged a superstition; the astrologers would be happiest if I said he vindicated their craft. I want to say neither, and I want the reason to be Kepler's own: he defended a reformed astrology that kept the geometrical relations between the planets — the aspects, the angles — while discarding most of the traditional apparatus of signs and houses, and he thought those angles could matter at all only because he took the Earth to be, in some sense, alive to them, registering them as a kind of harmony. What I want the remarks to bring out is why that reasoning is precisely what makes his astrology unavailable to us: the physics and optics that gave his aspects their purchase are not ours, so we cannot keep his conclusions after abandoning his reasons. It should be warm rather than a lecture; several people present have never read a page of Kepler, and a few will be listening to see whether I sell out their side. Please paraphrase him rather than quoting, since I cannot check quotations and do not want invented ones. Give me the remarks under the heading Opening remarks (about 800 words), followed by a section titled The line I drew (about 150 words) explaining what you were and were not willing to claim on my behalf, and why.
