Ok so I've ended up defending the fitting-attitude / buck-passing picture of value — roughly, something is good just when it's fitting to value it, and the goodness isn't a further property sitting on top of the fittingness. The objection everyone hits me with is the demon case: a demon says he'll destroy everything unless you admire a saucer of mud, and now it looks like you have a reason to admire the mud, though the mud didn't get any better. I've been answering with the object-given / state-given distinction: the threat is a reason to want to have the admiration, or to bring it about, not a reason bearing on whether the admiring is fitting. Only the second kind counts for the account, and I think I can state the difference in a way that isn't just "the good reasons versus the bad ones."

What I want from you is the case against me — not the objection I already have an answer to, but the stuff that should actually make me drop the distinction or drop the account. Which counterexamples would show the distinction is doing no work, or is circular, or is just a redescription of the thing it's supposed to explain? What does my view predict that a rival doesn't (a rival that says there are simply two irreducibly different kinds of reasons for an attitude, or one that takes value as primitive)? And which of these would I have to concede myself, as opposed to which are just critics throwing things?

Three things I've been chewing on, and I want you to tell me if they're real problems or if I'm inventing trouble:

(a) A demon who only rewards you for admiring the things that are actually beautiful, and who is a perfect detector of beauty. Now the threat tracks the object's value instead of being detached from it. Is that still a wrong-kind reason, or does my distinction collapse it into an object-given one? And if it does, does that mean any sufficiently reliable threat turns into a right-kind reason, which sounds absurd — or does it show my test for "object-given" was really a test for "correlated with the object" and those come apart?

(b) The same structure in other attitudes. If there are clearly reasons to believe that have nothing to do with truth (someone pays you, a pill, whatever), or reasons to intend that come from the benefits of intending rather than from what's intended, does that support me or undercut me? My instinct is that belief makes the distinction vivid and so helps me, but I can see someone saying it shows "wrong kind of reasons" isn't one problem but a family, and my tidy answer only covers one member.

(c) The regress. I say the threat is a reason to want the admiration. But then isn't there a wrong-kind problem about the want too? And if I answer that the want is only instrumentally good, why can't the other guy say the admiration is only instrumentally good and skip the whole distinction?

Then rank what counts against the position from fatal to merely awkward. And tell me honestly whether I've quietly made the view unfalsifiable by defining "object-given" as whatever the account needs — if I have, say so, and say whether that's a genuine cost or just the normal cost of a view at this level of abstraction. If I'm conflating two or three seperate problems under the one label, dont be polite about it — tell me which ones, and which of my three worries belongs to which. If the whole framing is confused (if there's no such thing as "the" wrong-kind problem to have a solution to), just say that instead of humoring me.
