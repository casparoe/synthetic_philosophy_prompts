Something I've been turning over. When someone born blind gets their sight restored as an adult — congenital cataracts taken out, that kind of thing — the clinics never just hand them working eyes and wave them off. There's a whole rehab protocol: weeks of drills where they look at a shape and then feel it, or feel it then look, matching the two up, and the therapists clearly treat it as learning to see shapes rather than just suddenly seeing them. The patients often can't name a square versus a triangle at first even though they've told squares from triangles by touch their whole lives.

My quesiton is what that practice — the thing baked into the rehab, that you have to train the eyes to line up with the hands — is actually committing to. Does building a rehab around "you'll learn to see shapes" mean the practice presupposes a definite answer to the old Molyneux problem (would a person blind from birth, on gaining sight, immediately recognize a cube they'd only ever felt)? In particular, does treating shape recognition as something learned commit it to a "no" — that shape info is coded separately in each sense and has to be translated across? Or could someone who thinks shape concepts are amodal and shared across the senses, with only the low-level sensory coding being modality-specific, run the exact same rehab and mean something much weaker by it — that a brand-new input channel just needs calibrating, which says nothing about Molyneux either way? Sort out for me which bits the practice strictly presupposes, which bits it merely happens to sit well with, and what would actually have to change in how we'd run the rehab if we flipped to the other answer.
