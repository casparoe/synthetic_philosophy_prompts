Bit of a weird one. I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background, but I've fallen into this long-running forum game where the whole point is that the players vote on the rules, and the rules include rules about how you vote on rules. Was supposed to be a fun time sink. Instead I keep lying awake about it.

Three things happened and I cant tell if they're the same thing or three different things wearing the same coat.

(1) Someone moved to drop the bar for amendments from two-thirds to a simple majority. It got 55%. The proposer's argument was: this motion changes the threshold, the new threshold is a simple majority, 55% is a simple majority, therefore the motion passes. So the rule that decides whether the motion passed is the rule the motion itself creates.

(2) Our rule set has a clause saying it can't be repealed. First thing anyone did, obviously, was try to repeal it.

(3) Someone noticed that our rule saying "any change is judged by the rules in force at the moment of the vote" is itself a rule, so the only thing that can ever judge a change to it is the version of it you're trying to get rid of.

I've hit a version of this at work too — we had a CI check that required two approvals before you could change any CI check.

I'm sure people have chewed on this for centuries; amendment clauses, "can God make a stone so heavy he can't lift it," that whole area. What I want is the carving, not a label. Are my three cases one phenomenon or three? Is (1) the same problem as (2), or is self-validation a genuinely different animal from a clause that just refuses? Is this just the omnipotence paradox in a suit, or is the legal/rulebook version doing something the theological one isn't? And is there a standard way out — something like "the vote is governed by the rules in force at t, the change bites at t+1, so nothing ever validates itself" — and does that actually dissolve the circle or just relabel it? If there's an established name for the family, great, but I care more about which of my three it covers and which it doesn't. No reading list please, I won't read it.

Say how confident you are in each identification, and tell me the one you're least sure about. Finish with a table: case | nearest established concept | what that concept actually covers | where it fails to fit my case | verdict (same thing / close cousin / different problem / nothing exists).
