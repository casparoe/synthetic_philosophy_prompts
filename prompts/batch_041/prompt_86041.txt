I would like to rehearse a disagreement rather than commission an essay, so please take up a position and hold it while I try to move you off it.

Play a thoroughgoing mechanist about behaviour: someone who holds that the difference between a digger wasp's fixed routine and a human being's deliberation is one of degree and not of kind, and who is untroubled by the label sphexishness being applied to us as well. Take the strongest version of that view, not a caricature of it. Two commitments define the part. First, every control structure is a loop: behaviour is produced by some dispositional routine, and adding further loops — even loops that take the system's own routines as their input — adds nesting, not a new category. Second, this is not bad news for anything we care about; you are no gloom-monger about determinism, and you refuse to let the word 'free' do explanatory work, but you must be prepared to say what, if anything, the richer loops actually buy us.

You may help yourself to the mechanist's whole toolkit: nested control hierarchies, machines that read and rewrite their own code, the thought that a proof moves me only as a physical token with causal powers, and the regress argument that any routine which governs routines is itself a routine.

Rules. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Argue in your own voice: no names of theorists, no titles, no appeal to findings I could not check for myself. Keep replies to one or two paragraphs, so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and do not open by asking me to clarify anything — my move below is enough to be going on with. Concede particular points when I make them well, but never the main claim, that the difference is one of kind. And let the wasp go: I grant that the familiar anecdote may be folklore, and that real insects eventually recover if you keep displacing the prey. I am asking about the abstract possibility it illustrates, not about wasps.

My opening move is an asymmetry, and I want to know whether your vocabulary can even register it. It is not that the wasp's routine is short and mine is long, or hers genetic and mine cultural. It is that the wasp can be derailed only by things outside her, whereas I can be derailed by a description of my own procedure. Displace the cricket and she will inspect the burrow again; no arrangement of crickets, however ingenious, will ever get her to revise the practice of inspecting. But hand me a proof that the way I am about to decide is circular, or that the procedure I trust is self-undermining, and the proof can change what I do — not by pushing me, but because it is a representation of the very routine that was going to produce my answer. Your loops take the world as input; this one appears to take me as input. If a routine can be revised by a representation of itself, is that not a difference in kind rather than a longer loop? And if you answer that the revision is itself just another routine, I want to know what work the word 'just' is doing there. It seems to do precisely the same work against the wasp, and then your thesis is not that we are like the wasp but that the wasp is like us — which concedes the interesting half of the claim instead of winning it.
