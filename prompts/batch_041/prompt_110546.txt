I am preparing a seminar on Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education and want to pair it with William Golding's Lord of the Flies — not the standard reading about the darkness of human nature, but a reading through Locke's actual educational machinery. Treat the plot as common knowledge and do not quote the text; I want interpretation, not citation. The claims I want tested are the specific ones: that habit and practice, not precept, form character, and must be laid down early; that esteem and disgrace are the mainsprings of discipline, which makes the rod both cruel and inept; that children should be reasoned with as rational creatures rather than commanded; that a tutor must study a particular child's temper and suit the method to it; and that the schoolroom corrupts precisely because a boy there is shaped by other boys rather than by one well-chosen adult. The island looks to me like Locke's system with the tutor deleted. Using concrete touchstones — the first assembly and the conch, the painted faces and the hunting dance, Piggy's glasses, Simon's death, the officer on the beach — say which scenes dramatize which claims and which quietly embarrass them. Does the island confirm Locke's warning about boys shaping one another, or show that character formation is impossible without a social order he takes for granted? And does the novel exploit the tension between Locke the anti-innatist and Locke the tutor who tells us to study a child's native temper? End by saying what the novel contributes to this debate that argument alone does not, and be explicit about where the reading strains and the novel resists it.
