Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Albert Camus's case against the death penalty, and I came away unsatisfied with what either of us could defend. Write an essay that settles the dispute, and defend a verdict rather than surveying the debate. The crux is this: Camus's abolitionism seems to run on two engines at once — a moral claim that state killing is itself the gravest kind of murder, deliberate and cold-blooded in a way no private crime can match, and a pragmatic claim that the penalty fails to deter, is often botched, and forecloses any remedy for judicial error. Determine which of these does the real work, whether the two can be held together, and what follows for the whole position from two of Camus's own moves: his proposal to replace the scaffold with a life of hard labor, which he describes as harsher than death yet reversible, and his concession that capital punishment may have been fitting in a pious age that could still offer the condemned atonement. Two test cases should carry weight. First, imagine an execution that is genuinely instantaneous and unannounced, so that no dread precedes it — does the moral argument survive, or was the wrong all along about suffering? Second, grant the strongest retributive case for death as deserved for atrocities — does the argument still go through, and if so, on what grounds? Reach a conclusion about which reconstruction of Camus's position is strongest and whether it holds together, and say plainly where you think he fails. Write in American English.
