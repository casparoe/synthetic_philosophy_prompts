I'm setting the take-home paper for my final-year Nietzsche seminar (mostly third-years, plus a couple of MA students who sit in), and I need one essay question on the eternal recurrence, together with a model answer and a marking scheme I can hand to my co-marker. The trouble is that every year the answers come out the same: they tell me about the demon who turns up in your loneliest loneliness in The Gay Science and offers you this life again and again, they mention the cosmological sketches in the notebooks, they announce that it's either a piece of metaphysics or else a thought experiment that's really a test of how you feel about your life, and then they stop. I can mark that in my sleep. What I want is a question that forces them past that fork in the road.

Here's the crux I'd like the question built around, so the question itself has to make them confront it: the thought-experiment reading seems to need the recurrence to be more than a description, since the whole point is supposedly that your attitude to it could change how you live; but the cosmological reading makes it a description of what happens anyway, in which case your affirmation is just one more link in the chain and amor fati starts to look like the most cheerful possible fatalism. So: can the doctrine do any work that a purely conditional "what if it were true" couldn't do, and can it do that work without quietly ceasing to be the doctrine? To stop them simply reciting the secondary literature, I want the second half of the question to test their answer against a variant that a tutee of mine came up with and that I've not seen anywhere: the recurrence of the pattern rather than the token — every life comes round again, but not the identical life; you return as the same sort of person in the same sort of situation, with the details shuffled. She calls it the "pattern" version. Ask them whether that variant rescues the transformative reading, or whether it rescues it by throwing away the very thing that made the original thought weigh anything at all. I have a feeling there might be something a bit like this in the ancient Stoic discussions, though I wouldn't rely on it.

So please: one essay question, doable in a 90-minute exam; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it. Make the marking scheme say plainly what seperates an A from a B — I want the B answer to be the one that gets all the labels in the right places but never notices the tension. Answer in British English, please.
