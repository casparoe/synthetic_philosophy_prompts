I'm a third-year philosophy student in an upper-level epistemology seminar, sixteen of us, one 75-minute meeting a week, and the instructor has given each student one session to run. Mine is the session on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I'd like to avoid the version everyone does, where the two arguments go on the board, the class agrees that one premise is doing all the work, and we go home. Our instructor keeps saying the two are the same problem wearing different clothes, and I want the class to feel whether that's true rather than be told it. So please design me one runnable activity for that 75 minutes — rules, materials, roles, sequence, debrief — not a discussion plan and not a lecture outline.

Three constraints come out of things I've noticed in seminar. First, give the two paradoxes a common surface: the class should be placed in a position where each person stakes something individually on each of many claims, and then, by the rules of the game, is made to stake something on the whole set at once, so that the "each one is fine, together they aren't" structure is enacted rather than recited. Second, I don't want the force to be merely logical; the social shape should show up too, so build in a role — call it whatever you like — for one participant who is forced to be the one certifying the conjunction and whose payoff is strictly worse than everyone else's no matter what the group does, so that the class can see the inconsistency has to be paid for by somebody in particular. Third, no naming: the words lottery, preface, closure, and conjunction, and the names of either argument, should not appear until the debrief. Dress it in some mundane institutional fiction rather than a bare list of propositions — an inspection, a committee, a sweepstakes, an underwriting desk, your choice — provided the fiction doesn't do the philosophical work for them. It must be low-tech: whiteboard, timer, index cards, no money, no laptops.

What I need from the write-up: the setup and materials; the rules and the sequence of phases with rough timings; the payoff or scoring scheme spelled out concretely enough that I can run the session straight from the page; then the debrief, mapping specific moments in the activity onto each paradox and onto the main escape routes — denying that each individual claim is rationally believable, denying that belief agglomerates over conjunction, or accepting that we are rationally inconsistent in a harmless way — and saying which of those the activity itself pushes the group toward and which it leaves cold. Then the failure modes you expect the first time I run it: where the group will short-circuit the structure, where the scoring will generate an unintended dominant strategy, where someone will object that the whole thing is rigged and thereby dodge the philosophy, and what I should do about each. End with an honest paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief myself. Please commit to a single design and make definite choices about mechanics, numbers, and timings rather than offering me three variants to pick between; where something is genuinely a judgment call, tell me what you'd choose and why.
