I'm in the second year of a graduate program in philosophy, and this term I've been sitting in on a seminar on philosophical method whose whole focus is thought experiments and the way they do or don't earn their conclusions. The instructor keeps insisting that an intuition pump is a piece of argumentative machinery with moving parts -- a scenario, a stipulated setup, a lever designed to move one intuition rather than another -- and that whether the machinery is well built is a separate question from whether the conclusion someone bolts onto it is true. I've been keeping a running tally of my own verdicts on particular pumps, and I've reached the point where I can no longer tell whether I have a coherent standard or just a pile of prejudices. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to, and to be honest with me about which of them I should abandon.

Here is the tally. The trolley case, where you can divert a runaway trolley from five people onto one, seems to me a legitimate pump: any theory of when killing is permissible has to say something about that choice, and the scenario does not appear to have the answer baked in. The violinist case -- you wake up surgically attached to a violinist and are told you must stay that way for nine months to keep him alive -- also seems legitimate to me, because it isolates bodily autonomy as something distinct from the right to life, and the intuition it produces is about exactly that. The experience machine, where you can plug into a life of perfectly convincing simulated satisfaction, likewise strikes me as a legitimate pump: the fact that many of us would decline is real evidence about whether we value anything beyond experienced satisfaction. By contrast, the Chinese room seems to me an illegitimate pump, because it installs a person who plainly does not understand Chinese inside the system and then invites us to judge the whole system through that person's eyes; the conclusion that the system does not understand is engineered into the setup rather than discovered by it. Mary's room, the scientist raised in black and white who finally sees red, strikes me as a legitimate pump whose conclusion is routinely oversold: I accept that the intuition of her learning something new is genuine and well elicited, but I do not think it establishes that physicalism is false, and I blame the inference rather than the scenario. And the footbridge variant, where you must push a large man off a bridge to stop the trolley, strikes me as illegitimate, because it seems to work on physical revulsion at hands-on killing rather than on any moral feature the underlying theory must track. I notice that the trolley case and the footbridge case differ only in personal contact and muscular effort, which I have just called incidental, and yet I give them opposite verdicts; I also notice that the Chinese room and the experience machine both ask me to imagine something strange and then consult a gut reaction, and yet I bless one and condemn the other. My tentative proposal is that a pump is legitimate just when the scenario is one the target theory is obliged to give a verdict on and the intuition it elicits is driven by the very feature the theory is about, and illegitimate when it secures its verdict by loading some feature incidental to the target so that the intuition is really about something else -- but I suspect this either collapses the trolley and footbridge verdicts into one or makes the experience machine illegitimate, and I can't see which way to jump.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without further help, with each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it. Please tell me which of my verdicts the best version of the principle cannot accommodate and why, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: suppose a friend proposes a pump in which neuroscientists can predict a listener's judgment that a melody is beautiful purely from the acoustic waveform, and concludes from our discomfort that beauty is in the head rather than the music. I also want to know whether the principle, as you state it, forces me to choose between keeping the trolley verdict and keeping the footbridge verdict. Please give me your reasoning in prose and then end with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing your best one-sentence statement of the principle.
