For the past few weeks a friend and I have been arguing about Hempel's ravens in a small reading group, and he has talked himself into the view that the paradox is not a curiosity about confirmation but a reductio of the very idea of inductive evidence. He is a physicist by training and likes his paradoxes to terminate in a clean absurdity; I am the one who has to teach the confirmation unit next term, so I would rather not concede the point. The trouble is that when I try to locate the mistake I find myself defending premises I would happily have written on an exam. His argument runs as follows.

(1) "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens"; the second is just the contrapositive of the first. (2) Logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by exactly the same evidence. (3) A generalization of the form "all Fs are G" is confirmed by any instance of it, that is, by any object that is both F and G; so any object that is neither black nor a raven is an instance of "all non-black things are non-ravens" and confirms it. (4) The white shoe by my front door is neither black nor a raven. (5) So by (3) the shoe confirms "all non-black things are non-ravens," and hence by (1) and (2) it confirms "all ravens are black." (6) Nothing in that reasoning turned on the object being a shoe: every object that is not a black raven does the same work — a stapler, a cloud, a red herring. (7) Take the red herring. It is neither white nor a raven, so by the same route it is an instance of "all non-white things are non-ravens" and confirms "all ravens are white"; being neither green nor a raven, it equally confirms "all ravens are green," and so on for every colour except red. (8) Assuming there are any ravens at all, "all ravens are black" and "all ravens are white" cannot both be true. (9) So one glance at a fish confirms each of two mutually incompatible hypotheses, and in fact confirms a whole family of mutually incompatible hypotheses at once. (10) Whatever "confirms" means in (3), then, it cannot be a relation that tracks evidential support, since nothing that genuinely supports a hypothesis can support its incompatible rivals at the same time; so the inference from an observed instance to a generalization carries no epistemic weight, and no observation can ever give us reason to accept a universal claim. Inductive evidence for generalizations is impossible.

I am not prepared to accept (10) — I am not going to say that a fossil gives me no reason to believe anything about dinosaurs — and I am especially reluctant to surrender (2), since the thought that whether my evidence supports a hypothesis depends on which of two logically equivalent sentences I happen to use in stating it strikes me as absurd. But I cannot find the false step on my own. Where does the argument fail? If a particular step is at fault, identify it, explain why it looked acceptable, and tell me what your diagnosis costs me elsewhere — in particular, which of (1) through (3) I would have to weaken or restrict, and what I would lose by doing so. If instead the derivation up to (9) is sound and only the final move is too quick, say so plainly, and explain what someone who wants to keep (3) should say about the red herring.
