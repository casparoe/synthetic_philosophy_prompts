I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in condensed matter — and since retiring I have been reading my way through the eighteenth century. I have lately finished Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness, and I find myself returning to a tension in it that I cannot resolve, and that I suspect is not meant to be resolved. She insists that great passions are necessary for happiness, and that we must remain susceptible to illusions — that a person who has seen through everything will not be happy. Yet she also praises the love of study as the most dependable source of happiness, and study, as I understand it, is the business of dissolving illusions. I want a poem rather than an essay because this is not a thesis to be defended but a condition to be held, and verse can hold it in a way that prose cannot.

Write me a sestina in the voice of Du Châtelet herself, addressed to a younger woman who has asked her how to be happy. Let the six end-words be: illusion, passion, study, reason, self, and time. I want the obsessive return of the same words to do the work of showing how the mind circles back to its consoling pictures even when it knows better, and I want the sense of each end-word to shift as the poem proceeds — let illusion begin as a necessary comfort and end as a self-deception that is also a kindness, and let study begin as the pursuit of truth and end as a refuge. The envoi should leave the question open rather than resolving it; it should not say that illusions are really truths, or that study is really an illusion. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the Discourse would recognize its author's predicament, and clear enough that someone who does not could still read it as a poem.
