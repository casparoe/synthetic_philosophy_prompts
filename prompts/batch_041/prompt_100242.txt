I have been turning over, on my own, a claim I keep running into: that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg — that the particular sperm and the particular egg you came from are somehow part of who you are, so that in any situation in which either had been different, the resulting person would not have been you. I find this both compelling and slippery, and I have managed to argue myself into what feels like a contradiction.

On one reading it seems nearly trivially true. A person's genetic makeup traces back to one specific sperm and one specific egg; how could a different pair yield the same person?

But then I think about identical twins. My understanding is that they begin as a single fertilized egg — one sperm, one egg — which then divides into two embryos. So the two twins trace back to the same sperm and the same egg, and yet they are unmistakably two people, with different lives and different thoughts. If one origin can produce two people, then the origin cannot be what makes each twin the particular person they are; and if it is not what makes me me, the original claim seems to lose its grip. To sharpen the puzzle, imagine the division were done deliberately in a laboratory: an egg is fertilized, the early embryo is separated into two, both are implanted, and two people are born. Now suppose that in some other situation the embryo had not been divided and only one person had been born. By the claim in question, that single person would have the same sperm and egg as each of the twins, so which twin — if either — would they be? The claim seems unable to tell us, which makes me suspect it asserts less than it appears to.

So my question is whether the original claim is simply false, refuted by the twins, or whether I have misunderstood what it is actually asserting. If it is not refuted, what exactly is it claiming, and how can two people share an origin without being the same person? I would also like to know what work, if any, the claim does outside this puzzle — for instance, whether it bears at all on the ordinary thought that a person might have been someone else, or on whether parents who choose among embryos are responsible for one person's existence rather than another's.
