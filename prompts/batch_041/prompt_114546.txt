I've been chewing on the old fight over whether history can be objective — the nineteenth-century ambition to render the past exactly as it was, set against the suspicion that any account of it is shaped by whoever tells it. I'd like that argument sent up. Write a deadpan museum wall label, around 500–600 words, for a site where a contested event happened, issued by an invented body — call it the Bureau of How It Actually Was — that certifies the text as free of interpretation. Let the humor come from the label quietly doing everything it promised not to: deciding where the story begins and ends, hiding whoever acted behind the passive voice, granting a disputed event two symmetrical "sides," and closing on a line that tells visitors what it all meant. Give it the institutional furniture of a real wall text — a title, a date range, a source note, a line of visitor guidance — and let at least one of those pieces undercut the label's own claim without anyone noticing. A reader who has followed the sceptics should find it fair rather than cheap, and a reader who hasn't should still catch what is being mocked. Invent the Bureau and its staff; don't cite real historians.
