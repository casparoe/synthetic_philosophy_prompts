So I keep getting into this argument with a friend and I want your take on it. Lemkin, the guy who actually invented the word "genocide" back in 1944, had a really broad idea of what it meant. For him it wasn't mainly about killing people -- it was about dismantling a group's whole way of life: its institutions, its language, its religion, its economy, all the stuff that makes it a group in the first place. On his picture you could commit genocide against a group without shooting a single person. Then when the UN actually sat down and wrote the Genocide Convention in 1948, they narrowed it down a lot. Now you need one of five specific acts (killing, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions of life meant to destroy the group, preventing births, forcibly transferring children out of the group), and you need the specific intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such. Political groups got dropped entirely, and the cultural stuff only kind of sneaks in sideways through a couple of those listed acts.

Here's what I want from you: compare these two conceptions properly -- the broad Lemkinian one and the narrow legal one -- and then actually commit to which one is right about what makes genocide distinctively wrong. I don't want a wishy-washy "they both capture something important" ending. Give me a verdict: which better tracks the moral horror of the thing, and which is the better concept to use in practice. This is where the politics of naming comes in, obviously -- states go out of their way to call things "ethnic cleansing" or "mass atrocities" or "crimes against humanity" precisely to dodge the g-word and the legal duties that come with it, so there's a real question about whether the narrower legal category is doing us any favors even if it's more defensible in a courtroom.

To test both conceptions, run them against a couple hard cases. Say a state that never massacres anyone but methodically stamps out a minority's language, bans its religious practice and forcibly assimilates its kids into the majority. Or a campaign that tries to annihilate a political movement -- a group of people bound by ideology rather than ethnicity. Does each conception handle these the way you'd want, and does that change your verdict? Also worth weighing: is there something valuable about the legal definition being *narrower* than the moral one -- like the special stigma of the word only works if it's not stretched to cover everything bad? Waht do you think the tradeoff there actually is.
