I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a single decision. My protagonist has spent her life administering a large philanthropic fortune, and in the novel's central scene she must decide whether to commit the last of it to an intervention whose near-term effects are documented and whose long-term effects no one can trace. I have read enough moral philosophy to know that this difficulty has a name — I have seen it discussed under the heading of cluelessness — and enough of it to suspect that most of the arguments I find are about whether the difficulty is fatal, rather than about what it actually is. What I want from you is an essay, not a survey: take a position on the controversy and defend it.

To keep you off the well-worn track, here are the questions I have not been able to settle from my notes.

First, a distinction I keep needing and have not seen drawn cleanly. Call the first kind of downstream effect a ripple: an effect that propagates through an identifiable mechanism — a donation that changes what a ministry believes it can afford, a kindness that shifts a local norm, a precedent that is later cited — so that even though no one has in fact traced it, an observer with enough information could in principle have estimated its direction and rough size. Call the second kind shrapnel: effects that scatter through chaotic, nonlinear systems, where arbitrarily small differences in initial conditions produce arbitrarily large differences later, so that the act's contribution to the far future is not merely unknown but arguably not well-defined at all. A consequentialist who cannot see her ripples has an epistemic problem. A consequentialist who cannot see her shrapnel may have a different problem, or none at all. Tell me which, and why the distinction either does or does not survive scrutiny.

Second, the leveling objection, which I suspect is the real crux. If shrapnel is everywhere, then every act — funding a vaccine, buying a coffee, staying in bed — is followed by an unbounded spray of far-future consequences that no one could have predicted. That seems to make all available options equally clueless. Does cluelessness then become practically inert, because it cannot discriminate among options and so cannot tell my protagonist anything? Or does the leveling instead show that consequentialism cannot guide action at all? These are different diagnoses with different consequences, and I want you to commit to one.

Third, the cancellation move: the suggestion that because we have no reason to expect the unknowable far-future effects of an act to run systematically good or bad, we may treat their expected value as zero and let the knowable near-term effects decide. Say whether that move is defensible. Consider at least two pressures on it: that the distribution of far-future impact may be heavy-tailed, so that an absence of systematic direction does not entail a zero expectation; and that some acts may correlate with outcomes in ways we cannot articulate but have no business ignoring.

Fourth, the scope question. Is this a problem for consequentialism in particular, or for any decision procedure that must act under uncertainty? And does your answer change if consequentialism is taken as a criterion of right action rather than as a decision procedure for agents? My protagonist is not a philosopher and must decide by Tuesday, so the decision-procedure question is the one I care about, but I do not want it confused with the criterion question.

Then reach a verdict. It may be that cluelessness leaves consequentialism standing as a criterion while hollowing it out as a guide; it may be that the leveling objection dissolves the problem; it may be something I have not imagined. I do not need the verdict to be comforting, only earned.

One requirement throughout: state how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and identify the single point you are least sure of, with a reason. I would rather have a modest conclusion that knows its own weak point than a confident one that does not.
