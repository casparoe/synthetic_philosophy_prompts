You are helping me prepare for the question period after a talk I am giving to a philosophy of mind reading group next month. My thesis is a strong reading of Douglas Hofstadter's suggestion, in I Am a Strange Loop, that a self is a self-referential pattern rather than a physical object, and that because we weave models of other people into our own patterns, a self can survive in diluted form inside the brains of others. I want to push this further than Hofstadter does. I claim that an I is not merely echoed or remembered in another brain; it can be genuinely distributed across several brains at once.

My central move is to define what it is for a self to be present in a brain. I propose that a self, S, is present in a brain, B, to the degree that B's dispositions to attend, judge, feel, and act would, across a wide range of counterfactual situations, track what S would have attended to, judged, felt, and done. On this account the self is a tracking profile, not a token system, so a single I can be realized by more than one brain jointly, and presence comes in degrees rather than all or nothing.

The group is about a dozen people and splits into three clusters. First, two hard-nosed functionalists who happily accept multiple realizability but insist on causal continuity and on the token identity of whatever system implements a mind; they will say I have confused a type with a token, and that two brains running the same profile are two selves, or at best one self and one copy. Second, a couple of scholars working on Buddhist no-self, who will press that my account is anatta in a new coat of paint, and that a profile is never a bearer of anything, so nothing in my picture can own an experience. Third, a phenomenologist and a graduate student in embodied and enactive cognition, who care about first-personal givenness and mineness, and who will insist that no one has ever experienced being distributed.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the six to eight objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way the relevant cluster would put it rather than the way I would. For each, provide a two-minute spoken reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold firm on, especially on whether my account can carry the moral and prudential weight we expect of selves, such as blame, grief, survival, and anticipation. I have roughly fifteen minutes for questions, so weight the list accordingly. Close with the one objection I am hoping nobody asks. Write in American English, in a formal register.
