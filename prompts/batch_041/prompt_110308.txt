I'm trying to pin down Augustine's definition of a lie and I keep getting tangled in the opening chapters of On Lying. Here is the passage I'm stuck on, in the old translation I have:

"Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

A few lines further on he adds that "the fault of him who lies, is, the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind," and immediately before the passage above he sets jokes aside on the ground that the joker's tone and manner give "a most evident indication that he means no deceit," so that jokes "have never been accounted lies."

My difficulty is that these two markers come apart, and I can't tell which one is doing the real work. The double-heart test looks purely inward -- it asks only whether the speaker utters what he takes to be false -- and by that test the whopper I tell at a party about having once wrestled a bear, told with a grin that shows I mean no deceit, would still be a lie; conversely the test says nothing about the man who utters something he sincerely believes true while deliberately arranging his words so his hearer draws a false conclusion, as when a wife tells the doctor, truthfully, "he never drinks at home," intending the doctor to conclude her husband has reformed. The deception test reverses both verdicts: it excuses the joker and convicts the misleading speaker. Which of the two is the criterion Augustine is actually relying on, and is there a single statement of his definition that yields a stable verdict on both cases without simply collapsing into one of these tests?
