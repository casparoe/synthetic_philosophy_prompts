In "Why Socialism?," his 1949 essay for the first issue of Monthly Review, Einstein argues that the root of the evils of his time is the economic anarchy of capitalist society. The profit motive, joined to competition among the owners of capital, produces recurring cycles of boom and depression, wastes labor, and rewards selfishness at the expense of cooperation; it cripples individuals by reducing education to an instrument of career advancement and starving the impulse to creative work; and it concentrates capital in a few hands, giving private owners effective control of the press, radio, and schools, so that ordinary citizens find it nearly impossible to reach objective conclusions about their own political interests. The remedy he proposes is a socialist economy in which the means of production are owned by society and directed by a plan that adjusts production to the needs of the community, together with an educational system oriented toward social goals — though he cautions at the close that a planned economy is not yet socialism, that it may be accompanied by an all-powerful bureaucracy and the complete enslavement of the individual, and that protecting individual rights against such a bureaucracy remains an unsolved problem.

Please argue that Einstein's diagnosis and his remedy cannot be held together: that the two evils he treats as most serious — the stunting of the individual and the capture of public opinion by concentrated power — are not cured, and may well be intensified, by the economic order he recommends, and that his closing caveat concedes the difficulty rather than answering it. Build the argument from premises Einstein himself accepts, rather than from the standard objection that a central plan cannot make use of dispersed economic knowledge.
