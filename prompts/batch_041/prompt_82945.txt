I sit on a heritage committee that has spent three meetings on whether to take down a Victorian statue and what, if anything, should replace it. Every meeting ends with the same one-liner — 'you can't erase history' — and it has been made to do at least four different jobs: to remind us that the past happened and no vote can undo it; to warn that removal destroys part of the surviving record; to predict that future generations simply will not know what the man did; and to insist that removal would amount to a moral verdict on the past, which we have no standing to deliver. Give me a menu rather than a defence of either side: the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, from the trivial to the radical, a verdict on each, and a note on which reading is doing the work in each of those four uses. Set it out as a list I can circulate to the committee.

Two wrinkles I would like you to build in. One member says a monument does not assert anything but presupposes something — that a statue in the square does not so much state a claim about the past as set the background against which the town asks its questions, so that taking it down would change what is easy to think rather than what is known. Is that a genuinely distinct reading, and does it make the slogan true or false? Another member proposes keeping the statue and adding an explanatory plaque, and the committee cannot agree whether that answers the objection or concedes it. Say which readings the plaque satisfies and which it cannot touch. Finish by telling me which single reading I should assume when someone offers the line and declines to elaborate.
