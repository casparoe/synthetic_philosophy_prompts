A colleague defends the Church–Turing thesis by way of an analogy I keep running into in textbooks and seminars: a Turing machine, she says, is nothing more than an idealization of a person doing a long calculation by hand — a clerk with a pencil, a fixed rule-book, and an unlimited supply of paper — so the claim that every effectively computable function is computable by a Turing machine is not a stipulation about the word "computable" but a substantive truth about what mechanical symbol-manipulation can achieve, and a truth we have independent reason to accept, since we know from the inside what it is to follow a rule.

I would like the analogy audited, not the thesis defended. Set out the correspondence: what plays the role of the paper, the pencil, the clerk, the rule-book, the clerk's finite span of attention. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, which features of the source — the unlimited paper, the perfect reliability, the absence of insight or Gestalt recognition, the discreteness of the symbols — are quietly imported into the target rather than earned, and which of the resulting disanalogies actually matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do. Finally, does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it all along — and if a better analogy is available for the thesis's support, what is it?
