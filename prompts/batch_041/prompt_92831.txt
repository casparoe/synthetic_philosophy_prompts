After thirty-five years in experimental physics I have retired and, to the mild alarm of my former colleagues, taken up philosophy of mind. I have been reading about the color phi phenomenon — two dots flashed in quick succession in different colors, where observers report seeing the first dot change color in mid-flight — and about the two ways one might explain it. On the "Orwellian" account, the brain first produces an experience of the dot in its original color and then, once the second dot has been presented, revises the experience (or the memory of it) so that the change seems to have occurred earlier; on the "Stalinesque" account, the brain withholds the experience until all the information is in and then stages a single coherent version of events. Both are said to involve "filling in" the color change.

My difficulty is this. It seems to me that the phenomenon demonstrates the Orwellian account — the experience of the change is caused by a stimulus that arrives after the experience it revises — and if that is right, then the timing of experience comes apart from the timing of the neural events that produce it, which amounts to a kind of retrocausation at the level of consciousness. Having spent a career on the causal structure of the world, I cannot see how to accept that without giving up on physicalism. I even sketched an experiment to confirm it: have subjects signal the color of the dot at the midpoint of its apparent path, and compare their reports with the stimulus timing.

Before I embarrass myself by writing this up, I would like to know where my reasoning has gone wrong. Does the color phi phenomenon really tell us which of the two accounts is correct, or does it leave the question open? Is there a fact of the matter about when the color change entered the observer's experience — and if the two accounts are empirically equivalent, what should a physicalist conclude about the very idea of "the timing of experience"?
