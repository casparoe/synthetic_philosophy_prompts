I'm part of a small reading group that has spent the better part of a year working through the literature on the repugnant conclusion — the result that any view ranking outcomes by total welfare seems committed to preferring an enormous population whose lives are barely worth living over a much smaller population living very well — and through the long parade of attempted repairs: averaging welfare rather than summing it, setting a critical level below which additional lives contribute nothing, discounting the value of extra people, giving priority to the worse off, and person-affecting views that count only people who exist or will exist. Every repair seems to generate a counterexample of its own, and the group has split cleanly: some of us think the repugnant conclusion is a bullet we simply have to bite, others think it shows the whole framework is broken, and nobody has moved in months. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about which outcomes are in fact better, a verbal one that turns on what "better" could even mean when the populations being compared differ in size, two groups answering different questions under a single heading — say, which outcome is better versus what we are permitted to do in bringing people into existence — or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Engage the positions concretely, and tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is.

One hypothesis I want you to take seriously, though I may be misremembering where I encountered it, is that the impasse is neither a failure of cleverness nor a standoff of brute intuition but a matter of over-constraint. My impression is that there are formal results showing that no way of assigning value to populations can simultaneously satisfy a short list of conditions that each seem almost too obvious to deny — that betterness is transitive, that adding a life that is clearly worth living cannot make an outcome worse, that a population is not automatically better merely for being larger, that we cannot be required to prefer an enormous number of barely-worth-living lives to a small number of flourishing ones, and several more of the same character. If something in that vicinity is correct, then the debate may be stalled because the question as posed has no answer, and the productive move would be to ask which condition to surrender rather than to keep hunting for the missing theory. Assess that hypothesis honestly: if it misdescribes the state of play, or if the formal results I half-remember do not actually bear the diagnostic weight I am putting on them, say so rather than accommodating me. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change in the question — or argue that none should be expected and explain why.

Throughout, keep separate what is genuinely settled in this literature from what is actively contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Where the answer depends on a judgment call rather than a result, flag it as such. Please write in American English.
