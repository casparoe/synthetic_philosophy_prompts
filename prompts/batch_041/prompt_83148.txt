I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time now, and I've accumulated a set of views about coherence arguments for expected utility maximization that I've never tried to lay out all at once. I'd like an audit before I inflict them on anybody else. Take the role of a philosopher of decision theory — someone fluent both in the formal literature and in the informal, blog-level way these arguments usually get presented — and think carefully rather than quickly; I'd rather have a slow, careful answer than a sweeping one. Start by restating each commitment in your own words and mapping which ones presuppose or imply which, so that I can see the structure you're working with before you start adjudicating.

Here is where I've landed.

(1) A rational agent's credences have to be probabilities. The Dutch book argument is what shows this, and it's the main reason I'm a Bayesian.
(2) A rational agent's preferences have to be transitive and complete enough to be representable by a real-valued utility function; the standard representation theorems are what make expected utility maximization the correct theory of instrumental rationality rather than a mere bookkeeping convention.
(3) Completeness is not a requirement of rationality. Two options can be genuinely incommensurable, and when neither is better than the other, choosing arbitrarily between them is rational — and so is declining to choose at all.
(4) Money-pump arguments are decisive. I take the familiar pump against intransitive preferences seriously, and I take the sequential versions, the ones that convert an incomplete preference into a guaranteed loss, to be genuine refutations too.
(5) Expected utility maximization is a substantive and revisable theory of choice, not a tautology about what "rational" means. Coherence arguments on their own establish much less than they're usually claimed to.
(6) It's rational to turn down a St. Petersburg-style gamble, and to ignore Pascal's-mugging offers, even when the expected value is infinite.

I have a nagging sense that something in that list doesn't hang together, but I honestly can't tell whether I'm looking at a real conflict or just at the usual looseness of blog-level decision theory. So: tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For each one, say what exactly has to give, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs me elsewhere — and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I don't want to be told that everything is fine, and I don't want everything flagged either; the discriminating work is the point.

One request about how to pitch the answer. Some of this is settled among people who actually work on it and some of it is very much not, and I'd like those kept apart explicitly. Where a view is contested, say that it's contested and by whom, in general terms; don't present a live disagreement as though it were the consensus. If part of what looks like a tension is really an ambiguity in how I've stated one of the items, say so and give me the two readings. I'd rather have a shorter answer that marks its own uncertainty than a confident one that papers over it.
