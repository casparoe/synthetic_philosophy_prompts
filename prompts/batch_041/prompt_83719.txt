I am teaching a single seventy-five-minute session on heritability to a graduate seminar in education policy. The students can read a regression table but have no genetics; most arrive with one of two reflexes — that a high heritability estimate settles whether an intervention can work, or that the heritability literature is a pseudoscientific way of naturalizing inequality — and by the end they need to be able to say precisely what a heritability coefficient is a coefficient of. Do not explain heritability to me. Plan how I should teach it.

Lay out the order of the ideas with the dependencies made explicit: what must be in place before the word can even be defined for them, what can wait, and which single step, if rushed, will make everything after it sound like genetic determinism no matter how carefully I phrase it. Choose the one example that carries the whole session — it should be a case in which the trait is highly heritable and yet the population mean has moved a great deal when the environment changed — and defend that choice against the three I keep circling and rejecting: twin studies of cognitive ability (about which they will have opinions before I open my mouth), height (which some will have met), and a plant-breeding demonstration (which they will find irrelevant). Say what the carrying example must have and what it must lack.

Then give me the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface, and mark the exact point in the sequence at which I head each one off. I am expecting 'heritable means genetically caused', 'heritable means unchangeable', and 'a heritability of zero means the environment is what matters'; tell me the ones I am not expecting. Two ideas in particular have to land as obvious rather than paradoxical, and I want you to say where in the order they belong: that a trait can be highly heritable in a population and still be moved a great deal by changing the environment, and that a heritability estimate is a property of a particular population across a particular range of environments and does not travel to other populations. I also want them to see why equalizing environments can raise a heritability estimate rather than lower it, and why heritability within a group tells us nothing about the causes of differences between groups.

Tell me what I should openly decline to take on in seventy-five minutes, and say so in the room. End with one question to put to the room that would show me whether they have the concept rather than the slogan, and describe what a wrong answer to it would look like. Give me one committed plan with your reasons, not a menu of options.
