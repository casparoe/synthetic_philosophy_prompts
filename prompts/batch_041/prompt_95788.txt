You are a philosopher of technology whose work sits at the intersection of the economics of delegation and contemporary AI safety. I'd like you to help me audit an analogy, and I'd ask you to brainstorm before you commit to a verdict, rather than reaching for a tidy answer.

A colleague in my reading group argues roughly this: worrying about agentic AI is a category error, because we already have centuries of experience managing powerful, opaque, self-interested agents — we call them contractors and firms. When I hire a builder I cannot inspect her mind, cannot be sure her interests match mine, and cannot write a contract covering every contingency. What I can do is structure incentives, monitor outputs, withhold payment, sue, and fire her. We have scaled exactly this arrangement up to organizations vastly more powerful than any individual, and civilization has not collapsed. So the safety problem for agent AI is a management problem — principal–agent design, monitoring, liability, credible exit — and management is a solved-enough problem. The tool-AI camp, on his telling, resembles someone who refuses ever to delegate, insisting on doing everything by hand with a hammer because a hammer cannot betray you.

Take that seriously, then take it apart. Begin by laying out the correspondence as a table: what plays the role of principal, agent, contract, incentive scheme, monitoring technology, sanction, exit option, shared background assumptions, and the competitive environment the whole arrangement sits inside. Include, as candidate rows, the things you might think have no counterpart on the AI side at all.

Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in whether the following survive translation: the contractor's legal personality and exposure to liability; the state's monopoly on force standing behind the contract; the fact that she cannot copy herself, cannot rewrite her own preferences mid-job, cannot run a thousand times faster than her principal, and needs her principal roughly as much as he needs her; the shared language, culture, and common sense that let two parties interpret an ambiguous clause the same way; and the fact that markets select contractors for competence rather than for willingness to forgo certain catastrophic options. Say explicitly where specification gaming and corrigibility concerns land in the mapping — whether they appear as ordinary contractual incompleteness or as something the analogy has no room for.

Finally, separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the conclusion from the ones that are merely colorful, and give a verdict on whether agent AI really is just a management problem. If the analogy cannot carry that conclusion, propose a better one and say what work it does. And since the tool-AI side of the comparison — the hammer — is doing the same kind of analogical work in reverse, tell me what it gets right and what it gets wrong. Argue from the concepts; I don't need a literature review or citations.
