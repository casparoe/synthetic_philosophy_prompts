I trained as a marine biologist and spent most of my working life on fish stock assessment; I retired six years ago and have since been reading philosophy in a fairly disorganised way, mostly bioethics and the philosophy of medicine. What follows is a question I have not been able to answer from the literature, and I would rather ask it properly than guess.

In my old field there is a well-known idea, the shifting baseline syndrome. Each cohort of researchers treats the depleted stock it first measured as the natural reference point, so a long decline becomes invisible and every generation's sense of what is normal is a little worse than the last. I began reading the enhancement literature expecting to find the same idea in medical dress, and it is close, but the difference seems to me to matter. In the ecological case the baseline is misremembered. In the medical cases I have in mind, the baseline is moved by the very intervention that is supposed to restore people to it. Three instances:

1. A condition defined by position in a distribution. If shortness is defined as falling below the third percentile, then no amount of treating the short can reduce the number of people who are short. Treatment changes who occupies the tail and leaves the category exactly as full as before.

2. A threshold on a continuous measure. When a cut-off for cholesterol, blood pressure, or blood sugar is revised downwards, people who were healthy under the old rule become patients under the new one, though nothing about them has changed.

3. A correction that becomes a requirement. Once glasses, hearing aids, or an ordinary drug are cheap and universally used, a capacity that was a normal feature of middle age is reclassified as a deficit, and the person who declines the correction becomes the deficient one.

The common structure, as I see it, is that the standard is defined relative to what people can do, and what people can do is increasingly defined by the intervention, so the line between treating a defect and improving a healthy person has no fixed point, and the more successful the intervention, the more people end up on the wrong side of it.

Three things I have found are near misses. The ecological concept is about observers forgetting, not about a norm that is endogenous to the population being measured. The familiar complaint that medicine keeps annexing ordinary life concerns categories multiplying, not thresholds being relative. The arms-race objection in the enhancement debates concerns a purely comparative loss, in which nobody is called ill, only outcompeted. In my cases the norm is statistical and the label is categorical and institutional.

Has this been given a name? If it has, I want to know what it is called and, more usefully, what it covers: whether the distribution-relative case and the threshold case are one phenomenon or two, and where the edges are. If it has not, I would like a term and a definition with boundary cases, including what merely resembles the pattern without being it. And I would like to know what follows from it. Does it show only that the line between treatment and enhancement is vague and permanently contested, or that no principled line can be drawn at all? I take those to be different conclusions, and I cannot tell which the phenomenon supports.
