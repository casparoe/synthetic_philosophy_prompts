"Typogenetics is Hofstadter's stripped-down artificial genetics. Strands are strings over the four letters A, C, G, T. Enzymes are chains of amino acids, and each amino acid codes for one typographical operation — cut the strand, delete a base, switch two neighbouring bases, insert a base, copy the strand, slide along the strand until it hits a base of a specified kind. A strand is translated into an enzyme the way DNA is translated into protein, and the enzyme then binds to a strand and carries out its operations in order. So a strand can build the enzyme that then rewrites it, and Hofstadter's puzzle is to find a strand that, through the enzymes it encodes, reproduces itself. What lifts this above a puzzle is the DNA–Gödel isomorphism. DNA is at once a physical molecule that chemistry pushes around and a code that specifies a message; a Gödel number is at once an arithmetical object that arithmetic pushes around and a statement about arithmetic. In both cases one and the same item is both a thing and a description of a thing, and that double role is what lets the system turn back on itself. Hofstadter's conclusion is that self-replication is a special case of self-reference: an organism copies itself only because its DNA can describe itself, so the possibility of life follows from the same theorem that gives us Gödel sentences. And since typogenetics is a formal system in which self-replicating strands can exist, he concludes that the mind, which he treats as a self-replicating pattern, is a formal system too — which is why he thinks artificial intelligence is possible in principle. The reason DNA is such a good analogue of a Gödel numbering, incidentally, is just that both are codes: arbitrary symbols standing for something else."
