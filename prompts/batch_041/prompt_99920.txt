For most of my adult life I took it for granted that to say what one means, plainly and without decoration, is simply what honesty requires, and that the softeners and evasions with which people dress up their speech are at best harmless weaknesses. Two experiences in the past year have left that assumption in pieces. I have been working in a Dutch office, where colleagues I have come to trust tell me to my face that a draft is weak or that a proposal will not be funded, and I have slowly come to hear these remarks not as hostility but as a form of respect I had not known I was missing. In the same period I have been in close correspondence with a friend in Japan, whose refusals arrive dressed as practical difficulties and whose disagreements arrive dressed as agreement, and I have come to see that her manner of speaking is not dishonest but exact, once one knows how to read it. What has turned this from an idle observation into something that keeps me awake is a small incident. A close friend asked me, a few days after a diagnosis that I happened to know was serious, whether I thought she would be all right, and I said yes.

I want to hear the argument I have been unable to have with myself, in the form of a dialogue between the two people whose habits of speech I have been living between.

Write a dialogue between two speakers, each of whom argues one of the positions I sketch below, and neither of whom is a fool. The first speaker holds that plain speech is the default form of respect: that indirection which obliges the hearer to decode what was meant shifts labour and risk onto the hearer, who may not know the code and cannot decline the task; that the white lie is wrong chiefly because it is paternalistic, presuming the hearer cannot bear or make use of the truth; and that a world in which reassurance cannot be trusted degrades the shared epistemic ground on which everyone, including the person being comforted, depends. The second speaker holds that speech is a relational act before it is a transmission of information: that a true statement can do damage no quantity of information-value repays, and that the duty to preserve another's standing and dignity can outweigh the duty to state; that much of what the first speaker calls lying is not lying at all but a conventional token whose meaning is fixed by the practice in which it occurs, as the reply that one is well is fixed by the ritual of greeting; and that the first speaker's insistence on the literal is itself a local custom wearing the costume of a universal ethic.

Both speakers should be as intelligent and as well informed as the other. They should answer each other's actual points, concede where the other has landed a blow and press where the other has not, rather than trading positions at cross-purposes.

Have them work through the following, in whatever order the argument takes them:

- the difference between the ritual reply (the "I am well" that no one takes as a report), the protective falsehood told to spare a feeling, and the outright deception told to avoid a consequence, and whether the three stand or fall together;
- whether the indirect speaker who fully intends to be understood as refusing has lied or merely misled, and what that distinction is worth;
- the thought that every manner of speaking shifts a burden somewhere — the plain speaker shifts the emotional burden onto the hearer, the indirect speaker shifts the interpretive burden onto the hearer — and whether that framing settles anything or merely relocates the question;
- whether Dutch directness is better described as truthfulness or as one more convention of candour, and whether the same question can be put to the Japanese manner without insulting either;
- my friend's question and my answer, on which they should return a verdict: was I kind, was I cowardly, or is the question itself malformed?

End the dialogue with each speaker naming the single strongest point the other made that they cannot fully answer, and saying what it would cost them to accept it.
