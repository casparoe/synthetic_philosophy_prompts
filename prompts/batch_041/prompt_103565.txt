You are a philosopher and historian of medieval philosophy, equally at home in fourteenth-century scholastic action theory — above all the ethics and psychology of John Buridan — and in contemporary decision theory, social choice, and the design of tie-breaking rules in allocation systems. You are precise about the difference between what a historical author is on record as holding and what you are inferring on his behalf, and you never claim more certainty than you have.

I want you to predict Buridan's views on a family of problems that arose only long after his death. Do not write a survey of what has been said about Buridan's ass; I want a reasoned reconstruction of what Buridan himself would have thought about the following modern situation, argued from his actual commitments.

Take the following as given. They are descriptions of modern practice, not anything Buridan knew:

- Many modern allocation systems must rank people — applicants to oversubscribed schools, patients awaiting an organ, candidates for a single slot — and are designed so that whenever two people are ranked equally by every relevant criterion, the tie is broken by a lottery or by a pseudo-random number generated inside the algorithm. This is not treated as a failure; it is intended, and defended as the fair response to a genuine tie.
- Contemporary decision theory typically treats indifference as harmless: if two options are genuinely equal in value, any choice between them, including an arbitrary or random one, counts as rational, and later regret over which was picked is treated as an error. Some versions go further and treat a stable, pre-committed tie-breaking rule as the rational agent's way of being decisive.
- Economists and computer scientists sometimes distinguish a tie from an incomplete preference: cases where the agent has no attitude at all between two options, so that no ranking exists to break. Sequential settings are said to expose the problem, because a choice must be made before the missing preference appears, and it cannot be unmade.

Work through the following, in order:

1. Reconstruct the commitments that constrain the answer: what Buridan held about the will and the intellect, about choice among means, about what happens when two courses are judged equal, and about whether a choice is possible there at all. State plainly which of these you take to be securely his and which you are inferring.
2. Would he accept the modern verdict that choosing arbitrarily among equal options is rational? Or does a lottery merely relocate the decision to a higher level — the choice of a rule — rather than resolve it at the level of the will?
3. If an agent adopts a standing policy of breaking ties by lot, has it thereby given itself a preference, for decisiveness or order or fairness, and so escaped the tie? What does that do to Buridan's suspension of judgment?
4. How does he handle irreversible choices, where suspension until circumstances change is unavailable? Does this force an amendment, and what is the smallest amendment that would save the view?
5. Which modern assumption is he most likely to reject outright, and which of his own claims is a modern reader most likely to find untenable?

Two things to get right along the way. The donkey illustration is a later satirical addition rather than an example Buridan himself put forward, so do not treat it as his own. And Buridan wrote as a master of arts rather than a theologian, so keep theological appeals out of the reconstruction.

Before drafting, begin with a short scratch section in which you list the candidate commitments and set aside the ones you cannot support. Then give a structured answer under these headings: Reconstruction, Prediction, The strongest objection to my prediction, and Where I am least confident. Do not supply citations, reading lists, or references; reason from the ideas themselves. Where you need a concept Buridan lacked, say so, and use only what I have supplied above plus what he could have framed in his own vocabulary.
