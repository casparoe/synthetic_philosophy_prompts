Lets do the 2020 Democratic presidential primary through Arrow's impossibility theorem, and I want you to be honest about where the fit breaks.

Setup, so we're on the same page and you dont have to reconstruct it: big field, no ranked ballot, plurality in most states with a 15% viability threshold for delegates, and the sequence everyone remembers -- a moderation lane that split four ways, then collapsed when Buttigieg and Klobuchar dropped out right after South Carolina and backed Biden, which is the standard explanation for his Super Tuesday sweep. Warren stayed in past Super Tuesday and kept taking votes that Sanders people insist were theirs. The lazy version of this is "spoilers and vote splitting." I want the real mapping.

So: who are the players, what are the alternatives, what are the preference profiles? Which of Arrow's conditions are actually doing work here -- and I'm especially interested in unrestricted domain, because if primary voters' preferences are close to single-peaked on one left-right axis, you get a median-voter result and the paradox never fires. So is the chaos evidence of genuinely multidimensional preferences (ideology vs. perceived electability), or is it the plurality rule, or the calendar, or the delegate threshold, or something that isn't the aggregation rule at all? Same for IIA: name the irrelevant alternative whose entry or exit flipped the social ordering, and then say whether it's "irrelevant" in Arrow's technical sense or whether people are just waving the theorem at a coordination problem.

Then the other direction. Arrow assumes a fixed set of alternatives, fixed voters, sincere preferences, and a single aggregation. The primary had strategic exits, an endogenous candidate set, sequential voting, and rules that are part of the game. Tell me what the theorem genuinely explains about this episode, what it can't touch, and whether the episode is evidence for Arrow's pessimism or just evidence of a badly designed mechanism. Also: when Buttigieg and Klobuchar dropped and endorsed, did voters change preferences, did the same preferences get represented differently, or was it a coordination device revealing what was there all along -- and does that distinction matter to any of the formal results, or only to pundits?

One more, and this is the part I actually care about: was there plausibly a Condorcet winner in that field, and does the question even make sense under a sequential calendar with strategic dropout? Finish by saying what the theorem can't give you -- it says no rule satisfies everything, not which rule to pick -- and whether that's a reason to think ranked-choice voting would have helped the Democrats or a reason to think the theorem gets misused whenever someone brings it up.
