I'm teaching a seminar on how writers handle countries that aren't theirs, and this term we're doing The Quiet American. What I want out of you is a reading of the novel through the question of national character -- whether that's even a coherent thing to talk about at all, and whether it's ever OK to generalize about a people.

Whats eating me is this. The book seems built to warn you off generalizing. Pyle is the man with the York Harding books, the one who knows what Asia needs, and he's the one whose Third Force ends up with a bicycle bomb going off in a street full of civilians. The moral looks like: stop theorizing about a place and look at it. But the narration is a machine for turning out types -- the American, the French, the Englishman who won't take sides until he does -- and Phuong is barely a person on the page, she's a few gestures and a silence that two men hand back and forth. So the novel is doing the exact thing it's diagnosing. Does it know it's doing it? And is there a way of generalizing that the book is actually defending -- Fowler's cynical kind -- against Pyle's? Take a position instead of telling me both men are flawed.

Then I want the philosophy done properly, not just stereotypes are bad. When is a statement about a people a summary of facts, and when is it a causal claim about what makes them that way? Is the nation even the right unit to generalize over? Every claim about Americans is true of some set of Americans, so what fixes the reference class? And what about the cases where the generalization makes itself true -- Benedict wrote her book on Japan without ever going there, out of films and interviews with Japanese emigrants, and then Japanese writers used it to build a whole genre about their own special character. If a description of a people can reshape the people, in what sense was it true when it was written? Thats the live question for me and I think the novel has a version of it too.

Point me at two or three scenes to put in front of the class, and be honest about where the reading breaks down and the book resists me. And say what the novel gives this argument that a philosophy paper can't.
