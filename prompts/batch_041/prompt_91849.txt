For the better part of a year, three of us have been circling the same question — why do promises bind? — and I would like to stop arguing about it and see it staged instead. One friend holds what I'll call the will account: a promise binds because the promisor has exercised a power over her own future and made herself the author of her own constraint, so nothing outside her will — no sale, no transfer, no third party's convenience — can change what she owes, and an undertaking she was never free to refuse was never a promise in the first place. The second friend holds the reliance account: the point of promising is to give someone else something to stand on, so the obligation answers to the promisee's reliance and expectation, and the benefit of a promise may pass to whoever is now entitled to rely on it while the burden stays exactly where it was. I have drifted toward a third and duller view, which I've seen traced to Hume and restated in the last century as the 'practice conception' of promising: that promising is a practice rather than a private act of will, and that what binds is whatever the practice says binds — its rules about who may promise, what counts as promising, and who may release — so that if the practice is rewritten, the obligations are rewritten with it. Neither friend will accept that, and I can't defend it against them in the abstract.

So write me a short story that stages the disagreement instead of settling it by narration. Set it in a small harbour town in the 1890s, where a new office called the Registry of Undertakings has just opened. For a fee the Registry records promises, issues certificates, sells to third parties the right to collect on a recorded promise, discharges a promise on the promisee's signed instruction, and will even 'naturalize' undertakings made before it existed — but it records only promises given for value, and a bare, gratuitous promise is outside its jurisdiction altogether. My protagonist is a boatbuilder whose yard burns in the third act and who has time to finish exactly one hull: the one he promised a shipping firm for a fee (recorded, and since sold on to a speculator who has never met him), or the one he promised the son of a drowned friend for nothing at all, which the Registry will not touch and which the son has already rearranged his life around. The Registry's clerk is a believer in the practice and cannot see what the fuss is about; the son, short of money, tries to sell his 'right' to the speculator and is told there is nothing to sell; the speculator's agent is decent and embarrassed by his own position. Keep it to roughly 1,200–1,800 words, and obey three constraints. First, no character may lecture or name the three accounts — the disagreement has to be visible in what people demand, offer, refuse, and forgive. Second, include at least one scene in which the will, reliance, and practice accounts each predict a different answer to the question 'what does the boatbuilder owe, and to whom?', and let the plot turn on the fact that the gratuitous promise is the one everyone agrees matters most and the one the practice cannot see. Third, end on the town's own uneasy settlement without telling me it is the right one. Then, after the story, add a note of no more than three sentences saying which of the three accounts the ending in fact vindicates and where each of the other two is left exposed, and give the last line in exactly this form: \answer{will}, \answer{reliance}, \answer{practice}, or \answer{none}, if you judge the story shows all three to be incomplete.
