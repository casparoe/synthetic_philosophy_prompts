Write a satirical job advertisement for a fictional residential care provider that has undertaken to honor each resident's advance directive exactly as written, whatever the resident now says or seems to want. The piece should run 600–700 words and hold a perfectly straight face: no authorial nudges, no winking asides, no signal that the author finds any of it absurd. The comedy must come from the substance of the job — a reader who works on advance directives and personal identity should find it accurate and fair, while a reader who does not should still be able to follow what is happening.

Present it in the deadpan sections of a real corporate posting — about the role, key responsibilities, what you will bring, what we offer, how to apply — and give the position an innocuous internal title of the kind an HR department would invent, something on the order of "Continuity Adjudicator" or "Directive Liaison." The satire lies in the gap between the flat administrative register and what the responsibilities, read literally, demand. The posting should assign its holder, as routine duties: reconciling a signed directive with a resident who by every outward sign is content and asks to be kept alive; determining whether a resident whose values have changed is still the person who signed, and what the employer is to do when the answer is no; weighing the interests of the signer against those of the person now in the bed; and judging when a resident's present cheerfulness is reason to set the directive aside and when it is precisely the diminished condition the directive was written to anticipate. It should gesture at the practice's noble ancestry — the sailor who had himself bound to the mast so the song could not move him, having chosen his course while still able to choose — and let the analogy quietly strain, without ever announcing that it has. Invent a little institutional jargon for the role, plausible coinages for the metrics such a department would track, and use it without explanation. A short handover note from the outgoing holder of the post, in the same flat corporate voice, would be welcome, and should let that person disclose, without self-pity or complaint, what the work has cost them.

The advertisement must not cite any literature, name any philosopher, or contain a character who explains the joke.
