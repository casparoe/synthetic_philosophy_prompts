Question 3 (10 pts). A three-judge tribunal has to decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract. Each judge must judge three propositions: P = the defendant did the act in question; Q = the contract forbade doing that act; C = the defendant is liable. All three judges accept the rule that C is true just in case both P and Q are true. Their judgments are:

Judge 1: P yes, Q yes, C yes
Judge 2: P no, Q yes, C no
Judge 3: P yes, Q no, C no

Each judge is individually consistent. (a) Work out what happens if the tribunal takes a majority vote on each proposition seperately. (b) Define the premise-based procedure and the conclusion-based procedure, and say what each yields on this profile. (c) Say which procedure, if either, delivers a collective judgment set that is consistent. (d) Explain what the discursive dilemma is meant to show about the prospect of a group holding a single coherent set of judgments.

And here's what the student wrote:

Each judge is consistent on their own, and they all agree on the rule linking P, Q and C. If we take a majority vote on each proposition by itself, we get P yes (judges 1 and 3), Q yes (judges 1 and 2), and C no (judges 2 and 3). So the tribunal accepts both premises and rejects the conclusion that follows from them. That's the discursive dilemma: the collective set of judgments comes out inconsistent even though every individual's set is fine.

There are two ways to run the vote instead. In the premise-based procedure the tribunal votes only on the premises P and Q and then derives C from the agreed rule; here that gives P yes and Q yes, hence C yes, so the defendant is liable. In the conclusion-based procedure the tribunal votes only on C and ignores the premises; here that gives C no, so the defendant is not liable. The two procedures can therefore disagree even though they're fed exactly the same individual judgments, which is the dilemma in a nutshell.

Of the two, the premise-based procedure is the better one. It keeps the collective verdict tied to reasons the group has actually accepted, and the group's final verdict is the one that a majority of its members endorse as their own verdict. The conclusion-based procedure, by contrast, produces a verdict that floats free of any premises the group has accepted.

What this shows, I think, is that a group can't really be a single rational agent. List and Pettit prove that once you demand that the collective judgments be consistent and that the rule respect some basic fairness conditions, no aggregation method can satisfy everything -- the inconsistency always comes back. So attributing a coherent set of beliefs to a group is ultimately incoherent, and really this is just Arrow's theorem turning up again in the domain of belief instead of preference.
