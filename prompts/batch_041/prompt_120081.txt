For most of my working life I was a geophysicist; I retired five years ago and have been working my way through philosophy since, largely philosophy of physics. Next month the continuing-education discussion group I belong to has asked me to lead its session on Gödel's rotating-universe solution and on what he took it to show about time, on the assumption, I think, that anything with equations in it is mine to explain. What I want from you is a plan for the session, not an account of the argument. The group meets for fifty minutes in a room with a whiteboard; the members are intelligent and scientifically literate but almost entirely innocent of general relativity, and most of them have never read a page of philosophy of physics.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no closing summary. The first should give the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, beginning from whatever has to be in place before the passage of time can even be examined as a claim. The second should name the single case, picture, or analogy that will carry the whole session and say what it must have and must not have; I had thought of carrying it with something from my own field, the way a rotating body drags a local frame around with it, but I am content to be told that this misleads and to be given something better. The third should set out the misconceptions the group will bring or form, in the order they will surface, with the point in the sequence at which each is best headed off. The fourth should give me the one question to put to the room at the end that would show whether the argument has landed rather than merely impressed them. The fifth should tell me what to refuse to explain at this level, and what to say in place of explaining it.

Two things about the group bear on the plan. One member is already convinced that Gödel demonstrated that time is unreal, and he treats this as the same claim as the familiar one that the passage of time is an illusion because the future is as fixed as the past; I cannot tell whether these are one claim or two, and I suspect the session fails if that conflation is left standing. And the group's instinct, which I share, is that a solution to Einstein's equations is interesting only if it describes the universe we actually inhabit, so the plan needs to make clear, at the right moment and without hedging, why a model that is almost certainly not ours can nevertheless carry an argument about time at all.
