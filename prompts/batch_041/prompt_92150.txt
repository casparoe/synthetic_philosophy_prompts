I run a ninety-minute session in a graduate workshop on the ethics and statistics of automated decision-making, and I want to build the whole session around a game rather than a lecture. The room is twenty-four people, deliberately mixed -- roughly half from statistics or machine learning, the rest from public policy, law, and philosophy -- seated at six tables of four, with me facilitating and one teaching assistant handling materials and time. What I want them to come away having felt in their hands rather than their heads is the specific shape of the fairness impossibility results: not the vague belief that scoring tools can be biased, which they arrive with already, but the discovery that a single imperfect risk score used under a fixed capacity constraint cannot satisfy several fairness conditions that each sound non-negotiable on its own, and that they will end up deciding which group absorbs which error rather than finding a rule that avoids the choice. The deadlock has to arise from arithmetic they do themselves, and the session should be able to end with the room deadlocked without that reading as a failure of the exercise.

The mechanic I have in mind is a certification panel. Each table of four is an independent panel that must unanimously certify one decision rule for an already-built risk-scoring tool, and each panel member holds a stamp card carrying veto power. The first stamp belongs to the model risk officer and says: among the applicants flagged for manual review, the share who actually go on to default must be the same in both applicant groups -- the flag has to mean the same thing whoever carries it. The second belongs to the fair-lending officer: the share of applicants who would have repaid but are flagged anyway must be equal across the two groups. The third belongs to an applicant advocate: the share of applicants who would have defaulted but are not flagged must be equal across the two groups. The fourth belongs to the operations lead, who represents nothing about fairness at all and holds only the resource constraint: the manual review team can absorb at most about a quarter of all applications, and the tool must catch enough defaults to beat what the lender does today -- set that recall floor yourself so that the constraint is genuinely binding but still satisfiable at some threshold, tell me what you set it to, and adjust either number if your dataset requires it. Every panel works from the same printed table of sixty to eighty applicants. Please construct that dataset yourself and give it to me as a printable table: two groups whose default rates genuinely differ, a score from zero to one hundred that is a useful but clearly imperfect predictor with no group label anywhere inside it, and enough rows that the four stamps can be computed with a calculator in ten minutes. Show me a worked example of each stamp on a small toy table, and check on the actual numbers that no single global threshold, and no pair of group-specific thresholds, can earn all four stamps at once; tell me in the handout where the pinch is. Panels may use group-specific thresholds if they wish, but a panel that does must state in writing which group's error rate went up and by how much. They get thirty minutes to negotiate and must leave either a signed certification with a written rule or a written statement of what they could not agree on, followed by fifteen minutes of gallery walk in which all six rules are posted and read. I would also like a sealed envelope the facilitator opens only if the room converges on a fix suspiciously fast: a short memo from a regulator saying the panel may, if it chooses, adopt a documented random draw in the borderline band of scores. That memo should be genuine -- it should let a panel buy something it could not buy deterministically -- but the debrief has to be honest about what it costs, and about whether it rescues any of the four stamps or merely relocates the complaint. I do not need the names of theorems, the names of the people who proved them, or any citations or reading list; I know that literature and will supply it myself, so please spend the entire answer on the activity.

The debrief is where I need the most help, and I want it mapped to particular moments rather than to ideas in general: which round generates the pressure corresponding to which part of the impossibility, what each panel's failure looks like from the inside, and what a panel that believes it has succeeded has actually done. Be concrete about how this will predictably go wrong the first time. I expect at least the following. Some panel will clear all four stamps by luck on a finite sample, so I want a second dataset -- last year's applications -- in hand so that any apparently successful rule can be retested and usually broken. Some panel will quietly redefine a metric rather than give it up, and I need a stated rule for when definitions freeze. The operations lead will be ignored until the final ten minutes unless the role has something to do early. And several participants will leave believing the lesson is that the tool should be scrapped or that human judgment should decide instead, which is not what the arithmetic shows, so I need language for that. Finish with a frank account of what this exercise cannot reproduce, and do not soften it: I suspect it cannot make anyone feel the prior question of whether the target variable is the right thing to predict at all, nor the fact that the result concerns any imperfect score rather than this dataset, nor the possibility that the honest resolution is to change what happens to a flagged applicant rather than to choose a metric. Tell me which of those I must simply supply myself, and what the activity will actively mislead people about if I do not.
