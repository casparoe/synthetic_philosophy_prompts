Long arguement with a friend last night about who's allowed to leave who, and I want a straight answer, not a tour of the literature.

His line: a region only gets to leave if the country agrees. Madrid says no, so Catalonia's out of luck; Westminster said yes in 2014, so Scotland was fine. I'm pretty sure that's not the whole story, but every time I try to say why, I end up somewhere I don't like.

My gut, case by case:

- Scotland 2014: legitimate, obviously. Both governments agreed to the vote, one clear question, 55-45, everyone accepted the result. If it had gone the other way I'd have said the rest of the UK had to negotiate.
- Catalonia 2017: not legitimate. The court said no, the unionist parties told their voters to stay home, turnout was around 40%, and they declared independence off the back of it anyway. Even though I think Catalans should get a vote.
- Quebec 1995: legitimate as a vote, but if it had squeaked through 50.5 to 49.5 on a question that was really sovereignty plus an offer of partnership with the rest of Canada that hadn't been negotiated, I think Ottawa could legitimately have said that wasn't clear enough to act on.
- Kosovo 2008: legitimate, unilateral, no permission from Belgrade. Because of what happened in the 90s.
- Bougainville: PNG signed up to holding the vote, 98% said yes, and years later Port Moresby still hasn't acted on it. That feels like a wrong. But nobody there was being crushed, so my "you can only go it alone if you're being crushed" instinct doesn't touch it.
- New Caledonia: three votes, all no, but the last one was boycotted by the independence side and Paris now talks as if the file is closed forever. That feels wrong too, and again there's no oppression in the picture.
- Orkney and Shetland voted heavily against Scottish independence. If Scotland had left, I think they'd have had a decent claim to go with the UK instead. But then the No-voting chunks of Catalonia get a claim to leave Catalonia, which I hate.
- Taiwan: genuinely don't know.

So is there one principle that gets all of these? The nearest I can state is: unilateral exit only as a remedy for serious injustice, otherwise you need the state's consent. But that leaves Bougainville and New Caledonia unexplained, and it makes my Orkney instinct nonsense. The other candidate, any group that thinks of itself as a nation can vote whenever it likes, gets Orkney right and turns everything else to mush.

And I can't get past the question of who's actually voting. My friend says the region, obviously. That's obviously right for Scotland and feels like theft somewhere else, and I can't say where. Also: are we talking about one question or two seperate ones, the moral right and the legal one?

Pick a side. Give me the principle you'd actually defend, as many clauses as it takes, run it through my cases, tell me which of my verdicts has to go, and say what it predicts for a region that wants out of a region that just got out, and for a nation defined by language and scattered across three countries rather than by any territory. Committed answer please, no fence-sitting.
