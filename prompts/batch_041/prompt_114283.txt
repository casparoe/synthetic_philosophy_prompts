Two colleagues of mine — both working on decision theory, from different directions — spent most of a seminar reception relitigating a question that neither would concede, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general essay on the topic.

The question was whether expected-utility theory gives utility any content over and above the choices it is fitted to.

One of them opened as follows. The expected-utility representation theorem says that if an agent's preferences over lotteries satisfy certain axioms, there exists a utility function whose expectation the agent maximizes, and that function is unique up to positive affine transformation. That uniqueness class, he said, is exactly the problem: all the theorem licenses is rescaling and shifting, so it delivers no quantity. Claims such as "she likes x twice as much as y" remain meaningless. The only thing that changed relative to the older ordinal framework is that the objects being ranked were enriched from sure outcomes to lotteries. Cardinal utility, on this view, is a representation of an ordinal preference relation over a larger domain, and nothing more.

The other replied that this cannot be right, because the move from ordinal to affine invariance is precisely what makes comparisons of utility differences meaningful. If the function is fixed only up to positive affine transformation, then the ratio of two utility differences — how much is gained from x over y, against z over w — is well defined. That is the quantity needed to state diminishing marginal utility, to define risk aversion, and to run the classic argument for buying insurance. Ordinal utility cannot even express those claims. So the theorem adds content, and the content is the agent's attitude toward risk, which is a fact about the agent rather than a redescription of a list of choices.

He answered that attitude toward risk is itself read off choices over lotteries: offer someone a sure thing against a gamble, see which she takes, and infer concavity or convexity. Had she chosen otherwise, a different function would have been inferred. So the alleged fact about the agent is inferred from exactly the data that revealed-preference theory uses; the enrichment is in the experiment, not in the mind. And once the axioms fail — Allais-type patterns, framing effects — the apparatus stops describing anything and becomes a normative ideal that the agent is failing to meet. One may keep the function; one may not call it her utility.

She replied that the Allais point cuts the other way. If the axioms are normative, then what gets represented is the agent's rational preferences, and it becomes a substantive question whether her actual choices track them. That is a genuine gap between preference and choice. On his view there is no gap at all — choice simply is preference — and then "she chose badly" can mean nothing except "she chose differently from how I would." On that view the paternalism question cannot even be posed.

He answered that the gap can be reconstructed without any cardinal quantity: model the agent as having preferences over time, or over menus, or over states of the world in which the choice is made, and the apparently mistaken choice is just the maximizer of a different preference relation over a richer domain. That buys the gap back, but only by making the theory unfalsifiable, since some preference relation over some domain will rationalize any data. Her cardinal utility, he said, is purchased at the price of the theory's having no empirical content.

She said that if those are the options, she prefers the theory that says something and is sometimes wrong.

He said he prefers the one that knows it is a bookkeeping device and does not dress its rescaling conventions up as discoveries about welfare.

I would like each exchange adjudicated in turn — who is right about what, and where each of them overstates — and then an account of what both are missing. Be explicit about what would change your mind: name the argument or consideration that would flip your verdict, and say where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. Present the verdict as a numbered list matching the exchanges, followed by a short closing paragraph, in no more than 800 words.
