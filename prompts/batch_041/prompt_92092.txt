I sat in on a graduate seminar in the history of analytic ethics last term, and the last two sessions were given over to G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) — in particular the closing chapter, where he argues that the two greatest goods we can know of are personal affection and the enjoyment of what is beautiful, and where he relies on his principle of organic unities, the thesis that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts, so that a whole may be better or worse than its parts taken separately. Two of the students, Dana and Rafael, carried an argument out of the seminar room and into the corridor, and I ended up standing there as the audience. I could not tell who had the better of it, and I would rather not settle it with my own half-formed intuitions. So please act as a philosopher who works on value theory and knows this material well: think the issues through carefully before you answer, and open with a brief, jargon-light inventory of the distinct claims that are actually in play here — what is being claimed about what makes a friendship valuable, what is being claimed about the difference between something being valuable in itself and being valuable in virtue of something else, and what work the organic-unity idea is being asked to do. Then adjudicate.

Rafael began: "The interesting thing about Moore is that his view of friendship is not soft sentimentalism — it is a claim about intrinsic value. Affection for a person is good in itself, and it is not good merely as a way of producing pleasant feelings. And the object of the affection matters: affection aimed at someone who is genuinely good is worth more than the same feeling aimed at a figment or a fraud, and affection for someone worthless is not a good at all. That is a real commitment, and it is the right one." Dana came back: "That is exactly the part I don't believe. Everything he says about friendship is a claim about a state of mind — the enjoyment, the appreciation, the affection as felt. He never tells us what the other person contributes except by being the occasion of the feeling. If that is the story, friendship is a means to valuable experience, and calling it a state of consciousness does not change that. Moore wanted friendship to be one of the two highest goods; what he actually delivers is hedonism with better manners." Rafael: "No — that is what the organic-unity doctrine is for. The friendship is not a container of valuable experiences. The whole, two people actually related as they are, is itself valuable, and its value is not the sum of the values of its parts. That is precisely why the object can matter: the whole is different when the affection is aimed at a real and good person than when it is aimed at a figment, and that difference in the whole's value cannot be traced to any difference in the felt state alone." Dana: "Then tell me what makes the whole valuable, if not its parts. If the value of the whole is not the value of the parts, it materializes when you put the parts together and comes from nowhere. That is not an explanation, it is a promissory note. And the move doesn't even buy you what you want: on your own account the value of the friendship depends on facts about the other person — that she exists, that she is who she is, that she has not secretly been a fabrication all along. So the friendship's value depends on something outside itself, and friendship turns out to be good conditionally, in virtue of its object, which is a different theory wearing the same name." Rafael: "The doctrine is not ad hoc, and Moore needs it elsewhere — think of his retribution case, where a vicious person's suffering forms a better whole than either the viciousness or the suffering alone, though both parts are bad. And intrinsic value in Moore's sense means value a thing has in virtue of its intrinsic nature, not value that survives the deletion of everything else in the universe. The friendship's nature includes its object; that is why dependence on the object is not dependence on something alien to it." Dana: "The retribution case makes my point for me. What improves the combination is the relation between the parts — that the suffering is deserved, that it fits. And the value of a friendship depends on the relation between two people, not on the intrinsic nature of either taken alone. Once value turns on relations and histories you have left intrinsic value behind, and you should say so rather than invoke an organic unity to keep the word. I would rather have the honest relational view: friendships are valuable because of what they are between people — which is why a friendship with a figment is not a friendship at all, and why a friendship that is betrayed is not retroactively worthless, but is a different thing seen from outside than it was from inside." Rafael: "If that is your view, then the instrumentalism charge was never right. A relation cannot be a means to experiences that could have occurred without it. So the value of friendship is not the value of the experiences it produces; and if you are content to say the relation is valuable for its own sake, we agree about the substance and are only quarrelling about whether to keep the word 'intrinsic'." Dana: "Not quite. I still think the value of a friendship is inherited from the value of what it is made of — the people and their states — and that Moore cannot say that without either admitting instrumentalism or making the whole's value a free-floating addition. And 'valuable for its own sake' is exactly the phrase that needs unpacking: I can value something for its own sake without thinking its value is intrinsic in Moore's sense, and I suspect that is all friendship ever needed." Rafael: "Then you owe me an answer to this: if the value sits in the relation rather than in the two people's states, and relations can be one-sided, unacknowledged, or unknown to the parties, what makes the relation rather than the states the bearer of the value? I don't think your view is any cheaper than mine."

I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a summary of the two positions. For each of the moves above — Rafael's opening claim about intrinsic value and the object, Dana's instrumentalism charge, Rafael's appeal to organic unities, Dana's "value from nowhere" objection, Rafael's retribution analogy and his reading of intrinsic value, Dana's relational alternative, Rafael's reply that the instrumentalism charge has collapsed, and the final exchange about 'valuable for its own sake' — rate the move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and give a one- or two-sentence justification of each rating. For every move, identify the load-bearing premise it depends on and say whether that premise survives scrutiny. Where the two are talking past each other rather than disagreeing — for instance, if they are using a technical term like 'intrinsic' in different senses, or if one of them has quietly shifted from a claim about value to a claim about explanation — say so explicitly and explain what the disagreement looks like once that is cleaned up. Then state what each party is missing that neither has noticed, and close with a verdict on who is more right overall, together with a short sketch of what you take the defensible position about the value of friendship to be. Be rigorous and specific, do not retreat into the verdict that both have a point without saying which point is which, and do not cite or gesture at any literature — I want the arguments assessed on their merits, as if the two of them had invented the positions themselves.
