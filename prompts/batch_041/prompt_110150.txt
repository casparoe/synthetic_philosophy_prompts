I am a philosopher of mind, and in three weeks I am presenting a work-in-progress paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop, and I would like to be properly prepared for the discussion rather than merely braced for it. The thesis I am defending is that phenomenal consciousness, as it is ordinarily conceived — a private, intrinsic, ineffable glow that attends certain states of the brain — is best understood as a user illusion in roughly the sense in which the desktop of a computer is one: not a portrait of the underlying machinery, and not a deception that anyone perpetrates, but a self-representation pitched at the level of the things an agent can act upon, and designed so that the mechanism stays out of sight. The constructive half of the paper is that the interesting explanatory target is not why there is a glow but why we are so unshakable in judging that there is one, and that this judgement is downstream of the very machinery that furnishes us with a usable and non-transparent self-model; the illusion, on my account, is not that we undergo experiences but that we take them to have a special intrinsic feel that introspection reveals. The room is small but hostile in a specific and predictable way. Two analytic philosophers of mind, both of whom work on higher-order theories of consciousness, are convinced that illusionism is self-undermining, since an illusion is still an appearance and an appearance requires an appearer; a vision scientist who studies change blindness and metacognition will want to know precisely which empirical results are supposed to be doing the explanatory work rather than merely being consistent with the view; and a philosopher of biology who is allergic to design talk will hear the interface metaphor as smuggling in a designer, or at any rate a purpose, through the back door. I have given a version of this twice before, and on both occasions the discussion went immediately to the question of who, exactly, is supposed to be having the illusion.

I know this literature well and I do not need citations, a reading list, or a summary of the standard positions. What I want from you is a question-and-answer preparation sheet for the discussion period, in which the objections are ordered by how likely they are to be raised and how much damage they would do if I fumble them, each phrased the way the person in question would actually phrase it rather than in neutral textbook form, each accompanied by what I should concede without hesitation, a reply that will fit in about two minutes of speech, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. I would particularly like you to flag the places where the interface metaphor is doing genuine work and the places where it is merely decorative, and to tell me whether the philosopher of biology's objection is really a distinct objection or the higher-order theorists' objection wearing different clothes. Please also tell me which concession I should make in the first five minutes of the talk, before questions, in order to take the wind out of the most damaging line of attack; and end with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, together with your estimate of whether I can answer it at all.

Write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a long and candid letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables, even where a list would be the obvious temptation.
