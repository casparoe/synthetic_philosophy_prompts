I'm a philosophy graduate student, and I've been asked to contribute a short position piece to a departmental workshop on formal epistemology whose theme this year is bounded rationality. I've drafted something on the problem of logical omniscience, but I'm too close to it to see whether the central move actually works, and my supervisor is away until the end of the month. I'd be grateful for detailed comments before I circulate it to the group.

The draft runs roughly as follows. I start from the familiar complaint that any possible-worlds semantics treating belief as truth in all doxastically accessible worlds makes every agent believe all logical truths and every logical consequence of what she believes. I don't think the interesting issue is closure as such. I claim the real culprit is a mismatch of granularity: belief attributions are hyperintensional — believing that P can fail to be substitutable with believing that Q even when P and Q are necessarily equivalent — and the standard semantics simply isn't sensitive to the distinctions we care about. My proposed fix is to relativize belief not to an idealized consequence relation but to what I call the agent's reasoning regime: the set of inference rules she is actually disposed to apply, together with whatever she treats as basic. On this view, logical omniscience is an artifact of attributing belief relative to a regime that no one has. I then try to keep a genuine normative dimension by distinguishing between what an agent is committed to, given her beliefs and the rules she endorses, and what she actually holds, i.e. has in her head. The omniscience intuition, I suggest, is really tracking the first notion, while ordinary belief talk tracks the second.

What I want from you is a candid assessment, not encouragement. First, is the reasoning-regime account genuinely different from the existing awareness strategies, or am I just relabeling the same maneuver of restricting which formulas an agent is said to entertain? Second, I worry that the account collapses under what I call the trivial-inference objection: if I believe P, believe that P implies Q, and my regime includes modus ponens, it seems absurd to deny that I believe Q — yet my view seems to permit exactly that, since I may never have performed the inference. Does the commitment/holding distinction really rescue me here, or is it an ad hoc dodge? Third, is there a principled way to individuate reasoning regimes that doesn't make belief attribution circular — that is, that doesn't already presuppose which inferences the agent really takes to be valid? I'd also welcome any suggestion for framing the piece so that it doesn't read as yet another terminological proposal, which is the fate I most fear for it.
