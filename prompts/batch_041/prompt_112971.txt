I spent my working life in experimental particle physics, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy — lately the classical Indian epistemology of the Nyāya school, which divides the means of knowledge into pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison) and śabda (testimony), and attaches to two of them conditions that none of the methodology I was trained in would have thought to state. The first is that an inference yields knowledge only when its reason is invariably accompanied by what it is meant to establish — the relation the school calls vyāpti — and that a reason admitting of even one case of deviation, savyabhicāra, is not a weak reason but no reason at all. The second is that testimony yields knowledge only when the speaker is a competent authority on the matter and the hearer genuinely grasps what is said, so that the warrant turns on facts about the teller and about my understanding, not on the content alone. I am a beginner and may have some of this wrong; correct me where I do.

I should like to see the apparatus applied to an episode from my own field, though not from my own collaboration. In September 2011 the OPERA group reported that muon neutrinos sent from CERN to Gran Sasso, some 730 kilometres, had arrived about sixty nanoseconds earlier than light would have. The collaboration had hunted for instrumental effects and found none, reported a significance of six sigma — a measure, as I have since had occasion to reflect, of precision rather than of accuracy — and asked the wider community to scrutinise and replicate the result. Many of us outside the collaboration never credited it, not least because the burst of neutrinos from the supernova of 1987, some 160,000 light-years away, had reached us hours before its light. By the spring of 2012 the anomaly had dissolved: a fibre-optic link from a GPS receiver to the master clock was loose, and a clock on an electronic board was running fast. Nobody had lied and no measurement had been cooked; the whole result rested on a chain of calibrations that everyone had taken on trust.

Map the episode onto the apparatus: what was the reason, what was the conclusion, where exactly was the invariable accompaniment assumed, and which of the four means of knowledge was really doing the work when the timing chain was trusted. Then run the analysis the other way. If our best inferences — a six-sigma result, a blind analysis — never deliver invariable accompaniment, must the framework count them as something short of knowledge; and what is it to be told something when the speaker is not a person but a collaboration that hedges its own assertion and asks to be checked. End with a verdict on the framework itself: does the episode confirm it, strain it, or fall outside what it was built to handle, and where does it simply have nothing to say.
