Imagine that a consortium building a powerful AI assistant is trying to specify the assistant's value-aggregation layer, and that its designers have settled on a procedure they call sphere sovereignty. Under sphere sovereignty, a user's own values govern any decision that affects only that user; when a decision affects several users, the system falls back on maximizing the number of users whose own stated values are satisfied, counting each user equally and treating every user's preferences as fixed data rather than as something the system is permitted to revise. The designers add a further constraint: the assistant must remain neutral among ends, declining to rank one user's preference for a life of contemplation above another's preference for a life of accumulation, on the grounds that ranking ends would amount to imposing a particular conception of the good on people who never agreed to it. They are now worried that the procedure has no way to handle a user whose preferences were shaped under conditions of domination, and no way to say what should happen when the majority's satisfied preferences are of a kind the minority regards as degrading.

Suppose John Stuart Mill — the author of On Liberty and Utilitarianism, speaking with the full force of his mature commitments — were brought forward to advise this design team. Reconstruct the positions he would take, reasoning only from arguments and commitments already present in his own work, and be explicit wherever the modern proposal forces him to choose between strands of his thought that he never had to reconcile. Address, among whatever else you judge relevant, whether his account of liberty could tolerate an institution that treats preferences as beyond correction, whether his distinction between kinds of pleasure commits him to ranking ends in a way the designers forbid, and what he would make of the designers' appeal to the tyranny of the majority as a reason for neutrality.

Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Each point should state one position Mill would take together with the strand of his work that supports it, and at least one point should identify a place where the proposal leaves him in a tension he never had to resolve.
