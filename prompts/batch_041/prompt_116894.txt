Eighteen months ago I took over a small team and deliberately changed what it ran on. The old culture was the one I grew up in: you did not want to be the person who dropped the ball, because being that person was not something you could undo. I replaced it with what I took to be the more decent arrangement — we discuss the act and not the actor, and the vocabulary of character is out of bounds. I had absorbed the standard contrast: guilt is about what you did, shame is about who you are, and any half-civilised institution should prefer the first.

What I did not anticipate was what happened to the apology. Under the old arrangement, dropping the ball was not a bounded event with a bounded remedy; the only exit was not becoming the sort of person who does that. Under the new one it is precisely bounded: you name it, you own it, you say what you will do differently, and the account is settled. That is now what happens, and it happens repeatedly, by the same people, with what I am fairly sure is genuine feeling each time. The apology has become the price of the act. And the one colleague who is still, in the old sense, ashamed of herself has not repeated anything in a year; she is also the person everyone finds hardest to work with, because she will not accept the absolution we keep offering and keeps reopening matters we regard as closed.

So the thing I keep needing a word for is not the object of these two emotions but their exit conditions. Guilt appears to have a bounded exit — an act can be acknowledged and repaired, and then it is finished. Shame appears to have none, since a self cannot be repaired; its only exits are concealment or becoming someone else. If that is right, the two emotions deter differently: guilt taxes the act, shame prevents it. And the very feature that makes guilt morally attractive, that it is answerable and can be discharged, is what makes it compatible with repetition.

Is there a name for this? If there is, I want to know what it covers and, more, what it leaves out, because the two labels I have been offered do not fit. Moral licensing is about a good deed buying a bad one; here nothing good is banked in advance — the payment is made afterwards, in the same currency as the harm. Restorative justice is a proposal about how to respond to wrongdoing, whereas what I am describing is a claim about how two emotions are built. If there is no name, I would like one, with the edges drawn: what has to be true of an emotion for this to apply to it, whether it extends to emotions whose objects are not acts at all — grief, anger at the dead, regret that has no addressee — and where the reasoning goes wrong, if it goes wrong. Because as it stands it looks like an argument for preferring the less decent of two emotions, and I would rather be shown that it is not.
