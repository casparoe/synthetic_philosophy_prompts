I've been sitting in on a decision-theory reading group this term — I'm finishing a master's in economics, and my roommate, who regards all of this as a solved problem, keeps telling me to stop reading about it — and the one piece that has genuinely rearranged my thinking is Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility. The core of it, as I understand it: a rational agent need not weight probabilities linearly; she may instead apply a risk function to the cumulative probabilities of her outcomes, ordered from worst to best, so that the weight an outcome receives depends on the cumulative probability of the outcomes ranked below it. Risk aversion, on that picture, is not a peculiarity of my utility curve but a permissible attitude toward chance as such. This stopped being an academic question for me in March, when I turned down an offer that beat the one I accepted on every expected-monetary measure, purely because of how the gamble was structured, and found that my options were to call myself irrational or to call the dominance argument question-begging. Since then I have wanted to know how much of what I did is a taste that expected utility theory can absorb into the utility function, and how much is an attitude toward chance that it cannot.

So here is what I want, and I want it as a prediction with a spine rather than a survey of the literature. What would Leonard Savage have made of risk-weighted expected utility? He is the obvious interlocutor, since the theory is largely defined against the axioms he made canonical, and he never had the chance to see it. Reason from the substance of his position — what he took to be constitutive of rational preference, how he handled the Allais preferences he confronted directly, how he thought about the scope of the theory and the size of the world it was meant to govern — rather than pointing me to the places where he says it; I'm after the argument, not the references, and I would rather have your reconstruction than a list of things he might have said. Which of his commitments force him to reject the theory outright, and which leave him room to absorb it? In particular, take a definite position on the obvious counter-move on his behalf: that the risky prospect is simply part of the outcome, so that a taste for or against chance is one more component of utility and the risk-weighted agent is, once properly described, an expected-utility maximizer after all. Does that move work for him, or does the rank-dependence of the weighting block it? My own hunch — you should feel free to reject it — is that everything turns on whether he would allow a lottery over lotteries to be treated as reducible to a single lottery, and that this is where he would plant his flag.

Finish with the concessions: how much of the Allais-type behavior would he have been willing to certify as rational in retrospect; whether he would have insisted that the disagreement is about rationality at all, or only about how small a world the theory must fit; and what evidence or argument, if any, could have moved him. Say "he would say X" and "he would resist Y," give the reasons, and make the calls even where his own writing pulls both ways.
