I run the exceptions desk at a mid-sized humanitarian org. I'm not a philosopher, I'm just the person who gets the awkward requests. We have a short public list of things we say we never do: we don't pay armed groups for access or to get our staff released, we don't hand beneficiary data over to governments, and we don't decieve people to get their consent to be in our research. Maybe a dozen times a year some country office asks for an exception, and the argument is always the same shape: fine, this one thing breaks the rule, but doing it prevents several more breaks of the very same rule down the line. Five more detentions if we don't pay. Thousands of patients dropped if we don't hand over the list. A study that would save a lot of lives if we're allowed to be sneaky just this once.

That's the bit I can't get my head around. If the whole reason we have these rules is that each person matters and shouldn't be used as a tool, then we should care a lot about the extra people who get hurt when we stick to the rule, so why not break it? But the moment we start weighing the numbers we don't have rules anymore, we have a cost-benefit sheet, and the rules were supposed to be exactly the thing that stops us doing that.

So please turn this into a decision procedure my desk can actually run. The order of the questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it just stops with a no, and where it hands the call to a human because it's a value judgment and not a fact. It has to be runnable by a smart non-philosopher in an afternoon on incomplete information, and it has to keep whatever it is that makes a rule a rule rather than a number to be traded. I want it to be able to say: the fact that there are more people on the other side doesn't cancel the reason not to do this to this one person. But it still has to admit that some exceptions are genuinely right. Throw in a step for the what-if-everyone-copies-us worry, and be clear that that's a seperate kind of reason from the one the rule is made of.

Then run it on three: (1) the payment to get two staff back, where not paying has correlated with more detentions but we can't prove it; (2) the beneficiary list, where refusing probably kills the programme for thousands and handing it over puts a few people in real danger; (3) a case I'll hate, where a field team wants to mislead a local official to move medical supplies through and save several lives, and I suspect your procedure says no. I want to watch it say no to me. Anything that overrides a rule has to go to the board, so the output has to be something I can put in front of them.

At the end, tell me straight which steps are just judgment dressed up as procedure, and what this thing can't do at all.
