I chair the strategic planning committee of a mid-sized regional hospital, and I am writing to ask for help with a problem that has stalled us through three consecutive meetings.

Last year we commissioned three independent teams to design a ten-year model for the hospital. The first would rebuild us around outpatient and community care; the second would shrink us into a smaller, more specialised inpatient service; the third would bind us to the university as a teaching and research partner. Each proposal is internally coherent and, judged on its own terms, strong. They are also mutually exclusive in a way that is more than budgetary. The community model depends on revenue that the specialist model is designed to generate; the teaching partnership needs inpatient volume of precisely the kind the community model exists to reduce; and the staffing assumptions, referral patterns, and capital commitments of each proposal are calibrated to its own internal logic. When trustees propose taking the best from each, the result is a fourth thing that is worse than any of the three on almost every measure we care about. I have taken to calling this the pick-and-mix fallacy in our internal papers, but I cannot say precisely what is fallacious about it, and two trustees reasonably reply that a preference ordering over parts implies an optimum over combinations, so declining to look for that optimum is timidity rather than rigour.

Three things I would like from you. First, an account in plain language of what makes taking the best of each incoherent in a case like ours, and what would have to be true of our options for it to become coherent. Second, our medical director's objection: since whatever we choose will be the thing we chose, describing it as the best available plan is empty flattery — it means no more than the plan we endorsed. Is that objection sound, and if it is not, what must be true for a comparison of whole plans to be a real comparison rather than a restatement of our decision? A related worry, which I cannot put any better than this: I genuinely want each of the three models, in the sense that each of them, considered on its own, is a good thing to want. Can someone want all three and still be sincere in choosing one?

Third, we have adopted a procedural rule that I would like you to evaluate: no one may argue for or against an element of a proposal in isolation; every objection must be stated as a claim about a whole plan. Is that rule a way of forcing honest reasoning about wholes, or a way of insulating a whole plan from criticisms that would be obvious if we examined its parts?

Finally, if the plan we pick requires closing a service that is genuinely good in itself and that many of our staff value, I will have to tell them that we are not saying their service was bad — only that it cannot be had together with things we judged better in combination. Is that a coherent thing to say, or is it a dignified way of describing a loss? I do not need references or a reading list; I need the argument, and if possible the wording I can use with the board.
