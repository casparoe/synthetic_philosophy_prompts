The following is a take-home exam question from my moral philosophy course, along with one student's answer to it. I'd like you to grade the answer the way I would have to when assigning a course grade, and to explain the grade.

The question:

Hypocrisy is standardly said to undermine the standing to blame. (a) Explain what is at stake in distinguishing a standing objection from an objection to the truth or fittingness of the moral judgment being made. (b) Three accounts of what is objectionable about hypocritical blame are on the table:

- (i) the insincerity account: the hypocrite's blame is defective because she does not really accept the standard she applies to others;
- (ii) the equality account: the hypocrite claims an exemption for herself from a standard she applies to others, thereby denying the equal moral standing of the person she blames;
- (iii) the dispositional account: what makes blame hypocritical is a settled character defect — a standing tendency to be lenient with oneself and severe with others — so a single episode of blaming someone for something one is doing at that very moment does not by itself make one a hypocrite.

Assess which account, if any, best captures the phenomenon, and say what follows for whether a hypocrite's blame should be disregarded by its target.

The student's answer:

The question asks what standing adds to the ordinary question whether a piece of moral criticism is correct, and I think the answer is: something distinct. A standing objection does not deny that the act was wrong or that the judgment is true; it denies that this particular person has the authority to hold the wrongdoer to account for it. This is why "you did the same thing" is a bad reply when aimed at the moral judgment itself: even if the accuser is a hypocrite, the wrong may still be a wrong. Standing is a matter of the relation between blamer and blamed, not of the content of the judgment, and that is what distinguishes a standing objection from tu quoque used as a fallacy.

Turning to the three accounts, I think (i) and (ii) are closer to each other than they look, and that (i) is the deeper of the two. The reason the hypocrite's blame is defective is that she does not really accept the standard she invokes; her condemnation is a performance. The equality account is making the same point in different words — the hypocrite's blame is defective because it is insincere, claiming for herself an exemption she would not grant to anyone else. The real defect is expressive: the hypocrite misrepresents her own commitments, and it is this misrepresentation, rather than the bare fact of inconsistency, that removes her standing.

I am less persuaded by (iii). It seems to me that the paradigm case of hypocrisy is exactly the one the dispositional account sets aside: blaming someone for something you are doing yourself, right now, is the clearest case of hypocrisy there is. Whatever we say about settled traits, the person who says "how could you" while doing the very same thing has no standing to say it. The dispositional account looks like a way of protecting repeat offenders from criticism rather than a description of the phenomenon.

One consequence: because the hypocrite lacks standing, her criticism can be disregarded. Since she has no authority to hold the wrongdoer to account, the wrongdoer has no reason to answer her, and the criticism carries no weight for anyone else either — if the only person in a position to raise the matter is a hypocrite, the matter simply goes unraised.
