I am setting the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I need one essay question on Dennett's critique of qualia in 'Quining Qualia'. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard summary of the Chase and Sanborn case. Instead, it should force students to distinguish between the epistemic and the metaphysical readings of Dennett's claim that qualia are an incoherent posit, and to say which of the four criteria traditionally attributed to qualia — ineffability, intrinsicality, privacy, and direct apprehensibility — each reading puts under pressure. To ensure that students cannot simply reproduce the published literature, the question should introduce a new intuition pump that is structurally analogous to Chase and Sanborn but not identical to it. Here is the case I have in mind: two subjects, call them X and Y, both undergo a procedure that systematically alters their sensory receptors. X reports that the world now looks different to her, but she has no reason to think her visual system has changed. Y reports that the world looks the same to him, but he has reason to think his visual system has changed. Neither can tell, from the inside, which of them is correct. The question should ask students to assess whether this case supports or undermines Dennett's conclusion, and to explain what the case reveals about the relationship between first-person report and third-person evidence in the debate over qualia.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, with marks totalling 20, and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. The examination is three hours long, and students are expected to spend about 45 minutes on this question. You are an experienced examiner in philosophy of mind, so please think carefully about the crux of the question before you begin: the best answers will not simply agree or disagree with Dennett, but will show how the new case cuts differently depending on which reading of 'qualia' is in play. Do not include any citations or references; the examination is closed-book.
