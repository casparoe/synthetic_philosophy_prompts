I am a retired inorganic chemist — kinetics, mostly — who has spent the past several years reading philosophy of biology in a haphazard, self-taught way. I can usually tell a fair summary of a position from a tendentious one, but not in this area, and I would like your help auditing a passage.

The passage comes from a popular book on evolution that I read some time ago and no longer have; I am reconstructing its argument from my notes, so the wording below is mine and not the author's. The chapter on the levels of selection runs roughly as follows. Since the only entities copied with high fidelity from one generation to the next are genes, and since selection just is the differential survival of such copies, the unit of selection is the gene; everything else — chromosomes, organisms, colonies, species — is a vehicle, a temporary vessel built by genes for their own propagation. An organism is therefore best understood as a survival machine whose behaviour is ultimately directed at the interests of the genes it carries, and the organism's own flourishing enters the theory only insofar as it serves those interests. Because selection can only see the gene, a trait that benefits a group at a cost to the individual cannot evolve by selection; appeals to 'the good of the group' are not explanations at all but admissions that we have not yet located the real, gene-level benefit. The chapter grants that the sterile workers of social insects look like an exception, but explains them away by kin selection: the workers are helping copies of their own genes in their sisters, so the apparent altruism is gene-level selfishness in disguise. It concludes that the debate over group selection was settled long ago, that any evolutionary explanation which stops at the group is unscientific, and that recent attempts to explain human morality as an adaptation for group cohesion are refuted by the same reasoning.

What bothers me, and I think it is not merely an outsider's suspicion, is that a bookkeeping claim — you must track the entity that is actually replicated — appears to be run together with a causal claim — that nothing above the gene can be a cause of evolutionary change — and that the chapter needs the second to reach its conclusion while only having established the first. I would like you to go through the passage and tell me, sentence by sentence, what a careful philosopher of biology would accept, what they would call a standard simplification, and what they would reject outright. In particular: is the 'unit of selection' one question or several, and if the phrase is doing more than one job in the passage, where exactly does the slippage occur? How would a defender of the gene's-eye view answer the charge that the view makes group selection impossible — and are the gene's-eye and group-selection perspectives rivals at all, or different partitions of the same variance? And do the concluding claims, that group-level explanation is unscientific and that group-selected human morality is refuted, survive once the simplifications are corrected, or were they resting on the conflation from the outset? I am trying to learn to conduct this kind of audit myself, so please be concrete about which sentences carry which errors, and say what a fairer summary of the position would look like.
