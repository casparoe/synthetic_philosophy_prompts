I've been working through the literature on the lottery and preface paradoxes for a seminar on the epistemology of rational belief, and I keep hitting the same obstacle: nearly every worked example is either a book with a preface or a fair lottery, and both come with so much built-in scaffolding — an author, a publisher, numbered tickets, a draw — that the case does half the philosopher's work before she starts. I'd like you to build me a purpose-made thought experiment that reaches the same tension with none of that apparatus, so that the puzzle has to be generated by the case rather than merely recognized from a familiar example.

Specifically, construct a case in which a group — not a single author — is collectively justified in each of a large number of claims but is not justified in their conjunction, and is then required to commit to the conjunction in one public act (a joint report, a signed recommendation, a shared plan, something of that kind). Constraints: no lottery, no book or preface, and no numerical probabilities or explicit probabilistic thresholds anywhere in the story; the individual claims should be independent of one another and each supported by ordinary, quotidian evidence rather than by a clever setup; the case should make the tension practical as well as doxastic, so that the group must act on the whole set at once even though its members would each resist asserting the whole set; and the conflict must arise from the group's own fallibility rather than from any claim being stipulated false. Keep it tight and realistic — a research group, a hospital committee, an engineering team, whatever fits.

Then do the analysis. State exactly what the group believes and what it may assert, and show how three standard responses to the paradoxes come apart on your case: (i) keeping each belief but abandoning closure, so that the conjunction need not be believed; (ii) abandoning the principle that high evidential support suffices for belief, so that the members never believed all the claims to begin with; and (iii) accepting that rationally held beliefs may be jointly inconsistent. Tell me which single structural feature of the case is doing the real work — the independence of the claims, the requirement to act, or the aggregation from members to group — and what becomes of the case if that feature is removed. Say how a defender of whichever response fares worst would attack your construction rather than revise her view, and whether that attack succeeds. Finally, since I'll be using this in a seminar, tell me how confident you are that the case genuinely generates the conflict and genuinely discriminates the three responses, and single out the one point in your construction you are least sure of.
