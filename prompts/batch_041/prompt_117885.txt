I did a PhD on some very boring Precambrian sediments and then spent 32 years in the oil business doing lab and modelling work, and I've only gotten seriously into philosophy of science since I retired. So forgive me if I've got some of the furniture in the wrong place, but here's where I've landed and I'd like you to try to knock it down.

The position: what makes a claim scientific is that it sticks its neck out. It forbids certain things from happening, and if one of those things happens you're obliged to give the claim up. A scientific claim is one that tells you in advance what you'd have to see to be wrong, and the more it rules out, the more scientific it is. Everything that doesn't do that is mathematics, metaphysics, or noise. I got this from Popper and I know there's been alot of pushback since, but I still find the core of it right, and I want to know what actually counts against it rather than just being told it's old fashioned.

Three things I'd like you to sort through:

First, the criterion looks bad against things I'm sure are science. Evolution, the historical geology I did my thesis on, cosmology — none of them obviously forbid anything you could watch fail in a lab, and yet I'd bet my pension on them over any single lab result. Does that count against the position, or have I just stated the criterion sloppily?

Second, someone at my reading group said the criterion is funny in its own mouth, since a claim is scientific only if it's falsifiable isn't itself falsifiable. I don't think that's a killer, I don't think its meant to be an empirical claim, but I want to know whether I'm quietly helping myself to an exemption there, and what the exemption costs me.

Third, and this is the one that really bugs me. When I apply my own test honestly, the young-earth crowd and the scientific racism of a century ago come out looking very scientific — they stuck their necks out, they made checkable predictions, they were refuted, and they carried on anyway. String theory comes out looking unscientific. If my test gives the wrong verdict on those, in what sense is it the test? My suspicion is that falsifiability is really about testability and not about warrant, and I'd like you to say what's left of the whole demarcation project if that's right.

So: give me the things that count against the position, ranked by how much they should actually move me, and tell me which of them a committed Popperian would cheerfully concede and carry on. I especially want to know whether the fixes that get made — sophisticated falsificationism, research programmes, the whole thing being a proposal about how to use a word rather than a claim about the world — leave the view with any exposure left at all, or turn it into something that cannot lose. And if it can't lose, tell me straight whether that's a bug or whether it's just what happens to a view that's really about norms rather than facts. Also: what does this position predict that a Bayesian or a Kuhn-style account doesn't?

What would move me most is a case where everyone now agrees the thing is science, and yet for a long stretch nobody could have named an observation that would have sunk it, and the field was fine with that.
