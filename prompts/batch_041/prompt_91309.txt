I teach a 90-minute session on persistence and identity across time to a metaphysics seminar of roughly fourteen final-year undergraduates and taught master's students. By the end of it I want them to have felt the force of the four-dimensionalist picture — that an object is extended through time in something like the way it is extended through space, so that it has temporal parts as well as spatial parts — rather than merely being able to recite the position. I would like you to design an activity that does this, runnable inside the session, with no lecture up front.

The contrast I want them to collide with is this. Nobody finds it puzzling that a single object is red at one end and blue at the other; we simply say it has a red part and a blue part. But when the same object is red at one time and blue at another, we are pulled towards saying it is one and the same thing that is now red and was blue, and we resist the parallel move of positing a red temporal part and a blue temporal part. I want the activity to make that asymmetry feel like something the participants have to argue for rather than something given.

Please give me the rules and any materials; the roles, or the incentive structure if it is a game; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the pieces of the philosophical picture — the wholly-present versus extended-in-time contrast, the problem of how one thing can have incompatible properties at different times, the option of relativising properties to times, and the cost of each option. Participants should run into the difficulty themselves, through the rules, roles or scoring, before any of it is named.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle each failure. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce, which I will have to supply in the debrief.

I would rather the activity turn on a case that is not the usual ship or the usual statue-and-lump, so that students cannot lean on answers they have met before; a case with no obvious folk intuition, or one I can generate on the day out of the participants themselves, would be ideal.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the design's claims about what participants will feel and do, and single out the one point about which you are least sure.
