Please answer this as a professional philosopher with a specialist's interest in the logic of generalisation, rather than as an engineer or a statistician, and think it through carefully before you commit to a verdict.

A colleague in computer science runs studies of concept learning. In each task a subject is shown a small set of labelled drawings and must then sort further drawings correctly. His human subjects mostly agree with one another and with the answer he had in mind. Current models, though, often produce a rule that sorts every labelled example exactly as his labels do but is plainly not the rule he intended; when he marks these wrong, the models and some of his students object that a rule fitting all the data cannot be wrong. He also concedes, when pressed, that in building the tasks he sometimes sets aside drawings that fit his own rule because they feel wrong to him.

He has asked me for a verdict, and I would like your help in giving him one. Can fitting every example be the whole of correctness here? If not, what makes the intended rule the right one, and what is the disagreement really about? He wants something he can defend in a paper, so a shrug will not do, and I would like at least one concrete illustration of a rule that fits all the data and is nonetheless the wrong rule. Write in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables; a letter is the register I want.
