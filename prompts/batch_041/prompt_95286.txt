I am leading a single seventy-five-minute session in an interdisciplinary graduate seminar — the room will contain students from law, public policy, and economics, two philosophers, and no one who has taken a course in value theory. There is no reading; nothing is assigned before or after, and no one will look anything up during the session. I will put one thing on the board and build everything from it. My subject is a position in axiology that I want to state precisely here, because it is the only content the audience will have, and because it is not the version of the view they would find if they went looking:

(i) Suffering and enjoyment are both genuine values, and they are commensurable in the weak sense that it always makes sense to ask whether some amount of suffering is worse than some amount of enjoyment is good. (ii) The comparison is not, however, a single scale of exchange. There is a threshold of intensity. Suffering at or above that intensity is lexically prior to enjoyment: any instance of it is worse than any amount of enjoyment, however large, and no quantity of enjoyment can compensate for it. (iii) Suffering below the threshold is traded off against enjoyment at an ordinary rate, so minor annoyances can be outweighed. (iv) The threshold is a feature of the value ordering, not of anyone's psychology, attention, or capacity to notice.

Do not explain the position to me. Plan how I should explain it. Tell me the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, and identify the load-bearing step — the one the audience must accept for the rest to land, and what I should do if they balk there. Then tell me the one example that should carry the whole session: what it must have and what it must lack to display the threshold structure rather than merely illustrate that suffering is bad. I had assumed I would use the obvious case — one person tortured for a year against an astronomically large number of people each having a mildly pleasant afternoon — so tell me whether that can carry it, and if not, what it lacks and what to use instead.

Map the misconceptions the audience will bring or form, in the order they will surface, and mark the exact point in my sequence where each must be headed off: I expect that lexical priority will be heard as diminishing returns, that the threshold will be taken as an empirical fact about human beings, and that the whole thing will be filed as ordinary negative utilitarianism with a caveat. Say which of these will actually occur and which I am wrong to worry about. Tell me where in the sequence the objection that a lexical ordering cannot be represented by a real-valued function belongs, and whether to raise it at all. Tell me what to refuse to settle at this level, and say so out loud in the room.

Finish with one question I can put to the room at the end that discriminates between someone who has understood the threshold structure and someone who has merely learned to say that agony beats pleasure. Do not supply readings, names, or citations of any kind; assume the audience gets none. Write in American English.
