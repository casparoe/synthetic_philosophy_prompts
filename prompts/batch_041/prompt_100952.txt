I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been arguing with editors and other writers about whether a platform is ever right to take a piece of writing down. The argument I keep losing to goes like this: speech should be left alone because argument is a marketplace. Let every claim be offered, let readers bid, and the true ones will outcompete the false; the answer to bad speech is more speech. My friends pair this with the harm principle — the only legitimate ground for restriction is harm to others, and being misled or offended does not count, so the market will clean up whatever looks dangerous — and they use the pair to argue that almost nothing should ever be removed.

I find the picture persuasive in the abstract and shaky in practice, and I want to know how far it actually holds as an argument rather than as a mood. Lay out the correspondence for me: who or what is the seller, the buyer, the good on offer, the price, the competition, the clearing mechanism, and the referee. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. A real market needs a currency, a way for buyers to tell one good from another, and a feedback signal that punishes bad wares; I suspect speech has none of the three, but I want the disanalogies that actually matter for the intended conclusion separated from the ones that are merely decorative. Does the conclusion — that restriction is self-defeating and we should let the market run — survive once the breaks are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? And if the market fails as a picture, what better image is there for what free argument actually does, and does the harm principle still draw its line where my friends say it does?
