I'm not an academic, I did a completely unrelated degree and I've just been reading Freud on my own since a friend lent me a couple of his books, so if I've got any of this wrong then say so, I'd definately rather know. The way I understand his line on religion: it's a wish-fulfilment thing, we invent a father in the sky because we're helpless in front of nature and death and we can't bear how much being civilised asks us to give up; religious ritual is basically the collective version of the hand-washing and counting that obsessional patients do; and underneath the whole thing there's the story about the sons ganging up on the primal father, with the guilt from that getting handed down. And he plainly thought religion was on the way out, a childhood thing that humanity would grow out of as it got more rational.

What I'd like from you is careful speculation about what he'd have made of things that only came up after he died. He got out of Vienna and died not long afterwards, so most of the twentieth century happened without him. I dont mean vague stuff, I mean the specific developments where I honestly cannot guess what he'd say:

1. The scientists who study religion now mostly dont think it's a comfort we invent or a guilt we carry. They think it drops out of ordinary quirks of the mind: we're over-eager to see agents behind events, we love patterns, we copy whatever the adults around us do when we're small. Does that help his case or undercut it? It gets you religion without needing fathers or guilt at all.

2. Religion didn't die. It drained away in western Europe but went gangbusters in Africa, Latin America and parts of Asia, and often in the loud ecstatic singing-in-tongues form rather than the quiet compulsive-ritual form his comparison fits. And it barged back into politics in a lot of places. Does he change the timetable, or the theory?

3. Loads of people now say they're spiritual rather than religious. Yoga, mindfulness apps, crystals, therapy-speak about finding your real self and being kind to your inner child. Is that religion in different clothes, or the opposite, or something his framework just can't get a grip on?

4. There's a new wave of research where a single dose of a psychedelic gives people an overwhelming feeling of being at one with everything, and they come away calling it the most meaningful experience of their lives. He has a passage where he takes seriously the idea that this kind of feeling of being continuous with the whole world is the root of religion, and then more or less shrugs it off as something left over from infancy. Would the research budge him, or would he just file it as more wish-fulfilment?

5. His last book on Moses seems to argue that there's a real murder buried under the story, that Moses wasn't who the story says he was, and that the memory of it somehow got passed down to later generations and turned into guilt. That sits oddly next to the its-all-an-illusion line, since it makes religion rest on something that actually happened. When those two Freuds come apart, which one wins?

6. He died just as the worst of the century was starting, and his own family got caught up in it. Does what came afterwards make him look like a prophet of the death drive, or does it just make the whole religion-as-infantile-comfort story look too tidy?

For each one I want: what you think he'd actually say, which of his own commitments the answer is hanging on, and the places where two of his ideas would pull him in opposite directions. Be honest about where he'd have nothing to go on and would just be guessing, and about anything on the list that you think flatly falsifies something he claimed while he was alive. If religion is still going strong a hundred years later, was he simply wrong that it would fade, and would he have owned up to it? Dont bother telling me which book or page anything comes from, I'm not writing an essay for anyone, I just want the argument. And say plainly where you think the whole framework creaks.
