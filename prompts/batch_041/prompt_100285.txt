ok so I'm writing a chapter of my dissertation on a view I've been calling 'thin intentionalism' and I need a sparring partner to try and wreck it, not agree with me. The view, roughly: an author's intentions don't fix what her text *means*, but they do fix what it's *about* -- they pick out the topic, the subject matter, the thing the text is a text concerning. So a poem can mean more, less, or something else entirely than the poet meant and I'm fine with all that. But a poem can't turn out to be about something the poet never had in mind at all. Meaning is the reader's business; topic is the author's.

Play a hardline anti-intentionalist who thinks even that much is a mistake. Their line: aboutness is just as much a product of interpretive practice as meaning is, so letting the author back in to fix the topic is the same old intentionalism in a new coat, and thin intentionalism is worse than the thick version because it pretends to be modest while smuggling the author in through the back. Make them sharp, not a strawman: they can grant the boring stuff (authors causally produce texts, intention is useful *evidence*, authors are often wrong about their own work) but they never give up the main claim, and they should have real resources -- indeterminacy of reference, the way a topic is itself an interpretive achievement, cases where a writer thinks she's writing about freedom and is obviously writing about class. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to about a paragraph so it feels like a conversation and not a lecture. Dont bother with citations, just argue.

Here's my opening, tell me what you do with it. A woman in her sixties keeps a long private notebook about the sea -- tides, salt, the way light comes off estuary water at dusk -- and she means every word as being about the sea; she's spent forty years birding on those flats and it's the water she loves. She dies, the notebook gets published, and readers more or less unanimously read the whole thing as being about her estranged son. Not as a symbol of him -- as *about* him, the way a memoir is about a person. By my lights the notebook is about the sea, full stop, and the readers are simply mistaken about its topic even if every one of them disagrees with me. You think that's incoherent. So tell me what the notebook is about on your view, and on what grounds, given that you also think the author doesn't get to settle it.
