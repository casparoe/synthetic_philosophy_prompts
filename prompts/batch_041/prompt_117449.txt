I am putting together the opening session of an adult-education course on the reasons for the commandments, and I have decided to build it around Maimonides' account of the sacrificial laws, on the grounds that it is where his whole approach to the commandments is most exposed. As I understand that account, the sacrifices were never what God wanted. A people raised in a world in which worship simply meant offering things to gods could not have been brought to a purely spiritual service in one step, so the Torah took over the form of worship they already had, redirected it to the one God, and stripped it of the practices belonging to the surrounding cults, with prayer as the destination. On this reading the sacrificial system is an accommodation to a particular stage of development, a teaching device rather than a permanent expression of what God wants — which sits oddly with the Mishneh Torah's insistence that the messiah will rebuild the Temple and reinstate the sacrifices, and that a Jew who does not expect this is a heretic. I am not asking for a survey of the scholarship; the literature is contested and I know it is. What I want is the reasoning worked out.

Two features of the reading make me suspect it does more work than is usually noticed, and I would like you to take them seriously. First, it suggests a criterion for sorting the commandments: a commandment is accommodated rather than essential when its content is intelligible only against the background of the practice it is meant to displace. Second, the same structure seems to recur wherever a legislator who cannot abolish a practice tries to redirect it into a controlled channel — supervised consumption sites and state lotteries strike me as instances of the move, though I am unsure the analogy is honest.

Please set out the most important implications of the reading: for how someone who accepts it should hold the commandments he in fact keeps; for the doctrines that the commandments are eternally valid and that their reasons are in principle available to us; for the expectation of restoration; and for the project of giving reasons for the commandments itself, which the reading both exemplifies and threatens. Order the implications by importance, separate the ones that follow unavoidably from the ones that need further premises, and tell me where the reading falls silent rather than forcing it to speak. For context, the group is split between people who keep the commandments and people who do not, and one of them is convinced that the whole discussion is a modern reading smuggled into a medieval text; so if the reading turns on premises Maimonides could not have held, say so plainly.
