At a summer ethics seminar I attended, the instructor introduced a position he called lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and summarized it roughly as follows: "Negative utilitarianism holds that suffering matters more than happiness, and the lexical-threshold version adds that below some level of well-being the two are not commensurable, so no amount of pleasure, however great, can offset the suffering. It follows that a moment of mild discomfort outweighs any amount of happiness, that a life containing suffering is not worth living, and that the rational goal would be to end all sentient life as quickly as possible."

I suspect this is a caricature, since the phrase "lexical threshold" seems to be doing work the summary ignores, but I don't know the position well enough to say where it goes wrong. Could you audit the summary clause by clause? Which parts would a defender of such a view accept, which are subtle distortions — for instance about what the threshold marks, about what is being weighed against what, and about whether a lexical ranking is the same thing as the two being incommensurable — and which are simply false? And if the accurate parts are retained, does anything about whether life is worth living, or about ending it, actually follow? I would also value a short account of how the view bears on the general question of whether pleasure and pain are commensurable, since I take that to be the crux.
