I read the rationalist blogs, so I know the standard warnings about metrics — Goodhart's and Campbell's laws, the cobra effect, the whole gallery of agents who hit a target by cheating it. The pattern I keep running into isn't that, and I can't find a name for it.

The shape: a statistic goes up. Nobody cheats, and the statistic is honest — audit every number and you'll find it correctly computed from real cases. But the rise comes from a change in which cases the statistic is computed over, not from any change in the performance it was meant to track. And the change in membership is produced by ordinary local decisions, made by people with no view of the aggregate and no stake in the number.

Two instances. A hospital's reported mortality falls because the sickest patients are increasingly transferred out — every transfer defensible on clinical grounds, the rate a true rate over the patients actually treated, the improvement entirely a fact about a population that got easier. A support team's average first-response time falls because agents clear the easy tickets first and let the hard ones sit; the unanswered ones aren't in the average yet, the average is correct, and the growing backlog is precisely the thing the metric was a proxy for.

The general form, as best I can state it: a true statistic over a drifting set, where the drift is uncoordinated rather than strategic, and the drift happens to flatter. Proxy and target come apart with no one doing anything they can be criticized for.

What I want is the carving, not a label. Does this have a name? If it's case-mix drift, or a selection effect, or cream-skimming, say which and say what that name commits me to that my description doesn't — cream-skimming seems to require intent, and the hospital isn't the one doing the selecting. If nothing fits, coin a term and define it against Goodhart and against survivorship bias, with the boundary cases marked: does the phenomenon survive someone eventually noticing and steering the set deliberately, and does it require that the damage be invisible in the metric itself, or is that just an artifact of my two examples?
