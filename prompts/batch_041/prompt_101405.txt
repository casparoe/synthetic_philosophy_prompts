A friend of mine — a lapsed machine-learning researcher who now reads more philosophy than code — has spent the better part of a year sorting every cognitive system he meets into the four-rung ladder that Dennett used to draw, and he has been tiresomely consistent about it. The rungs, as he tells them. Darwinian: the creature learns nothing in its own lifetime, its repertoire is fixed at birth, and the only test its "hypotheses" ever face is whether it and its lineage survive. Skinnerian: it can learn within its lifetime by emitting behaviour and keeping what the world rewards, so the loop closes in the world rather than in the head. Popperian: it can run the test inside itself first — entertain a course of action, follow it out to its consequences, and discard it unperformed, so that the hypotheses die in its place rather than it dying in theirs. Gregorian (Dennett's term, after the psychologist Richard Gregory): it does its testing through tools, artefacts and culturally transmitted thinking aids, so that a good part of the generate-and-test loop runs through things outside its own skin.

His verdicts, each of which he will defend at length:

- a bacterium swimming up a sugar gradient: Darwinian;
- a pigeon that learns to peck a key for grain and can then be shifted onto a different reinforcement schedule: Skinnerian;
- a New Caledonian crow given a puzzle it has never seen, which appears to work out a two-step solution and abandons a first attempt without ever carrying it out: Popperian;
- a schoolchild doing long multiplication with pencil and paper, the carries living on the page rather than in her head: Gregorian;
- a spider spinning an orb web: Darwinian, and emphatically not Gregorian, the web being a product of the creature rather than a tool the creature thinks with.

Now the case he has been dodging. Take a present-day large language model: asked a hard question, it writes out a chain of reasoning in its context window, calls a calculator where it is permitted to, and then answers.

What I want is not so much the label as the rule he is running, and the other rules that fit his earlier verdicts just as well. Work out, in particular, whether he is sorting by the highest rung a system can reach at all, or by the highest rung actually doing the causal work in the episode in front of him. Say what each of those two readings predicts for the language model; say which of them his earlier verdicts genuinely support and which they leave open; then give me your best guess at the verdict he will actually give, with your reasons.

Then give me the case that would settle it: one further creature or system, described precisely enough to be judged, on which the two readings must disagree, so that his answer to it would betray which rule he is using. And if you think the real ambiguity lies elsewhere — in what counts as a tool for the Gregorian rung, say, or in whether the ladder ranks individual organisms or lineages — say so instead, and give me the discriminating case for that reading.

Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each of your claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of. Please answer in British English.
