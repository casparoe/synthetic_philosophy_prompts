I have been asked to run a session at the university decision-theory reading group, which for the past term has been working through the literature on Pascal's mugging, infinite ethics, and the limits of expected-value maximization, and I want a Q&A prep sheet before I go. The audience is unusually mixed, so I need objections calibrated to them rather than to the internet. There are three decision theorists who are committed expected-value maximizers and treat Pascal's mugging as a solved embarrassment; they will reach for bounded utility, for the claim that the offending prior is illegitimate, or for the claim that unbounded stakes cannot exist at all. There are two ethicists: one a Kantian who rejects aggregation on principle, and one a population ethicist who is convinced that the utility monster and the repugnant conclusion are the same animal wearing different clothes. There is a philosopher of probability who is openly skeptical that interpersonal welfare comparisons are even well-defined. And there are half a dozen people from the local rationalist meetup who have read the Sequences and will want to take the discussion to the meta level. My thesis is that the utility monster is not an objection to consequentialism but an objection to one dispensable component of the standard consequentialist procedure, namely treating the social objective as the unweighted sum of an unbounded individual welfare scale evaluated under a heavy-tailed prior, and that once you recast the monster as a Pascal's-mugging problem in the social setting (Pascal's mugging being the argument that a tiny probability of an enormous payoff can dominate expected value), the ethical objections aimed at the monster miss their target, while the problem that survives turns out to bite every decision rule and not just the utilitarian ones. My constructive proposal, which is the part I expect to get hammered, is to pass each individual's contribution to the social objective through a bounded increasing function and to calibrate the cap by a procedure I lay out in the talk, and I claim this preserves Pareto, anonymity, and the intuitively correct verdicts in ordinary cases while defusing the monster. The toy case I use to motivate this is a population of a billion people with ordinary bounded welfare plus one agent whose welfare is ordinary with overwhelming probability but, with a tiny probability, scales superlinearly in the resources it consumes, so that expected-value maximization hands it essentially everything under any prior with a sufficiently heavy tail. Please give me the eight or so objections this room will actually raise, not the ones a generic ethics class would raise, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do to the talk, each phrased the way the person who will raise it would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a blunt note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front so that I do not look like I am dodging, which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, where the genuine crux sits in each disagreement as opposed to the proxy fight, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
