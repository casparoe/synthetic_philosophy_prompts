I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, and this year I want to close the unit with Kurosawa's Rashomon rather than with yet another journal case study. My students will have read the usual positions: the conciliatory or equal-weight view, on which learning that an epistemic peer disagrees obliges me to move some way toward their opinion; the steadfast view, on which I may hold my ground if I can point to an asymmetry between us; and the third, total-evidence line, on which the disagreement is just one more item in the ledger to be weighed against the first-order reasons rather than something that mechanically halves my confidence. What I want from you is a reading of the film that is faithful in both directions — one that does not reduce Rashomon to an illustration of a position, and one that does not bend the epistemology until the film comes out looking prescient.

What I keep circling is that the film is saturated with disagreement and yet seems to contain not a single pair of peers. The bandit's testimony, the wife's, the dead samurai's through the medium, and finally the woodcutter's withheld fifth account come from parties who differ in what they saw, in what they have at stake, and in what they are willing to admit; and the woodcutter, who had been our best candidate for the disinterested witness, turns out to have had his own reason to conceal what he saw. So I would like you to work through what the conciliationist debate takes for granted and the film withholds: the independence requirement, on which I may not let my own first-order reasoning be the very thing that answers the peer's objection; the notion of higher-order evidence and of a disagreement as a defeater; the assumption of a shared evidence base and shared priors, without which splitting the difference has no determinate target; and the deeper assumption that there is a single determinate fact of the matter for both parties to be right or wrong about. Ask, too, what kind of disagreement the film actually stages — factual, normative (the men disagree about what honour required quite as much as about who did what), or the faultless sort that relativists about taste describe — and whether any of these is the kind the literature is really about. And tell me what the film's form contributes: it has no neutral camera, so we are never placed in the position of the ideal observer whose evidence the parties supposedly share, and I suspect this is not incidental but the whole point.

Finally, I want your verdict. Does Rashomon refute the presuppositions of the conciliation-versus-steadfast debate, or does it merely show that lived disagreement is messier than the idealized cases on which the debate runs — and if the latter, which side, if any, the film favours once the disanalogies are admitted. Say also what becomes of the argument when the parties themselves disagree about how much weight disagreement should carry, since that regress seems to me to threaten both views equally. End with what the film shows that the arguments alone cannot: whether the last movement at the gate — the woodcutter taking up the abandoned child after the priest's faith in men has been shaken — resolves the epistemological problem or quietly changes the subject. I have no use for citations or a reading list; I want the reading.
