You are grading a short essay answer in a third-year undergraduate course on consequentialism. The question is worth 20 marks. The question and the student's answer are below.

Question:
Consider the following case. Priya has £100. She can either donate it to a reliable charity that will fund a cataract operation for one person, or spend it on a birthday present for her friend. She knows the immediate effects of both options with near-certainty. Both options will also set off an indefinitely long chain of further effects—call this the open-tail feature—on markets, on her friend's character, on future generations, and so on, which she cannot survey and to which she cannot assign stable probabilities. Explain the problem of consequentialist cluelessness as it arises in this case. Does it show that Priya cannot be guided by consequentialism? In your answer, distinguish the epistemic problem from any metaphysical problem, and assess at least one proposed response.

Student's answer:
Consequentialist cluelessness is the problem that, in a causally complex world, we cannot know the full consequences of our actions. Priya faces the open-tail feature: the chain of consequences is open-ended, so she cannot even list all the relevant outcomes, let alone assign them probabilities. This means the expected value of donating and the expected value of buying the present are both undefined.

This is not merely an epistemic problem. If the expected values are undefined, then there is no fact of the matter about which action has better consequences. Consequentialism therefore cannot say that either action is right or wrong; it is a metaphysical problem about the structure of value, not just a limit on our knowledge.

One response is to distinguish simple from complex cluelessness. Simple cluelessness is uncertainty about which outcome will occur; complex cluelessness is uncertainty about the probabilities themselves. The response says that simple cluelessness is manageable by expected-value reasoning, but complex cluelessness is not, because there is no stable probability distribution to use. This response therefore concedes that cluelessness is a serious problem for act consequentialism.

A second response is to restrict consequentialism to foreseeable consequences. Rule consequentialism does this by asking which rules generally have good consequences, rather than which particular acts do. Since Priya can foresee that donating to the charity will fund a cataract operation, she has enough to act on. The open-tail consequences are irrelevant to the rule.

I conclude that cluelessness is a serious challenge to act consequentialism, but not to consequentialism as such, because rule consequentialism avoids it.
