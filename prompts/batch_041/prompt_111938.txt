I teach an upper-level seminar in social and political philosophy — 16 students, one 90-minute session, no preparation expected. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel both the pull and the instability of the idea that the economic base explains everything else. The mechanics should carry it: two or three rounds of a small economy with deliberately unequal endowments, in which a chamber elected by whoever holds the most production points writes the rules for the next round, and whose members remain players in the economy, so that their interests and their stated principles can come apart; a payoff currency that is never money but only the right to set the next round's rules or to reallocate endowments; and no framing lecture — base and superstructure should not be named until the debrief. Participants should first find that the rules reliably track economic power and then, in the final round, meet an outcome that the distribution of endowments does not predict. The debrief should map what happened onto three distinct explanatory questions — why an institution has the content it does, why it persists, and why it changes — and onto the manoeuvre of conceding the superstructure a relative autonomy while still calling economic relations determining only in the last instance. Give me the roles, materials, and timeline, plus a frank note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it; be honest as well that a 90-minute game can make the distinction between base and superstructure feel indispensable and unstable at once, but cannot settle whether it is well-formed, and that the last-instance formula will still look like a hedge rather than a thesis when the session ends.
