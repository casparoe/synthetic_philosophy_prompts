I want a consistency check on my position on risk before I commit it to a policy memo, and I would like you to be the auditor rather than the advocate. Here is where I have landed. (1) Where an activity threatens serious or irreversible harm, lack of full scientific certainty is no reason to postpone cost-effective protective measures. (2) The doing/allowing distinction carries no intrinsic moral weight: failing to prevent a harm I could have prevented is, other things equal, as bad as bringing it about. (3) In my own life I am an expected-value maximiser and will take any bet with positive expected value, however the odds are stacked, provided I can take it repeatedly. (4) For catastrophic or irreversible risks, however, I would use a maximin rule — choose the option whose worst outcome is least bad — rather than maximise expected value. (5) The burden of proof should rest on those who want to introduce a new technology, not on those who would restrict it. (6) I am unmoved by the complaint that precaution itself creates risks, that suppressing a technology may cost lives, since those counterfactual harms are just the sort of speculation the principle tells me to discount.

Tell me which of these are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say which commitment has to give and what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs. I am not attached to any particular one of the six, so do not assume I would sacrifice (1) or (3) rather than the rest, and be explicit about the pairs where a careless reader would cry contradiction but the commitments can in fact be held together. Please write in British English.
