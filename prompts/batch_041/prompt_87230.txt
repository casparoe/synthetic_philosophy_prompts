I am assembling the closing session of a graduate seminar on adaptationism, and I would like to end it with a poem rather than with a summary of the debate. I would be grateful if you would write one.

Let me set out the material, since the poem should not depend on the reader's prior familiarity with the literature. The critique I have in mind takes its name and its central image from an architectural fact: when a round dome is set on a square base carried by round arches, the geometry forces a set of roughly triangular surfaces into existence between the arches and beneath the dome. Nobody chose them, and their number and shape are fixed by the structure that was chosen; they are what the architecture leaves over. The builders of such churches decorated those surfaces with mosaics, and a later visitor looking up is likely to conclude that the surfaces were made in order to carry the mosaics, so that the mosaics explain the architecture, when in fact the architecture explains the mosaics. The argument usually credited to Gould and Lewontin applies this figure to living things: many traits that are confidently explained as adaptations, as features selected because they do some job, may be byproducts of constraints of structure, development, or history, with the job read into them after the fact. Explanations of that kind tend to take the shape of a story told in the past tense, in which a use is narrated as though it were a cause.

The debate does not stop there, and neither should the poem. Three further moves matter. First, a byproduct can be recruited for a real use: a capacity that could not have been selected for, because it appeared too recently in the history of the species, may nonetheless be indispensable now. Second, the figure can be abused — calling something a byproduct is an easy way to decline to explain it, and someone who says "byproduct" to every proposed function is not reasoning any better than the person who finds a purpose in everything. Third, and this is the one I find most interesting, the byproduct's continued presence can itself be a choice: the arches might have been built differently, or the leftover surfaces engineered away, and the fact that they were kept can be an adaptation in its own right, so that the very thing offered as an example of non-selection may be evidence of selection.

Here is what I am asking for. Write a ballad of six or more quatrains in ballad measure — four lines to the stanza, alternating iambic tetrameter and iambic trimeter, with the second and fourth lines rhyming. Each stanza should close on a refrain, and the refrain must be word-for-word identical every time it appears; it should be a whole line, not a fragment. The refrain carries the argument, so let me be exact about what it has to do. On its first appearance it should read as an expression of the assumption that everything exists for a purpose, as something a person might sincerely and even movingly say. On its last appearance the same words should read as a statement of the opposite: that the thing in question is there because the structure required it. Nothing in the refrain may change; only the stanzas around it may change, and the change must be earned in the lines between.

Beyond the refrain, the poem should contain at least one moment in which a byproduct is shown being put to a use it could not have been selected for, and at least one moment in which a character uses the thought that something is only a byproduct as a way of avoiding a question, with the poem making clear that this is a dodge rather than a discovery. If the third move described above, the byproduct kept on purpose, can be brought in without crowding the poem, I would welcome it.

I want a scene rather than an argument: a place, at least two voices, something that happens or is shown. I am not asking for a lecture in verse, and I am not asking the poem to settle the question; a reader should finish it with both readings still live. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the debate finds nothing to wince at, and self-contained enough that someone who has never heard of it finds a good poem.

A few constraints on presentation. Write in American English. No headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicized labels, no tables. If you want to say something about the choices you made, put it in a single short paragraph of plain prose after the poem; the poem itself should not need notes, names, or apparatus to be understood.
