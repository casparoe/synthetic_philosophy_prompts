I teach a small third-year seminar that mixes game theory, proofs and a bit of decision theory — 14 students, one 80-minute session, no computers in the room, and I've been trying to find a way to do the strategy-stealing argument that isn't just me drawing a Hex board and narrating the proof while they nod. What I want them to actually feel is the weird thing at the bottom of it: that you can be certain the first player has a winning strategy in a game, in the sense of being able to prove it, while having no idea what that strategy is and no way to find out from the proof itself. And I want the sharper bit underneath that, that the whole trick leans on one premise — that having extra resources can never hurt you — and that the moment you drop that premise the argument dies. So: design me a runnable 80-minute session, probably pairs or groups of three with some plenary at the end, that gets them to the contradiction themselves before anyone says the name of the argument. Materials I'm happy to print: Hex grids (I was thinking 5x5 for the tournament and then something like 8x8 or 9x9), a few Chomp bars as paper grids, red and blue pens, index cards, and some sealed envelopes if you want them. My rough thought, which you should feel free to throw out, is that they first play enough small games to notice that nobody ever draws, and that they run a separate little test of whether being handed a free extra stone ever actually hurts a player — both of which they can check by playing, not by being told. Then, once those two things are in the room, arrange it so they're forced to ask what follows if the *second* player had a winning strategy, and let them run that down to the contradiction in their own words. The Chomp boards are there because I want a second game where the same move works but where, unlike small Hex, nobody in the room is going to be able to produce the actual winning first move, and I want them to sit with how galling that is.

So what I need from you: the rules and win conditions, the exact setup and materials, a timeline with rough minutes, what the facilitator does and does not say at each stage, and a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no money. Two constraints on the staging. First, don't let it become a puzzle-solving session where the "win" is finding a strategy — if they spend the whole hour hunting for a Hex strategy they'll fail and get bored, so build in a hard cutoff and something that turns the group from players into reasoners. Second, the debrief has to map specific moments from the activity onto specific parts of the argument — the no-draw step, the monotonicity step, the hypothetical second-player strategy, the quantifier flip — and it has to distinguish between what they established by playing a bunch of boards and what the proof actually licenses for all board sizes, since the small-board evidence doesn't give them the general result and I don't want them confusing the two. I'd also like a short closing hook: people around me in AI-adjacent circles throw around the phrase strategy stealing for agents in the world, roughly the thought that a capable enough agent can just adopt whatever strategy is working for someone else, and I'd like the last five minutes to ask whether the thing they just proved transfers to that or whether the resemblance is superficial — but I don't want the activity to depend on that framing, so keep it as an optional coda.

And be straight with me about two things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — my guess is they'll hand-wave the extra-stone-can't-hurt premise because it feels obvious in Hex, and I'll get at least one student insisting that we've now found a move or that the empty envelope makes the whole thing a trick — and give me the facilitator moves for each. Then tell me honestly which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply from the front: I suspect it's that they'll leave with the existence result but no felt sense of what it's like to know a winning move exists and be unable to name it, and maybe that they'll walk away thinking strategy stealing is constructive when in Chomp it famously isn't. If you think a misère variant where the player who completes a connection loses is a better way to break the monotonicity premise in front of them, say so and work it in, but don't hand me results about it — I don't want to assert anything I haven't checked, so keep it as a thing for them to test.
