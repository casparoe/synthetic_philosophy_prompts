Right, so our political economy reading group has been going round in circles on the socialist calculation debate for what feels like a year now, and I want a proper diagnosis rather than the same two moves replayed. Roughly how it goes: one side says Lange and the market-socialism crowd basically answered Mises — fine, no real market for capital goods, but you can have a Central Planning Board do trial-and-error on prices, adjust on excess demand, and you land on the same allocation as a competitive equilibrium, so the "calculation problem" is a solved technical problem and the Austrians just retreated to grumbling about incentives and politics. The other side says that's a bait-and-switch: the point was never "can a set of equilibrium prices exist" but whether any real process can generate and use knowledge that's dispersed, tacit and constantly changing, and an equilibrium you'd only reach with a benevolent omniscient auctioneer solves nothing.

My own suspicion — and I'd like it tested rather than just nodded at — is that these are three seperate arguements wearing one name: one about existence of a solution (pure GE / welfare economics), one about institutional epistemology (can a process discover and transmit the relevant knowledge), and one about incentives and motivation. If that's right, then alot of the "stalling" is people winning one of those three and claiming the whole thing, and the impasse is partly manufactured.

But here's the bit I genuinely can't sort out and want you to push hard on: is the middle one — the epistemic/institutional one — a conceptual claim or an empirical one? Because if the calculation problem is really a claim that the planner's problem is informationally or computationally intractable, it starts to look like an empirical claim about what processes can do, which in principle could shift as our tools shift (I keep seeing people say modern computing settles it one way or the other, which strikes me as way too quick). And if it's conceptual instead, what concept is doing the work — is "tacit knowledge" meant to be a deep fact about minds, or just a description of where technology happens to be right now? There's a related fork I keep tripping over: allocating given wants versus discovering wants and techniques in the first place.

So: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, engage with what each camp really says, and be honest about what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or whether none should be expected and why.
