I'm closing out a graduate seminar on pronatalism and population size. We spent the term on whether a larger population is a good thing — the total view that sums welfare over everyone who ever exists, the average view that asks only how well off the people there are, and the policy arguments that quietly lean on one or the other. For the last session I want to end with a poem whose form does the arguing for me, and I'd like you to write it.

The form is a poem that grows. One line in the first stanza, two in the second, three in the third, and so on through six, so that it keeps adding. Two rules govern the additions. First, every added line must be good on its own — nothing that reads as filler, nothing that is there only to make the count come out. Second, the poem must get worse as it gets bigger: by the last stanza it should feel diluted, and a reader should be unable to point to the line that spoiled it, because no single line did.

Against that, set a counter-voice that refuses to grow: one line, unchanged in wording and in length, returning between the stanzas. It is the voice that says the size of the population is not what matters, only how the people in it fare, and that it would rather there were fewer of them and that they were better off. It should be serious rather than smug, and it should not be refuted.

End without settling it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who has thought hard about population ethics recognizes both positions as themselves — the growing poem as the view that more good lives make a better world, the fixed line as the view that keeps its eye on the average — and would not wince at either. Don't name either position, don't name any philosopher, and don't explain the poem once it ends. Begin with the poem itself.
