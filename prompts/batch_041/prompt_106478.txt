I've been running wine tastings for about eight years now — I'm not a philosopher, I just sell the stuff — and I've got myself into a proper knot over something. For our serious tastings we always go blind: foil over the bottle, no labels, nothing. That's the whole point, right? Otherwise people just taste the price tag and the year. But I've also read enough pop-psych to know that what you expect changes what you actually taste, not just what you're willing to say about it. So now I can't see how both can be true at once. If expectation is already inside the tasting, what exactly is the foil supposed to be protecting? Someone told me there's a name for the second thing — cognitive penetration, I think? — and that philosophers argue about whether it's even real.

What I want from you: work out what the practice of blind tasting actually presupposes about perception and about belief. Does it need perception to be a seperate stage that beleif only touches afterwards, so it can be "cleaned up" by taking the label away? Does it need there to be a fact of the matter about how the wine tastes that's independent of the taster? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and could someone who thinks perception is thoroughly penetrated by expectation still defend blind tasting as a method, or is the method incoherent on that view? I'm also suspicious that we're not consistent — the same trade that swears by blind tasting is the one that says a great glass in bad company tastes worse, and nobody sees a contradiction there. Tell me whether the practice strictly presupposes one particular view or is really underdetermined, so that two or three different theories would all license doing it. And don't just give me the tidy version — separate the bits that people who actually work on this agree about from the bits that are genuinely still contested, and don't pass a live dispute off as the consensus.

Skip the reading list, I'll never get to it; I just want the argument. Keep it fairly plain, I don't know the jargon. And put the short answer on the very last line as \answer{...} with a phrase of a few words naming the view you think the practice presupposes.
