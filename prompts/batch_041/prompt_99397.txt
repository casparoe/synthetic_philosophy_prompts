I'm on the interpretive content team at a mid-sized natural history museum — we write the labels, the audio-guide scripts, and the little "did you know" cards, and we turn over maybe forty or fifty of these a month, so it's the kind of thing where I can't stop and have a full philosophical crisis every single time. The recurring headache is functional language. A draft will say the crest is for attracting mates, or this gut enzyme evolved to break down a particular plant compound, or the wing markings function as a warning signal, and then our curatorial consultant (who did a philosphy of biology minor and never lets us forget it) sends back comments saying we're smuggling purpose into nature and that half our "for"s are unfalsifiable. I've read enough to know there are basically two big camps here — the one that says a trait's function is whatever it was selected for over its evolutionary history, and the one that says a function is just the causal role a part plays in keeping the system doing what it does, history be damned — and I genuinely can't tell which one our house style should commit to, or whether we can get away with quietly not committing.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my writers can actually run on a draft label: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where it tells us flat out to cut the functional language, and where it has to hand the call to a human because it's a values question rather than a science one. It has to survive contact with the annoying cases we actually get: traits that were selected for one job but now do another (feathers keep being cited at me as the classic), traits that do something useful today with no reason to think they were selected for it, traits that were selected for something they no longer do at all, and — the one that scares me — claims about human traits where saying what something is "for" is politically loaded and somebody is going to be angry whichever way we go. Then actually run it on three drafts: a bird crest we want to describe as being for mate attraction, a human appendix label, and one where we'd be calling a behavioral difference between the sexes an adaptation. Be honest about where the procedure is just doing word-police work versus where it's genuinely settling something, and tell me which of my three it's going to give an answer I won't like. Don't worry about references or citing anyone, I'll add the scholarly backup myself once I know what position we're taking.
