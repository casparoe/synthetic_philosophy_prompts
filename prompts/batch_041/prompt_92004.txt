I am in a comparative history seminar, and a classmate has produced an argument that I have not been able to fault, even though I am convinced it proves far too much. She calls its central premise the Uniqueness Requirement and states it as follows: to explain why an event occurred in one case rather than in another, an explanation must name a factor that was present in the case where the event occurred and absent from the case where it did not. Her target is the question the seminar has been circling all term, sometimes under the name of the Needham problem: why did the transformation of natural knowledge and the industrial breakthrough happen in Europe rather than in China? Her argument runs: (1) The question asks why E occurred in A rather than B, so any acceptable answer must name a factor present in A and absent from B. (2) If the named factor was also present in some third case C in which E did not occur, then the factor does not explain E, since its presence evidently does not suffice for E. (3) Every candidate factor on offer fails one of these tests. Competitive states were not unique to Europe. Long-distance trade, credit instruments, and sophisticated commercial institutions flourished elsewhere. A culture of careful observation and mechanical tinkering is attested in several societies. And the institutions we are most tempted to credit, the university and the printing press, either have close analogues elsewhere or arrived too late to do the work. (4) The question therefore has no answer. (5) Nothing in the argument turns on Europe or China in particular, so the same reasoning applies to any contrastive question in history, whether it concerns the Great Divergence, the Reformation, or the origins of the First World War. (6) Since no historical case is distinguished from every other case by a single factor, contrastive historical questions are answerable only when the two cases differ in exactly one respect, and that never happens; so they are pseudo-questions. (7) The literature on the Great Divergence has therefore been asking a question that cannot have an answer.

She treats this as a discovery rather than a reductio, and offers a further consideration in its favour. The question, she says, has no stable answer because the event to be explained has no stable identity: depending on whether one dates the divergence to the eighteenth century, to the sixteenth, or to the late Middle Ages, the explanatory burden shifts entirely, and an explanation whose target can be relocated at will is not an explanation of anything. I would rather keep the conclusion that the original question is at least partly confused than accept (6), but I suspect that keeping either costs me something I do not want to pay, and I cannot see where the argument goes wrong, if it does.

For what it is worth, I am confident that contrastive questions sometimes have perfectly good answers — I know why the fire started in the kitchen rather than in the garage — which is why I think the argument proves too much. Which step should I reject? Tell me why that step looked so plausible in the first place, and then price the alternatives: if I rejected a different premise instead, what would I have to give up, and which premise is genuinely least costly to abandon?
