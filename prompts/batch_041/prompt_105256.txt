I have been trying to reconstruct the tacit policy behind a colleague's rulings on higher-order knowledge — the question, in effect, of when knowing something puts one in a position to know that one knows it, and so of whether knowing is luminous — and I would like you to predict her ruling on a case she has not yet considered. Her rulings so far are as follows.

She holds that a subject who sees a clearly red patch in good light knows the patch is red and knows that she knows it; that a subject who has just worked through a short proof of a simple arithmetical claim knows the claim and knows that she knows it; that a subject who is told a mundane fact by a trusted friend, where the friend might easily have been teasing and the subject has no way to check, knows the fact but does not know that she knows it; that a subject in pain knows she is in pain and knows that she knows it; and that a subject currently thinking about Vienna knows she is thinking about Vienna and knows that she knows it.

The untested case: a subject reads a value off a well-calibrated instrument whose workings are entirely opaque to her, in circumstances in which the reading could not easily have been wrong, and she is wholly confident in it; she knows the value. Will my colleague hold that she knows that she knows it?

Please give me, in no more than 500 words: (i) your prediction for the untested case; (ii) the candidate rules that fit all five earlier rulings — I have noticed at least a safety-flavoured one and one that turns on whether the subject has access to her own grounds, and I suspect a third that turns on felt certainty; (iii) what each candidate predicts about the untested case; (iv) your own bet among them, with your reason for preferring it; and (v) the single further case that would most cleanly separate the rules that survive.
