As a novelist, I have spent the past year writing a book whose central problem is what it is for a creature to represent something, and I keep returning to the philosophical debate over naturalizing mental content. The debate appears stalled. One camp holds that a state's content is fixed by its biological function—what it was selected to indicate; another holds that content is fixed by information—what the state reliably covaries with; a third holds that content is fixed by the state's role in the system's overall economy. Each camp has counterexamples the others cannot absorb: a perfect duplicate with no evolutionary history, a state that tracks a predator that has been extinct for a thousand years, a state that carries information about nothing because its normal cause has disappeared. My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but several: some parties are asking what makes a state a representation at all, others what gives it this content rather than that, and others what distinguishes belief from desire. Diagnose the impasse for me. What is each side actually committed to, is the disagreement verbal or substantive, and what—if anything—would count as progress: a distinction, a change of explanatory target, or an argument that no progress should be expected? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions. Please answer in American English.
