When I read work on the neuroscience of free will, I keep meeting a methodological pattern I have no name for. To locate the moment of a conscious intention, an experiment has the subject watch a fast-moving clock and report where the hand stood when the urge to act arose; to locate the moment of becoming aware of something, it has the subject report the clock position at the moment of awareness. But the report is itself an act the subject performs on purpose — monitoring the clock, registering a position, choosing to speak — and each of those has the same kind of neural buildup the experiment is trying to time. So the capacity under investigation, the subject's ability to consciously monitor and report his or her own states, is also the instrument doing the measuring. A gap of a few hundred milliseconds is then read off, and a sweeping conclusion is drawn from it: that conscious will is an afterthought, that awareness arrives late. The circularity even regresses, since checking whether the timing report is accurate would require the subject to time the report, which is another report of the same kind.

Is there an established name for this specific circularity — a self-report used to time or detect a mental event that is itself an instance of that event? It seems sharper than the generic unreliability of introspection, since the trouble is not that people report inaccurately but that the act of reporting belongs to the same class as the act reported; and it sits near, without coinciding with, the demand-characteristics worry and the homunculus objection. If a term exists, tell me how it is defined and how it parts company with those neighbors. If none does, coin one, and give me its definition and its edges — above all, when a self-report genuinely is an instance of the process it reports, and when that self-reference is harmless.
