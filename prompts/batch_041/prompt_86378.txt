You are a philosopher of science with a specialty in reasoning under uncertainty about our own place in the universe — the borderland where cosmology, astrobiology, and the philosophy of probability meet. Begin by sketching, for yourself, the candidate concepts that might be in play here, then tell me which of them survive scrutiny and why.

I am an amateur who reads a great deal about the Fermi paradox — the old observation that the galaxy ought to be full of civilizations and yet looks silent — and I keep colliding with a pattern I have no word for. Let me give you two instances.

First instance: someone argues that if intelligent life were common, at least one civilization would by now have spread across the galaxy in a way we could not miss; since we observe nothing of the kind, intelligent life must be exceedingly rare. Second instance: someone argues in the opposite direction, that the silence is exactly what one should expect if we happen to be among the earliest civilizations, since the first civilization to begin expanding would rapidly preempt the rest, and the fact that we are here at all, not yet preempted, tells us something about how early we are.

The pattern I think I detect in both is this. A conclusion about how many civilizations there are, or how long they last, is drawn from a fact about us — that we exist now, that we see nothing. And that step only goes through if we are entitled to treat ourselves as a typical or randomly selected member of some class of beings. But no one ever says which class. Are we a typical civilization? A typical life-bearing planet? A typical moment in the history of a typical observer? Typical among all observers who will ever exist? Each answer changes the arithmetic, sometimes by orders of magnitude, and in my reading the choice is made silently — and it always seems to be the choice that lets the author's conclusion come out. I have also watched writers slide from one class to another inside a single argument, so that being early quietly becomes being rare, and being rare quietly becomes being lucky, with nobody flagging the transition.

What I want to know is whether this has a name. Is there an established concept for the specific failure mode in which the load-bearing assumption is a choice about which class we are supposed to be a random member of, and that choice is made to fit the desired conclusion rather than argued for? I suspect it sits near the anthropic-principle literature, and I suspect there is also a purely statistical ancestor of the problem that has nothing to do with cosmology. What I cannot tell is whether the thing I am describing is simply that older problem, a special case of it, or something that deserves a label of its own.

So please do the following. (a) Identify the nearest established concepts. (b) Say precisely how the phenomenon I have described differs from each of them, and which, if any, fits best. (c) Tell me whether it already has a name or whether it needs one; if it needs one, propose a name and define it sharply enough that I can sort cases into those that fall under it and those that do not. (d) Give me at least one boundary case — something that resembles the pattern but should not be counted as an instance — and explain the difference.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. If specialists disagree about whether the move I am complaining about is a fallacy or a legitimate form of inference, say so plainly and do not present one side as the consensus. And if part of what I have said is confused or rests on a mistake, tell me so rather than humoring me.
