A colleague of mine who teaches decision theory has been writing up escalation episodes for a casebook, and I have been collecting her verdicts. She called an airline's decision to keep a loss-making route alive for three more years, against its own analysts' forecasts, irrational; its stated reason was that it had already spent too much on the route to abandon it now. She called a founder's decision to fund a second version of a failed product rational: the pilot data for the redesign were strong, the next build was cheap, and the money already spent played no part in the decision. She called a house-buyer's decision to bid past her own stated maximum irrational, since the buyer's reason was the money she had already paid for surveys and legal fees. She called a firm's decision to match a rival's every price cut rational, on the grounds that exiting would have ceded the market and each cut was the best response to the rival's last move. And she called a company's decision to double down on a failing product irrational even though the market later turned and the product succeeded, saying that a lucky outcome does not make the decision a good one.

Next week she is judging a case of a different shape. At a charity fundraiser, a $100 bill is auctioned under a rule that the two highest bidders each pay what they bid, and only the highest bidder takes the bill. A woman who entered knowing that rule and is now the second-highest bidder at $90 — so that if the bidding stops here she pays $90 and receives nothing — raises to $105, explaining that she has already put in $90 and cannot walk away from that now. Predict my colleague's verdict on whether that $105 bid was rational, but I am less interested in the verdict than in the work behind it: the candidate rules her earlier verdicts leave standing, which of them this new case separates and how, which one you would bet she is running and why, and what further case would settle it if her verdicts so far do not.
