I am a retired biologist who has spent the past several years reading moral philosophy, and I have been trying to make my own judgments about bringing people into existence cohere. My verdicts are these. A couple who conceive a child they know will live in severe, unremitting pain, with a life not worth living, do something gravely wrong. A couple who decline to conceive a child who would have had a rich and happy life do nothing wrong at all; they wrong no one, since there is no one to be wronged. Between two possible children, one destined for a good life and one for a still better one, there is no reason to prefer the latter, and choosing either is blameless. Yet when a happy child is in fact born, I do not treat the good her life contains as a matter of indifference: were she to die in infancy I would grieve a loss, not merely a loss to her parents. And a life that is only barely worth living I am inclined to think it wrong to start, though I cannot say why the threshold falls there rather than higher. What I cannot do is fit these verdicts together. Is there a single principle — perhaps one that treats the badness of suffering and the goodness of happiness as asymmetric with respect to creation but symmetric within a life already begun — that yields all of them? If not, which verdict should I surrender, and what does the surviving principle imply about a case I have not listed: a couple who can have only one child and must choose between one who will be happy and one who will be miserable, where choosing the happy child seems plainly right yet seems to rank possible people against one another in a way the asymmetry otherwise forbids?
