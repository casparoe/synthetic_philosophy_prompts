I am preparing a graduate seminar on the distinction between doing and allowing, and before the students begin assessing the rival accounts I want them to have in hand a specification of what an adequate theory of the distinction would have to deliver. Please write that specification.

By a theory of the distinction I mean an account that assigns to any case a verdict on whether the agent did something or merely let it happen, and that does so in a way capable of bearing normative weight — that is, an account that can explain why the difference matters where it does and why it is idle where it is idle. Treat the specification itself as the deliverable; I do not want a survey of the literature.

Set out the desiderata as a numbered list. For each, state the requirement and then the case or consideration that motivates it. I want the list to register at least the following pressures, and to add whatever else you judge belongs:

1. The cases the theory must classify aright, including the cases in which our act/omission verdict and our wrongness verdict come apart in both directions — the cases where killing and letting die seem equally wrong, and the cases where they plainly do not.
2. The distinctions it must not collapse: in particular the distinction between doing/allowing and the distinction between intending/foreseeing, and the distinction between the causal question (what made the difference) and the normative question (what may be done).
3. The fact that many paradigm omissions are in fact sustained by continuous positive intervention — a ventilator, a feeding tube, a pump — which threatens any appeal to a natural course that an omission merely lets run.
4. The baseline problem: relative to which counterfactual or status quo is an omission identified, and the fact that the intuitively correct baseline shifts across cases.
5. Whether the account may make the distinction turn on the agent's intentions, given that much of the criminal law and of clinical ethics wants the distinction to turn on conduct rather than on motive.
6. Whether the distinction is binary or scalar, and what follows for its use in rules that must be applied by people who cannot inspect motives.

Here are four cases I intend to use as fixed points, and the theory must handle all four: (a) the ventilator pair — one physician who never initiates ventilation, another who initiates it and later withdraws it, with the same prognosis and the same death; (b) sustained rescue — a patient alive only because a machine acts continuously, so that ceasing the maintenance is itself a positive act, set beside an agent who initiates a fatal process, both of which are actions; (c) the engineered omission — a person or firm that arranges matters in advance so that a foreseeable death will result from what they will then not do, the later inaction being deliberate and pre-planned, so that the question becomes whether advance design converts an omission into a doing; and (d) the institutional omission — two health systems with identical mortality figures, one reaching them by declining to treat and the other by discharging patients to die at home, where there is no individual positive act anywhere, only a rule.

Then, and this is the part I most want: identify any pair, or larger set, of these desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument for the incompatibility. If the list as a whole turns out to be unsatisfiable, say which requirement should be abandoned and why. Finally, in a few sentences, name the single requirement that does the most to exclude the standard families of account — the causal, the intention-based, and the baseline or status-quo accounts — without scoring each of them in detail.

Keep the register that of moral theory; I do not need citations or a reading list.
