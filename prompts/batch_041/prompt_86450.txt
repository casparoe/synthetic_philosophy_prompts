I have been unable to fault the following argument, although I am convinced that its conclusion is false. It concerns two hypothetical surgical protocols for replacing a human brain, neuron by neuron, with silicon surrogates of identical causal profile. In both protocols the surrogates are mass-produced units whose settings are loaded from a scan of the neuron they are to stand in for; the two protocols differ only in when and where those units are brought into operation. Under the hot-swap protocol, each surrogate is first installed in parallel with the neuron it will replace, so that it receives the same inputs and its outputs are matched to that neuron's; the neuron is then silenced and removed. No signal path is ever idle, and the system as a whole is functioning at every moment. Under the blueprint protocol, the same surrogates are assembled into a complete brain in a separate room and switched on, and the original is only then anaesthetised and dismantled.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) What a person perceives, remembers, decides and feels at a given moment is fixed by the causal organisation of the processes running in her, and not by what those processes are made of.
(2) So replacing any single component of her with a substitute that plays exactly the same causal role leaves her mental life at that moment untouched.
(3) A change that leaves a person's mental life untouched cannot destroy that person, nor bring a new person into being.
(4) So a person survives any number of such single-component substitutions, made one at a time, however many.
(5) Hence the wholly silicon system at the end of the hot-swap protocol is the same person as the one who entered it, and she has been continuously conscious throughout.
(6) Nothing in (1) to (4) depended on the biological parts leaving before the silicon parts arrived, or on the silicon parts being installed in the same room, or on the two processes being interleaved at all. What did the work was the preservation of the organisation at every stage; the order in which the material was exchanged is no part of the organisation.
(7) So the system switched on under the blueprint protocol is likewise the same person as the original.
(8) But under the blueprint protocol the original is still there, awake, with her own stream of experience, and can be kept alive indefinitely alongside the silicon system.
(9) Two distinct people cannot each be the same person as one original, since identity is one-to-one and transitive.
(10) Therefore one of the two protocols fails to preserve the person, even though (1) to (4) cover the hot-swap protocol step by step and (6) appears to leave nothing by which the blueprint protocol could be distinguished from it.

Nor does the argument stop there. If (7) is granted, there is no evident reason why a hundred such systems should not be built, or one built from a blueprint recorded years before the original's death; a person could then never be killed so long as a sufficiently detailed description of her survived, and the question which of the resulting systems is her would have no answer. That cannot be right.

I am drawn to (1), and I have some attachment to (5): were the hot-swap protocol run on me, I would expect to be the one who woke up in silicon. I cannot accept (7). Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what would each of the available exits cost me? Please also say whether the argument is invalid at that step or valid with a premise I must pay to give up, what survives of the case for gradual replacement once the blueprint case is abandoned, and whether the trouble lies in the transfer from one protocol to the other or somewhere earlier in the chain.
