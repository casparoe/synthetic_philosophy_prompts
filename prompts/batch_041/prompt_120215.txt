Last night I argued with a friend for the better part of three hours about Aristotle's defence of natural slavery, and I want to know how exposed my side of it actually is. My friend's line is the familiar one: the doctrine in Book I of the Politics is an excrescence — a piece of fourth-century Greek prejudice that Aristotle inherited rather than argued for, and that can be lifted out without disturbing anything else in his ethics or his politics. Excise it and the household loses a member; nothing else moves.

I defended the contrary claim, that natural slavery is load-bearing, and I want to state that claim precisely so you can hold me to it. A commitment is load-bearing in a philosopher's system when dropping it forces the revision of something he holds independently of the passages in which that commitment itself appears — not merely the deletion of the passages about slaves, but a change in the shape of some further doctrine he needs. My case is that the slave is not a stray item in the household but the element that completes Aristotle's typology of rule. He insists that rule over a slave, over a wife, and over a child differ in kind, and of the three only the slave relation has a subject who lacks the deliberative part altogether; it is also the relation that mirrors the rule of soul over body, which his psychology needs him to be able to point to in the political domain. Remove the natural slave and either the typology collapses into a single relation with three degrees, or the soul-body analogy loses its political work.

Here is what I want from you, and I would rather you be unsparing than even-handed. First, the case against me: which passages in the corpus, which philosophical objections, and which internal tensions tell against the load-bearing thesis, and how decisive is each? I already know the obvious reply — that Aristotle himself appears to concede the criterion is unverifiable in practice, since nature does not reliably mark the difference in the body and the soul's beauty is not visible — and I want to know whether that is fatal or merely awkward for me. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of them a reader sympathetic to my reading would concede as a real cost rather than brush aside.

Second, tell me what my reading predicts that the rival reading does not. If the two are indistinguishable over the same texts, say so, and tell me which texts would discriminate between them. Third, tell me whether the thesis can be falsified at all, or whether any inconvenient passage can be absorbed by refining what I mean by load-bearing; and if it cannot be falsified, whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or simply what exegetical claims are like.

What would move me most is either a passage in which Aristotle treats the institution as merely conventional, or a place where the same structural work is done by other means, so that the slave drops out of the system without loss. If the doctrine turns out to be inert — normative in form, with no institutional upshot, once one notices that Aristotle regards slavery by conquest as unjust — then I think my friend wins, and I want you to tell me if that is where the evidence points.
