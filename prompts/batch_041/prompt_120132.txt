I sit on my department's working group on historical standards — we were convened to produce guidance for staff and graduate students on when it is permissible to evaluate past actors, practices, and institutions, given the field's widespread sense that judging the past by present standards is a kind of category error, and the countervailing sense that this caveat has hardened into a conversation-stopper that mostly protects the comfortable. Our draft has swollen into something slightly absurd — a tiered vocabulary of permitted disapproval, a form to be filed before one may call anything cruel — and somewhere around the fourth revision it occurred to me that the compliance memo is the ideal vehicle for the problem, precisely because it cannot help indicting itself. So I would like you to write that document properly: the working group's internal handbook, in the deadpan voice of a committee that has fully internalized its own authority. Numbered sections and definitions; a schedule of evaluative terms ranked by anachronism risk; at least one form that must be completed before a historian may apply a moral predicate to the past; an appeals procedure; a hardship exemption; one or two case precedents written in the flat register of an adjudication; and, if it suits the genre, a sidebar or appendix. Roughly 800 words.

The comedy has to come from getting the underlying positions right, so that a philosopher of history laughs rather than winces. Two things in particular should be dramatized rather than announced. First, the problem of whose standards are supposed to count as the standards of the period: the handbook should quietly require the applicant to specify the provenance of the standards being invoked — which class, region, institution, confession, gender — and should thereby discover that the procedure has no principled stopping point, since the standards of any period were contested, and since the very acts that provoke the question were frequently condemned by someone at the time, which makes the exemption swallow the rule. Second, the reflexivity: the committee must employ some standard to decide which uses of evaluative language are anachronistic, so the handbook needs a clause attempting to license the committee's own judgments, together with a future-proofing provision requiring that any permitted judgment remain valid under the standards of later centuries — and you should let the regress run rather than rescuing the committee from it. Do not resolve the tension on the committee's behalf, and do not let the prose wink; a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized from the machinery alone, while someone who has never encountered the debate can still follow what is at stake. For that reason, please keep the document free of the names of historians, schools, and works — the committee speaks only of 'the field', 'current best practice', and its own authority — and invent whatever internal jargon it needs, so long as the jargon does real conceptual work rather than merely sounding bureaucratic.
