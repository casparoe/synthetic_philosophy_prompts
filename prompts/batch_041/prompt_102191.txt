I'm a third-year student in a pragmatics seminar, and we've been working through Grice's account of speaker meaning, the cooperative principle, and the conversational maxims. I can follow the standard examples of flouting and particularized implicature, but our instructor has set us a thought experiment that I find genuinely difficult, and I'd like a structured judgment rather than a general summary. The thought experiment is a linguistic community whose members share a norm of cooperation — they all want communication to succeed — but each speaker also has a private payoff that can conflict with the hearer's interest: a speaker may benefit if the hearer forms a false belief about the speaker's intentions, or if the speaker avoids commitment to a proposition while appearing to be committed. The cooperative principle is still the public norm, but it is not assumed to be action-guiding on every occasion; speakers can exploit it strategically. I want to know how much of Grice's framework survives that pressure.

Please rank the four maxims — Quality, Quantity, Relation, and Manner — by how much of their original explanatory and normative force remains in this setting. Give each a tier or a score, and for each one say what it still explains, what it loses, and whether the loss looks like a defect of Grice's framework or like evidence that the maxim was always a special case of something more general. I am especially interested in whether any maxim becomes a mere convention that can be flouted without generating an implicature, and whether the saying/implicating distinction collapses. Please separate what is settled in the pragmatics literature from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested reading of Grice as the consensus.
