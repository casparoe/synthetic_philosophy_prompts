I've been defending a particular line on indicative conditionals for a couple of years now, and I want to find out how exposed it really is, because I keep getting the same objection from colleagues and I can't tell weather it's serious or just lazy. Here's the view. Indicative conditionals are truth-apt: a conditional of the form if A then C expresses something that can be true or false, and it isn't a truth-function of A and C, so it isn't material implication and its truth value isn't fixed by the truth values of its parts. But I'm not a full-blooded propositionalist either, in that I don't think the conditional is a Boolean function of the two propositions. And I accept the equation: my credence in if A then C should equal my credence in C conditional on A. Two restrictions. First, the equation covers only simple conditionals, ones where neither the antecedent nor the consequent already contains a conditional; compounds get handled by whatever semantics I give for embeddings. Second, and this is the part I actually care about, the equation doesn't have to hold at every probability function, only at the credence function the agent actually has -- I'm not applying it to that credence function conditioned on something else. I know the triviality proofs and I know they need the equation at shifted probability functions, or applied to conditionals inside conditionals, so I'm fairly relaxed about them.

So what I want from you is the serious version of the worry. Not triviality kills conditional probability, which I've heard a hundred times and which I think begs the question against the view. I want the specific things -- formal results, cases, intuitions, empirical findings, whatever -- that someone holding exactly this package should be moved by, sorted by how much they'd actually cost me. Say plainly which ones I can shrug off and which I can't. And be honest with me about two things: whether restricting the equation to the agent's actual credence function makes it do no work at all, and whether the whole position is an unstable halfway house between full propositionalism and the view that conditionals have no truth value -- if it is, tell me which horn I'm really standing on. If the view predicts something its rivals don't, say so; if it predicts nothing extra, that's worth knowing too.

This is for a graduate seminar I'm running in the fall and also for a paper I'm trying to finish, so I'd rather have the sharp version than the polite one. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, ordered from the most damaging to the least, with no preamble and nothing summing up at the end -- and if there's something the view predicts that its rivals don't, fold that into one of the five rather than tacking it on.
