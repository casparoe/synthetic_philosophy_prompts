Take the Salem witchcraft crisis of 1692 and analyze it as a case of a society tightening under threat, then tell me where tightness--looseness theory breaks. I do not need the psychology of the accusers or the sociology of panic; I need the norm structure. The setting is a colony whose charter had been revoked and whose political order was still unsettled, a frontier war that had brought raids close to the settlements, and a village split into rival factions. Within months, a special court was hanging people on evidence -- spectral testimony above all -- that ordinary courts would not have admitted, and the incentive structure was perverse: those who confessed survived, and those who held out were executed.

I want four things. First, the mapping. Which norms actually tightened, who enforced them, what the sanctions were, and what came to count as proof. Second, a test of a distinction I keep reaching for. Tightness seems to bundle two things that can come apart -- how hard a society enforces its shared norms, and how rigid its procedures are -- and Salem looks like the first rising while the second collapsed. Is that the right carving, or does the framework already have the resources to describe what happened? Third, the framework predicts that tightening subsides as threat recedes; the war went on and the hangings stopped anyway, so say what actually ended them and what that does to the theory's causal story. Fourth, the verdict: is this a confirmation of the functionalist premise that tightening is a coherent response to danger, or an anomaly the theory cannot absorb -- and does the episode bear at all on the normative question of how much rule-following a society should demand, or only on the descriptive one?

Assume I know the standard version of the theory: societies under chronic threat enforce shared norms more strictly, tolerate deviance less, and loosen again as the threat recedes. What I want is the case run through it in both directions.
