Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I spent thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist — neutron scattering, mostly — and since I retired I've been sitting in on philosophy of physics seminars and reading far more than is good for me. I've got a bee in my bonnet that I'd like turned into a short research proposal: the kind of thing you send to one of those small foundations that funds late-career interlopers, or to a workshop, with maybe four to six thousand words of actual work at the end of it.

Here's the half-formed idea. Everyone argues about whether the quantum state is real — the psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic business — and they argue it almost entirely in the language of non-relativistic quantum mechanics, a wavefunction over the configurations of particles. But that object isn't the fundamental one. In field theory you're dealing with operator algebras and states on them, and the configuration-space wavefunction is at best a derived, frame-dependent bookkeeping device. I keep wondering wether the whole realism question is an artifact of that non-relativistic setting. And there's one specific thing nagging me: in the algebraic setup you get results like the one where the vacuum is cyclic for the algebra of observables localized in any bounded region (Reeh-Schlieder, I think — the name I'm fairly sure of, the exact statement I may be garbling), so that acting with observables confined to a tiny region can approximate any state in the entire theory, including states with excitations arbitrarily far away. That seems to make a straight reading on which the state just encodes some agent's information about a local system look untenable, and untenable for reasons that have nothing to do with the usual no-go arguments of the PBR type. So my question, roughly: is the psi-ontic/psi-epistemic distinction a genuine feature of quantum theory, or a feature of the non-relativistic formulation, and does reformulating things in field-theoretic terms make the epistemic reading harder, easier, or simply meaningless?

Turn that into a proposal for me. I want the question stated so precisely that it could come out false — not a vague 'explore the relationship between X and Y'. Say why the people already in this fight haven't settled it, and specifically whether they've simply been working in the wrong framework or whether I'm making a fuss about a change of clothes. Lay out what I'd actually do with the time (I read slowly and my operator-algebra chops are rusty, so be realistic about scope), what a genuine result would look like versus a failure, and how to keep it from ballooning into a book. Don't give me a reading list or citations — I'll chase those down myself and I'd rather you not invent them. And please don't hedge: take a definite position on whether my question is even well-posed, and if it is, on which way you think it comes out, with your reasons. If you think I'm wrong to find this interesting, say so to my face.
