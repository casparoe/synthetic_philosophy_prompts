I'm a novelist midway through a book where my protagonist gets offered the Pasadena game — that coin-tossing monster of Nover and Hájek's, where you flip until the first head, and if it lands on the nth toss you win 2 to the n divided by n, positive when n is odd, negative when it's even. I need to know whether she should play it, and what each answer says about her. The snag, as I understand it, is that the expected value isn't a number: the series converges only conditionally, so you can rearrange the pay-offs and make it sum to anything you like, positive or negative, without touching the game itself.

So what I'd like is this. Below are the fixes I keep coming across, plus a couple I made up myself while pacing the kitchen. Put them in tiers by how much you'd trust each one to genuinely settle whether she should play, with a sentence per item saying why it lands in that tier. Then tell me which two of them definately can't both be held at once, and which single one you'd stake the whole novel on.

- Just cut the game off at some finite toss and take the expected value of the truncated version.
- Insist that a rational agent's utility has to be bounded, so the wild tails count for very little.
- Say the expectation is simply undefined, so decision theory goes silent here and she may do as she pleases.
- Say only finite state spaces are legitimate, so any game with infinitely many outcomes is out of bounds.
- Point out that every finite prefix of the game has positive expected value, so she should play.
- Swap the mean for the median outcome, which does exist in this case.
- Refuse to compute anything at all and treat the choice as a matter of character rather than rationality.

Keep it brisk — I'm on deadline and my editor wants the scene by Friday.
