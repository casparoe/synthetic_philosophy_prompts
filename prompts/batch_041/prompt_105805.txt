Ok, I've got a view I've been chewing on and I want you to try and break it for me.

The view: whenever two people who are both rational and have the same information want to make opposite bets against each other, at least one of them has to be wrong about something — and nine times out of ten what they're wrong about is the *other person's* information, not the world. So a market price is less a summary of what traders believe about the world and more a summary of what traders believe other traders believe (and what those traders believe those traders believe, and so on up). The upshot is you can't get an edge just by reading the news everyone else can read; the price already ate it. I guess you'd call it the no-free-lunch-from-public-info picture, and I think it's basically the no-trade theorem's epistemology wearing a suit.

Now, what would count against it? And please don't hand me the usual objection that people trade to hedge or for liquidity or because they're rebalancing. That just changes the subject — sure, trade happens, but does it happen for *epistemic* reasons? I want the stuff that should actually make me drop the picture.

A few things I'm chewing on, but feel free to ignore my framing:

- What would a world have to look like for someone to beat the market reliably using only public info, in a way that isn't just luck or a story about being faster? Is there a coherent mechanism, or does every version of it secretly smuggle in private info?
- Could persistent, rational disagreement between informed traders be *stable* while prices still track the truth? Or is that a contradiction in terms — do I just define "rational" so that any lasting disagreement counts as error, which would make my view unfalsifiable and therefore worthless?
- Here's a wrinkle someone threw at me: set up a market where, to place a trade, you also have to publicly post your reason for it, and the reason gets scored later. Does that break the no-trade intuition, or is it just a second, correlated signal that gets priced in too?
- And what about prices that are partly about themselves — a bet on whether the market will go up, say? Does self-reference do anything interesting to the story or is it a red herring?

What I want from you: sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd actually be, from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which ones a committed defender of the view would *themselves* concede count against it, versus which they'd just wave off. And be honest about whether the position risks anything at all — if it turns out to be true by construction, say so, and tell me whether that's a problem or just what you'd expect from a theorem-shaped claim. I'd be most moved by a concrete case where the price is plainly doing useful epistemic work yet someone reliably profits off public info — show me the machinery.
