I'm in a small reading group on Bayesian epistemology, and last week a friend of mine — a probabilist by training, and the most stubborn person in the room in the productive sense — put an argument on the whiteboard that he calls a strengthening of Aumann's agreement theorem, and I have not been able to find the crack in it. He wrote it out roughly as follows. (1) Fix a finite state space with a common prior P and an event E. (2) Each of two agents receives soft, uncertain evidence: each is handed a partition of the space, and after the observation each replaces P by a new probability Pᵢ formed by Jeffrey conditionalization — that is, Pᵢ(·) = Σ꜀ P(· | c) · Pᵢ(c), where the Pᵢ(c) are the agent's own new weights on the cells of the partition. (3) Suppose it is common knowledge between the two agents that agent 1's posterior for E is p and agent 2's posterior for E is q. (4) Aumann's proof turns on a single fact: each agent's posterior is a version of the conditional expectation of the indicator of E given that agent's information, so the law of iterated expectations applies and one can show that P(E | the pair of posteriors is (p, q)) equals both p and q. (5) Jeffrey conditionalization is simply a way of forming a posterior on the basis of information, so that same fact holds of it, and the averaging step goes through unchanged. (6) Therefore p = q: even agents who update on soft, uncertain evidence, and whose posteriors are common knowledge, cannot disagree. (7) So any persistent disagreement between such agents is a lapse of rationality rather than a difference in what they have seen. (8) But two equally careful forecasters looking at the same blurry satellite image can reasonably land on different posterior probabilities for rain tomorrow, and neither is being irrational. So the strengthening in (5)–(7), if it were good, would prove far too much.

Here is what I want from you. Tell me which numbered step or transition to reject, and then do the harder part: explain why that step looked fine — what makes the transplant from (4) to (5) feel so harmless — and what rejecting it costs. If the repair leaves a weaker but still substantive result for uncertain evidence, state it and say exactly how much weaker it is; if the only honest verdict is that no such extension survives and that the original theorem simply does not generalize this way, say that. I would also like the price list: which of the other moving parts — the common prior, the common-knowledge assumption, the equation of rationality with Bayesian updating — one could give up instead, and what each surrender would cost relative to keeping Aumann's theorem intact. For my own part I would most like to keep (8), and I am attached to the theorem itself; I am less attached to (7), which I have never liked.

Two requests about how to answer. First, be willing to push back on my framing rather than politely working within it: if the "strengthening" is not even a candidate theorem and the whole exercise is just to spot a non sequitur, or if the real difficulty lies somewhere I have not thought to look, say so plainly and redirect me. Second, calibrate: tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and where your residual uncertainty sits — in particular, whether any part of your verdict depends on a reading of Jeffrey conditionalization that a reasonable person could contest. I would rather have a short, sharp, honest answer that names one step than a survey that spreads suspicion evenly across all eight.
