Im chairing the opening session at a small two-day workshop on the unity of the proposition — 25 people, mostly people who work on exactly this (one Fregean, a couple of early-analytic historians, someone doing grounding and fundamentality), plus two first-year MA students who got roped into helping with the coffee and have no idea what any of it means. I have to give the ten-minute opening remarks and I keep writing the same bad draft. Could you write the speech for me?

What I need it to do: state the problem so the two beginners actually get it by minute ten, without the specialists wincing. The honest way in is Bradley's regress — relations have to relate, relating them takes another relation, and off it goes — but if I state it that way and then tack on that this is why Bradley thought relations aren't real and there's only the Absolute, the whole weekend sounds like a museum exhibit. Half the room will quietly decide the problem is fake and spend two days talking past each other.

So the speech has to make the regress feel live: that every story about what ties the parts of a proposition into one thing — Frege's unsaturated concepts, the early Russell's attempt, the tie being the act of predicating rather than an ingredient in the proposition, the tie being a matter of grounding — either presupposes the unity it's supposed to explain or just starts the regress over. I'd like that menu laid out honestly, without pretending to settle it, plus a plain-spoken note on which of those options look like the same move in different hats. And it should run the nested case at least once, a proposition with a proposition inside it, because thats where every version I've heard starts sweating. I also want the deflationary option named — that the regress only gets going if we treat the predicate as one more name in the pile — and then set aside for the weekend rather than resolved, because if thats the answer we can all go home.

No reading list please, I know the literature, and don't build it out of other people's sentences. Its opening remarks, not a lecture: warm, a bit dry, and it should land on one sentence that hands the room a question to chew on rather than a thesis.
