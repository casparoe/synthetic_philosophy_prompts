I have been thinking on my own about the old puzzle of whether a person could have come from a different sperm and a different egg, and I keep getting stuck on what exactly the "origin" is supposed to be. A friend of mine works in a fertility clinic and has been describing things to me that I cannot fit into the usual framing: mitochondrial replacement, where the nuclear DNA of an embryo comes from one woman's egg and one man's sperm while the mitochondria come from a separate donor egg, and cloning, where the nuclear DNA is taken from an adult and placed into an emptied donor egg whose mitochondrial lineage is entirely its own. Ever since, I have been unable to say whether the origin that matters is the particular gametes, the parents those gametes came from, or the genome that resulted from them.

I would like you to construct a single thought experiment built to order, in which two views give opposite verdicts about the very same individual and it is clear which feature of the case is doing the work. The first view is origin essentialism: an individual could not have existed had they come from a different sperm or a different egg. The second is a view I shall call genome essentialism: what is essential to an individual is the genetic material they ended up with, so the same genome arising from different gametes would still be the same individual. Your case should hold the genome fixed while varying the origin, and hold the origin fixed while varying the genome, so that the two views are forced apart in one direction and forced together in the other. Constraints: use only what a real clinic could do or a real mutation could produce — no teleportation, no souls, no time travel, no twin earths; keep it short enough that I could describe it to a friend in two or three paragraphs; and let nothing turn on the gametes being unobservable or on our not knowing which gamete was which. I want to use this in an argument with the same friend, so the verdicts must fall out of the two views rather than being stipulated by me.

After the scenario, walk me through what each view says about each variant and why, state plainly which feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Then tell me honestly how a defender of whichever view comes out worse would attack the case, and whether any part of the resulting dispute strikes you as merely verbal. As a final pressure test, check the case against the awkward fact that a single fertilised egg can split into two individuals, in case that undermines the picture the two views share. Please answer in British English, and give me the reasoning rather than reading lists or citations.
