I'm the civilian ethicist on a targeting review cell — I sit in with the JAG and the intel folks when a strike package is contentious, mostly stuff that's lawful on paper but that somebody up the chain has a bad feeling about, and I write a one-page note on whether I think the harm to civilians is intended or merely foreseen. We do two or three of these a week, I usually have about 48 hrs, I dont have a veto, and the commander signs off, so whatever I write has to be usable by people who've never read a page of philosophy and who will read the first paragraph and skip the rest. I keep citing Anscombe at people — the Truman business, her claim that choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder, and her disgust at the way double effect gets hauled in to quiet doubts about bombing a city rather than to raise them. But I've never turned any of that into something my colleagues could actually run, and I think my own notes have gone soft: I've started accepting "that's a side effect, not a means" whenever the officer saying it seems sincere, which is probably the exact move she was warning about. So: write me a decision procedure — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a no, where it stops and hands the call to a person because the question is about values rather than facts, and where it says outright that the honest answer is unknown and the file should not move. Keep it to something I can run in one sitting with the file open, and mark clearly which steps are Anscombe and which are me inventing filler.

Then run it on three cases. (1) A strike on a regional electrical substation, where the theory of victory is that blacking out the capital pressures the government into negotiating, and we predict with high confidence that dialysis and ICU patients die within days — the planners say the deaths aren't the point, the blackout is. (2) A bridge carrying military resupply, with a bus full of civilians on it at the moment of the strike, where we'd hit the bridge whether or not the bus was there. (3) A dual-use telecom hub carrying military command traffic, where the military value is genuine but a large part of why it's on the list is that destroying it will also blind and paralyze the civilian side, and the planners keep bringing that up approvingly in the brief. I expect this procedure to come out worse for us than our current process on at least one of these, and I'd rather you say which than hedge.

Last thing: tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire — my worry is the case where the honest answer to one of the questions is "we don't know" and the whole thing just slides through on momentum — and what argument or evidence would actually change your mind about whether this is the right way to operationalize her. And if you think I'm misreading Anscombe, that she wouldn't let the intended/foreseen distinction do even this much work as a diagnostic, say so plainly; I'd rather find that out now than in front of a general.
