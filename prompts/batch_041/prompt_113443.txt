I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a metropolitan daily, and I came to the restitution beat sideways, by way of the genetics of ancient populations — and I sit as one of the two lay members on the collections and restitution advisory panel of our city's encyclopedic museum, an appointment the trustees made after the last row over the provenance of the ethnographic galleries. We meet a week from Thursday to settle a claim that has been open for four years, and the chair has asked me to draft the panel's recommendation, on the theory that I write for a living. The claim covers thirty-one objects taken from a royal compound during a punitive military expedition in the 1890s — the sort of case everyone now believes they know how to handle, because the Benin Bronzes have made the punitive raid the paradigm of the restitutable acquisition — except that ours has three features which make the standard scripts useless. There are two claimants: the national commission that runs the source country's museums, and the descendants of the deposed ruler, who argue that the objects are regalia belonging to a lineage rather than to a state, and that the commission would put them behind glass in a capital five hundred kilometres from the community that made them. Unlike the Parthenon Marbles, which is at least a quarrel between two governments over one collection, ours is a quarrel about which of two claimants is the right kind of entity to receive a culture's things. Six of the thirty-one are, on the claimants' own account, not inert: they carry an ongoing ritual function, and only certain people may handle or look at them, which means that the public display both claimants nominally want may be a category error rather than a compromise. And our own governing deed, drafted in the 1890s and amended twice since, permits the disposal of objects only for deterioration or duplication, so counsel advises that a permanent transfer would need either a court order or a private act of the legislature, which the trustees will not seek in an election year.

The live question, then, is not whether to give the objects back — nobody on the panel now defends keeping them — but what should count as giving them back: a long-term loan that leaves our title intact, a transfer of title to the commission, a transfer to the lineage, or an arrangement that splits custody, ritual authority, and legal title among different parties and provides for the objects to travel. Please draft the memo I'll put before the trustees, roughly 1,200 words, with a clear recommendation among those options or a reasoned fourth, a concrete timetable, and a serious account of the best case against my recommendation — including the objection I find hardest to answer, that a loan, however generous, leaves the claimants as supplicants petitioning us for permission to see their own ancestors' regalia, and that keeping the title while returning the objects is simply a way of describing the status quo so that it sounds like a remedy. The panel is not a seminar, so I don't need a survey of the literature; I need an argument the trustees can defend in a public meeting and that a reporter other than me can put to them. Assume that the objects' ritual status is contested even among the claimants, that the deed's restriction is real but not absolute, and that whatever we recommend will be read as a precedent for the four hundred other objects in the collection with similar provenance. And please be explicit about what the memo takes a museum to be for, since that seems to decide the question about title.
