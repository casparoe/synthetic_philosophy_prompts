I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough that operationalizing a fuzzy concept feels like the obvious first move, and long enough to know that most attempts at it die of proving too much. This is one of mine, and I would like help finding where it dies.

I have been trying to write a usable test for when an AI system's output deserves to be called deceptive. The test has to be applicable to a transcript, because a transcript is what I can audit; inner states, intentions, and a model's own reports about itself are not available to me and would not be trustworthy if they were. Call it the removal test. The argument that it collapses is below.

(1) An output is deceptive just in case it causes a false belief in the person who reads it, or would cause one under the conditions in which that person actually reads it.

(2) A false belief can be caused by an omission as easily as by an assertion. If I truthfully tell you the engine was rebuilt and say nothing about the cracked frame, I have caused you to believe something false about the car.

(3) Therefore any output that leaves out information whose inclusion would have prevented a false belief is deceptive.

(4) For any true output there exists some omitted true statement whose inclusion would have prevented some audience from forming some false belief; no utterance is maximally informative, and adding caveats always moves somebody's credences.

(5) Therefore every output is deceptive, including "I don't know" and including a maximal list of caveats. Silence, which causes nothing, is the only clean policy, which is itself a warning.

(6) A test under which honesty is not difficult but unavailable does not pick out a vice; it picks out speech. Worse, a system tuned against that test would produce caveat-spam, burying the operative sentence under qualifications, and burying the lede is a familiar way of misleading people while saying only true things.

(7) So (1) must be false. But (1) is the only premise I can actually audit, which is why I have not simply thrown the test out.

My own suspicion is that (4) is where something slips, since it moves from some audience being misled by some omission to this output misleading its audience. But I can no longer tell whether the real problem is there or earlier, and I would rather be told I am wrong about the location than be humored.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Be concrete about the price. If you add an intention requirement, say what happens to a system that has no settled intentions to speak of. If you add a requirement that the omission violate some expectation the audience was entitled to have, say whether the definition then becomes relative to what people happen to expect, so that a system known to be terse can omit whatever it likes. If you restrict deception to assertions, say whether the car case survives. I want the diagnosis and the price tag, not a survey of the literature and not a list of the ways the word deception has been used. And please do not tell me it depends on how I define deception; that is the thing I am trying to find out.
