Historians treat the agreement of two independent sources as far stronger evidence for a claim than the assertion of a single source, however confident, and they discount a source with a stake in the story it tells. Yet the sources that agree are often not independent at all: one copied the other, both descend from a lost earlier account, or both repeat a rumour already in circulation. Reconstruct the theory of evidence that the corroboration rule presupposes. What must independence mean for agreement to be probative, and is it something one can observe directly, or does identifying it require a prior reconstruction of how the sources came to exist, which is the very history the rule is meant to help establish? Separate what the rule strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where the practice is underdetermined between rival accounts of historical knowledge, and describe what would change if historians abandoned the rule and estimated each source's reliability separately.
