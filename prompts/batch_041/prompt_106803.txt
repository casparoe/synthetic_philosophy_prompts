I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, working at the intersection of comparative cognition and animal ethics, and I would like your help dismantling a slogan that has begun to feel unanswerable in my department. In seminars and journal club, the claim that the difference between humans and other animals — or between vertebrates and insects, or between creatures with a nervous system and things that merely respond to their environments — is a difference of degree, not of kind gets repeated as though it were self-interpreting, and it is now being used to reach conclusions that cannot all be true at once. One colleague treats it as establishing that insects have at least some moral standing; another treats it as showing that the gap between swatting a fly and swatting a dog is therefore merely one of degree, and so morally trivial; a third, pressing the reductio, says that if we take it seriously it would make a thermostat marginally sentient. I suspect the three are running together several quite different claims, but I cannot yet separate them cleanly.

The immediate provocation is a proposal circulating in my department to operationalize moral status by assigning each taxon a sentience coefficient between 0 and 1 — a honeybee at 0.05, say, a mouse at 0.6 — with welfare protections scaled proportionally, against a scientific backdrop of organisms that exhibit nociception but no evidence of any central integrative structure. A colleague waved the slogan at me as though it settled whether such a scale is even coherent.

Before you respond, please adopt the role of a philosopher of mind and moral philosopher with expertise in the metaphysics of consciousness, the logic of vagueness, and graded theories of moral status, and think carefully and slowly rather than reaching for the nearest familiar framing. Begin by brainstorming on the page every distinct proposition the sentence could be expressing, and only then organize your answer.

I would like you to lay the readings out as a menu rather than selecting one and defending it. In particular: separate the metaphysical reading (the property itself is a continuous magnitude rather than a binary), the epistemic reading (we cannot in principle locate a sharp boundary), the semantic reading (the predicate is vague, so there is no fact of the matter about borderline cases), and the moral reading (moral status is proportionate to the degree of the underlying property) — and add any further readings you think are needed to do justice to how the phrase is actually used. For each, give me the precise claim, and a verdict: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a thesis that is true of consciousness but not of moral status (or the reverse), or simply false? Please flag wherever the slogan is being made to smuggle in a claim that needs an independent argument.

Then, for each of my three colleagues, tell me which reading they must be relying on, and whether the slogan under that reading actually delivers their conclusion. I am especially interested in two further questions. First, how do gradience and vagueness come apart — does the claim that the property is continuous entail that there is no fact of the matter about where the boundary falls? Second, can a smooth underlying scale generate the discrete permissible/impermissible verdicts that law and common morality seem to require, and if so, where does the cut come from if not from the scale itself?

Close by telling me which reading I should adopt if I want to defend the sentience-coefficient proposal, which I should avoid, and what the strongest objection to the proposal would be on that reading. I am not after a literature survey or a reading list — I want the arguments, laid out so that I could bring the menu to my next meeting.
