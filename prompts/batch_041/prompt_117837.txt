I need you to turn the demandingness debate into something I can actually run in a 45-minute session. My clients are mostly smart non-philosophers, alot are burning out, some are trying to decide whether to donate a kidney or quit a job, and I'm not a therapist, so I need to know where to stop and refer out. I have to run the procedure by my supervisor, who is a philosopher, so it needs to be defensible. Don't give me a reading list or citations; give me the procedure. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it says this is required, where it says this is optional, and where it hands the decision back to the client because it's a values call. Keep seperate the demandingness objection and the integrity objection, because I think they come apart: one is about how much sacrifice is asked, the other about what kind of sacrifice it is. Build in the distinction between the cost of acting and the cost of not acting, and the idea that some sacrifices damage the agent's capacity to keep acting morally over time. And handle the supererogation problem: if an act is morally best, how can it be optional? Then run it on two cases: (1) a client who is considering donating a kidney to a stranger, and (2) a client who is considering skipping their sister's wedding to work on a political campaign they think is high-impact. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire.
