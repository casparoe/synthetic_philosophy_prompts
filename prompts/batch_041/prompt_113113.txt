I run a small monthly seminar on the philosophy of notation — fifteen people, a back room of a bookshop, one guest a term — and I have spent the last few months, between sessions, trying to draw a typeface of my own, badly, which has left me unable to stop thinking about a question I used to find merely cute: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Next month's guest is a type designer who has spent a decade building generative systems that draw letterforms, and who has lately been defending a deliberately unorthodox line: that there is no essence of a letter at all, that 'a'-hood is a role a shape can come to occupy rather than a hidden core it shares with every other 'a', and that the category is held together by the way letterforms travel in families and by the job a shape has to do within a system of contrasts. He is a fluent talker and reaches for the same two moves whenever he is cornered: any proposed essence, he says, is either so abstract that it excludes nothing, or so concrete that it excludes the single-storey 'a' on his own studio wall; and the interesting question, he says, is generation rather than recognition, since a system that can produce a hundred new 'a's in a hundred styles has done something no definition could do.

Write me eight to ten questions that get him past both moves and make him commit to details: the double-storey/single-storey split and what it implies for any shared core; whether the Greek alpha and the Cyrillic а are the same letter or merely similar shapes, and what turns on the answer; what he takes the style dimension to be a dimension of, given that he denies there is a style-neutral core for it to vary; whether a shape can be an 'a' before any community has read it as one; and how his view copes with the ugly cases — the child's scrawl, the letterform mangled by a photocopier, the shape that looks like a plausible 'a' but that no reader would accept, and the perfectly legible 'a' in a script nobody alive can read. Put them in the order I should ask them, give at least four of them a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, add a one-line note to each saying what a merely defensive answer would sound like, and finish with a question that forces him to say what would count as evidence against his own position. Please write in British English, and keep each question short enough to be read aloud.
