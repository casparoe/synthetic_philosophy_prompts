I am in my final year of secondary school. I have taught myself to program over the last three years, mostly small game AI in Python — finite state machines, behaviour trees, some pathfinding, and, from a tutorial I worked through last summer, a small reinforcement-learning agent that learns a grid game by trial and error. On that side I am comfortable. On the philosophy side I have one popular book on free will and a few arguments picked up from a friend, and I have reached the limit of what I can do with them.

The book introduced me to the digger wasp. The story, as the book told it, is that the female paralyses a cricket, drags it to her burrow, leaves it on the threshold while she goes inside to inspect, and that if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she is inside she hauls it back to the threshold and inspects the burrow again; the loop can be repeated indefinitely. The moral was that behaviour which looks purposeful from the outside can be a fixed sequence run off whether or not it does any good, and the book's word for behaviour of that kind was sphexish, after Sphex, the genus the wasp belongs to. The worry it raised was that we might be sphexish ourselves, only with more layers. I have since read that the anecdote is old and has been questioned, and that the wasp may be more adaptable than the story makes her, but the argument seems to survive on some version of it.

The positions, as I understand them, are these. First: everything we do is a longer version of the wasp's loop, so the difference between her and us is one of degree, and free will is an illusion. Second: the difference that matters is flexibility — learning, monitoring what one is doing, responding to reasons — and since we have it and the wasp does not, the comparison fails at the first step. Third: the argument about determinism is beside the point, because free will consists in acting on one's own desires rather than under external constraint, whatever the machinery underneath.

What I cannot do is say what any of this is a claim about. So I should like the dispute restated, as faithfully as possible, in the vocabulary I actually think in: state machines and transition tables; conditionals, loops and subroutines; call stacks; the difference between a policy I wrote by hand and one learned from reward; exploration and exploitation; random seeds; inputs I cannot predict; programs that rewrite their own code; programs that can read their own source.

Then the residue report. Which parts of the dispute map cleanly onto that vocabulary, which map only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in it at all? I am most interested in whether the idea of having been able to do otherwise has an honest translation. A deterministic program given one input has exactly one run; the same program given another input does otherwise; I cannot tell whether that is the right kind of difference or a pun on it. Where the vocabulary forces a choice — whether to treat me as one program or as many, whether my reasons are inputs or comments — please say that it is forcing a choice rather than quietly making one for me. And tell me whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary, something a program simply cannot express, or a sign that the claim being translated was less clear than it sounded. I would rather be shown exactly where the comparison breaks than be told that computers are only tools and the comparison is inapt.
