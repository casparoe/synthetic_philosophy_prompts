I'm writing an essay on A Clockwork Orange and I want to use it to get at the Dennett/Caruso fight over just deserts — the basic desert question, the one they go back and forth on in Just Deserts. The obvious reading is that the Ludovico technique is a reductio of the quarantine model: the Minister's whole pitch is forward-looking (crime down, prisons uncrowded, plus a bit of election-year PR), nobody in the room asks what Alex deserves, and the result is a boy turned into a mechanism that can't do harm. Caruso's reply, I take it, is that this isn't the quarantine model at all — nobody quarantines anyone with nausea drugs and eye clamps — and that what's wrong with Ludovico is the coercion and the permanence, not the absence of desert. Please actually adjudicate that instead of picking whichever side flatters the novel, and say whether the book and the film pull in different directions on it even if the arguments don't.

The chaplain is what's bugging me. He's the only person in the room who talks about choice and moral agency, which makes him sound like the retributivist, but I don't think Dennett would claim him either — Dennett's line is that we should go on holding people responsible because taking that stance is part of what makes them responsible agents, and the chaplain watches the state demolish the conditions for that stance and then goes back to his stereo. Is he a Caruso-style critic with bad arguments, or something else? And does Dennett's worry about the public-health quarantine model — that it still has to say why this person should bear the cost, and for how long — show up anywhere in the story, or is that a gap the novel just papers over?

Two more things to work in. First, the narration: Alex tells his own story in Nadsat, he's charming about it, and by the end the same reader who was appaled in part one is rooting for him against the writer whose wife he raped. If retributive reactions are that easy to hijack, what happens to Dennett's picture, where our desert talk is downstream of the stance we take toward people? Caruso would call that a point in his favour. Second, the last chapter — the one the American publisher cut and Kubrick never filmed — where Alex simply outgrows it at twenty-one on his own. Does that vindicate Dennett (give him the responsibility stance and enough time and he's fine, Ludovico was never needed) or Caruso (the whole penal apparatus was gratuitous cruelty)? Pick one and let the other side have its best shot.

Keep to plot that any general reader would know, don't quote the book or the film at me, and don't just tell me the novel refuses to take sides — if it doesn't engage Caruso's actual version of the view, I'd rather hear that.
