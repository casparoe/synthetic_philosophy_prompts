Had a two-hour argument last night with my friend Priya — she does Kant — and now I have to actually go present this thing, so I need to get my head straight. Here's the situation. Next Friday is a work-in-progress talk, and the room is a specific kind of mixed: mostly analytic Kantians and contractualists, a couple of people from the nursing school's ethics group who teach care ethics as a *practice* rather than a disposition, one political philosopher whose whole thing is the political economy of care labour (she's the dangerous one, I think), and two or three grad students from disability studies who'll come in late and ask the question everyone else was too polite to ask. My thesis — which is not the thesis I started with, and honestly not the one I wanted — is that care ethics doesn't have the problems people usually pin on it. Parochialism, essentialism, "it's not really a theory," all that: I think those are red herrings. The real problem is a *stopping rule* problem. Care is indexed to need as it appears from inside a particular relationship, and attention to a particular other's need has no internal stopping point — there's always more you could notice, more you could do, another way you're already failing them. The justice tradition at least pretends to have a stopping rule (what's owed, to whom, what's left over for you). So every move people make to cap care's demands — "I can't care for everyone," "I have to preserve myself in order to keep caring," "the practice of care has constitutive norms that set its limits" — either borrows a stopping rule from somewhere else (respect, ought-implies-can, the internal goods of a practice) or just asserts a preference and calls it a limit. Which is awkward for me, because I actually think care is the right place to start. I just think its defenders have been dishonest about the price of admission, and I'd like to say so without getting eaten alive.
