I am running the opening session of a doctoral proseminar on the epistemology and ethics of AI, for students trained in machine learning and engineering rather than in philosophy. Most of them have met the trolley problem and the Chinese Room second-hand, and they arrive with two reflexes that pull in opposite directions: that philosophical cases are unrealistic and so cannot tell us anything about the world, and that their own favoured scenarios -- the model that merely predicts, the optimiser that merely maximises -- are neutral descriptions of how things stand rather than cases with a point of view. I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard. I do not want to give them a survey of the literature on thought experiments. I want them to leave able to tell when a case is doing argumentative work and when it is doing rhetorical work, and to say which is which without either gullibility or cynicism.

The one idea I want to land is this: a thought experiment's persuasive force and its argumentative force are separable, and the gap between them is diagnosable rather than a matter of taste.

Plan the session for me; do not deliver it.

1. The order in which the components must come, and why each depends on the one before it. In particular: what has to be in place before the notion of an intuition pump can be introduced at all without sounding like an accusation?

2. The one case that should carry the whole session. Say what it must have and what it must lack. My constraint on it: within the hour, the same case, re-described, must flip the room's verdict, and the flip must not be attributable to the re-description being more realistic. Tell me why the obvious candidates -- the trolley, the Chinese Room, Mary's room -- are the wrong carriers for this room, or, if one of them is right, why.

3. The misconceptions this audience will bring or form, in the order they will surface, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I expect at least these: that a case which could not happen shows nothing; that thought experiments are empirical claims in disguise; that an intuition which changes when a detail changes has thereby been refuted; and that calling a case an intuition pump is a refutation of its conclusion.

4. I have a three-question test I want the session to culminate in, and I would rather they derive it than receive it. (i) What verdict does the case invite, and what is that verdict being used as evidence for? (ii) Which details are producing the verdict, and does the argument need those details or only the verdict? (iii) Does the inference from the verdict to the conclusion run through a principle the room would accept independently of this case? Plan the arc so that the test arrives as a summary of what they have already done, not as a handout.

5. The check I can run in the final ten minutes that would show the idea has landed rather than merely been admired.

6. What to leave out at this level, and how to say so without making it sound like a promise of later revelation.
