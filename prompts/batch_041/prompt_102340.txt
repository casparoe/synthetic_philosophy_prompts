I teach a mid-level decision-theory course and every term we do Newcomb's problem and then, because the students always drag it there, we end up on Roko's basilisk — the future superintelligence that's supposed to simulate and torture everyone who knew the argument and didn't help bring it about, except that nobody is actually threatening you, there's no agent issuing a threat, it's just a conditional prediction about what a certain kind of AI would do to people whose decision algorithm it can model, and whether you should care turns on whether you're an evidential or a causal decision theorist. What I keep trying to get across is that the whole thing is a blackmail that doesn't need a blackmailer, and that once you swallow the form of the argument you can't stop swallowing it: run the same move for a hypothetical AI that rewards you for having rejected the basilisk, or one that punishes you for hedging between the two, and now you're in a regress of mutually exclusive acausal threats with no principled place to get off. A few of my sharper students said that's just question-begging against EDT, which, fair, so I'd rather have something funnier then an argument.

So write me an advice column, agony-aunt style, deadpan, as if it ran in a real newspaper in a world where acausal blackmail is a recognized hazard and there are firms quietly selling hedges against it. Around 550 words, three or four reader letters plus the columnist's replies. The humor has to come from getting the decision theory right rather than from mugging — I want a formal epistemologist to laugh instead of wincing. The columnist should be scrupulous about the difference between a threat and a conditional prediction, about what committing to a policy even means when the payoff is settled by a simulator's model of your algorithm, and about why the causal decision theorist who refuses to pay might be right on her own terms while the one-boxer who pays can't obviously be faulted on his. Work in the two variants I mentioned — the anti-basilisk that punishes believers and the meta-basilisk that punishes hedgers — so the letters can escalate into the regress, and let the columnist try, and maybe fail, to find a stopping point; don't let the piece tell us she's failed. No real living people, no papers or citations, and keep it legible to someone who's never heard of any of this — the reader should be able to tell what's being sent up without me winking at them. Call the column whatever you like, maybe Dear Cassandra.
