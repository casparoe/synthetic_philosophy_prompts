A reading group I belong to has been circling the same question for years, and I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on the question itself. The question is whether a human person could have originated from a different sperm cell and a different egg cell than the ones that in fact produced them. One party to the dispute, working in the essentialist tradition usually credited to Kripke, answers no: for each of us there is a particular pair of gametes such that nothing produced from any other pair would have been us, and this is meant to follow from a general essentialism about origins that applies to organisms and artifacts alike. The other party does not deny that the thought of coming from other gametes is alien, but denies that its alienness is evidence of impossibility: on that view the thought is imaginative confusion, or a claim about similarity dressed up as a claim about identity, or the residue of a picture of persons as individuated by something over and above their causal history. What strikes me is how much the two sides share. They agree on the biology, they agree that the intuition is nearly universal, they agree about the logic of identity across worlds, and they even agree that the interesting question is about the particular gametes rather than merely about the parents. Every new case someone raises — gametes synthesized from somatic cells, so that no ordinary sperm or egg stands at the head of the causal chain; an early embryo that divides, so that the zygote is not the person; a conception requiring two eggs, one contributing the nuclear genome and the other the mitochondrial genome — is met by the essentialists with the reply that it does not touch the historical relation they care about, and by the anti-essentialists with the reply that it shows the historical relation was never the interesting one. Nobody moves.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a metaphysical disagreement at all but an epistemological one: the two sides agree about which propositions are true and differ about what an intuition is evidence for, so the real argument belongs to modal epistemology rather than to the metaphysics of persons. I am not confident in this. The alternatives I want weighed are that the sides are answering two different questions under one heading, one about the de re necessity of a person's origin and one about how a phrase like the sperm and the egg I came from gets its reference; that a concealed empirical question is doing the work, namely whether there is always a determinate pair of gametes for the essentialist claim to be about; that the impasse rests on bedrock intuitions about individuation that neither side can argue for; or that the question is simply ill-posed.

There is also a proposal from one member of the group that I cannot assess and would like you to. She suggests replacing gametic essentialism with what she calls lineage essentialism: what is essential, on her view, is not the particular cells but the continuous causal-historical chain of cell division and transmission running from the parents' germlines to the resulting organism, so that necessity attaches to the lineage rather than to its initial terms. Her claim is that this redescription leaves every disputed case exactly where it was — no former counterexample remains a counterexample, and no former confirmation remains a confirmation — and that this is a virtue, because it shows the original dispute was about the wrong thing. Is that a genuine dissolution or a relabelling that quietly changes the subject while leaving the original question untouched? And if it is a dissolution, does it decide which of the diagnoses above is right?

Argue the diagnosis from what each side actually says rather than surveying positions, and answer the last question candidly: given your diagnosis, is there anything — a distinction, a case, a formal result, a change of question — that would move this dispute, or should we expect it to run indefinitely? If the latter, say why.
