My city just appointed me to its memorial and recognition commission, which is the little body that decides what the city officially calls certain historical events and what language goes on the plaques. I'm the replacement for someone who moved away. Our next meeting is in ten days, there's exactly one petition on the agenda, and I'm definately the swing vote on whether the city puts the word genocide on a plaque and in a resolution. I did not expect to spend my first month on this, and I should say up front that my grandmother's family is one of the groups in case 1 below, so I know I have a dog in this fight and I'm trying not to let that drive my read.

Here's our record, reconstructed from the minutes:

1. A state campaign against a religious minority that lasted about a decade: massacres, forced marches, churches and monasteries destroyed, part of the group forcibly converted, roughly fifteen percent of the group killed, the rest scattered abroad. We used the word.

2. A famine caused by a state's grain requisition policy that killed over a million people in a particular rural occupational class, with internal memos showing officials knew what was coming. We voted it down, and the resolution used the phrases mass death and crime against humanity instead.

3. Massacres by a state of an organized political opposition and its families, tens of thousands dead, no ethnic or religious dimension at all. Voted down.

4. An armed movement that held its own territory but was never a recognized government killed about six thousand people from a small ethnic group over two years and drove the rest out of the region. We used the word. That group's diaspora lobbies us every year.

Now the petition in front of us. A militia that ran a province for about a decade in the middle of the last century went after a small ethnoreligious minority: a series of massacres that killed maybe a fifth of them, forced conversion and forced marriage of a lot of the women and girls into the majority group, the group's shrines and manuscript collections burned, the language banned in schools, and the rest either fled or quietly assimilated. The militia's commander wrote an order about the language ban and said in a speech that these people had no place in the province. Some of the dead were killed for sheltering the militia's political enemies, which our staff lawyer says complicates whether they were killed as such. The state looked away the whole time and later gave the militia's leaders amnesty. Today there are maybe two thousand elderly speakers left, no community institutions, descendants who mostly don't identify, and one small diaspora group abroad asking us to act. There's also a case pending in another country's courts that might use our resolution as evidence, or might be embarrassed by it.

Our chair keeps saying we're not a court, we're a memory. The vice chair keeps saying that if we spend the word on everything it stops buying anything. I've also had a man at two hearings quote Lemkin at me, and my understanding is that his version was wider than the 1948 treaty, that he counted wrecking a group's language, religion and economic life as part of the same crime, while the treaty narrowed it to a list of specific acts against national, ethnic, racial or religious groups. I dont know how much of that is real.

What I want: given what this commission has actually done, what should I vote, and which of the four earlier cases is the closest analog? If our old votes don't really settle it, say so, and tell me what principle I'd be adopting with each way I could go. I care less about the abstract right answer than about what our own record commits us to.

And please tell me how confident you are in the read you're giving me and which part of it you're least sure about. I'd rather see where you're guessing than get a smooth answer. American English, please.
