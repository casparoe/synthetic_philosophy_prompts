I spent thirty-five years as an entomologist, most of it on social insects, and since I retired I've been teaching myself philosophy — badly, but with a lot of enthusiasm. Last year I let myself get talked onto the ethics advisory commitee of the regional land management agency. Mostly it's dull work: someone proposes a spray program, we check it isn't obviously cruel or illegal, we sign. This one has me stuck.

The proposal is to eradicate an introduced ant across the whole coastal strip. Not knock it back — kill it, down to the last queen if they can manage it. The thing about this ant in its introduced range is that the nests stopped fighting each other. They merged. As far as the ants are concerned there is one colony running from one end of the strip to the other: workers from either end accept each other, they forage as a single operation, and there are no borders anywhere inside it. One entity, in every functional sense you'd care about.

The philosopher on the panel has latched onto this, and I think she's right that it matters and wrong about how. Her claim is that if it really is one entity, it's a candidate for being one mind. Not that ants are conscious — she's careful about that. But that a system integrated like this one might have a point of view, interests of its own, and that if it does, then what we're proposing isn't pest control. It's the killing of a single thinking thing, which she says is worse than killing billions of insects, not better, because billions of insects aren't anybody.

I can't just wave her off. But I've spent my whole life inside this animal's mechanism. Each worker is a handful of rules and a pheromone gradient. There's no ant that knows anything. There's no place in the nest where a decision gets made, no signal that stands for anything, nothing you could point at and say that's where the colony is doing its thinking. My instinct is that when you can look at the machinery in that much detail and there's no mind to be found anywhere inside it, the mind isn't there.

Her answer is that I'm looking in the wrong place, that a brain has no spot where the thought is either, and neither does a market, and we don't conclude from that that markets don't set prices. Then she asks the question I genuinely can't answer: if the colony does count as a mind, whose interests win when they come apart? The colony throws workers away by the tens of thousands as a matter of routine. If it's one thing, is that a sacrifice, or is it mass murder?

I have to bring a written opinion to the committee on the 14th. I need it to recommend a concrete course — approve the program as proposed, approve it with conditions, or recommend we adopt a standing policy for how colony-level interests get weighed in future cases — with reasoning the agency's people can actually use, and an honest statement of the strongest thing on the other side. Don't tell me it's a verbal dispute or a matter of definition; the people in that room have to sign something, and I'd rather have an uncomfortable answer than a hedged one. Also, please settle the practical question for me: if we grant her the premise, does anything about what we should do to the ants actually change, or does it turn out to be a fact about our vocabulary and not about them? And I don't need a reading list — I need something I can circulate.
