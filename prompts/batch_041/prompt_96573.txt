I have written novels for twenty years, and I have come to be troubled by a contradiction in the way my profession speaks about the moral authority of what it makes. On one side, publishing is scrupulous about disclaiming assertion: every novel carries a notice to the effect that its persons and events are invented, that nothing in the book is offered as a claim about the actual world, and that any resemblance to real persons is unintended. We say that fiction imagines rather than asserts, and that it is therefore not the sort of thing that can be true or false. On the other side, the same critics and the same authors who repeat this will praise a novel as true to life, as honest about human beings, as morally serious, and will condemn another as sentimental, as a lie, as false to the way people are — and we mean by this something more than that the book pleased or displeased us. When I have tried to make a character forgive a betrayal I was sure she would not forgive, I have felt the draft go dead in my hands, and I have said, and meant, that it was untrue.

Reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes, and especially its metaethics. Does the way we praise and condemn moral vision in fiction presuppose that there are moral facts a novel can get right or wrong, and if so, what kind of realism would that be? Can an anti-realist account of moral discourse — expressivist, error-theoretic, relativist, or constructivist — make sense of the same vocabulary, treating our verdicts on a novel's honesty as reports of the attitudes it elicits rather than as claims about a moral reality the book has tracked or missed? And how does the no-assertion disclaimer sit with any of this: is the claim that fiction asserts nothing consistent with treating it as morally true or false, or does the praise quietly retract the disclaimer?

Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is underdetermined, so that realism and several anti-realisms would each license it. Then say what would have to change in how we write, review, and teach fiction if the anti-realist reading were the correct one — whether anything in the practice would have to be abandoned, or only re-described.
