Last night a friend and I got into a long, increasingly exasperating argument about Schopenhauer's philosophy of music. My friend insists that Schopenhauer's central claim—that music is not a representation of the Ideas or of particular emotions but a direct copy of the Will itself—is either a piece of metaphysical poetry or a view that collapses as soon as you ask why a mediocre pop song and a late Beethoven quartet should both count as equally direct objectifications of the same blind striving. I found myself defending the view more than I expected, but I couldn't articulate how it is supposed to handle the difference between musical profundity and mere sonic stimulation, or why music's emotional power is not just a byproduct of its formal features. I have been asked to prepare questions for a panel at a small workshop on aesthetics, and one of the panelists is a philosopher who defends a broadly Schopenhauerian account of music along these lines. I want to press her in a way that forces the position to do real work rather than letting her restate the familiar slogans.

Please draft eight to ten questions for the panel, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question each on: how the claim that music copies the Will itself is supposed to differ from the claim that it expresses or represents emotion; whether music, on this account, is a representation at all, or whether it is better understood as a second objectification of the Will alongside the body, and what follows for the analogy between music and the body; why, if music bypasses the Ideas, it should be cognitively significant rather than merely causally arousing; and how the view can distinguish profound from shallow music without covertly importing standards of compositional craft that seem foreign to the metaphysics. I would also like questions on the status of melody, harmony, and rhythm in the account; on whether program music and opera are a test case or a distraction; on why music that copies suffering should be pleasurable rather than merely painful; and on whether the view can accommodate the historical variability of musical language—why, if music is a direct copy of something timeless, does it sound so different in different centuries? Press the standard objections, anticipate the replies she is likely to make, and make the follow-up to each question sharpen the pressure rather than simply asking for more detail.
