I am a philosophy professor preparing a seminar on personal identity and modality, and I intend to run a reflective-equilibrium exercise rather than a literature review. I have a set of verdicts about whether a person could have had a different origin, and I should like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

My verdicts:

1. I could not have come from a different sperm and egg. Had a different sperm fertilized the egg my mother actually contributed, that would have been a different person, a sibling, not me.

2. I could have come from the same sperm and egg but been carried in a different uterus, or in an artificial womb. The gestational environment is not part of what makes me me.

3. I could not have come from the same egg and a different sperm, nor from the same sperm and a different egg. Both gametes seem essential.

4. If the zygote that became me had split and produced an identical twin, that twin would not be me. We should share a genome and, in some sense, a zygote, but we are numerically distinct people.

5. If my embryo had been edited before implantation to change a gene for eye color, I should still be me. A small genetic alteration does not replace the person.

6. If a clone were made from one of my cells, with my nuclear genome placed in a donor egg, the clone would not be me. Same genome, different origin, different person.

7. If an embryo formed from a different sperm and egg were edited to have a genome exactly like mine, that embryo would still not be me. It would be a duplicate, not me.

8. If the egg from which I came had had its mitochondria replaced with those of a donor before fertilization, I should still be me. The mitochondrial contribution is not part of my identity.

9. If I had been formed from two embryos that fused into a chimera, I am less sure. Suppose the chimera has two cell lines, one of which would have become me and one of which would have become a sibling. Is the chimera me, or us, or someone else? My tentative verdict is that the chimera is a single person, but not simply me, because the second cell line contributes to the body and mind. Yet I also think that if one of the two embryos had simply died and the other had developed, I should be that survivor. I cannot get these two verdicts to sit comfortably together.

What I should like: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that states precisely what is essential to a person's origin and why. It should say what is doing the work in the cases above: the exact gametes, the exact zygote, the exact cell lineage, the genome, causal continuity, or something else. If the principle has several clauses, say which of my verdicts motivates each clause. It should tell me what it implies for cases I have not listed, for example: a person conceived with donor mitochondria; a person whose embryo was split and one half frozen for years; a person created by cloning who is then genetically edited; a person whose original zygote would have twinned but did not. It should also be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what I should have to give up if I keep the principle. If the verdicts cannot all be saved by one principle, I should rather see the simplest principle that saves most of them, with the cost stated, than a complicated principle that saves all of them by gerrymandering.

Finally, please say how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure about.
