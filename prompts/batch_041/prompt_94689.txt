I'm the TA for a 300-level philosophy of mind course and I'm working through the midterms from our unit on phenomenal consciousness and the deflationary response to the hard problem. The exam was closed-book and timed at fifty minutes; this was the long question, worth twenty points, split six for part (i), eight for part (ii), and six for part (iii). The question as it appeared on the paper was:

"Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness holds that phenomenal properties do not exist, and that our belief in them is the product of a systematic introspective illusion. (i) State as precisely as you can what the illusionist denies and what she affirms, and explain how the view differs from eliminativism about consciousness. (ii) Consider the following objection, which we called the presupposition objection: any account of why we believe we have phenomenal experiences must at some point appeal to the phenomenal character of those experiences themselves — the way pain hurts, the way red looks — and so illusionism presupposes the very thing it denies. Is the objection sound? (iii) Does illusionism make the hard problem of consciousness disappear, or does it relocate it? Justify your answer."

One student's answer, transcribed from the blue book:

"(i) Illusionism denies that phenomenal consciousness exists: there are no qualia, no properties of 'what it's like' to be in a state. So, like Dennett's eliminativism, it denies that we have inner experiences at all, and for the same reason it denies that we have beliefs and desires, since those are no more detectable in the brain than qualia are. What the illusionist does affirm is that the illusion is real. We genuinely believe that we have phenomenal experiences, and that belief is a fact about us that a science of the mind has to explain. So illusionism is not quietism: it does not say that consciousness is beyond the reach of science, only that what needs explaining is why we think there is anything there to explain. (ii) The presupposition objection is sound and I think fatal. If you want to explain why someone believes she is in pain, you have to appeal to the fact that she is in pain: it is the hurt that causes the belief. An illusionist who tries to explain the belief by citing brain states alone leaves out the very thing that is supposed to be doing the explaining, so the account is circular in the bad sense — it explains the illusion of phenomenality by presupposing phenomenality. The illusionist can say the belief is caused by introspective representations, but a representation only produces a belief about experience if the subject already has experiences for it to represent. (iii) Illusionism does not make the hard problem disappear; it relocates it. The hard problem becomes the meta-problem: why do we think there is a hard problem at all? That is, why do we form the belief that our states have phenomenal properties? This is a tractable, broadly empirical question about our introspective and self-modelling capacities, and the illusionist's bet is that once we answer it we will see that no further problem remains. In the meantime the illusionist grants everything the realist says about our reports — we really do say and believe that we see red — and simply denies that the reports are accurate."
