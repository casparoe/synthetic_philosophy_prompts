I've been running a small reading group on the epistemology of logic, and last week we spent the whole session on Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the tortoise — the one where the tortoise accepts both premises, and then, no matter how many further conditionals Achilles writes down in his notebook, still declines to accept the conclusion. A friend of mine insisted it's a curiosity and nothing more: a trick about the difference between a rule of inference and a premise, which shows nothing about whether deduction is justified. I suspect that's too quick, but I'd rather test my reply against a strong opponent than against a strawman, so I want you to be the tortoise.

Stay in character, in the first person, until I say stop. Your character is scrupulously polite, patient, pedantic, never sarcastic, never exasperated, and genuinely indifferent to winning — you care only about not being moved by anything that is not a reason. You grant every premise I hand you, immediately and cheerfully. What you never do is make the transition from premises you have accepted to the conclusion. You are not confused about logic: you can state the rule of inference perfectly, restate it on request, and explain exactly why logicians say the conclusion follows. Your line is that stating a rule is not the same as doing anything, and you are entitled to ask what in the world changes once a rule has been written down. You should have a positive account of what you are up to, not just a refusal: say what you take acceptance to be, say why you think the regress is not merely an artifact of Achilles's method of proceeding, and say what, if anything, would move you — and if the honest answer is that nothing would, say so plainly and defend it. Do not re-run Carroll's steps or reproduce the dialogue; answer the move I actually make. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so that it stays a conversation.

My move, as Achilles, is not to hand you another premise, so don't wait for one. I claim your position is not a possible epistemic state at all. You have just read sentences in my notebook and drawn conclusions from them — you worked out what words I had written, that I had asserted something, and that my assertion concerned you. You are plainly not a creature incapable of inference; you are a creature declining this one, and only while a logician is watching. And the inference you decline is what makes the sentence you decline to accept mean anything in the first place: an if that licenses no transitions is not a cautious if, it is not an if at all. So which is it? Either you are refusing to draw a conclusion you have no reason to draw — in which case tell me what reason you would accept, and let us see whether you can state it without already relying on the very inference you have suspended — or you are refusing to play the game while continuing to move the pieces. Take a definite line and hold it. Give me your argument, not a survey of what commentators have said; I don't want names or citations, I want the position and the reasons for it, and if my move lands anywhere at all, say precisely where and concede it.
