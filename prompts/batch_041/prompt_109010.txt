I'm in a small reading group on cliodynamics — we've been grinding through Turchin's structural-demographic model: secular cycles, elite overproduction, immiseration of the general population, the fiscal squeeze on the state, and that composite index he builds (the political stress one, PSI). Last week it blew up. One guy kept saying 'history repeats itself' as if it settled everything: he thinks the model shows the current wave of instability was foreseeable decades in advance, so nobody gets to act surprised, and — his words, roughly — the elites always overproduce, the cycle always turns. Another person used the same sentence to go the other direction: if history repeats itself then it's a treadmill, cycles gonna cycle, so no policy matters and nobody's really to blame. Our supervisor, who's allergic to the whole project, said the trouble with 'history repeats itself' is that it's either trivially true or plainly false and never anything in between. Then a physicist in the group said the interesting question is whether the secular cycle is meant to be a limit cycle (an attractor you'd slide back into even after a huge shock) or just a driven oscillator (repeat the shocks, get a similar-looking trace), and everyone nodded like that was the same point as what they'd been saying. It's definately not, right?

What I want is a menu: the distinct precise claims 'history repeats itself' could be making, ordered from the vacuous up to the genuinely substantive, with a verdict on each — trivial, defensible-but-empirical, false, or unfalsifiable in practice. A few that occur to me, though I don't know what to do with them: it could be about the same events recurring (obviously false); the same kinds of events recurring at some characteristic timescale (which is roughly what any rate-based forecasting needs, and seems very weak — we "predict" earthquakes that way without anyone saying the earth repeats itself); the same mechanisms grinding out similar phases (stronger, and I think this is the real claim); a strict periodicity (probably false, and probably not even what the model says); people making the same mistakes because they don't learn from them (a claim about agents, not structures, and it smells like advice wearing a proverb's clothes); or a claim about historians rather than history, i.e. we keep telling the same story-shapes no matter what the evidence is. A friend offered the line that history doesn't repeat so much as rhyme — he credited Twain, I don't believe the attribution, and I can't tell if the rhyme version is a different reading or just a nicer way of saying one of the above.

The practical thing I need is this: which reading do you actually have to hold for the forecast to be interesting rather than a horoscope, and which reading is needed for the 'nothing can be done, no one is to blame' move — because I'm fairly sure my two friends were running different readings and swapping them whenever it got awkward. If there's a reading on which the slogan is true but tells us nothing about history in particular (some general fact about populations, feedback and saturating resources), say so and say why it's still doing rhetorical work for them. And tell me whether the limit-cycle-versus-driven-oscillator framing is a real, separate distinction or just the same menu with better vocabulary. Lay it out as a list I can bring back to the group without getting shouted at.
