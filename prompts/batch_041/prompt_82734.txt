Here's the half-formed idea. I work on Lakatos on research programmes, where the load-bearing requirement is that a progressive programme predicts novel facts: the protective belt gets patched, and the patch earns its keep by entailing something nobody expected. But 'novel' never gets pinned down, and there are two readings in circulation — a fact is novel if it wasn't known when the theory was put forward, or a fact is novel if the theory wasn't built to accommodate it, which is a claim about what the theorist used rather than about the calendar. Those come apart on the cases the framework is usually sold with (the perihelion of Mercury, the positron, Prout's hypothesis), and I don't think anyone has walked through the standard cases to see whether the two readings give the same verdict on which shifts were progressive. I want to add a third reading of my own, which I suspect is the right one: a fact is novel for a programme when no move that the programme's positive heuristic licenses would have entailed it. So novelty is relative to the programme's own recipe for patching the belt, not to dates and not to what some dead theorist had in mind — and part of the job is arguing that this isn't the second reading in new clothes, since the positive heuristic is a public methodological object and 'what the theorist used' isn't. The question is whether the choice among the three readings changes any historical verdicts or is a distinction without a difference. If they all agree everywhere, or if every disagreement turns out to rest on facts about what a particular theorist happened to know, the project is dead, and I'd rather find that out from the proposal than in month seven.
