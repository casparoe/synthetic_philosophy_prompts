I'm writing an essay on whether the left–right distinction survives its own incoherence, and I'd like you to make the strongest case for a claim I've come round to. The claim is this: the distinction is real, but what holds each side together is not a programme or a bundle of issue positions; it is a shared default about how durable social arrangements are to be explained. When a hierarchy, an inequality or an institution persists, one side treats the persistence itself as evidence that someone is keeping it in place, while the other treats it as evidence that it is doing work that would otherwise have to be done another way. Everything visible — the coalitions, the platform reversals on trade, war, centralisation and civil liberties, the way each camp keeps reassembling after its stated positions have swapped — is downstream of that one asymmetry. Argue for it, and separate as you go what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus; I'll source the piece myself, so no citations are needed. Give the argument as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, in British English.
