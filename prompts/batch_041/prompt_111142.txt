I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level philosophy of science seminar (about twenty students) on whether the special sciences have laws of their own or whether every higher-level regularity is a convenience for talking about constituents. I want a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the problem with their own hands before any of the vocabulary is in the room: no lecture up front, and I will not say reduction, emergence, autonomy, supervenience, or the name of any position until the debrief.

Build it around this substrate, which can be run with people and a clock rather than with software. Tape a loop of about twenty positions on the floor, one per student. Each student either occupies a position (a car) or leaves it empty. The rule is local and simultaneous: on each beat, a car moves one position forward if and only if the position immediately ahead of it is empty, and otherwise stays where it is; nobody overtakes; an empty position becomes occupied only by the car behind it. The number of cars is fixed within a round; I, or a student playing traffic authority, may add or remove cars between rounds. Before the session, hand-check the rule on a ring of a dozen cells at two or three densities so that you know what to expect.

I want them to come away having seen, from the inside:

- that complete knowledge of the micro-facts buys them nothing — coaching an individual driver, or rearranging who stands where, changes nothing once the loop settles, whereas a single change in how full the loop is changes everything;
- that the aggregate relation, namely how many cars cross a fixed point per beat as a function of how full the loop is, is the stable, projectible, controllable thing, though it is a fact about no car in particular;
- that at higher densities the loop settles into a state in which a stoppage travels backwards around the loop while every car that moves at all moves forwards — a lasting, moving, nameable thing that no single car's description contains (watch how readily they personify it, blame it, and try to steer it);
- that the micro-facts do not rank the aggregate quantities. Throughput and each driver's own speed point in opposite directions, and above about half full the loop breaks sharply: someone is always stopped, and no arrangement of the same cars fixes it. The group should have to choose a target before they measure, and then see that the target, not the rule, decides which regularity looks lawlike.

Deliverables: rules and materials; roles, including the authority and how it is chosen; a beat and a resolution procedure that keeps the updates simultaneous and honest; a scoring scheme with something small at stake and no real money; a minute-by-minute plan for the 75 minutes; and the moment of intervention, with the predictions the students commit to on paper beforehand. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the philosophical options, says which ones the session genuinely supports and which it merely resembles, names what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and is frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — I expect the gap between hard to predict in practice and irreducible in principle, and the question of whether higher-level causal claims are true, to be things I must supply myself; tell me how to say them.

Keep the whole thing runnable with tape, index cards, and a phone timer.
