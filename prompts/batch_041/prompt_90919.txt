Ok, so this is kind of a weird one and I'm not even sure it counts as a real philosophy question, but it's been bugging me for weeks.

A few months ago I did something I used to roll my eyes at: I made a Ulysses contract with myself. Long story short, I was drinking too much and kept breaking promises to myself, so I wrote up an agreement with my brother — if I drink before June, I owe him two grand, and I can't just tear it up later. He holds the thing. Its worked, which honestly surprised me.

Ever since then I keep noticing how everybody talks about constitutions the exact same way. My buddy in poli sci said the Constitution is basically a Ulysses contract — the country ties itself to the mast so a future, dumber version of itself can't do what it'll want to do in the moment — and that this is the whole justification for making it hard to amend and for letting judges say no to the majority. I think he's half right and half cheating, but I can't articulate why, and it's driving me nuts.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Concretely:

- Give me the correspondence table first. Who or what plays Ulysses, the ropes, the mast, the crew, the wax in the crew's ears, the sirens, and the moment the danger has finally passed?
- Then tell me where the mapping actually breaks. I care especially about these three: who's bound vs. who's doing the binding (in my case it's me, in the Constitution it's a bunch of dead guys I never met); the fact that Ulysses only has to hold out until he's out of earshot, whereas a constitution binds indefinitely; and the fact that Ulysses tells the crew ahead of time to ignore him if he begs to be let go — so the whole scheme depends on the enforcer disobeying the bound person, which sounds a lot like the thing people complain about when they say courts override the people.
- Which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion he wants (that entrenchment and judicial review are legit), and which are just noise?
- Be honest at the end: is the analogy carrying the conclusion, or would the conclusion stand on its own if you threw the analogy out? And if there's a better analogy for what a constitution is doing, tell me what it is.

One more thing — does it change anything that my contract has a third party (my brother) who actually cares about me and wants what present-me wants, while in the constitutional version the enforcer is a panel of judges who don't obviously have that kind of relationship to the people they're binding?

Write it in American English, and don't go easy on the analogy just because I'm kind of attached to it.
