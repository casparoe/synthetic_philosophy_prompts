My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about probability, and it ended in a disagreement I cannot settle by myself. The argument was not about any particular calculation. It was about whether the probability judgments I kept making, case by case, are anything more than the residue of arbitrary decisions about which class of comparable cases to count. She maintains that every verdict I gave rested on a different choice of reference class, that I have no consistent rule for making those choices, and that my confidence is therefore unearned. I want to know whether she is right. Here are my verdicts, stated as plainly as I can manage.

1. A coin is described to me as an ordinary fair coin, and I am asked the probability that the next toss lands heads. I say one half. I am then told that this particular coin has landed heads on nine of its last ten tosses. I still say roughly one half, perhaps a shade above; I treat the run as luck, not as evidence that the coin is biased.

2. A die comes from a factory I have every reason to believe makes fair dice. It has come up six on each of its last twenty rolls. I now say the probability of six on the next roll is high, well above one sixth. I take the run as strong evidence that this die is loaded. This sits awkwardly beside the coin verdict, and I am aware of that.

3. A patient has a disease. Counted as a man of his age, his five-year survival is about sixty percent; counted as a member of a subgroup carrying a particular biomarker, it is about twenty-five percent; counted as a patient at his particular hospital, it is about forty percent. I say the probability that he survives five years is closest to the biomarker figure, even though that subgroup is by far the smallest of the three classes.

4. I am told that an adult was selected from a crowd by a procedure I cannot see, and asked the probability that the selected person is left-handed. I say I cannot answer until I know the procedure, and that different procedures would make different answers correct, though the crowd and the person are the same either way.

5. A doctor tells me a patient has a condition that will either resolve on its own or become chronic, and asks me for the probability that it resolves. She says she can give me the fraction of patients whose conditions resolve, or the fraction among patients like this one who resolve, or the fraction among those who will in fact resolve once we later classify them as having resolved. The third answer is one by construction. I say that third answer is not a probability at all, and that this is not merely a matter of taste.

6. I am asked the probability that a particular named stranger, about whom I know only a name and a city, will vote in the next election. The narrowest class that contains only him is the class of people exactly like him in every respect I know; taken literally, that class has one member, and the answer is just whatever he does, zero or one. The broadest class, all adults, gives a national turnout rate. I say the truth lies somewhere between, and that the slogan that one should use the narrowest relevant class cannot be the whole story.

When she pressed me for a rule, I offered this: the probability of a case is its frequency in the narrowest class containing it whose members all resemble it in respects that are causally relevant to the outcome, provided that class is large enough for the frequency to be stable. She replied, fairly, that the phrase doing all the work is causally relevant, that I seem to invoke relevance one way in the coin case and another way in the die case, and that in the biomarker case I let relevance override sample size while elsewhere I let sample size override relevance. I could not answer her.

What I want from you is the principle, or small set of principles, that could underwrite these verdicts if anything can. State it precisely. For each clause, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Tell me what it implies about cases I have not mentioned, in particular one where the only class available is defined by the very outcome being predicted, and one where the narrowest class that seems relevant has only three members. Then tell me honestly which of my six verdicts the principle forces me to give up. If you conclude that no single principle survives, and that I must choose between a complicated one that saves every verdict and a simpler one that overturns one, say which verdict you would overturn and why. Finally, tell me whether my friend's charge is correct, or whether there is something my verdicts are all tracking that she has not seen.
