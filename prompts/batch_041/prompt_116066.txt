Ok, this one is for a writing group I'm in, so I want you to really commit to it. Take the role of a satirist who also happens to know classical Chinese philosophy cold — someone who could write for a comedy site on Monday and argue about Mencius at a dinner party on Tuesday. Before you draft anything, spend a few lines thinking through the doctrinal moves you want to plant and where in the piece each one lands, then write it. Please dont skip the thinking part, and take your time.

The material you're working from: Mencius thinks our nature is good — the good is already in us, we just have to not lose it, and most of the evil we do is the world wearing us down. Xunzi thinks our nature is bad and the good we have is manufactured: ritual, teachers, repetition, people forcing themselves and their children into shape until it takes. What makes it fun is how much they agree about everything else — both think anyone at all can become a sage, both think the difference between a decent person and a rotten one is cultivation. They only disagree about where the good comes from, inside or outside. Two things worth knowing, and these are the bits that make the joke land: Xunzi's term for the manufactured good is usually translated as artifice, and it does not mean fake or phony, it means made by human effort. And there's a lovely problem waiting for him — if our nature is bad, where did the sages who invented the rituals get the good that let them invent the rituals?

Now transpose that fight into a charter school.

Deliverable: the Parent FAQ page from the website of a fictional charter-school network, in the voice of its founder, who is a thoroughgoing Xunzian and has no idea that he is one. Numbered Q&A, each question a normal parent's perfectly reasonable objection, each answer a doctrine, deadpan, no winking. Roughly 600-700 words of that. Then append, as a leaked internal email, around 200 words from the network's Director of Student Well-Being, who is a thoroughgoing Mencian and also has no idea that she is one.

Invent all the specifics — the network's name, the founder's name, the crest, the rituals. I want at least: a Monday standing bow everyone does, a conduct log parents have to sign, a three-week habit cycle thats treated as if it were a law of nature, and a founder's tic where he points out that the warped board he had pressed straight is now load-bearing and holding up the wall it hangs on. Concrete and boring is funnier than zany.

Hard constraints:

- The words Mencius, Xunzi, Confucius, Confucian, Daoist, China, Chinese, and the name of any actual existing school must not appear. Not once. It's a present-day American institution all the way through, and nothing in the piece should read as an allegory that someone could spot without knowing the tradition.
- No quotations from any historical text, no citing anything, and dont paraphrase anything into quotation marks. Everything in the piece is your own invention.
- The founder has to stay Xunzian the whole way through. If he concedes anything, it has to be a concession a real Xunzian could make. He should have genuine answers ready for the obvious objections — that this is indoctrination, that it's cruel, that it treats children as material to be worked.
- Same for the well-being director in the other direction. Her warmth has to be the real thing, and her plan has to be visibly too thin to run a building with four hundred children in it. A reader should feel both of them losing, for opposite reasons, without you ever saying so.
- What's being satirized isnt either philosopher, its the way an institution picks up one of these stories and runs a building on it. Stay fair to both.
- A reader who knows the tradition should be able to tag each paragraph — thats the sprouts, thats the stripped mountain, thats the artifice-doesnt-mean-fake move. A reader who doesn't should just think its a funny school.

Give it a headline and the school's URL line at the top if you want. Dont end on a summary — end on one of them being kind about the other.
