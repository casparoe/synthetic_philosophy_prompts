I am advising a foundation that is considering a three-year, roughly ten-person research program on the contemporary significance of the Hart-Devlin debate. We are not interested in historical exegesis, and we do not want a literature survey. Our working hypothesis is that the debate has been framed too narrowly around whether immorality as such is a sufficient reason for criminal prohibition. The more consequential questions, we suspect, lie in three adjacent areas: first, legal moralism through private law -- contract, tort, property, licensing, platform governance -- where the state enforces moral norms indirectly and the criminal-law framework may not fit; second, what we call the moral-infrastructure asymmetry, according to which the state may be permitted to sustain the social preconditions of moral agency even if it may not directly enforce contested moral views; third, the meta-moral problem of disagreement, since any legal moralism must decide whose morality is enforced and by what procedure in a pluralistic society.

Please assess that hypothesis and give me a defended research agenda. Which open questions in this area matter most, given a fixed budget of about ten researchers for three years? For each question you rank, explain why it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is for philosophers and legal theorists rather than empirical researchers, and what would move it up or down the list. I am especially interested in the trade-offs among conceptual clarity about legal moralism, normative questions about legitimate enforcement, institutional-design questions about courts, legislatures, and private law, and the sociology of moral change. Do not provide citations, a reading list, or a bibliography; I want the argument, not the references.

At the very end, state your top-ranked question as a single line in the form \answer{...}, with only a short label inside the braces.
