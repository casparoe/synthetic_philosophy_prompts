My PI has a favourite Putnam line she deploys about once a lab meeting — 'meanings just ain't in the head' — and it's started doing a lot of load-bearing work in arguments where I'm not sure it's licensed. Two recent examples. (1) I asked whether there's any fact of the matter about what a text-only model means when it emits 'water' — she said no, obviously not, meanings ain't in the head, so nothing inside the model can settle what its words are about, you'd have to look at what it's causally hooked up to. (2) In seminar she used the same sentence to say that two people with identical brains raised in different environments would mean different things by the same word, so introspection can never tell you what you mean. I think (2) is way stronger then (1) and I suspect they don't even come out of the same claim, but I can't say why.

So: can you lay out the distinct precise claims 'meanings just ain't in the head' could be standing in for, roughly from the boring to the radical, and give a verdict on each — platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or something a lot of careful people think is just false? The seams I'd guess matter:

(a) 'meaning' as what a natural-kind word picks out versus 'meaning' as the content of a thought — externalism about reference vs externalism about content.

(b) whether only the word's extension is fixed from outside, or whether the twins' *thoughts* differ too, so that they don't merely refer differently but believe different things.

(c) whether there's still a 'narrow' layer of content — some role, character, or rule that stays identical across the twins — that survives the slogan, and whether that layer is enough to run a psychology on, or whether the slogan is really the claim that no such layer exists.

(d) the self-knowledge version ('I can't know what I mean just by introspection'), which looks like an extra step on top of the semantic claim.

(e) the social version, where what fixes meaning is my community's usage rather than the stuff in my environment — the elm/beech sort of case, where I couldn't tell the two apart but my community could.

(f) the ones I suspect are straight-up conflations: sliding from 'meaning isn't in the head' to 'the mind extends beyond the head', or to 'there's no fact of the matter about meaning at all, so let's just measure behaviour'.

Two more wrinkles I keep tripping on. The classic version of the thought experiment is set centuries back, before anyone could tell the two liquids apart — does the slogan need the hidden difference to be in principle discoverable, or does it hold even then, in 1750? And does it survive a case with no causal history at all — something with my exact brain state that just came into being, or a model that has only ever read text and never touched the stuff?

For each reading, tell me whether it would actually licence (1), whether it would licence (2), and which reading my PI probably has in mind when she says it. Lay it out as a list I can bring to the meeting — I want to know which ones are true but boring, which are interesting but contested, and which aren't really what the sentence says at all.
