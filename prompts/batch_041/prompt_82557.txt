I run a small theory group — three doctoral students and a postdoc — with roughly two years of funding remaining, and our topic is the problem of logical omniscience: how to model agents that are rational and consistent without thereby knowing every logical truth and every consequence of what they already know. I do not want a survey or a reading list. I want a defended research agenda: which open questions in this area most deserve the group's effort, in what order, and on what grounds.

To sharpen the question, please stress-test rather than merely apply a frame I have been developing. Any model of a non-trivial but non-ideal agent seems forced to surrender at least one of three things: the factivity of the attitude (what is known is true), closure of the attitude under logical consequence, or the classical consequence relation itself, on which any logical truth follows from any premise whatever. Call whichever item a model gives up its omniscience debt. My conjecture is that no model escapes all three debts, that the real differences among models lie in which debt they pay and at what interest rate, and that a further adequacy condition ought to be imposed: a model must represent logical competence as a monotone dial, so that an agent can be made more or less logically capable without its knowledge set behaving non-monotonically along the way.

Given that frame, rank the open questions. For each, explain why it is upstream of the others rather than downstream, how tractable it is for people with a background in logic and no empirical apparatus, and what would move it up or down the list — a proof, a countermodel, a worked application to some concrete reasoning agent. I am especially interested in whether the monotone-dial condition does genuine work by ruling out otherwise attractive options, or is merely an aesthetic preference, and in which live questions my trilemma distorts or cannot see at all; I would rather be told that it is a red herring than have it applied by rote.
