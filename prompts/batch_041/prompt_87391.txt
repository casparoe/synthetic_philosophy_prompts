I have spent a term defending a position I do not hold, because a colleague's argument for it looks airtight and I cannot find the seam. The topic is the old one: why knowledge should be worth more than true belief. Here is the argument.

(1) A belief is a state with a job — to be true. Its epistemic value is whatever value it has in virtue of doing that job well.
(2) How well a belief does that job is settled by two things and no others: what it says, and whether it is true. Fix the content and the truth-value, and the value is fixed.
(3) Knowledge and mere true belief differ in neither. In the lucky guess and in the knowing case alike, the same proposition is believed and the same proposition is true.
(4) So knowledge and mere true belief are epistemically on a par, however the belief was arrived at.
(5) Hence the question why knowledge is better than true belief has no answer; the man who has walked the road to Larissa is no better off than the man who guesses the way right.
(6) And further: rechecking a result, replicating an experiment, seeking a second opinion, or drawing out consequences of what one already believes cannot raise the epistemic value of a belief, since none of it alters the content or the truth-value. Such work is worth doing only when it might turn a false or unsettled belief into a true one. Once you truly believe that p, there is nothing further to be gained epistemically by inquiring into p.

The last step is what I cannot accept, and I cannot see where the argument cheats. Consider four cases in which I am strongly inclined to say that two beliefs with the same content and the same truth-value differ in worth. Two forecasters both say it will rain tomorrow and both are right; one used a model, the other a coin. I would act on either, but I would stake my reputation only on the first. A pill installs a true belief with no supporting evidence whatsoever: I would rather have the physicist's belief than the pill's, though the proposition and its truth are identical. Testimony: I would rather be told something by someone who knows than by someone who is merely right, though I hear the same sentence and it is equally true. And nobody thinks it pointless to have a second reader check a proof, though the proof was already sound.

My colleague replies that all four cases track the believer rather than the belief — they concern what sort of person holds it, or what else that person believes, not the belief itself. He offers a diagnosis which he thinks should help me and which I think cuts the other way: the lucky believer's belief has no follow-through. Ask him why he holds it, or what would have to be the case for it to be false, and there is nothing to say. But follow-through, my colleague insists, is a feature of the man and not of the belief, and premise (2) is about the belief.

I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (2), and I am willing to give up (2). But I want to know the price.

Tell me which step you reject, and why it looked fine. If the argument is valid and the cost falls on a premise, say which premise is cheapest to abandon and what abandoning it commits me to. In particular: does the replacement principle still earn the name epistemic, or has it quietly collapsed into prudence? Does it survive the objection that if the belief is already true, there is nothing left for the extra feature to do? And what does your principle say about a case I have not listed — someone who holds a true belief formed by a reliable process he could neither describe nor defend, and who would drop it at the first word of an authority? Is that knowledge, and is his belief worth less than one held by someone who can answer for it?

I am not after a survey; I want the seam.
