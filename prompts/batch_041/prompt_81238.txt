I'm three weeks into a seminar on animal ethics, and the section on wild animals has left me with a list of gut verdicts that I can't get under a single heading. I've been keeping the list on my phone, and it runs roughly as follows. A fawn is being swept down the river behind my parents' house: I wade in and pull it out, and I'd think someone who let it drown to save their shoes had something wrong with them. A fox has been sleeping under the shed all spring with mange and a weeping sore on its flank: I trap it, drive it to the vet, pay for the antibiotics, and feel entirely fine about it. But in a hard February, when the deer in the woods behind us are visibly starving and I could put out hay for the price of a takeaway, I don't — and I have a fairly firm sense that I shouldn't, that I'd be propping up animals who are going to die anyway and dragging next spring's population up past what the woods can carry. A hawk has decided that my bird feeder is a buffet; I could net it or move it and I don't, and taking the squirrels' side against a hawk feels like a betrayal, even though a squirrel's death is no prettier than the fawn's would have been. Yet a hedgehog that turns up in the garden crawling with ticks and fly-strike goes straight to the vet, and if I could pick an eye-worm out of a finch's eye I would, without hesitating — even though in both of those cases I am killing the creatures that are eating the animal I'm rescuing. I should also admit that a mouse in the kitchen gets a trap without a flicker of what I feel about the fawn, and that when I say my own convenience doesn't enter into any of this, I'm not sure I believe myself.

So the pattern seems to be: accidents and disease, yes; starvation, no; a proper predator, no; a parasite, yes; and my own convenience, apparently, whenever it suits me. The best principle I've managed is something like: I may relieve suffering that isn't doing anyone any good, but I mustn't take sides between two animals who are each pursuing their own good, and I mustn't do anything that turns a wild animal into a dependent. That doesn't survive the fawn (the river isn't pursuing anything, but then neither is the winter), and it doesn't explain why the tick loses its case and the hawk wins.

A friend in the same seminar thinks I'm fiddling while Rome burns. She wants to drop the fawn business and fund a contraceptive bait programme instead, on the grounds that nearly every wild animal born dies young and dies badly, so the humane move is to have fewer of them born rather than to rescue the photogenic ones. My gut says the fawn and her programme differ in kind, but I can't say why, and I suspect it may only be that one is a photograph and the other is a spreadsheet. Is there a principle that gets my list, or close to it? If no single principle does, I'd rather be told which verdict has to go, what the repaired principle says about my friend's programme, and what it says about the case I keep dodging: the pond behind the barn that dries out every June with a few thousand tadpoles in it, which I could keep topped up with a hose.
