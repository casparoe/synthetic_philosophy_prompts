I have been arguing for a week with a friend about an argument he built himself and then told me, with some satisfaction, is a version of something called Bradley's regress. I cannot find the exit, and I want a diagnosis rather than a reading list or a set of citations. Here is his argument, as near as I can put it.

1. A proposition is a whole with parts. The proposition that the cat is on the mat contains the cat, the mat, and the relation of being-on; what distinguishes it from the proposition that the mat is on the cat, and from a mere heap consisting of the cat, the mat, and being-on, is that in the proposition these constituents are combined into a single thing.

2. Wherever several things are combined into one, something must do the combining; a plurality does not unify itself. Call whatever does it the tie.

3. The tie cannot be nothing over and above what it ties, or it does no work at all; so it is one more item standing alongside the items it was to tie.

4. But then the tie is itself only one item among several, and a plurality of items is precisely what needed unifying. So a further tie is required to tie the original tie to its items, and that further tie raises the same demand in turn.

5. The demand can therefore never be met: every proposition, if it has parts at all, requires an infinite series of ties.

6. A proposition is grasped in a single act, and an infinite series of distinct items cannot be grasped in a single act. So no proposition with parts is ever grasped; and a content that is never grasped is not the content of any thought, and says nothing.

7. Yet something is said and some things are true. So something above must give.

He then says the trouble is worse than that, because nothing in the argument depends on propositions being the wholes in question. A molecule, a knot, and a sentence are all wholes with parts, so by the same reasoning none of them is one thing either, and there are no composite objects at all. I offered what I thought was an escape: the tying of a knot is not a further piece of rope, so perhaps the unity of a whole can be a structural feature of it, a shape rather than an extra constituent. He replied that this only moves the problem, since a shape must be the shape of those parts, and something is now needed to tie the shape to them; and I could not tell whether he had refuted me or merely repeated himself.

What I want from you is a verdict. Say which numbered step should be rejected, or, if the argument is valid as written, say which of its steps is the least costly to abandon. Then explain why the step you reject looked plausible in the first place, what a reasonable person would have meant by it, and what giving it up costs. I would most like to keep step 1 and to avoid step 6, and I am willing to give up step 7 only if nothing else works; tell me what that preference commits me to.

Then take each of the recognizable exits and price it. One is that the tie is not a further item but the relation's own tying, as opposed to the relation considered merely as one more thing on a list. Another is that unity is primitive, so that the parts are abstractions from the whole rather than materials it was assembled from. Another is that the regress is real but infinite, and therefore harmless. Another is that there are no parts at all. For each, tell me what it must say about a case I actually care about: what distinguishes the proposition that the cat is on the mat from the proposition that the mat is on the cat, and from the mere collection of the cat, the mat, and being-on. If an exit cannot draw that distinction, say so plainly.

Write your answer in continuous prose, as you would in a long letter to someone who asked you this face to face. No headings, no numbered steps, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables.
