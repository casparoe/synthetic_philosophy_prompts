Next month I'm giving a paper at our work-in-progress seminar and I need to know what I'm actually going to get hit with in the Q&A. My thesis is that Keynes's late view of the good life -- the one you piece together if you read the memoir he read to the Memoir Club in 1938 alongside the economic possibilities for our grandchildren essay -- is not, as people usually say, just Moore's ideal minus the moralizing. It's a two-stage view. During the economic stage, acquisitiveness and calculation are instrumentally licensed; once the economic problem is solved, we're supposed to live off the intrinsic goods, the states of mind Moore cared about, friendship and love and beauty and knowledge. I argue the two stages don't fit together, because the dispositions the first stage trains you into are not the dispositions the second stage needs. I've been calling it the carry-over problem. And I think Keynes half-knows this, which is why he keeps reaching for the old-fashioned virtues -- avarice is a vice, usury is a misdemeanor, don't worry about tomorrow -- which is exactly the moralism he'd thrown over in the memoir. So the late position is a hybrid, and I want to argue the hybrid is unstable.

The room is mostly historians of economics and moral philosophers and they don't overlap much. Two of them work on Moore and the Apostles, they know Principia Ethica's treatment of organic unities and the method of isolation better than I do, and one of them thinks Keynes basically misread Moore. There's a Bloomsbury historian who won't accept that a memoir read aloud to friends counts as philosophy and will keep pressing me on public doctrine versus private self-presentation. A welfare economist will want to know what any of this buys you for measuring well-being or for policy. And a first-year grad student who's read the economic possibilities essay will ask the naive question that's actually the hard one: was Keynes a hedonist or a perfectionist?

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them, ordered by how much damage they do to me, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one I'm hoping nobody asks. And write it as one continuous email, no headings, no bullet points, no numbered list, no bold, no tables -- just paragraphs, I'll read it on the train.
