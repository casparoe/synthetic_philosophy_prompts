I am a high-school student, and I am writing to ask for help with a prediction. All year my school's philosophy club has been working through Kant's universalizability test — the requirement that one act only on a maxim that one could will as a universal law — and our faculty advisor, who runs the sessions, has taken on the role of adjudicator: a member proposes a maxim, and she rules whether it passes or fails. Her explanations are brief, and after a term of this we still cannot agree on which version of the test she is applying. The maxim for next week's session is mine, and I would prefer to see the ruling coming.

Her record so far, with the maxim and the gist of her reason in each case:

1. I will borrow money from a classmate, promising to repay it on Friday, when I know I will have nothing on Friday. Fails: if everyone did it, a promise would be worth nothing, and there would be nothing to borrow against.
2. I will invent an excuse for a missed deadline when I have no real excuse. Fails: if everyone did it, excuses would stop working and the penalty would fall anyway.
3. I will copy the answers of the person sitting beside me, when the teacher is not looking. Fails: if everyone did it, marks would stop measuring anything, and the mark I gained would be worth nothing.
4. I will cancel on a friend whenever a better invitation arrives. Fails: if everyone did it, nobody could make plans at all.
5. I will give up my seat on a crowded bus to someone who needs it more than I do. Passes: everyone could do it, and it would still work.
6. I will practise scales for half an hour a day so that I can play the pieces I like. Passes: nothing about it depends on other people doing otherwise.
7. I will tell a friend that they have food in their teeth, even though it will embarrass them. Passes: being unwelcome or inconvenient is not the same as failing the test.

Three readings of the test have survived our arguments. The collapse reading: a maxim fails only if universal adoption would destroy the very practice or convention on which the maxim depends — promising, excuse-making, examinations, and the like — so that the action could no longer be performed; a maxim that merely loses its edge because everyone is doing the same thing has destroyed nothing, and passes. The point reading: a maxim fails whenever universal adoption would leave me unable to achieve what I was acting for, which includes the case in which what I was acting for was to be ahead of other people and the advantage cancels out once everyone does the same thing, even though the practice itself survives untouched. The using-people reading: a maxim fails if acting on it involves deceiving or coercing someone, that is, treating a person merely as a tool, whatever would happen if everyone acted on it.

My maxim is this: I will arrive at the ticket office at dawn, hours before it opens, so that I am ahead of the people who turn up at opening time.

What will she rule, and why? Which of the three readings best explains the record above, and is there some further reading that explains it just as well? What does each reading predict about my maxim, and which prediction would you bet on? Finally, name one further maxim that would separate whichever readings are still tied once my case is decided, and give the verdict that each reading returns for it.
