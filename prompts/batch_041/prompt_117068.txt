I'm a third-year undergraduate enrolled in a philosophy of mind seminar, and the convenor has asked me to draft the questions for the moderated Q&A with next week's visiting speaker. Her work extends the intentional stance to large language models, and we've been told explicitly not to let her simply restate the position. I'd like eight to ten questions built around her view as I summarise it below, phrased the way I would actually put them in the room.

Her line, as I understand it, is this. Attributing a belief to a system is warranted when the attribution lets us predict and explain that system's behaviour better than the alternatives available to us. She denies that this makes the attribution a mere metaphor, and insists it can be literally true that a model believes something, provided we read 'believes' as a stance-relative predicate rather than as a name for an internal state. Two commitments do most of the work. First, the warrant she requires is local rather than global: an ascription can be fully warranted in one context and void in another, and she is content to say that a model believes P and believes not-P at once, because the two ascriptions are indexed to different prompts or registers. She treats this fragmentation as a feature rather than a defect, and claims the language-model case merely makes vivid something already true of human believers. Second, when pressed with the fact that the system is at bottom predicting tokens over learned features, she replies that this is a description at a different level and does not compete with the intentional one: the implementation is not the content.

Please include at least one question on each of the following:

1. The 'proves too much' worry: the same criterion appears to license belief ascriptions to thermostats, markets, and nation-states; if it does, what remains of the claim that ascribing beliefs to a model is more than a convenient manner of speaking?

2. The challenge from mechanistic interpretability: if the design-level story can generate all the same predictions, what does the intentional-level ascription add, and what would it take for a design-level finding to count as evidence against one of her ascriptions?

3. The fragmentation move: whether a notion of belief that tolerates P and not-P within a single system retains any cross-contextual predictive content, or whether it collapses into a disposition to emit certain outputs in certain contexts.

4. Normativity and function: beliefs seem answerable to evidence and to truth in a way that a system trained to minimise a prediction loss is not, so she owes an account of what the system would have to be like for an ascription to be criticisable rather than merely useful.

5. The practical upshot: what, if anything, follows for how we build, deploy, or treat these systems, and whether she accepts that her view sits comfortably alongside treating them as instruments.

For each question, add a follow-up aimed at the answer she is most likely to give; I would rather the follow-up anticipate the standard reply than ask her to spell it out. Finish with a question about what evidence or argument could count against her position, and make that final question one she cannot answer by repeating that the intentional level is autonomous.
