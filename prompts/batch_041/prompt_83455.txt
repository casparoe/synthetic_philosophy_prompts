I have no academic background in this area, so I apologize in advance if I garble the vocabulary. I have been trying to think this through on my own and keep going in circles. Somewhere I picked up a result usually called Good's theorem on the value of information. My understanding of it is this: if you are the kind of chooser who selects whichever option has the highest expected payoff, then information that arrives before you choose cannot make your choice worse in expectation, because at worst you can disregard it and choose exactly as you would have chosen without it. I take it the expectation is calculated before you see what the information says, and that the theorem concerns the quality of the decision rather than whether you are pleased by the news.

Here is what I cannot square with that. A friend was offered a free screening test for a condition that has no treatment and does not shorten her life. The result would not tell her anything she could act on; she says she would spend the rest of her days waiting for symptoms, and that she would probably stop making long-term plans. She declined, and she has even asked the clinic to record that she does not want to be told if a later test happens to pick the condition up, which she says is because she does not trust her future self to refuse a second time. I want to tell her that she is simply being irrational, since the theorem is a mathematical fact and a rational person cannot be made worse off by information. But the only defence of that verdict I can find is that she could always ignore the result, and that defence strikes me as empty, because she cannot un-know it. When I try to imagine a version of her case where she could act on the result, I am not sure the theorem helps me either: it seems to say something about the value of choosing with information, not about whether obtaining it is worthwhile.

So I would like to know whether I have the theorem wrong, or whether my friend is genuinely making a mistake. If the theorem does not settle her case, I would like to understand precisely what it does establish, and where the reasoning I keep reaching for breaks down.
