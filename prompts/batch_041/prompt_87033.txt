For a recorded interview series on ethics that I run for advanced undergraduates, I am preparing a long conversation with a philosopher who has defended for more than a decade the position associated with John Taurek's 1977 paper in Philosophy and Public Affairs: that the number of people who would be saved is not in itself a reason of any weight, that each person's claim to be rescued is equal in strength to every other's, that equal claims cannot be added together, and that a rescuer who saves the single person rather than the five has not, for that reason alone, acted wrongly. My guest has fielded the familiar objections many times, so I want questions that get past the versions of them he has already rehearsed. Draft eight to ten interview questions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Write each follow-up so that I can put it to him the moment he finishes the first question, and order the pairs so that the pressure accumulates: each question should exploit a concession that the previous answer has forced him to make.

At least one question should fall under each of the following headings, since these are the places where I take his position to be thinnest.

First, indifference versus permissibility. Whether his view is committed only to the claim that a rescuer may save either group, or to the stronger claim that there is nothing to choose between the outcomes, and what he thinks we are saying when we say, as most of us want to, that the world in which the five are saved is the better world.

Second, the lottery. Whether the fair-chance procedure usually offered as the practical upshot of his view is derived from the equality of the claims or is an independent proposal, and whether the rescuer is required to run a lottery, permitted to, or free to ignore it.

Third, scaling. Whether the numbers begin to count at some magnitude, so that saving a thousand is required even if saving one is not, and what he says to the suggestion that a view without a threshold makes a mockery of triage.

Fourth, aggregation inside a life. Whether he allows that one person's claim can be stronger than another's because more is at stake for that person, death say against permanent paralysis, and how that is supposed to differ in kind from the aggregation he rejects across persons.

Fifth, rescue versus allocation. Whether the same reasoning governs a hospital with one ventilator and several patients, or a health service dividing a fixed budget, and, if the view is meant only for emergencies, what is supposed to mark the emergency off from the allocation.

His book takes a line I particularly want to test: he grants that a rescuer may be permitted to save the many, but insists that this permission is a permission to follow her own preference and not a reason that the many's claims supply. I would like questions that press on whether that distinction can bear the weight he puts on it, using cases I have been mulling and cannot settle. A lifeboat that can carry either one adult or two children, where the adult is the only person aboard who can operate the radio. A case in which the single person will die within the hour and the five will live a month before dying of their injuries. A case in which the five are the ones whose negligence caused the accident and the single person is an uninvolved bystander. A case in which the alternative to one person's death is five people's permanent paralysis. A case in which the single person is the rescuer's own child.

I do not need you to answer any of these; I need the questions that put them to him. Please also include one closing question that would be worth ending the episode on, and one question about what his view implies for what we may teach children, or blame adults for, in matters of rescue.

Some constraints. Do not name secondary sources, supply titles, dates, or page references, or tell me what some other philosopher has said: my audience has not read the literature, and I would rather the questions carry their own argument. Do not attribute to my guest any position beyond the one described above; where a question presupposes a commitment he has not explicitly taken on, phrase it as a question about whether he is in fact committed to it. No biographical questions, and nothing about how he came to the problem. For each question, add a sentence on what a genuine concession from him would sound like, so that I can recognise one if he makes it.
