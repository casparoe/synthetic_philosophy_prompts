I have been trying to decide whether to regard a proposed mind-uploading procedure as a way of surviving or as a way of being replaced by a copy, and I have found that the question turns on a comparison I cannot make cleanly on my own. The procedure would replace the neurons of a living brain one at a time with silicon devices that reproduce the replaced cell's input-output behaviour exactly and are wired into the surrounding tissue so that the brain's overall activity never pauses.

Two accounts of what it is for a person to persist are on offer. The first, which I will call the causal-continuity account, holds that a person persists just in case the causal-functional organization of her brain is carried forward by an unbroken, spatiotemporally continuous chain of processing, each stage causally responsible for the next; the material that implements the organization is irrelevant, but the causal link between stages is not. The second, which I will call the pattern account, holds that a person persists just in case the right functional pattern is instantiated, however it came to be instantiated; causal ancestry from the earlier instance is irrelevant.

Please compare the two accounts. I would like a verdict on each of the following cases rather than a survey of opinion:

1. The one-at-a-time replacement described above, carried out while the subject is awake and conversing.
2. A variant in which a complete silicon duplicate is assembled alongside the biological brain, allowed to run in parallel with it for a time, and then left to continue on its own when the biological brain is switched off, so that the pattern that goes on was never causally produced by the pattern that stops.
3. A variant in which the replacement occurs only during periods of dreamless sleep, so that no single continuous stream of experience spans the transition.
4. A case in which the biological brain is left intact and the silicon system continues beside it, yielding two systems each of which satisfies the account's condition on persistence.

Then three further questions. First, the procedure has no privileged step at which the subject might cease; does the absence of such a step tell in favour of either account, or does it instead support a third position, defended in the literature on personal identity, on which survival is a matter of degree and neither account's all-or-nothing condition is the right form for an answer? Second, can either account do justice to the subject's first-person testimony that the procedure is experienced as an unbroken continuation, or does that testimony sit equally well with both? Third, what must each account assume about whether a silicon device can realize a conscious state, and is that assumption doing any independent work in the comparison, or is it merely presupposed by both?

Finish by saying which account you think is more likely to be true, and by identifying the point at which the two disagree most deeply, as opposed to the cases on which they merely return different verdicts.
