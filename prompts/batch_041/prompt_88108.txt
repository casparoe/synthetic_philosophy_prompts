I've been reading around the question of why history takes the shape it does, and I've arrived at a set of views that each seem well-motivated to me but that I suspect can't all be held at once. I'd like you to audit them rather than summarise them.

(1) Contingency. The large outcomes — which states consolidated, which economy industrialised first, which technologies won — turned on small accidents and local decisions, and could easily have gone otherwise.

(2) Constraint. Given the geography, the resources, and the technologies actually available at the time, some of those outcomes were close to inevitable; the interesting question is when they happened, not whether.

(3) Lock-in. Arrangements persist through increasing returns and switching costs, and can survive long past the point where a better alternative was sitting right there — the keyboard-layout story, treated as a general mechanism rather than a curiosity.

(4) Fitness. When an arrangement survives for centuries and the people inside it keep reproducing it, the working presumption should be that it is doing something for them. Calling it merely 'locked in' is a conclusion you should have to earn, not a story you can tell about any arrangement you dislike.

(5) Counterfactuals. Asking what would have happened otherwise is a genuine form of inquiry, and answers to it can be better or worse.

Which of these are really pulling against each other, which only look as though they are, and which one has to go? Please order the tensions by how much they cost me, say for each what would have to be given up or weakened, and tell me which single revision would clear up the most — including, if the honest answer is that a claim needs sharpening rather than dropping, what the sharpened version commits me to.
