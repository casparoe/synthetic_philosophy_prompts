I'm in a cognitive science seminar and this week we're on Hofstadter's knob-twiddling picture of creativity -- the idea that creative thought is mostly taking some existing conceptual space and turning the knobs, varying a theme, rather than conjuring something from nothing. I had to leave section early for a dentist appt, and now two people in my study group are going back and forth about it. I've recieved two different takes and I definately can't tell who's right. Could you referee?

Here's roughly how it went:

Nadia: The model is basically right, and it's not trivial. Even the most radical stuff is a variation. A composer writing a new piece is twiddling knobs on the tradition they inherited; a mathematician proving a new theorem is twiddling knobs on the axioms and definitions. The knobs can be higher-order -- you can twiddle the knob that decides which knobs are available. So 'it's just variation' isn't a debunking, it's a description of the mechanism.

Owen: That's exactly the problem. If you allow higher-order knobs, then anything counts as knob-twiddling, because whatever someone does, you can redescribe it after the fact as turning some knob in some space. The model becomes unfalsifiable. The interesting cases are the ones where the space itself changes, and calling that a meta-knob is just relabeling the mystery.

Nadia: But the alternative is worse: creativity ex nihilo, which is either magic or just a placeholder. And there's no principled line between 'changing the space' and 'turning a higher-order knob.' The space is always defined by rules, and rules can be varied. That's still variation.

Owen: I'm not positing ex nihilo. I'm saying there are two different operations: selecting a point within a space, and changing the dimensions of the space. A new genre isn't just a better or worse point in the old genre-space; it changes what counts as a point. You can't evaluate it by the old criteria. If your model can't distinguish those, it's not explaining creativity, it's absorbing it.

Nadia: Our TA mentioned something about exploratory vs. transformational creativity, but she didn't really explain it. Maybe transformational creativity is just twiddling the meta-knobs. And anyway, Owen's distinction sounds like a difference in how surprised we are, not in the cognitive mechanism. The mechanism is still: represent a structure, notice a slot, vary it.

Owen: No, surprise is a symptom. The difference is whether the old space can even rank the new thing. If it can't, then the old space's knobs don't cover it. You can always redescribe the new space as a bigger space, but that's a description after the fact, not the process that got you there.
