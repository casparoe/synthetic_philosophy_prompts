hey, for my philosphy club I have to do a case study instead of the usual Anna Karenina thing, so I picked the Sherlock Holmes mess. In 1893 Conan Doyle killed Holmes off at the Reichenbach Falls and readers were genuinely wrecked — people cancelled their subscriptions to the Strand and sent him furious letters, and he ended up bringing Holmes back about ten years later. So, the paradox of fiction: how can we be really moved by someone we know doesn't exist. The answers I've read are that we only make-believe we're upset, or that a mere thought is enough to move you, or that we're just wrong about our own beliefs. My own spin, tell me if it's a cheat: the reaction is three things wearing one coat — real grief for Holmes (the paradoxical bit), real anger at Doyle (not paradoxical at all, he's a real person who did something to us), and ordinary sadness that there'd be no more stories. I've been calling the middle one the reader's-grievance reading. Does splitting it like that dissolve the paradox or just relocate it, and does any one of the three answers cover all three pieces, or does each one only get a bit? And what does the case do to the theory — is the Holmes affair the sort of thing the paradox was built for, or does the anger at Doyle show it was asking the wrong question all along?
