I have spent two years writing a novel about memory, and I have reached the point where I need to know which questions I am actually writing about. The book follows a woman who spends a decade assembling an archive of a massacre in her village and then, deliberately and in full possession of her faculties, destroys it. I am not a philosopher and have no wish to smuggle a treatise into the fiction, but I also do not want the book to rest on received positions — that remembrance is a debt owed to the dead, that forgetting is a species of cowardice, that a community is constituted by what it refuses to release. Those formulations are familiar enough that a reader will supply them before I can complicate them, and I would rather build on genuinely unsettled ground.

So I want you to set me an agenda. Assume I have roughly three years and one book, that the effort at stake is mine, and that the goal is not a survey of the field but a decision about which unresolved questions should govern the novel's thinking and in what order of priority. Rank them and defend the ranking: tell me which are upstream of which, which are tractable for a novelist rather than a philosopher, which I should deprioritize because their resolution would not change much downstream, and what evidence or argument over the coming years would move a question up or down the list.

To make the territory concrete, here are the loose ends I keep returning to, in no particular order. First, I suspect that commemoration is itself a form of forgetting, since every memorial selects a version of events and releases the rest; if that is right, the supposed opposition between remembering and forgetting may be false, and I want to know what follows from that. Second, there seems to be an asymmetry between what one is obligated to remember and what one is still able to remember — can a duty attach to a memory one has genuinely lost, and if so, to whom is it owed? Third, I am drawn to the thought that forgetting might sometimes be owed rather than merely permitted: owed to the next generation, or to the descendants of perpetrators, or to oneself. Fourth, I keep noticing a distinction between the memory a community owes its own members and the memory it owes strangers, and I cannot decide whether it is a real distinction or a sentimental one. Fifth, and most privately, I am interested in the second-order case — forgetting that one has forgotten — and what a person or a polity owes in light of it.

Tell me which of these are live questions and which are dead ends, tell me what I have missed, and tell me which two or three I should build the book around. I care far less about the list than about the reasoning: what makes a question worth a novel's sustained attention, how the criteria of importance and tractability trade off against each other, and where the answers would actually bite.
