I'm in a reading group that's nominally on epistemology and we ended up arguing about whether you can actually learn anything by deduction. I said obviously you can, I find bugs in code that way and I got through a maths degree that way, and my friend said, fine, and then built this thing to show I'm wrong. I've been carrying it around for a week and I still can't find the exit.

(1) A valid argument is one where the premises can't be true and the conclusion false. (2) To believe the premises of a valid argument just is to be in a state that rules out the conclusion being false -- if someone says "I believe P, and P entails Q, but I have no opinion about Q," we don't hear that as a possible combination of beliefs, we hear it as someone who hasn't really taken P on board. Beliefs have to answer to consistency; thats what makes them beliefs about something rather than a bag of sentences in the head. (3) So if you believe the premises you can't disbelieve the conclusion; and if you can't disbelieve it, you believe it. (4) So there's never a case where you believe the premises, don't yet believe the conclusion, and then come to believe it by inferring it: either you already had the conclusion or you never really had the premises. (5) If you already had it, the inference didn't add a belief; if you never had the premises, the inference never got going. (6) So nobody ever comes to believe something new by deductive reasoning, and learning by deduction is impossible.

He says (6) isn't a reductio, it's the discovery, and that the whole picture of inference as a way of acquiring beliefs is a hangover from thinking of belief as a sentence in the head that gets copied around. He also says this is what people call the problem of logical omniscience, except that he thinks omniscience is the correct answer and our discomfort with it is the mistake. One person in the group says the culprit is (2), that he's trading on two different things we call belief -- one is what the belief commits you to, the other is what you actually have going on upstairs -- and another says (2) is fine and it's the slide from "can't disbelieve" to "believes" in (3) that does all the work, since having no attitude towards something isn't the same as having a positive one. I haven't been able to make either of those diagnoses stick, and I keep coming back to the fact that if the argument is sound then my friend hasn't convinced me of anything either, which feels like it ought to matter.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? Say I keep (2) but deny that believing P means you believe everything P entails -- then what is belief, and can I still say that having inconsistent beliefs is a defect rather than just an odd sort of combination? Or say I keep the whole argument and just insist that we do believe all the consequences of our beliefs, only implicitly, or dispositionally, or in a way thats parcelled out among fragments of the mind -- what is "implicitly" doing there, and does it leave (4) and (5) standing? I'd rather not give up (1), and I really dont want to give up the idea that reasoning can teach me something, but I want to know which one I'm actually paying with.
