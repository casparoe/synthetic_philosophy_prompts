For the last meeting of my undergraduate course on chance and credence I want to run a session on the Principal Principle instead of lecturing on it, and I have decided to hang the session on Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead. The play opens with the two of them betting on coin flips — one coin, tossed again and again, Rosencrantz calling heads every time — and it comes up heads ninety-two times running, at which point Guildenstern stops treating the run as a curiosity and begins to wonder whether they have fallen among supernatural or subnatural forces, whether the laws of probability have been suspended, and whether anything that happens to them was ever really open in the first place. My students arrive with the standard apparatus: the credence/chance distinction; the Principal Principle as the requirement that one's credence in an event, conditional on the proposition that its objective chance is some value x together with one's admissible evidence, equal x; the sense in which admissibility does the quiet work, since evidence bearing on how the chance came to be what it is — a weighted coin, a rigged toss — is exactly what licenses departure from it; and a first pass at the worry that if the world is deterministic there are no chances for the principle to be about, only our ignorance.

What I want from you is a reading of the play through that material, and I want it to be faithful in both directions rather than a dramatized textbook. Take the coin scene seriously as evidence and as reasoning: say whether Guildenstern's alarm is a fallacy at all, or whether a run of that length is precisely the sort of evidence that bears on the chance itself, so that he is making a legitimate inference where a gambler expecting tails on the ninety-third toss would be making an illegitimate one; say what role the admissibility proviso plays in distinguishing the two, and whether Rosencrantz, who never varies his call, is being stupid or is following the only sensible policy under uncertainty about the coin. Then take up what the play's own metaphysics does to the principle: its world is a script, the two of them are minor figures whose deaths were written before they were born into the play, and the troupe of tragedians enacts deaths on demand — so what, if anything, is the objective chance of an outcome in that world, and can an agent inside a script defer to a chance that has collapsed to zero or one? Is the characters' predicament genuinely aleatory, or is it purely epistemic — they do not know the script — and does that distinction matter for whether the Principal Principle has any grip on them? There is also a question I keep tripping over myself, which is that the Principal Principle is a norm for belief, whereas the coin in the play functions as a decision procedure, settling who has won; I would like you to say whether the play conflates the two, and whether there is a separate and defensible question about when it is rational to let a chance device decide something rather than merely to believe in accordance with it.

Finally, be honest about where the play resists the reading. Stoppard is writing about being a minor character, about having one's part assigned and one's lines supplied, about the arbitrariness of the roles one is handed, and a critic who treats the play as a dramatization of a probability seminar will be false to it; I would rather have a reading that marks the point at which the philosophical apparatus runs out and says plainly that the play is about something else. Close by telling me what the play shows that the argument on its own does not — what a reader comes away believing about deference to chance that no formulation of the principle quite conveys. No citations or references are needed; I know the literature and have read the play, so keep the discussion to the scenes themselves and do not worry about quoting Stoppard at length.
