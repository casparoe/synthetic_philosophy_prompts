I run a moral theory seminar and we've gotten stuck on Williams's integrity objection in a way that's starting to feel pathological — a whole session, twice, and the same eight people saying the same eight things. I want a diagnosis of the stalemate, not another round of it.

Here's the shape as I see it. The rescuer wades in and pulls his wife out; if you then ask him for his reason and he offers the thought that she's his wife, or worse that saving one's wife is permissible, then on Williams's view that's one thought too many — the point of the action is precisely that it doesn't run through that thought. The consequentialist reply is the indirect or sophisticated move: the criterion of rightness needn't be the agent's decision procedure, the agent can just have the dispositions and act from love, and the theory is none the worse for not being present in his head. Then the Williams side says: sure, but that only shows the theory has to be kept away from the agent, that it can only be true if it's self-effacing, and a theory of how to live that the person living it can't hold in view is the wrong shape for a theory of how to live. And we go around again.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, shoot it down if it's wrong — is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading. One side is asking what makes an action right and thinks any action should be defensible in impartial terms; the other is asking what it is to have a life that's yours, and thinks an ethics that can only be true when hidden from the person is not an ethics for us. If that's right, the word reason is doing double duty and half the heat is verbal.

But I also had a thought I want you to take seriously or kill: split what I've been calling transparency from defensibility. Transparency says the thought must not figure in the agent's deliberation; defensibility says the agent must be able to give the impartial reason if challenged. Maybe Williams only needs transparency and the sophisticated consequentialist only denies that defensibility is a problem — in which case they might not even be contradicting each other, and the apparent conflict is an artifact of running the two together. Is that split real and does it do any work, or is it just a redescription of the same standoff?

And test it on a case that goes the other way for me: a parent who hides evidence for a child who's committed a serious crime, acting purely from love and loyalty, with no impartial thought anywhere in the deliberation. Here I find myself wanting exactly the thought that Williams says is one too many. So is the objection about relationships as such, or about a particular way of holding them — and if it's the latter, is there a principled line, or does the whole thing collapse into taste about which attachments we admire?

So: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, a hidden disagreement about the point of moral theory, two questions under one name, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for — with reasons drawn from what each side is actually committed to. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? A distinction, a formal result about self-effacing theories, a better case, or an honest admission that we should stop arguing about the objection and start arguing about which conception of ethics we want.
