I've been reading a popular introduction to existentialism and want to know how much of its chapter on Sartre I should trust before I repeat any of it. Its summary runs roughly as follows.

Sartre's central claim is that human beings are radically free. Since there is no God and no fixed human nature, nothing determines what we do; every action is a free choice, and at any moment we could always have chosen otherwise — which is why Sartre says we are condemned to be free. Bad faith is the self-deception by which we hide this freedom from ourselves; the book's illustrations are the café waiter who has convinced himself that he is nothing but a waiter, and the woman who tells herself she has no choice but to go along with her companion's advances. Bad faith, on this reading, is essentially a lie we tell ourselves. And because our freedom is total, we are responsible for everything that happens to us and there are no excuses. The aim of existentialist ethics, the chapter concludes, is therefore to live in good faith — to acknowledge our freedom and refuse to identify ourselves with any of our roles.

I suspect the chapter is broadly right about the big picture but that some of the details are the kind of thing a specialist would wince at. Please go through it sentence by sentence: tell me what a careful reader of Sartre would accept, what is a standard simplification, and what is actually a misrepresentation — and then say whether the closing recommendation, to aim at good faith, follows from the parts that survive or whether it depends on the parts that don't.
