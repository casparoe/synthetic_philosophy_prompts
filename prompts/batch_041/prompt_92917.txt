Ok so I'm in this historiography / philosophy-of-history seminar and we just spent a week on the Great Divergence, the whole "why did the industrial revolution happen in Britain and not in China?" thing — the prof said it's sometimes called the Needham question. I came away convinced something is off with the question itself, so I built an argument for why, and now the argument proves way to much and I can't find where I went wrong. Here it is:

(1) A contrastive why-question — "why P rather than Q?" — is a good question only if Q was a genuine alternative to P, i.e. a way things could actually have gone, not just any state of affairs we can imagine.

(2) Whether Q counts as a genuine alternative always depends on what we're holding fixed in the background. Hold fixed that both regions had coal and markets and the salient alternative to "Britain" might be "the Netherlands"; hold fixed the broad civilizational trajectory instead and it might be "China," or "Asia as a whole." Change the background and the interesting contrast changes.

(3) So which Q a given why-question is asking is fixed by our explanatory interests, not by anything in the past itself.

(4) A question whose content is fixed entirely by the asker's interests has no answer that's determinate independently of those interests — two askers with different interests are literally asking different questions.

(5) For the industrial revolution there's no single privileged contrast: "not China," "not the Netherlands," "not Asia," "not the rest of Europe" all seem about equally defensible depending on what you care about.

(6) So "why Britain and not China?" doesn't pick out one determinate question.

(7) So it has no determinate answer — which I take to be just what it means to say a question isn't well posed.

(8) But nothing in this reasoning was special to China. "Why did the armistice come in November 1918 rather than October or December?" also has its contrast fixed by what we're interested in.

(9) So no historical why-question is well posed.

(10) But that's absurd — some historical explanations are clearly better than others and do answer the question they were asked. So at least one of (1)–(4) is false.

I'd really like to keep (1) and (2); they feel almost tautological to me, and I'm not especially attached to (5). So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up actually cost me? If I keep (1) and (2), am I stuck biting the bullet on (9)? And please be upfront about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it, because the prof made it sound like some of this is just live disagreement, and I don't want to walk into the seminar treating a disputed view as if it were the obvious consensus.
