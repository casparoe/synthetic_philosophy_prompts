I am a public-health physician and epidemiologist. My professional life has been spent drafting, defending and revising surveillance case definitions: the operational criteria — clinical, laboratory, epidemiological — by which a patient's symptoms become a countable case of a named disease. I am fluent in the trade-offs that govern that work. I know why a definition is better understood as a decision rule with a cost attached to each kind of error than as a description; why criteria that cannot be measured directly have to be inferred from circumstantial linkage; how a threshold written into a definition fixes the size of the epidemic it will report; and how a revision can silently rewrite the history of an outbreak that is already over. I also know that the wording of a case definition is negotiated by committees with interests of their own, and that a body which does not wish to count something has ways of not counting it.

I have been reading about genocide — about Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word because the law of his day had no room for the crime he wanted to name, about the treaty that fixed the word to a narrower and more demanding definition than the idea that motivated it, and about the long argument over when an atrocity may properly be called by that name.

Treat this as a translation exercise, not a request for a summary or an essay. Take the following, one at a time, and restate each in the vocabulary of case definitions and outbreak declaration:

1. The treaty definition: five enumerated acts, committed with the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a group of one of four listed kinds, and targeting it because of what it is rather than incidentally. Add the two requirements that do most of the work — the special intent, which is rarely confessed and is usually inferred from the pattern of acts, and the requirement that the part of the group targeted be substantial enough to threaten the group as a whole.
2. The gap between the coinage and the codification: an original conception that treated the destruction of a group's political and social institutions, its language, its religion and its economic existence as aspects of a single process, narrowed by negotiation into five acts and a closed list of group types.
3. The claim that whether an atrocity is a genocide is a question of law and evidence, and the opposing claim that the naming is itself an act with consequences, so that institutions have interests in not saying the word.
4. The suggestion that the category does something other than describe — that it constitutes as much as it classifies.

For each, give me three things: what carries over cleanly and without loss; what carries over only with distortion, and precisely what the distortion is; and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Then the residue report I actually want. Where the translation fails, say whether the failure is a defect of my framework — something epidemiology cannot represent — or evidence that the original claim was doing less work than its proponents suppose.

My own instinct, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that the special-intent requirement is the analogue of the criterion by which a case is counted because it is epidemiologically linked to a known case, without direct laboratory confirmation of the cause. Tell me whether that mapping is as good as it looks or whether it breaks, and if it breaks, where.

Finally, two things I suspect my vocabulary cannot say, on which I want your judgement. First, whether a framework whose categories distribute attention and resources rather than blame can carry the condemnatory force of the word at all, or whether the moral content survives in it only as an externality — something to be managed, like the stigma that attaches to a disease name. Second, whether my habit of treating the unit of counting as uncontroversial, so that a case is simply a case and the population at risk is a given, is a strength of my framework or its blind spot, when the group whose members are being counted is itself the thing in dispute.

Keep the translation honest. Do not let my framework's assumptions in through the back door and call it translation: if a criterion in my vocabulary presupposes something the legal category does not, or the reverse, say so explicitly.
