At work we're arguing about moving to a four-day week and my managers entire case is "time is money": pay stays the same, output doesn't, so the missing hours are just money walking out the door. The same phrase came up with a friend who worked in Japan at one of those karoshi-level companies — there, any hour not worked was treated as an hour stolen. I want a menu of what "time is money" could actually be claiming, from the boring to the wild, with a verdict on each: is it just that my hour has a market price, or that my time literally is a kind of money, or that leisure is money I'm declining to collect, or that idling is theft? Weber quotes Franklin on this in the Protestant Ethic — "Remember, that time is money. He that can earn ten shillings a day by his labor, and goes abroad, or sits idle, one half of that day, though he spends but sixpence during his diversion or idleness, ought not to reckon that the only expense; he has really spent, rather thrown away, five shillings, besides." Is that an accounting point or a moral one? And which reading is actually doing the work when someone uses the slogan against shorter hours — and does it survive the karoshi case, where the hours were paid for and the person broke anyway?
