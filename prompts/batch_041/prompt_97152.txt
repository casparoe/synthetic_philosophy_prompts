Write a poem in the voice of a display that is trying to be believed, and build it on a single formal constraint: a tail. The poem opens with a two-line stanza, and each stanza after it carries one line more than the one before, until the final stanza runs to seven lines, so that by the end the poem is hauling a burden it could not have managed at the start. The added lines must be load-bearing; if the surplus reads as padding, the conceit collapses, and the poem's argument is precisely that the surplus is not padding. Rhyme and metre are yours to choose; the tail is the form.

The concept to get right is the handicap principle, including the part that is usually skipped. A signal earns belief not because it is costly as such, but because the cost falls unequally: ruinous for a low-quality sender, affordable for a high-quality one, so that the display sorts senders rather than merely advertising them. Let the reader feel both sides of that asymmetry, the extravagance that makes the signal credible and the rival who attempted the same display and could not pay for it. In at least one line, mark the difference between a signal that cannot be counterfeited at all, the sort of thing the body simply cannot lie about, and a signal that could in principle be faked but is too expensive to fake. The poem should know which of the two it is offering.

Two further requirements. First, the receiver's response belongs inside the mechanism rather than outside it: the display is worth its cost only because something is choosing, so the poem should address a chooser and should concede that the price is paid before the verdict is known. Second, the poem must not pretend the display is proof; a handicap is evidence that the sender could bear the cost, and nothing more. Let the longest stanza end on the risk rather than the reward.

Accuracy matters more than prettiness here. A reader who studies signaling should find nothing in the poem to object to, and a reader who has never encountered the handicap principle should still find a poem.
