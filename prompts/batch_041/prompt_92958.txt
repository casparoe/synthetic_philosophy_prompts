I'm an undergraduate currently enrolled in a philosophy of mind course, and we've spent the past two weeks on panpsychism and on what our instructor calls the combination problem. The framing our seminar keeps circling back to is an analogy, and before I write my final paper I want to know how far that analogy actually holds.

The analogy runs roughly as follows. Just as a macroscopic physical object is nothing over and above its microscopic parts arranged in a certain way — a wave just is a great many water molecules moving together, with no extra waveness added on top — so a macroscopic experience is supposed to be nothing over and above a great many micro-experiences arranged in a certain way, with no extra macro-subject added on top. On this picture, consciousness combines the way physical stuff combines, and the emergence of one unified mind out of countless micro-minds is no more mysterious than the emergence of a wave out of molecules. The conclusion the analogy is being used to license is that panpsychism faces no special combination problem: whatever story we tell about how parts make wholes in physics, we can tell the same story about how micro-experiences make a mind.

I would like a careful audit of this. Please give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the water molecules, what plays the role of the wave, what plays the role of the laws governing the arrangement — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. I especially want you to press on whether the physical case and the experiential case are even the same kind of combination. What, if anything, in the water case corresponds to the fact that the resulting experience is had by a single subject? Is there any analogue, on the physical side, of the worry that many subjects cannot simply sum into one? And does anything in the water case correspond to the first-person character of experience, or is that precisely the feature with no physical counterpart?

Once the mapping is laid out, please separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the conclusion being drawn from the ones that are merely incidental, and then say whether the no-special-problem conclusion survives with the important ones on the table. If the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, tell me what a better analogy would be — or argue that the combination problem is the kind of thing that resists analogies of this sort altogether, and say what that would mean for the panpsychist's case.
