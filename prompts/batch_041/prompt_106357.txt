You are a philosopher of mind with a specialist's knowledge of the qualia debates and a good ear for the exact step at which an argument overreaches. Think this through carefully before answering, and set out your reasoning; begin by stating, in a sentence each, what work each step of the argument is doing.

A colleague has been pressing the following argument on me for weeks. I can't accept the conclusion, but I can't locate the flaw either.

The case. Two expert coffee tasters, Ines and Omar, are alike in every measurable gustatory respect: identical training, identical scores on discrimination tests, the same words for the house blend, the same shift from ordering it daily to ordering tea, the same expectations about how it will taste tomorrow. They differ in exactly one thing. Ines says the coffee tastes as it always did and that she has simply gone off it; Omar says the coffee itself now tastes different to him. Neither can re-taste the blend as it was twenty years ago; each has only a present memory of it.

The argument.
(1) A quale is a property of experience that is private to its subject, ineffable in public language, and intrinsic — not constituted by the state's causes or its effects.
(2) A property earns its place in our psychology only if it can in principle make a difference to something, at the very least to what its possessor is disposed to judge or to say.
(3) By (1), a quale is not constituted by its relations to stimuli, behaviour, or physiology. So no public fact — a discrimination score, a scan, a verbal report — can count for or against a claim about a quale.
(4) The subject's own awareness is the only remaining candidate. But her awareness of her quale is either a state of that same public kind (a judgment, a report, a disposition to answer) or it is not. If it is, (3) excludes it too. If it is not, it is inaccessible to her as well and cannot be what she is reporting.
(5) So nothing, not even the subject's own awareness, can count for or against a claim about qualia; the quale makes no difference to anything.
(6) By (2) the concept of a quale is idle, and if it is idle there is nothing for it to name.

Two things bother me. First, this seems to prove far too much: substitute 'pain' or 'after-image' for 'quale' and it appears to show that 'I am in pain' is not a genuine claim, which I won't accept. Second, I want to keep the first-person authority that makes the case interesting — Ines does seem to know something about her own case that no test can capture.

So: which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? Tell me what becomes of the private/ineffable/intrinsic conception of qualia if I give it up, whether any recognisable notion of qualia survives, and whether the same repair rescues pain or whether qualia and pain come apart here.
