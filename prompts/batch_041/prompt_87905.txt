Alright, I keep getting stuck on Moore's proof of an external world and I think I'm missing something basic. The moves as I understand them: he holds up one hand and says here is a hand, holds up the other and says here is another, and concludes that at least two external objects exist, so there's an external world. Then when someone asks him to prove the premises he more or less says he can't — he just knows them.

Heres my confusion. If you can't prove here is a hand, then the only way it can do any work as a premise is if it's the kind of thing that's simply given to you — self-evident, non-inferential, known by some immediate acquaintance that doesn't stand in need of support. Fine. But then what separates Moore from any confident person with a weird belief? A flat earther can say look at the horizon, it's obviously flat, I can just see it, that's not something I need to argue for. Everybody's favorite belief feels like that from the inside. So as far as I can tell Moore's proof reduces to him reporting that he's extremely sure, plus a valid bit of modus ponens, and that combo seems to prove nothing to anyone who isn't already sure. And it feels like it can't even engage a skeptic, since the skeptic's whole point is that the premise isn't given in that special way — which makes me think the proof isn't really an argument at all, just a refusal to play along dressed up as one.

Am I right that the proof has to be resting on some special non-inferential certainty, and if so is it just dogmatism with extra steps? If I'm wrong about that, where exactly does it go wrong — because I can't see how you get from I know it but can't prove it to this is a real proof without sneaking in the special-certainty thing. And I'd want to know what Moore would say to the flat earther, since that's the case that makes me doubt the whole thing. Answer in American English please.
