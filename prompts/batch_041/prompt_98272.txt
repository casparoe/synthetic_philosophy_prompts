Write a formal dialogue between two philosophers of biology who hold opposing views about what makes something alive. Both should be first-rate: neither is a strawman, each engages the other's actual arguments, and the exchange should run several turns rather than two monologues delivered in sequence.

The two positions are these. The first philosopher holds that life is fundamentally a matter of self-maintaining, far-from-equilibrium organization: a system is alive to the degree that it actively sustains its own structure and boundary by drawing on a flow of energy and exporting entropy, with reproduction and heredity treated as late and optional additions. The second holds that life is fundamentally a matter of being caught up in descent with modification: a system is alive to the degree that it carries a heritable, imperfectly copied structure that can be selected, with metabolism and self-maintenance treated as optional additions.

To give their disagreement something concrete to bite on, stipulate the following two cases. Treat both as given; the question is not whether they actually occur, but what they show.

The mineral lineage. In a certain geological setting, crystals of a particular habit, as they grow, leave behind a lattice that makes further crystals of the same habit more likely to nucleate nearby. Occasional defects in the lattice are copied into the next round of crystals, so that distinct lineages of crystals, differing in their defect patterns, persist and spread at different rates according to the local chemistry. Over long times the population drifts toward whichever defect patterns nucleate fastest under prevailing conditions. There is no metabolism, no boundary to maintain, and nothing like an internal organization kept in repair; the information is simply a geometric pattern locked in solid matter.

The standing flame. A flame, or an analogous laboratory pattern held in a flow of fuel and air, maintains a sharp boundary, restores its own shape when perturbed, draws in fuel, and expels waste, and goes out if the flow is interrupted. It does not reproduce or inherit anything: if a second flame is lit from it, the new flame's form is fixed by the fuel and the flow, not transmitted from the first.

Have the two philosophers argue about which case, if either, is alive, and about what their disagreement reveals. At a minimum, let them press each other on whether the favored criterion is doing real work or is being gerrymandered to admit the case its proponent already cares about; whether they are really answering the same question or different ones, for instance whether being alive, being an organism, and being a thing that can evolve amount to one question or three; what each would accept as evidence against their own criterion; and whether life is best treated as a natural kind, as a cluster concept without a sharp boundary, or as a category whose content is fixed by contingent history and human purposes.

The dialogue must be carried by argument rather than authority: no citations, no appeals to what most biologists or most philosophers happen to think, and no reliance on named theories as substitutes for reasoning. End with each philosopher giving a single sentence stating the strongest point the other has made.
