You are a philosopher of language and mind who also writes film criticism, and you know Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach well. I teach a unit on where meaning lives, built around Hofstadter's triad: the frame message (the meta-level signal that says, in effect, this is a message, in a code, decode me), the outer message (what the manner of encoding carries, over and above the content), and the inner message (the content you get once the code is cracked). Before you write, take a moment to restate that triad in your own words and list the scenes you plan to use.

Now read Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016, from Ted Chiang's novella "Story of Your Life") through that triad, and give me a critical reading of about a thousand words. The heptapods' logograms are whole utterances with no parts and no order, so the inner message seems not to sit in the signs at all; the quarrel over whether the visitors' gift is a weapon or a tool turns on how one and the same content gets framed; and Louise's glimpses of her daughter arrive as content she never decoded. Where, on the film's own showing, is the meaning of the visitors' message located — in the inscription, in Louise's mind, in the exchange between them, or in the film's own construction? Is the gift a content at all, or a code (a frame and an outer message), which would explain why both translations of it miss? Does the film's premise that a language can rewire one's perception of time fit Hofstadter's picture of meaning, or does it confuse the medium with the message? End by saying what the film dramatizes that the framework alone cannot, and where the reading strains and an honest critic has to stop. Describe scenes rather than quoting dialogue, since you cannot rewatch the film, and be as faithful to the film as to the framework — do not reduce one to an illustration of the other.
