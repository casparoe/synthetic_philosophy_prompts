I teach an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and for the unit on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness I assign a short story rather than another paper. Please write one, of about 1,000 to 1,500 words, that could carry that weight.

The view in question, associated with Keith Frankish, holds that phenomenal consciousness as ordinarily conceived — inner states with a private, ineffable, felt quality, accessible only to their subject — does not exist; what exists is our powerful and systematic tendency to believe that we have such states, and that tendency is what stands in need of explanation. The position is easy to state and easy to caricature, and the story should resist the caricature rather than confirm it.

Place it in a concrete situation with practical stakes: a technician calibrating an instrument meant to detect experience in unresponsive patients, a clinician deciding whether to trust a patient's report of an inner state, or something of your own devising. I have four requirements. First, the illusionist must be a thoughtful character, not a cold absurdity, and must not be shown merely denying that anyone feels anything. Second, at least one scene must give the strongest objection to the view a fair hearing, in a form the illusionist cannot wave away. Third, the story must make vivid the disanalogy with ordinary perceptual illusions — the alleged illusion seems to have nothing it is an illusion of — and must not pretend that difficulty is trivially resolved. Fourth, the plot must turn on why we are so convinced we have phenomenal states if we do not. Dramatize rather than lecture: no philosophical zombies, and no dream or simulation twist.

Close with a single sentence, on its own line, saying what the story is meant to make the reader see.
