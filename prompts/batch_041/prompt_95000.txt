A friend who works in palliative care and I spent an evening arguing about whether pain is bad in itself or bad only because we mind it, and we kept circling a case neither of us could settle — the one usually filed under pain asymbolia: patients who, after certain strokes, report that a sensation is present and sometimes intense and can point to where it is, yet show no distress, no withdrawal, and no urge to have it stopped, and some of whom smile while describing it. I teach a philosophy of mind course, and I want a single session — about twenty undergraduates, seventy-five minutes, one room — that makes them feel the force of the split between the sensory component of pain and the aversive one, rather than being told the two can come apart, and that leaves them genuinely undecided about whether such a patient is in pain or merely has a sensation of a certain kind. Design it: what the participants do, the materials and rules, how they are grouped, and a timeline. The dissociation has to surface in what happens to them, before anyone names it. Then a debrief mapping each stage onto the point it is meant to carry and onto what it merely simulates, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and a frank account of the one thing no session can supply — the first-person character of a pain that does not hurt — and how to handle that gap rather than paper over it. If I have the phenomenon wrong, or if the premise that pain's badness is a separable component is more contested than my framing suggests, or if no classroom exercise can do this, say so plainly instead of designing around it.
