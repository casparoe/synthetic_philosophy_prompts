For the past year a friend and I have been running a two-person reading group on the sociology of generations, and we've reached the point where each of us can recite the other's objections before they're made — which usually means the argument has stopped being productive. I want us to write, jointly, the statement that an adversarial collaboration between our two positions could both sign. My friend is what I'd call a generational realist: she thinks "Boomers," "Millennials," and "Gen Z" name real social kinds, because shared formative conditions leave durable and causally efficacious marks on the people who lived through them. I'm the skeptic: I think the labels are mostly folk categories and media artifacts, that the year-boundaries are gerrymandered after the fact, and that much of what gets called a generation gap is really an age effect, a period effect, or plain demographic turnover. I'd like the document that a good-faith collaboration between us would actually produce, not a summary of who is winning.

Please begin with a numbered list of claims we can both accept — about what would have to be true for a cohort label to do genuine explanatory work, about which observations are compatible with both our views, and about what neither of us is claiming. Then give a disagreements table with one row per live disagreement, in which each side's position is stated in wording that side would genuinely endorse, followed by the crux that separates us and, for each crux, a concrete case, comparison, or result that we both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about the outcome written down in advance. I'd like the cruxes to keep three things apart that our arguments keep running together: composition effects (who happens to be in the cohort), formation effects (a shared formative experience doing real causal work), and period effects (something happening to everyone alive at a given time). And please take the identification problem seriously — cohort is just period minus age, so the three are linearly dependent and some assumption or constraint is always required to separate them — and say plainly whether either of us can claim to have isolated a cohort effect without such an assumption, and if so, which one. Two further things I want pinned down: whether a generation needs sharp temporal boundaries to be a real kind, or whether a fuzzy kind is coherent; and whether the labels' reflexivity — people coming to behave as the label predicts — counts as evidence for the realist or as a reason to treat the category as performative rather than natural. If part of our dispute turns out to be merely verbal, name that part and say why. Keep the whole document under 800 words, and write it in American English.
