My reading group has two members who can't be in the same room without re-fighting Goodman's grue puzzle -- the original trick being a predicate that's true of a thing if it's green up to some future date T and blue after it. One of them is a commited Bayesian about confirmation (priors, conditionalisation, the lot) and thinks the new riddle of induction is a pseudo-problem that evaporates once you stop confusing hypotheses with the sentences that express them. The other is an old-fashioned entrenchment man: he thinks we project green rather than grue because of the history of use of the two predicates, and that the Bayesian has simply smuggled the riddle into the prior and rebranded it as rationality. Three years of this and neither has moved an inch.

Write me the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two of them, which both could put their names to. Specifically: (1) a numbered list of claims they can both sign -- about what the evidence does and doesn't favour, about what any solution would have to deliver, about which escape routes are dead; (2) the disagreements that remain, each phrased so that the side it belongs to would accept the wording as its own, with no stacking of the deck; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them -- and for each crux the case, arguement or experiment they both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If part of the three-year fight turns out to have been verbal, name that part and say why.

Two constraints. First, they don't get to hide behind the literature, because I've been needling them with made-up variants and I want those handled head on. (i) Swap the time index for an unrelated future event: a sample is grellow if it's green and the next supernova visible from Earth occurs before 2100, and blue if it occurs after. (ii) Swap it for an index causally tied to the sample itself: a fruit is grue-ripe if it's green while untouched and blue once a hand has touched it -- like the way a cut apple browns. (iii) Same as (i), but with a switch on a genuinely undetermined future event, the next decay in a particular lump of uranium, so that nothing now settles whether the predicate applies. For each variant, say what each of their accounts predicts, and say whether (ii) breaks the claim I take both of them to accept -- that no formal or syntactic criterion can sort projectible from unprojectible predicates. It looks perfectly projectible to me and it's got exactly the shape of grue, so I want that pressure felt.

Second: no reading lists, no citations, and don't spend paragraphs telling me what Goodman said. I know what he said. Keep the whole thing under about 1,200 words.

Finish with a single last line of the form \answer{...} containing the one crux the whole dispute really hangs on, as a short phrase -- or \answer{none} if you've concluded it's all verbal.
