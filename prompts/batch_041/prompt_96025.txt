I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a chapter on the badness of pain, and for the empirical half I've been sitting in on a pain clinic. Its practice, like most trials and coverage decisions, is to measure pain with a 0–10 numerical rating of intensity, to treat the patient's report as authoritative, and to titrate treatment toward lowering that number; unpleasantness and interference with life are recorded occasionally, if at all, and rarely drive anything. The same literature that grounds the clinic contains the asymbolia cases, typically following lesions of the insula or of the cingulate pathways, in which patients report that a stimulus hurts, localize it, and give it a high intensity rating, yet do not withdraw, are not bothered by it, and describe themselves as indifferent to it.

What theory of the badness of pain does the clinic's practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can: that badness attaches to the sensory state itself, that it attaches to the affective reaction, that it consists in the state's motivational pull, or something else. Then say which rival accounts the practice quietly rules out and which it merely fits, and where it is underdetermined — which theories would license it equally well. Finally, tell me what would have to change concretely if we took the asymbolia cases at face value: what would the clinic have to measure, what would stop being a treatment target, and what would become of a patient who reports severe pain but no bother. Answer in at most 700 words.
