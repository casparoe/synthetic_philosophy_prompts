Analyze the International Astronomical Union's 2006 reclassification of Pluto as a candidate verbal dispute. I want a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions — the apparatus illuminating the episode and the episode testing the apparatus — rather than a survey of positions. Everything you need is stated below; do not cite literature, and do not supply a reading list.

The apparatus I want you to use:

(1) A three-stage diagnostic. First, identify the key term and the sentence in which the parties appear to disagree. Second, determine whether the parties attach the same sense to that term — distinguishing carefully between what actual usage shows and what a speaker would stipulate usage to be. Third, if the senses differ, ask whether the divergent readings nonetheless yield a single shared question that the parties answer differently. If there is such a common question, the dispute is not merely verbal, because there is one proposition under dispute; if there is not, the appearance of disagreement dissolves.

(2) Two complications to keep separate. A dispute can be verbal in a second-order way: the parties know they mean different things by the term and are arguing about which sense ought to govern it, which looks like a normative question about concepts rather than a factual one. Alternatively, a rival diagnosis holds that in such cases the parties are not describing the meaning of a term at all but using it to propose a meaning, so the exchange is substantive — it has a real subject matter, namely which conceptual scheme we should adopt — without being a disagreement about non-linguistic fact. Against both, a third position insists that the question which sense is correct has an answer independent of our decisions, and that settling it by stipulation or vote is a change of subject rather than a discovery.

The facts to work with:

In August 2006 the IAU General Assembly in Prague adopted a definition on which a body counts as a planet in the Solar System only if it orbits the Sun, is massive enough for its self-gravity to make it nearly round, and has swept the region around its orbit clear of comparable bodies. Pluto, which had been counted as a planet since Tombaugh found it in 1930, satisfies the first two criteria but not the third; so do Ceres and Eris, the latter discovered in 2005 beyond Neptune and initially thought to be at least as large as Pluto. All three were assigned to a new category of dwarf planets, and the Solar System was declared to have eight planets. The decision was contested. Some astronomers objected that the third criterion is arbitrary and corresponds to no natural division among bodies, that it is sensitive to a body's location in a way that makes it unfit as a general criterion, and that on a strict reading even the Earth has not cleared its orbit of every comparable body. There is a precedent: Ceres was announced in 1801 as the missing planet between Mars and Jupiter and was counted as a planet for about fifty years, until the discovery of many similar bodies in that region made astronomers catalogue the group separately as asteroids; and the term had already changed its extension earlier, when the Earth was added to the class of planets.

What I want from you:

(a) Run the three-stage diagnostic on the episode and give a verdict at each stage. Be explicit about which second-stage claims are empirical claims about how the term was in fact used and which are stipulations about how it ought to be used, and say what would count as evidence for each.

(b) Say where the diagnostic breaks down. In particular: does the dispute survive as a disagreement about which sense we ought to have, and what kind of question is that? Might the parties after all be disagreeing about facts — about the structure of the Solar System, about what classification is for, or about which groupings the science needs? And are the vote, the textbook and museum consequences, the public reaction, and the refusal of some astronomers to comply features the apparatus can represent, or merely noise to it?

(c) Reverse the direction. What does the episode show about the verbal/substantive distinction itself? Does it favour the negotiation diagnosis over the verbal-dispute diagnosis, or the reverse? Does it vindicate the third position or embarrass it? If the term is best understood as functional or administrative — its correct sense simply being the best policy for classifying a population of objects — does that make the dispute more or less like a verbal one?

(d) Say what the Ceres precedent and the earlier inclusion of the Earth do to the analysis. If the term's sense has always been in flux, is the idea of a single shared question the parties answer differently still available?

Close with a one-sentence summary of your verdict, then a final line of exactly the form \answer{LABEL}, where LABEL is one of: verbal-first-order, verbal-second-order, substantive, mixed.
