I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar and I am writing the final exam. I want the single long essay question to be on Hempel's raven paradox, but I have a specific worry about it: my students can all recite the standard line that the green apple or the white shoe does confirm the hypothesis, just by a negligible amount, because there are vastly more non-black things in the world than ravens — and then they stop, as if the case were closed. I have been arguing about this with a colleague who thinks that line lets everyone off too easily. His position is that the standard resolution only works if you picture an open-ended universe with an inexhaustible supply of non-black objects, and that in a small closed world — he likes to say, imagine an inventory of exactly three objects: a black raven, a white shoe, and a red herring — the inspection of the herring is a perfectly substantial piece of evidence, so the paradox is not defused by calling the confirmation tiny; something in the framework itself has to give. He also wants to bolt a fourth condition of adequacy onto Hempel's list, to the effect that a piece of evidence confirms a hypothesis only if that evidence would have been less likely had the hypothesis been false, and he insists that once you add this condition the paradox is either dissolved or relocated, depending on how you read it. I am not convinced he is right, and I would like the exam to make the students take a side on it.

Please write the question, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme. The question should force the candidates to do four things at once: reconstruct the chain of reasoning that generates the paradox out of the two conditions Hempel treats as platitudes — that instances of a generalization support it, and that logically equivalent hypotheses are supported by the same evidence; distinguish Hempel's own way of explaining away the air of paradox, namely that we are tacitly smuggling in background knowledge we already possess, from the Bayesian way of accepting the conclusion while shrinking the confirmation to a sliver; and then confront the point my colleague keeps pressing, that whether, and how much, a red herring confirms depends on how the observation was obtained and on what else is already known, so that the confirmatory weight is not a function of the evidence and the hypothesis alone. Finally, they should say which of the framework's commitments has to be surrendered — the intuition that non-ravens are irrelevant, the equivalence condition, or the idea that instances validate — and defend the choice rather than merely naming it. Please make the question hard to answer by summary: an answer that reproduces the line that the confirmation is real but negligible and then stops should not pass.

The marking scheme should be built around the specific moves a top answer must make, with the marks each is worth out of twenty, and it should separate an A from a B by naming exactly which moves the B answer will omit or garble — for instance, treating the equivalence condition as obviously false, or confusing the smallness of the confirmation with its irrelevance, or conceding the colleague's three-object case without noticing that its force depends on an assumed sampling procedure. For each of the four required tasks, add a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. I would also like the model answer to do something the textbooks tend not to: work through the three-object inventory my colleague describes and say what actually follows from it, rather than gesturing at the general principle.
