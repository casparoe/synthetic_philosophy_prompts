I chair the public health advisory board of a mid-sized county, and our board is required to issue a written recommendation within the month on a pilot that the county council will vote on next quarter. As drafted by the county attorney, the pilot would remove criminal penalties for possession of small quantities of controlled substances for personal use and route anyone found in possession to a three-member Substance Use Review Panel — a clinician, a public health officer, and a retired judge. The panel would be empowered to dismiss the matter with a warning, impose an income-scaled civil fine, or order up to ninety days of supervised outpatient treatment. Completing a treatment order would expunge the possession record; noncompliance would trigger the fine, and persistent noncompliance would convert the unpaid fine into a criminal contempt charge. Two features are contested. The treatment mandate would attach only on a third possession contact within twelve months, on the theory that the first two contacts should be pure health referrals. And a member of my board wants to reserve one panel seat for a person with lived experience of substance dependence, which the county attorney objects would put a panelist in the position of adjudicating a category she belongs to.

The board is split, and I want our opinion to take a side rather than split the difference. One faction holds that the pilot is prohibition in therapeutic clothing: that a mandate enforceable by contempt is coercion however it is labeled, and that moving people from a courtroom to a clinic changes the currency of state power without reducing the amount of it. The other faction holds the reverse: that without the treatment mandate the reform merely abandons people to their dependence, and that a purely voluntarist version would let the county look compassionate while doing nothing. One member has circulated a note arguing that the panel should be understood not as paternalism at all but as an exercise of the county's power to prevent harms to third parties — nuisance, emergency-room costs, disorder — and that this reframing settles what the panel may legitimately do. I find that move too quick, and I am suspicious of all three slogans.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete disposition — approve, reject, or approve subject to specific conditions — and defend it, with the argument turning on the underlying stakes rather than on program mechanics: what it is for a state to coerce someone in the name of her own health; whether a non-criminal sanction can be a genuine reduction in coercion or only a redescription of it; whether the harm-to-others rationale actually licenses the mandate or merely relocates the justification; and how the panel's design — the three-contact trigger, the contempt backstop, the lived-experience seat — should be altered, if at all, to make the answer come out right. Give the strongest version of the case against your recommendation rather than a strawman, and state plainly what would have to be true for you to change your mind.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
