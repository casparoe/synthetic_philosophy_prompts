Im a novelist, about 60k words into a book about a woman who gives away most of her income, is quietly going broke, and is lying to her family about it. The whole thing turns on one scene where I need the reader to feel the pull of the drowning-child thing without me just re-running the pond, because everyone's read that a hundred times and their eyes glaze over. So I've been drafting alternatives and I want you to tier them S down to D by how much moral pressure they'd actually put on a jadded reader, and say in a sentence why each lands where it does.

1. The toll: a kid is drowning, and the only way to get her out is to hand $200 on the spot to the guy who pushed her in.
2. The queue: a kid is drowning, and there are fifty people on the bank, every one of them able to jump in, all of them just standing there.
3. The ledger: you can save the kid in front of you, but only by pulling $200 out of a fund that, by the numbers, would certainly have saved 1.2 other kids.
4. The second pond: you save her, and on the walk home you pass another pond, and another, and it sinks in that you'll pass one every day for the rest of your life.
5. The receipt: you save her, she grows up never knowing, and you don't even get the good feeling, because you're not the kind of person who gets the good feeling.

For each one, also tell me which knob of the original it's really turning — how close the victim is, how certain the rescue is, whether you're the only one who could do it, how small the cost is, whether it ever stops — and then which of those knobs, when you crank it, actually deflates the argument instead of just making it sadder. Then tell me the single one you'd write, and if there's an obvious sixth I've missed, slot it in where it belongs.
