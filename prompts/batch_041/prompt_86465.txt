I write a blog for working programmers and I want to build a decision tree that I can draw as a flowchart for a post provisionally called Which constructivist are you? The readers can program and have heard of the Curry–Howard correspondence, but they have no logic or philosophy training, and neither do I, so the tree has to do its own explaining.

The entry point I want to use is an observation from my own work rather than a definition. When I write in continuation-passing style, or reach for call/cc or an exception handler, I can get a value of type A out of something of type (A -> Void) -> Void; double-negation elimination starts to look like an ordinary control operator. I have also been told that the translation doing this work is essentially the same one that converts a classical proof into an intuitionistic one. I cannot tell whether this should make me relaxed about classical reasoning, on the grounds that it is just control flow and my machine really does control flow, or suspicious of it, on the grounds that classical reasoning is then a technique for arranging computations rather than a description of how things are. I want the tree to force that fork and to land the reader on a definite position whichever way they go.

Build the tree so that every branch node is a question the reader can answer from the cases and examples you supply, phrased so that answering it does not require already knowing which position it leads to. Use no more than seven branch nodes and between eight and ten leaves. These distinctions must appear somewhere in the tree, though you should decide where and in what order: whether a proof that something exists has to hand you a witness you could actually run; whether a mathematical claim that a function exists means that some rule exists or that we could in principle program one; whether Markov's principle — for a decidable predicate on the natural numbers, that not-not-there-is-an-n implies there-is-an-n — is acceptable; whether the axiom of choice, which I gather drags excluded middle back in with it in some constructive settings, is a reason to give up choice or a reason to give up constructivism; whether the metatheory used to explain intuitionistic logic may itself be classical, or whether that is a way of explaining a logic by means that the logic rejects; whether objects like choice sequences, arbitrary sets of natural numbers, or types considered up to isomorphism are legitimate mathematical objects with identity conditions of their own; and whether a logic grounds mathematics or merely describes what mathematicians do.

These positions must appear as leaves, in whatever order the tree produces them: Brouwerian intuitionism, with choice sequences and the continuity of functions on them; Bishop-style constructivism, which wants none of that; the Russian school, with Markov's principle and the identification of constructive existence with computability; predicativism, which refuses quantification over totalities that are not yet built; a type-theoretic constructivism in which choice is a theorem rather than an axiom; and one leaf for the view that constructivity is a property of proofs rather than of logics. Each leaf should carry the name of the position and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, the thing a defender has to swallow. Where the tree leaves a combination of answers unoccupied, say so, and say briefly whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored; where two positions come out at the same leaf and you cannot separate them, flag that rather than inventing a distinction.

Please do not give me citations, reading lists, or attributions of doctrines to particular authors; I cannot check them and I do not want guesses dressed up as scholarship. Names of positions are fine, and a name may be attached to a position only where the attachment is uncontroversial.

Write the answer in British English. At the end, say how confident you are in the tree as a whole and in the individual claims you make while building it, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
