Last night I spent four hours arguing with a friend about Tolstoy's account of art — the idea that a work of art is one person's feeling transmitted to other people by means of an external sign, that what marks the thing off is its infectiousness, and that the honesty and clarity of the feeling matter more than any amount of craft — and I have come away with my own intuitions in a mess I cannot clean up by myself. His position is that the whole view is a moralist's revenge on artists he couldn't stomach: that it turns criticism into counting who cried, that it licenses dismissing anything difficult or unfamiliar as counterfeit, and that it prefers a sincere failure to a brilliant lie. I found myself defending something uncomfortably close to Tolstoy and then discovering that I could not say exactly what I was defending. Please take the role of a philosopher of art who knows the transmission theories of expression and their critics inside out. Begin by brainstorming briefly about what might unite the cases below, think carefully before committing to anything, and resist the temptation to declare the pattern simple.

Here are my verdicts, one by one. My grandmother's lullaby, sung off key in a language I don't speak, to a baby who was already asleep: art, and not minor art. A flawless studio recording of that same lullaby by a trained singer who plainly means every note, which leaves me completely cold: a fine performance, but I don't think it's art at all. A television commercial that reliably makes me cry: not art, though it does to me precisely what art is supposed to do. A film that is obviously sincere and utterly infectious and whose feeling is a cruel one — a revenge fantasy that has a whole audience cheering by the end: art, plainly, but bad art. A difficult poem that meant nothing to me until I had read it twenty times with a commentary open beside me, and that I now care about more than almost anything else I own: art. A folk song nobody in particular wrote, which sixty thousand strangers can bellow together at a stadium without a single one of them singing it well: art in the fullest sense, more clearly art than anything hanging in a museum. A stain on a wall that happens to look like a face and moves me every time I pass it: not art. My own best attempt at a principle is this — art is communication whose point is the feeling itself rather than anything the feeling is a means to — but that seems to get the commercial and the cruel film right while saying nothing about why the note-perfect recording isn't art, or why the anonymous folk song is, so I suspect it is either too clever or too thin.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough that I can tell where they diverge; for each clause, the case that forces it; what it predicts about cases I haven't mentioned, in particular a song that moves me only because it was playing in the car the last time I saw my father alive, and a poem I know only in translation, where what reaches me is the translator's feeling rather than the poet's; and an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would have me abandon. If what survives is Tolstoy's view with repairs, say which repairs. If no single principle gets more than most of my cases, tell me which verdict is doing the damage.
