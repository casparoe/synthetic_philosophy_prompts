I'm giving the commencement address at my university's aerospace engineering graduation next month — 400 or so engineers and their families, plus a few of my colleagues from the philosophy department sitting in the back. I want to talk about space colonization, but not the usual destiny-of-humanity stuff, and not the "it's just colonialism in a spacesuit" line either, which is cheap and would also be a lousy thing to say to people who've just spent four years learning orbital mechanics. Here's what I actually want to say, and I need help saying it well. Every argument I hear for settling Mars is a pile of facts with a value hidden inside it, and the hiding place is almost never a premise — it's the verb. We say we'll settle it, open it, give humanity a backup, become a two-planet species, and the evaluation is already smuggled in before any inference even gets going. I know this is the is-ought gap, but I dont want the speech to turn into a lecture about it — I want the audience to feel the gap open up under a sentence they were nodding along to a second earlier, and then still have something to walk out with on their graduation day. It has to land for the engineers (no jargon, no telling them their life's work is suspect) and for the philosophers (who will notice immediately if I blur the fact/value line just to make it land). Write it, six minutes or so spoken. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, just continuous prose I can read off the page.
