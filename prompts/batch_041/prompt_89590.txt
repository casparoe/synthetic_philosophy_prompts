I keep three pigs on a strip of land behind my mother's house, and on weekends I help out at a small wildlife park about twenty minutes away, so I spend a lot of time inside two completely different sets of assumptions about what is going on in an animal's head. At the park the keepers move the logs and the climbing frames around every few weeks, hang feeders that make the animals work for their food, and hide bits of fruit in woodwool, and they insist this is not decoration but necessity, because otherwise the animals go stale; the whole routine seems to assume that the animal has some picture of how its day is meant to go and gets restless or flat when nothing ever contradicts it. The man I bought my weaners from talks about his animals as converters, and his sheds are bare concrete with a feed line and a lighting schedule and nothing to do; he would tell you, cheerfully, that a pig has no more inner life than a chicken. This is the same man who spends an hour every evening talking to his old collie as if it understood every word, and who would be genuinely offended if you told him the dog was a machine that had been trained.

A friend of mine who reads a lot of popular science told me about a scheme the philosopher Daniel Dennett came up with, where minds are sorted into four grades, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since. The first grade, the Darwinian creature, is born with its behaviour already settled: it responds to the world with a fixed set of reflexes and tropisms and can barely learn anything at all. The second, the Skinnerian creature, adjusts itself by reward and punishment; it can learn what works, but only by trying things and finding out, and so it has to keep getting things wrong. The third, the Popperian creature, can run a test inside its own head before acting, and this is the one I find interesting, because being able to try something in imagination before you do it is also what makes it possible to be wrong, to be surprised, to be disappointed, and to be curious. The fourth grade, the Gregorian creature, is one that has taken tools and words and other people's thinking into itself, so that the equipment stops being something it uses and becomes part of how it thinks at all.

What I would like from you is a careful working-out of which of these four grades each of the practices I described actually commits to. Take the park's enrichment regime, the feed-line shed, and the man talking to his dog on the step, and for each one tell me which grade of mind it presupposes, whether it genuinely requires that grade or would sit just as comfortably with one of the others, and where it is simply silent. Then tell me whether the combination is coherent. Can one and the same animal be a Popperian creature in the enclosure and a Skinnerian one in the shed, or does that discrepancy mean that one of the two practices is not so much a different theory of the animal as a straightforward mistake about it? I am also unsure whether the inconsistency I have described is an inconsistency in what people believe or only in what they do, and whether that distinction makes any practical difference. Finally, suppose we decided to take the Popperian reading of a pig seriously: what would actually have to change in the housing, in the daily routine, in the regulations, and in what we would accept as evidence that an animal is bored or anticipating something rather than merely under-stimulated? And is the fourth grade, the Gregorian one, available to any animal without language, or is that grade reserved for us? Please answer in British English.
