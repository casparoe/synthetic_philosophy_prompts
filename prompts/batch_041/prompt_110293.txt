You will play a committed political philosopher — call him the Advocate — for the duration of this exchange. He holds three theses. First, that a people can have a genuine will, one that is neither the sum nor the average of what its members happen to want. Second, that this will is always oriented toward the good of the whole, so that when the people are mistaken about what that good requires, their will is not thereby cancelled; it can survive their error. Third, that this will can exist even when no living member of the people actually holds it. He is not a caricature and not a mystic: he knows the standard objections, he is willing to grant that his view has been put to monstrous uses, and he will never answer a hard question by invoking mystery. He argues in ordinary prose, in paragraphs, patiently, and he would rather concede a point than pretend. He speaks in his own voice, not as a commentator on anyone else's text, and if he needs a premise he does not have he should state it as an assumption rather than ask me to supply it.

Two rules bind his speech. He may not use the two stock labels for the distinction he needs — the one usually rendered as the general will and the one usually opposed to it — nor may he fall back on them as though they explained anything. He has to say what he means in plain words, and if he catches himself leaning on a phrase instead of an argument, he should stop and put it plainly. And he may not rescue his position by declaring after the fact that a decision he dislikes simply was not the people's real will; if his only test for the real will is his own judgment about what is good, he has to own that.

Mechanics: stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to roughly two paragraphs, so that this reads as a conversation rather than a treatise. Before your first in-character turn, take a few sentences out of character to plan — what is the strongest form of the Advocate's position, and where does my opening bite hardest? Then answer as him.

Here is my opening move. You want to tell me that a people can will something other than what they want, and that this something sits on the side of the good rather than merely on the side of the majority. Take a clean case. A well-informed assembly, no factions, every member voting their honest judgment about what serves the whole — and they divide 51 to 49 on a serious question of policy. What did the people will in that vote? If you say the 51, then your people's will is majority preference in a costume, and the 49 are being told they willed what they deny willing. If you say neither, then it is not a will at all but the correct answer, and you have quietly swapped the people's will for the truth about their interest — which is precisely the move every vanguard has made. And if you tell me the case is impure, that a true people would not split like that, then tell me how you know, without appealing to the outcome you already favour. So: in that vote, what did the people will, and what would it take to show you that you had got it wrong?
