I'm in a philosophy of science seminar this term and I want to write my final paper on Hempel's raven paradox, but using a case from my lab rotation instead of the usual white shoes. The lab does human genetics on a rare variant — about 1 in 300 people in our cohort carries it — and so far every carrier we've found is affected, while we've also genotyped a few thousand people who turned out not to carry it, and most of them are unaffected. My PI keeps saying those clean non-carriers tell us nothing, and we've been argueing about it. I said well, by Hempel they do confirm the claim that all carriers are affected, because that's the same claim as all unaffected people are non-carriers, so every unaffected non-carrier is a white shoe. She wasn't impressed.

What I need for the paper is the most important implications of the paradox for what those controls actually buy us, and for how we should pick the next batch to sequence. Does it matter that our controls were recruited through the clinic rather than sampled randomly? Does the 1-in-300 base rate change how much each clean case is worth? And is there a real difference between the universal claim (all carriers are affected) and the causal claim we actually care about (the variant causes the disease) — because if what we're after is causal, maybe the controls are irrelevant for reasons that have nothing to do with white shoes. More generally: is this thing supposed to change what working scientists do, or is it a puzzle that only bites people who think confirming a generalization is just a matter of collecting instances?
