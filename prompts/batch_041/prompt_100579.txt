Q4. A sculptor takes a single lump of clay and moulds it into a statue, exhibited as Golem. Years later the statue is knocked over and crushed flat. No clay is lost; the lump is intact, just shapeless. The sculptor then re-moulds the same clay into a shape exactly like Golem's and puts it back on the plinth. Two philosophers comment.

A: "The crushing destroyed the statue. The lump was there before and is there after; the statue was not. What the sculptor has made now is a new statue."

B: "There is only one thing here, the lump. 'Statue' and 'lump' are two ways of describing it. Since that one thing never went away, the thing on the plinth is Golem, not a replacement."

(a) Name the position each is defending, and say what each is committed to about how many objects are present while the statue exists and about whether the thing on the plinth is Golem. (b) What does their disagreement ultimately turn on?

Student's answer:

"A is defending the constitution view and B the contingent identity view.

On A's view, while the statue exists there are two things in the same place: the lump and the statue. They are not identical, because the statue is essentially statue-shaped and the lump is not — it could have stayed a shapeless lump — and things that differ in their properties are not the same thing. So when the statue is crushed it stops existing and the lump goes on. On A's view the statue is the clay plus the shape it has been given, and that shape is a part of the statue which the lump does not have. Whether the new thing is Golem then depends on whether the constitution relation was interrupted, and since it was, A should say a new statue has been made.

On B's view there is only ever one object, so nothing was destroyed by the crushing, and the thing on the plinth is Golem. B can say this because the clay is the same clay and the shape is the same shape: the new thing is qualitatively identical with Golem, and that is what B means by its being the same statue. B avoids trouble with Leibniz's Law by denying that modal predicates like 'could have survived being flattened' are properties at all — they hold of the object only relative to a way of describing it.

So the disagreement turns on whether Leibniz's Law reaches modal predicates, and on how many objects can be in one place at once."
