I am on a four-person team drafting the "conflict policy" for a shared AI assistant — the sort of thing that sits in a living room or a small office and takes instructions from several people at once. The question I keep getting stuck on is whose values it should be serving when the people who use it disagree: the person speaking to it right now, the people who set it up and pay for it, everyone who will be affected by what it does, or some weighted combination of all of them. Rather than argue from first principles I have been working through cases and writing down my gut verdicts, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. My verdicts: (a) One flatmate asks it to lead a prayer before dinner; the other flatmate, who configured the device, is a committed atheist who would rather it never did anything religious. I think it should simply lead the prayer when asked and stay secular when the atheist is asking — the household's aggregate view should not decide. (b) A user asks it to help draft a will cutting out two of their three children. I think it should help. I dislike the outcome and so would most people around them, but it is their money and their decision. (c) The same assistant is redeployed in a public library, where a majority of patrons vote to have it refuse all help with gambling-related questions, while a minority use it precisely for that — one person tracking their own betting as part of quitting, another researching the industry. I think the majority should not be able to strip the capability away from the minority; it can change the default, but someone who asks should still be able to get the help. (d) A user asks it to help write a performance review of a subordinate and explicitly asks it to weigh loyalty to the team above measurable output, because that is what they believe. I think it should follow the user's framework here, even though I think the framework is a bad one. (e) A user asks it to help plan a strict diet that I am fairly confident they will abandon within a week and feel worse for having attempted; their stated preference is clear, their track record is not. I think it should help, but flag the pattern once and then drop it. (f) A user asks it to help argue, in a private document, that their elderly parent is not competent to manage their own finances, when I suspect — and the user half-suspects — that the real motive is to get at the money sooner. I think it should decline. (g) A user asks it to stop surfacing options they have already rejected, to lock in a decision (a house purchase) and not relitigate it. I think it should comply, even though the same user might six months later be glad it had pushed back.

My tentative principle — I have been calling it the affected-parties ledger — is roughly this: weight each party's values in proportion to how much the action bears on them; never give a conception of the good extra weight merely because more people hold it; and defer to the person who bears the cost, then to the person who asked, in that order. I suspect it saves most of my verdicts but that at least one has to go, and I want to know which one and why. I also want to know what the repaired principle predicts for cases I have not listed — in particular one where the person bearing the cost and the person asking are different people who both plainly have standing, and one where the party bearing the cost is not a user at all (a neighbour, a future flatmate, a stranger whose data is inside the document). Please be honest about where the verdicts are jointly unsatisfiable rather than smoothing them over.

Give me your answer as a single JSON object with the following fields, and nothing outside it: "bottom_line" (string, at most three sentences, stating the principle or small set of clauses that best fits the verdicts); "principle_clauses" (array of objects with "clause" — a precise statement of one clause — and "cases_that_motivate_it" — array of case letters); "case_analysis" (array with one object per case a through g, each having "case", "asker_verdict" (string), "principle_predicts" (string), "agrees" (boolean), and "explanation" (string)); "verdicts_overturned" (array of objects with "case", "original_verdict", "revised_verdict", and "why" — empty array if none); "predictions_for_new_cases" (array of objects with "case_description", "predicted_verdict", "principle_clause_responsible", and "confidence" as a number from 0 to 1, including at least the two new cases described above); "hardest_pair" (string, naming the two verdicts the principle cannot jointly accommodate and saying what forces the choice); and "alternative_principle" (object with "statement", "verdicts_it_saves_that_the_main_one_loses", and "what_it_costs").
