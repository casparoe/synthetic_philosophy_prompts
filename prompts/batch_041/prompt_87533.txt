I'm putting together a reading group on the problem that our actions' consequences ramify forever and we can't know them, the thing people call cluelessness, and before we start arguing about fixes I want the spec for what a fix would even have to be. So write me the desiderata any adequate theory of how to act under this kind of cluelessness has to meet.

Here's my rough list, keep or cut them as you think right. It has to give verdicts for ordinary decisions (give this money or not, take the job, vote, have a kid) without pretending we can estimate probabilities we can't. It has to distinguish the case where I genuinely can't know from the case where I'm just ignorant of facts that are knowable in principle, since those look like different problems. It has to say something about actions whose expected value looks undefined rather than merely unknown, where the good and bad branches go on forever and the sums don't settle down. It has to dodge the two flips into uselessness, where either the theory licenses anything at all because nothing can be justified, or it keeps demanding we maximise something we can't see. And it shouldn't make an act's rightness turn on facts nobody at the time could have got at, or we end up telling people they were wrong for reasons they couldn't have had.

For each requirement give me the case that motivates it, say which requirements are non-negociable and which are just the taste of one camp, and tell me whether any pair of them can't be had together. That last bit is what I care about most. Then say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the popular moves (ignore the far future, just do the obvious thing, treat it all as a coin flip, and so on).

Be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it, don't sell me one camp's line as if everyone agrees. British English please.
