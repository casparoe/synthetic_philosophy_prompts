I'm running a four-week discussion group for adults who aren't philosophers -- smart and well-read, but they've never touched philosophy of memory. The topic is the supposed duty to remember, and whether forgetting can ever be the right thing, even a duty in its own right. I've been chewing on this since reading some Margalit and Rieff and I want to teach it as one continuous arc, not a survey.

Plan the explanation for me rather than giving me the explanation. What has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them? My hunch is they need to stop treating "memory" as a single thing first, so help me figure out what the pieces actually are and what depends on what. Then: the one case to build the whole arc around, and what that case has to have -- it needs to make both the duty to remember and the case for forgetting feel live, so that neither side comes off as obviously correct. Then the misconseptions I should expect. My guess is they'll collapse forgetting into lying about what happened, and they'll assume that remembering is automatically good for the people doing the remembering. Tell me exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. Finally, one question I can put to them at the end that would show whether they've actually got the tension, rather than just nodding along that remembering is nice.

Two things I want to inject because I think they're the interesting part and not the stuff people have already heard a hundred times. First: a duty to remember is always owed to someone in particular -- the dead, or a group, or a future generation -- and the duty looks different depending on who's carrying it, so the "we owe it to them" move needs to be pulled apart rather than left as a slogan. Second: forgetting can be a kind of mercy, which means remembering and forgiving aren't the same thing and can even pull against each other. Work those in as load-bearing, don't tack them on at the end.
