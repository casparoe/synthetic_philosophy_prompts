The question of whether generations are real kinds has stalled in my social-ontology reading group, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The two camps are by now entirely familiar to each other. One holds that labels like Boomer, Millennial, and Gen Z name genuine social formations — cohorts shaped by a shared formative period, in something like the tradition running from Mannheim — and that dismissing them as marketing fiction mistakes a real social kind for a poor measure of it. The other holds that the boundaries are arbitrary conventions and that whatever differences we observe between named cohorts are better explained as age effects or period effects that would attach to anyone alive at the relevant time. Each side grants the other's empirical observations and still does not move. My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but two: an ontological or semantic one about what "real" is being asked to do, and a hidden empirical one about whether cohort effects can be identified at all, given that age, period, and cohort are linearly entangled and cannot be separated from a single snapshot. Diagnose the dispute — verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, or an empirical question in disguise — and say what would count as progress. Answer in one paragraph of no more than 250 words.
