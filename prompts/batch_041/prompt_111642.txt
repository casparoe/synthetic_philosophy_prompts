I am a novelist at work on a book about a behavioral ecologist who becomes obsessed with the question of whether human beings are just digger wasps with longer loops. By sphexishness I mean the pattern in which a wasp runs a fixed routine—digging a burrow, paralyzing prey, dragging it to the entrance, going inside to inspect, and then, if the prey is moved a few inches while she is inside, dragging it back to the entrance and inspecting again rather than adjusting—so that the behavior looks like a loop that cannot be exited from the inside. I want a decision tree that sorts the philosophical positions on whether human flexibility is a different kind of thing from this, or only more of the same, and what that implies for free will.

Please construct the tree as a classification instrument, not a literature review. Each branch node should be a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should be a named position (or 'unnamed' if nobody defends it) with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space first—the questions and the combinations of answers they allow—and only then locate the actual positions in that space. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I cannot use them, and I would rather have the logical space than a list of names.

The distinctions I care about are these: whether rigidity is a fact about the behavior or about the grain of description we bring to it; whether flexibility is a matter of degree or of kind; whether the agent can represent the rule it follows as a rule; whether the loop is invisible to the agent or visible but irresistible; whether the agent can revise the rule in light of a reason it could avow; whether the relevant interruption is external or internal, that is, self-monitoring; whether flexibility is online, within the act, or offline, between trials; whether the flexibility is stochastic variation or sensitivity to reasons; whether the agent's history and etiology matter; and whether determinism is relevant at all. The cases that must appear are the wasp and the moved cricket; a person with a checking compulsion; a bureaucrat following a rule after it has defeated its purpose; a chess player abandoning a prepared line; a novelist rewriting the same sentence for the tenth time; and someone who acts against her strongest desire for a reason she can avow.

I need the tree to end in at least these leaves: hard determinism; libertarian free will; reasons-responsive compatibilism; hierarchical or self-governance compatibilism; illusionism about free will; a deflationary view on which sphexishness is an observer-relative verdict rather than a natural kind; and a view I will call continuum naturalism, on which human flexibility is only sphexishness with more steps. If the tree can separate further positions—enactivist, existentialist, or eliminativist—include them, but do not pad. Aim for eight to ten leaves if the logical space supports them.

Please flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate, and note any combinations of answers that are unoccupied. For each unoccupied combination, say whether you think nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where you have to make a contested reading of a position to fit it onto the tree, say so in a short note.

Finally, I would rather you push back than comply politely. If the request rests on a confusion—if sphexishness is not one phenomenon but several, or if a decision tree cannot separate these positions because the real disagreements are about the descriptions of cases rather than the cases themselves—say so plainly, explain why, and tell me what a better instrument would look like. If you must choose between a tidy tree and an honest one, choose the honest one. Be explicit about which branches you regard as substantive, which are stipulative, and where you are least confident.
