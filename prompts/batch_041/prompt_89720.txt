My mate dropped this on me at the pub and now I can't sleep. He reckons he can prove supertasks are impossible — you know, doing infinitely many actions in a finite stretch of time — and the scary bit is his proof seems to kill off way more than just supertasks.

Setup: a lamp starts switched off. At 1/2^n hours before noon it gets flicked, for n = 1, 2, 3, ... : switched on at half an hour to noon, off at quarter of, on at 7.5 minutes to, and so on forever, infinitely many flicks, all of them done by noon. The question is whether it's on or off at noon. His argument:

(1) At noon the lamp is either on or off; a lamp has no third state.
(2) It started off, so for it to be on at noon it must have been switched on at some point before noon, and for it to be off it must have been switched off.
(3) A lamp doesn't flip itself, and the only things happening here are the switches. The state a physical thing is in at a time is fixed by what happened to it before that time, so whatever state the lamp is in at noon is the state the last switch left it in.
(4) But there is no last switch — every switch has another one after it, closer to noon.
(5) So at noon it can be neither on nor off, which contradicts (1).
(6) Therefore the whole setup is incoherent, and supertasks are impossible.

Here's why I can't just swallow it. It proves far too much: by the same moves you'd show that Zeno's runner never arrives, and more generally that any system undergoing infinitely many changes piling up toward a time can't have a state at that time at all, which seems to rule out heaps of perfectly ordinary continuous processes. And he pushes it further — he says a machine could use the same trick to run a program for infinitely many steps and then just read off whether it ever halted, which would decide the halting problem. That can't be right; undecidability isn't going to fall to a lamp.

I'd rather keep (1) — I really do think a lamp is either on or off — and I'm definately not giving up on the halting problem being undecidable. So which step do I throw out? Tell me why that step looked fine in the first place, and what it costs me to reject it: does the whole idea of a definite state at a limit have to go, or just his causal premise, and does whatever fix I adopt generalize to the halting-problem version, or does that argument survive on its own? And if it turns out the thing is actually valid and I have to drop a premise I like, say so and price it for me — seperate out what's the real flaw from what's just counterintuitive.
