I'm a high school student writing a paper for my philosophy club, and I've gotten stuck on a question about how hospitals handle pain.

Here is the practice I want to understand. In hospitals, pain is usually measured by asking the patient to put it on a scale from 0 to 10; that number goes in the chart, gets rechecked after medication, and drives the decision to give more, less, or stronger drugs. At the same time, everyone insists that pain is subjective and that the patient's own report is the authority — a nurse who doubts a patient's score is doing something wrong. In patients who cannot report, such as infants or people with advanced dementia, staff infer pain from behavior instead: grimacing, guarding, restlessness. And in chronic pain clinics the outcome that increasingly gets tracked is not the number at all but function — whether the person can sleep, walk, work, leave the house.

What got me thinking about this is a condition called pain asymbolia. After damage to a part of the brain called the insula, patients report that they feel pain — often they can still say where it is and how intense it is — but it does not bother them at all. They have the sensation without the suffering. Something similar can be produced by cutting certain brain connections, and a milder version is familiar from high doses of morphine. This looks like evidence that pain has two separable parts: a sensory part (where, how strong, what kind) and an affective part (how bad it is, how much it matters). Normally the two travel together, and it is the second one we actually care about.

My question is what our practice presupposes about why pain is bad. When the chart records 8 out of 10 and the team titrates drugs to bring the number down, are they presupposing that how bad a pain is is a function of how intense the sensation is — that the unpleasantness simply rides along with the intensity? If so, asymbolia looks like a counterexample: a high-intensity pain that is not bad at all, which would mean the practice needs patching, either with separate scales for intensity and unpleasantness or with a different theory of why pain matters, one on which the bad thing is the suffering rather than the sensation. But maybe the practice does not really presuppose that. Maybe intensity is just a practical proxy, or the practice is compatible with several theories of pain's badness at once — that it is bad because of the sensation, because of the suffering, because of what it prevents you from doing, or because of the warning it carries — and the scoring ritual merely fits some of these better than others.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and be explicit about the difference between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely happens to fit with. Tell me where it is underdetermined, so that several views would license the same behavior, and where two parts of the practice pull against each other — the 0-to-10 number treated as the target in acute care versus function treated as the target in chronic care, or self-report treated as authoritative while behavior is used as a substitute. Then say what would actually have to change — in measurement, in how drugs are titrated, in what counts as success — if we replaced the presupposed view with one on which the suffering rather than the sensation is the thing that matters.

Finally, please tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or piece of evidence would push you toward a different reading of the practice, and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong. Keep the whole answer to at most 800 words.
