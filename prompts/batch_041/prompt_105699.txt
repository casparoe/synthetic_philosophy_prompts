I've spent the past few months designing the wayfinding signage for a large hospital campus — the kind of system in which a green running figure over a doorway means the exit, a red circle with a bar through it means don't do whatever is pictured inside it, a triangle means warning, and a stylized bed with an arrow means the wards lie that way. Working on it, I've been struck by how much of the system is genuinely depictive and how much is not. The running figure really does look like a person running, and a reader who had never seen the sign before could probably get somewhere with it. But the bar, the arrow, the triangle-versus-circle distinction, and the whole color code carry no pictorial information whatever; they are conventions that have to be learned. And some of the signs say things that are not in the picture at all. A prohibition or a requirement is a norm, and no amount of looking at a picture of a cigarette will tell you that smoking is forbidden there, rather than being advertised, or merely depicted. The system is thus a hybrid of pictures, learned symbols, and rules — and the practice treats all of it as one thing: a sign that says something, which a reader can get right or wrong. I notice the same oddity on the production side: our style guide has to spell out in words what each sign means before any of us can draw it, and the standards committee argues about the wording, not the drawing.

This is why I've been reading about Otto Neurath, who developed a system of pictorial statistics in Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s — a forerunner of the modern infographic — and who hoped to build an international picture language that could carry social and scientific facts across language barriers, anchored in things publicly observable and checkable rather than in abstractions. He appears to have thought his pictures made claims, that is, said things capable of being correct or incorrect; yet he was careful enough to call the result not a language but a "language-like technique." What I want to know is what my own signage practice is committing itself to.

So please reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning that this practice presupposes. Does treating a pictogram as something that can be right or wrong require pictures to have something like syntax and a subject-predicate — a picture being, in effect, a sentence — or can a weaker, non-propositional account, on which a sign merely cues a response in a suitably trained viewer, explain everything the practice actually does? Does the reliance on arbitrary conventions and on deontic content (prohibition, requirement) undercut the Neurathian ambition to ground the signs in observable physical things, or is it compatible with a thinner version of that ambition? Which rival views of pictorial meaning does the practice quietly rule out — that pictures are irredeemably ambiguous and only context fixes what they say, that a pictogram system is just compressed natural language, or that depiction is a matter of resemblance rather than of convention? Tell me which features the practice strictly requires and which it merely happens to fit, and where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license the very same set of signs. Then say what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by its rival: would the iconic figures have to go, would the conventions become the whole story, and would anything be lost? I would rather have a precise, opinionated answer than a survey; close by naming the step in your reasoning you are least confident about and saying what evidence or argument would move you to the opposite verdict. Please keep the entire answer under 800 words.
