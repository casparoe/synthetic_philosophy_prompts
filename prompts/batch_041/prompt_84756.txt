I'm on the ethics and safety board of a mid-sized health insurer and Ive got to write the decision memo for Tuesday's meeting, and Im stuck on the framing, so I want you to write it. Here's the case. The product team wants to roll out an "adherence" program for people on chronic meds. Patients opt in and then get messages generated from their own data. Two features are causing trouble. First, loss-framing: "you've built up 40 good days, don't lose it now." Second, and this is the one: for patients who've uploaded voice memos, the app can synthesize a message in the patient's own voice, as if from their future self — "take the pill tonight, I want to be at my daughter's wedding." The team says it's opt-in, it's true, and it works: adherence jumps about a third.

Our draft policy defines manipulative influence as influence that "works by making the person's choice not really their own." Half the board thinks that line is useless because it just restates the problem. One member wants to approve everything on the grounds that it serves the patients' own long-term ends, which is what medicine is for anyway. Another wants to ban the voice feature outright as creepy and undignified, no matter how well it works.

I don't want to just split the difference. My own half-formed thought is that the draft line is bad because it asks about the outcome (was the choice really theirs?) when we should be asking about the transaction — something like: an influence is fine if the person could learn exactly why and how it was produced and still be glad they acted on it, and manipulative if learning that would undercut the very reason they acted. But I can already see two problems with that and I want you to push on them. First, lots of legitimate stuff might fail it — a good teacher, a friend's pep talk, my own alarm clock. Second, it's all about whether the person can reflect and endorse, but what if the deeper worry is that someone now has a lever over your choices that you never handed over and that you have no way to contest, whether or not it ever overrides your reflection? And a third thing someone on the board will definitely say: the real issue isn't autonomy at all, it's that the message implies things that are false or misleading ("I" am your future self; this is what you want), so it's really a deception problem wearing an autonomy costume.

So: write the decision memo. Commit to a criterion, say why it beats the alternatives — including the two I floated and the deception one — then apply it feature by feature, give me a concrete recommendation with any conditions and a timeline the team could actually work to, and be honest about the strongest thing someone on the losing side could say. No citations needed, I'll add those.
