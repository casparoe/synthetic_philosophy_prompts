I have been reading around the idea of program equilibrium and have reached the point where I want the criteria for judging such a theory settled before I look at any further constructions. The setting, as I understand it: fix a base game, then let each player submit a program instead of a strategy; the programs are run with the opponent's program, and if necessary their own source, as input, each returns an action on its owner's behalf, and a program equilibrium is simply a Nash equilibrium of this larger program game. The illustration everyone gives for the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is a program that cooperates if and only if the opponent's submitted program is textually identical to itself and defects otherwise; since the only way to elicit cooperation from that program is to be that program, deviating buys at best the mutual-defection payoff, so mutual cooperation is an equilibrium. This has been criticized as fragile — one stray whitespace character and both sides defect — and the alternatives I have seen try to replace the syntactic test with a proof-theoretic one, cooperating when the program can establish that the opponent's program cooperates. What unsettles me is that, on the folk-theorem-style characterization I have read, the payoffs achievable in program equilibrium are exactly the feasible and individually rational ones, which suggests a concept permissive enough to license almost any outcome and therefore to predict almost none. I would like to know what a good version of this theory is even supposed to be doing.

So write me the specification: the desiderata that any adequate theory of equilibrium among code-reading programs would have to satisfy, each stated as a requirement and each accompanied by the case, puzzle, or consideration that motivates it. I am especially unsure how to weigh the following, and want your verdict on each. That equilibria exist at all, and over what space of programs — arbitrary programs, at the risk that non-halting and the halting problem enter the definition itself, or some restricted class, at the cost of artificiality. That the solution concept be computable, or at least that agents could in principle reason their way to it. That the theory get the Prisoner's Dilemma right without over-generating, which means it must also deliver the correct null result in games where mutual inspection ought to buy nothing, such as a dominant-strategy game, so that it does not simply inflate what cooperation is available. That it give a definite verdict on self-referential and diagonal programs — a program that defects exactly when its opponent cooperates has no consistent outcome against a copy of itself, and I want to know whether that is a defect of the program, of the semantics of running programs on one another, or of the equilibrium concept. That it be robust to perturbation, since the striking result above collapses if the opponent's program differs by a single character, is only partially observable, or is known only up to semantic rather than textual equivalence. That it be invariant under the choice of programming language, or else say exactly how the achievable payoffs depend on that choice. And that the theory be explicit about what it is a theory of — a prediction about what happens when agents write code against one another, a normative claim about what rational agents would precommit to, or a formalization of superrationality — since that answer determines whether it should coincide with Nash equilibrium when inspection is uninformative.

Give me the list as a specification rather than a survey: each requirement, the consideration behind it, which requirements you take to be constitutive of the concept and which are optional ambitions, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. The part I most want is whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect that unrestricted existence, robustness to perturbation, and the ability to sustain non-Nash outcomes pull against one another, and if so I want to know which one a defender should surrender. You may score the known constructions briefly against the list if that makes the tension concrete, but do not bother with a reading list; I want the criteria and the trade-offs, not references.
