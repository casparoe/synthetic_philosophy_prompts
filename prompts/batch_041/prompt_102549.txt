I have been puzzling over a pattern in how central banks and comparable forecasting institutions communicate uncertainty, and I would like you to reconstruct the philosophy of probability it presupposes. The practice has two faces. On the first, an institution publishes a fan chart: a probability distribution over future inflation or output, drawn as shaded bands it explicitly labels with numerical coverage — thirty per cent, sixty, ninety — so that uncertainty is presented as a fully quantified, additive probability measure over a well-defined outcome space. On the second face, the same institution, often in the same document, runs scenario or stress-test exercises — a disorderly exit from a currency union, a pandemic, a cyber-attack on the payments system — and attaches no probabilities to them whatsoever, presenting them as severe-but-plausible illustrations rather than forecasts, and declining on principle to say how likely any of them is.

My question is not which half is right, but what each half commits its authors to, and whether the two commitments can be held together. Does the fan chart presuppose that every uncertainty that matters for policy is in principle measurable, that there is always a determinate probability waiting to be estimated? Does the scenario exercise presuppose the contrary, or merely a division of labour between the quantifiable and the unquantifiable? And does the combination — quantify the central projection, refuse to quantify the tail — presuppose a particular theory of what a probability is: a frequency, a degree of belief, or a logical relation between evidence and conclusion?

Bring Keynes into the diagnosis. His Treatise on Probability treats probability as a logical relation and a degree of rational belief, and distinguishes sharply between the probability of a proposition and the weight of the evidence bearing on it; in his later reflections on the General Theory he pressed the further claim that much of the economic future is not the sort of thing about which any calculable probability can be formed at all. I want you to say whether the fan chart presupposes precisely what Keynes denied, whether the unpriced scenario tacitly concedes his point, and whether Keynes could accept the fan chart if it were reinterpreted as an expression of weight of evidence rather than of probability. Then tell me what central-bank communication would have to become if we took his view seriously: would fan charts have to be abandoned, retained with a different interpretation, or supplemented by something else entirely?

In answering, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify any place where the two halves of the practice presuppose inconsistent things, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of probability would license the same behaviour. I would like you to state how confident you are in each of your main claims and to single out the point you are least sure of.
