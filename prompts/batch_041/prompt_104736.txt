I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend — a reinforcement-learning researcher — about whether the language models we both work with should be described as agents, and we kept talking past each other: she insists that anything with a policy and a goal is an agent by definition, I kept wanting to say that the model is doing something more like simulating a person who has goals, and neither of us could say precisely which further commitments our disagreement rested on. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist who is used to mapping conceptual disputes, and to build me a decision tree rather than an essay that sorts the positions on this question by what they actually commit you to. Begin by brainstorming the axes of disagreement — for instance, what the bearer of agency is supposed to be (the base model, the deployed system with its tools and memory, the training process, or the human–model loop), whether agency requires an internal representation of a goal or only behavior that is best predicted by attributing one, whether the model's self-reports ("I want", "I remember", "I'll try to avoid being shut down") are literal reports of states, in-character predictions, or moves in an interaction, whether agency requires a unified subject persisting across time, whether the attribution is meant to be true or merely useful as a stance, and whether the question is ultimately empirical, conceptual, or ethical — then lay out the tree. Each internal node must be a question a colleague could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, phrased around a concrete case rather than a label (e.g. a model that writes a first-person diary, a model that appears to pursue a goal it was never trained for, a model whose chain of reasoning looks like planning but whose outputs are unchanged when the plan is perturbed); each leaf should name a position and give one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

I care about several things in particular. The tree should include the "simulator" family of views, the plain "it's just a tool" view, and the view that the real agent is the human–model assemblage; it should distinguish views that treat agency as a matter of degree from views that treat it as all-or-nothing; it should mark any leaves it cannot separate and say why; and it should flag combinations in the logical space that nobody seems to occupy, with a brief diagnosis of whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Assume the audience is a mixed reading group of ML engineers and philosophers who know the standard terms of the debate but are tired of slogans, so please keep the prose tight, think carefully about whether each branch genuinely splits the positions rather than just relabeling them, and avoid resting anything on a particular paper or author's name — I want the tree to stand on the distinctions themselves.
