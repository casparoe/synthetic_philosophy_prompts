Right, so my reading group has been grinding away at the Condorcet jury theorem for about three months now and we've split into two camps who've basically stopped listening to each other.

Camp one thinks the theorem is the deepest thing anyone's ever said about why democracy works: bigger jury, better verdict, that's the whole story, and every real-world objection — voters are ignorant, they're not independent, they don't vote sincerely — is just the ideal being approximated badly. Camp two thinks it's a beautiful toy that tells you nothing about mass democracy, because the assumptions only bite when it's already too late, and they reckon the interesting result is the opposite one: if the average voter is worse than a coin flip, a bigger jury just marches you faster to the wrong answer.

I want to force them to write the joint statement they should've written. So please draft it:

- A numbered list of claims both sides can actually sign, stated plainly enough that neither feels stitched up. Include what each of them takes the theorem to be for — a normative benchmark, a descriptive model, or both — and what they agree it does and doesnt say. I'd be surprised if at least one of them hasn't quietly assumed the theorem is about aggregating preferences, when it's really about aggregating right/wrong judgements on a binary question, and those are different problems with different literatures. If you think part of their fight is verbal, say which part and why.
- Then a disagreements table: for each remaining disagreement, the neutral statement of what's at issue, then a one-line version of each side's position in wording that side would accept as fair.
- Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one of them. I think one is the independence assumption: whether it means statistically independent, or just conditionally independent given the truth, which is a much weaker thing. Another is whether the competence parameter means anything when the question is 'which policy' rather than 'did the defendant do it'. And then the messy case where competence varies across voters — does the clean convergence result survive that, or does the majority's reliability end up depending on the shape of the distribution? But have a look; I may have the wrong cruxes.
- For each crux, name a test, case, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts it'll show. If there's anything both would count as a real refutation of their own view, put that in too.

One thing I'd like you to consider building in. There's a tension I can't get them to see: the two things the theorem wants from voters — that they be better than chance, and that their errors be seperate — pull against each other the moment you let the voters talk. Deliberation is meant to raise competence, but it does so partly by making everyone's errors correlate, and the convergence is driven by the independence. So improving one degrades the other. If that's a genuine problem, spell out what it does to each camp's position; if it's a red herring, say why.

Around 900–1100 words. No bibliography or citations needed — just make it something both of them could actually put their names to.
