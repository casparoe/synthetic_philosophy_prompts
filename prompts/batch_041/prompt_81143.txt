I teach a third-year seminar on causal inference -- mixed bunch, mostly stats students plus a couple of philosophers, about 16 of them -- and I've got one 90-minute session to spend on Simpson's paradox. I've taught it the usual way and it's a dud: I put up the two tables, someone says "ah, it's confounding", everyone nods, and they leave with the takeaway "always look at the subgroups", which honestly seems worse than not covering it at all. What I want is an activity where they walk into the reversal themselves, commit to something before they see it, and it costs them a little when it flips.

My half-formed idea is some kind of committee -- they play admissions officers or a hospital board, we feed them cases in batches, they settle on a policy, and then we re-cut the exact same numbers a different way and the right call reverses. But I can't work out how to make the flips come out of the structure rather than me rigging it, or how to keep the whole thing from collapsing into "so the data lied", which is definately not the lesson.

So design it for me. Rules and materials, who does what, a timeline for the 90 minutes, the point in the sequence where they have to commit (before they've seen the aggregate, obviously), and a mechanism so the numbers are generated or tabulated by them rather than performed by me. Then the debrief: map each moment of the activity onto the actual moving parts of the idea. The moving parts I care about, roughly in order:

- that a reversal of this kind doesn't require confounding, and confounding doesn't require a reversal -- I want them to stop using "Simpson's paradox" and "confounding" as synonyms, which is the thing they do now
- that the arithmetic is boring (it's weighted averages with different weights) and the whole puzzle is about what we're allowed to infer from it
- the decision-theoretic reading, where it looks like a violation of the principle that if an option is at least as good in every state of the world then it's at least as good overall, and why that reading is tempting and what's actually wrong with it
- that nothing inside the numbers tells you whether to act on the subgroup view or the aggregate view -- something outside the table settles it, and I want them to feel how unsatisfying that is
- if there's room, the seperate ethics version: a policy that's better for every subgroup and worse for the population, and whether that's the same problem in different clothes or a genuinely different one

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing one group never budges once they've seen the aggregate, and someone games the batch order -- and how to handle that without turning it into a lecture. And be straight with me about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. No readings, I'll sort those out.
