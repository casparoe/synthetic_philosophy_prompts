Here's the thing that's been eating at me. My friend Dana is a conservator at a museum. She's spent ten years on a small panel painting and she says she knows — not believes, knows — that it's a genuine 15th-century piece and not a later copy. Then a lab runs a new pigment analysis and it comes back pointing the other way: the pigments look anachronistic for that date. Her colleagues tell her she now has to take the result seriously, drop her confidence, maybe give up the attribution. Dana says no, and this is the argument she gives:

(1) If I know the panel is genuine, then the panel is genuine.
(2) If the panel is genuine, then any evidence that it's a copy is misleading evidence — it's evidence that supports something false.
(3) Everyone agrees you're rationally permitted to disregard misleading evidence; that's basically what makes it misleading.
(4) So if I know the panel is genuine, I'm rationally permitted to disregard the lab result.
(5) And I do know it's genuine — ten years of connoiseurship, provenance, the whole thing.
(6) Therefore I'm rationally permitted to disregard the lab result and keep the attribution.

Then she pushes further: (7) the same reasoning works for any piece of counterevidence whatsoever, so once you know something you're permitted to ignore every future challenge to it. She says that isn't a bug, it's the whole point of knowledge — knowledge is supposed to be the thing you get to stop re-litigating.

I'm fine keeping (1), (2), and (5). I'm pretty sure something is off, because an argument that says knowing something licenses you to wave off all future evidence seems to prove far too much, and it turns knowledge into a license for closed-mindedness, which can't be right. But I can't find the bad step. My gut says the damage is somewhere around (2)–(4), but I might be wrong; it could be (5), or it could be the slide out to (7).

Tell me which step or steps to reject, why that step looked so plausible in the first place, and what giving it up actually costs.
