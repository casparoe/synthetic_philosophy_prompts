Imagine Descartes — his metaphysics intact, his ignorance of everything after his death total — handed a dossier of present-day findings and asked to draw out what his own principles imply. The dossier has five entries.

1. Conversational machines. Software systems now produce fluent, context-sensitive discourse and can sustain a conversation indefinitely. They are also embodied, and in robots they outperform humans at many physical tasks, so the second of his marks — that a machine will fail at something and thereby betray that it acts by the disposition of its organs rather than from knowledge — no longer cleanly separates them from us. Their defenders and critics agree that they run on something like mechanical arrangement: vast statistical machinery, no understanding.

2. Invertebrate minds. Octopuses and cuttlefish solve novel problems, use tools, and adjust means to ends in flexible ways, on nervous architectures quite unlike our own.

3. A public declaration. In April 2024 more than five hundred researchers signed the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness. It asserts strong scientific support for consciousness in other mammals and birds, judges there to be "a realistic possibility of conscious experience" in all vertebrates and many invertebrates (at minimum cephalopods, decapod crustaceans, and insects), and concludes that "when there is a realistic possibility of conscious experience in an animal, it is irresponsible to ignore that possibility in decisions affecting that animal."

4. Sentience law. Several jurisdictions have extended animal-cruelty protections to octopuses and crabs on the strength of such evidence.

5. Evolutionary continuity. The human/animal divide is now standardly treated as one of degree, with no specially conferred rational soul to mark a boundary in kind.

I want his verdicts, argued from his own commitments rather than ours. Specifically:

(a) For each entry, would he assimilate the case to the beast-machine, refuse the assimilation, or split the difference?

(b) The two marks he offers in the Discourse on Method — the appropriate use of language, and acting from knowledge rather than from the disposition of organs — were they presented as empirical diagnostics that a sufficiently clever beast or machine could in principle satisfy, or as downstream consequences of a prior metaphysical fact, namely that only humans possess a rational, immaterial soul? If the first, the conversational machine satisfies at least the first mark and should be credited with thought; if the second, the marks cannot carry the evidential weight he placed on them. Which reading would he adopt under pressure, and what does his choice cost him?

(c) On the permissive reading of his correspondence — animals have bodily sensations and passions while wholly lacking thought — how would he classify the octopus? On the austere reading — no felt experience at all — does he retain any reason, independent of his metaphysics, to prefer it?

(d) Would the precautionary reasoning of entry 3 be intelligible to him, or would his certainty that no beast has a rational soul render the acknowledged possibility of animal consciousness practically idle?

Give me his likely verdicts and the reasoning behind them, and identify the point at which his system comes under the greatest strain.
