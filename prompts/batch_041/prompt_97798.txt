I'm a philosophy PhD student putting together a unit on theories of punishment around Crime and Punishment, and the standard reading has started to grate on me. Everyone treats it as the great retributivist novel — Raskolnikov has to confess because he needs to be punished, Porfiry just sits back and waits him out — and I don't think thats quite it, so I want a reading that says what it actually is.

Here's the thing I want worked out. Read the novel against the retributivism/consequentialism split and make the distinction do real work, specifically between two things people constantly run together: punishment as something the offender deserves (owed, justified by desert alone, independent of any good it produces) and suffering as something that's good for him (penance, cure, moral rebirth). Dostoevsky obviously wants the second, and my students always read it as the first. Is that a confusion the novel commits, or is the novel actually more interesting than the textbook distinction? Where does Raskolnikov's punishment really happen — Siberia, or in his own head during the weeks before the police ever get him? If it's the second, what's left for the state to do, and does that make Porfiry a consequentialist or just a patient man? And Sonya's forgiveness: is it the antithesis of retributivism, or the thing retributivists quietly depend on and never theorize?

Commit to a reading and defend it — name the scenes that carry the weight, and say where the book resists the frame. Don't hedge by handing me both sides, and don't fall back on "it's Russian Orthodoxy" as the whole answer.
