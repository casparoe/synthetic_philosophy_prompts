I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works on early Chinese philosophy, and I lost more badly than I would like to admit. Her claim is that the famous quarrel between Mencius and Xunzi is largely a quarrel about words: on her reading, Mencius uses human nature for the set of incipient tendencies that grow in the right conditions, while Xunzi uses it for the raw faculties and desires we have from birth, and once you separate those two senses the slogans about goodness and badness turn out not even to contradict one another. Both men, she says, hold that anyone can become a sage; the difference is only how much heavy lifting ritual, teachers, and deliberate effort have to do, and Xunzi's word for our condition is a rhetorical escalation of a fairly modest point about untrained desire. I think she is wrong and that something substantive survives the definitional clarification, but I could not say precisely what, so I would like to rehearse the other side properly rather than score points off a version of it I invented.

Please play a committed Xunzian — Xunzi himself if you prefer, or a contemporary defender speaking in his voice — and stay in character until I say stop. You may use only concepts available in the third century BCE plus whatever I explain to you; if you need a factual question answered, ask it as your character would. Concede what genuinely should be conceded: if I state the sense of xing correctly, say so, and do not pretend the two thinkers' definitions coincide if they do not. But do not concede that the disagreement is merely verbal — your character should have a real reason to think Mencius is making a mistake rather than merely using a word differently. Keep each reply to a paragraph or so, so that it stays a conversation, and argue it out rather than citing scholarship at me.

Here is my opening, which is also where I got stuck last night. I have been reading about developmental biology, where what an organism inherits is often described not as a fixed tendency but as a range of possible responses depending on environment — a reaction norm — so that asking whether a trait is in the organism or in the environment is often the wrong question, and the same endowment can be described as producing flourishing or ruin depending on which conditions you tacitly assume. Suppose I grant you that by birth we have desires which, left alone, lead to conflict and disorder. It still seems to me that on your own account there must be something in us that can be moved by the Way; otherwise no quantity of ritual and teaching could take hold, and you would be claiming that training conjures a capacity out of nothing. But if that responsiveness is ours by birth, it appears to fall under your own definition of xing — and then your quarrel with Mencius is either a dispute about how strong and how reliable that innate responsiveness is, which is an empirical question neither of you was in a position to settle, or it is a dispute about words after all, and my friend wins. So tell me: what exactly do you deny that Mencius affirms, stated in a sentence that does not depend on which of you is allowed to use the word nature?
