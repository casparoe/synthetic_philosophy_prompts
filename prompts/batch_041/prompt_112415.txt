I've been trying for a while to get clear on what Zhuangzi's butterfly dream is actually doing, and I keep finding that the standard treatments slide over the part that seems most interesting. The usual move is to file it under dream skepticism and treat it as an Eastern cousin of Descartes' dream argument: I can't be certain I'm not dreaming right now, so the senses aren't fully trustworthy. But the story as I understand it doesn't quite fit that mold. Zhuangzi doesn't just wonder whether his present experience is a dream. He wonders whether he is a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. That's a doubt about which of two lived lives is the dream, and it's symmetric in a way the Cartesian doubt isn't. In the Cartesian case there's a privileged standpoint — the meditator's present thinking — that is supposed to survive the doubt and anchor everything else. In the butterfly case there's no such standpoint: whichever side you're on, the other side has an equal claim to being the waking state. And the same chapter contains a passage about a great awakening, in which we'll supposedly know that this life is a great dream, and in which people who think they're awake are called fools — but the passage also has the speaker say that in telling you that you're dreaming, he is dreaming too. That looks like a regress that would undercut any straightforward skeptical conclusion.

What I want is an explanation of the conceptual issue here, at the level of the arguments rather than the imagery. In particular: is the butterfly dream best read as a skeptical argument at all, or is the skepticism a byproduct of a different point — something about the transformation of things, the instability of fixed identities, or the impossibility of a neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate between perspectives? If it is a skeptical argument, how does the symmetry between the two dreams change its structure relative to the Cartesian version? Does the symmetry make the doubt stronger, because neither side can claim the epistemic high ground, or weaker, because a doubt that can't in principle be resolved by any possible evidence isn't really a doubt about anything? And how should the great-awakening regress figure into the answer — does it show that the skeptical reading is self-undermining, or does it just push the problem up a level?

I'd also like the explanation to distinguish between two things that often get run together: doubt about the external world and doubt about personal identity. Descartes' dream doubt is primarily about whether the world I seem to perceive is real; the butterfly dream seems to be at least as much about whether I am the person I seem to be. If that's right, then the butterfly dream may be closer to worries about the continuity of the self, or about whether there's a fact of the matter about which center of experience is the real one, than to worries about the reliability of the senses. I'd like to know whether that distinction is doing real work or whether it collapses on inspection.

Finally, I'd be interested in whether the right response is to say that the question is not well-formed, or that it's well-formed but unanswerable, or that it's answerable but the answer doesn't matter. Those seem like three very different ways of dissolving the problem, and I suspect that a lot of the disagreement about the butterfly dream is really disagreement about which of those moves is appropriate. Please explain the issue from the ground up, with attention to the structure of the arguments rather than to historical context or textual scholarship; I don't need citations or a reading list.
