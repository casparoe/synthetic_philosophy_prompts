I'm teaching a grad seminar on the unity of the proposition, and a student in it -- the kind who reads Bradley for fun -- has handed me an argument I can't get out of my head, mostly because I'm fairly sure it proves a lot more than he wants it to. He insists it's just Bradley's regress with the truth-theoretic stakes made explicit. Here it is, tidied up:

(1) Any complex -- a proposition, a fact, a heap of stones, the world -- is either nothing over and above its constituents or something over and above them. (2) If it's nothing over and above them, it's a mere plurality, not one thing; and a proposition that isn't one thing isn't the sort of thing that can be true. (3) So a complex must be more than its constituents: there must be something that unifies them. (4) Whatever unifies them can't itself be one of the things unified, since if it were, the very same question would just arise again about it and the rest. (5) So the unifier is a further item that has to be combined with the others. (6) Combining is a relation, and a relation that relates its relata is one more item that has to be combined with them. (7) Repeat. No complex is ever finished being unified, and an unfinished unification isn't a unification at all. (8) So there are no complexes: no propositions, no facts, no wholes, nothing with parts. (9) But (8) refutes itself -- it's a proposition, with parts, offered as an argument, with steps, and it presupposes the very complexity it denies. (10) So something above has to go.

What I'd like to keep, if I can: that propositions are genuinely one thing rather than secret pluralities (whatever that would even mean), and that at least some things are true. What I'm not willing to pay is the move where we just declare unification primitive and unanalyzable -- that's a promissory note, not a solution. So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? I especially want to know whether (4) is doing something sneaky. I can't tell whether it's a genuine constraint or whether it quietly assumes that anything occurring in a complex occurs in it as one more constituent -- which is precisely what the multiple-relation people deny. If the fix needs a principled distinction between occurring as a term and occurring as relating, or Frege's unsaturatedness and the mess that comes with it when you try to say that the concept horse is not a concept, tell me what that distinction actually buys and where it starts to strain. And if the frame is wrong -- if this isn't really a proves-too-much argument so much as a reductio of a premise I'm smuggling in by even stating it this way -- say so instead of playing along; if my wanting to keep both unity and truth turns out to be incoherent, I'd rather hear that than get a tidy survey of the positions. Don't hedge at the end: tell me what you'd actually give up, and how confident you are that the alternatives are any cheaper. I've probably got a typo or two in the numbering, ignore that.
