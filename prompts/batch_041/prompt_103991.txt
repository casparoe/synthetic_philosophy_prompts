I'm in a seminar on practical reason and we just spent two weeks on the internalism/externalism thing -- Williams's idea that if you say somebody has a reason to do something, that has to hook up with the motivations she already has, versus the view that there are reasons that apply to an agent whether or not anything going on in her head would ever move her. Then the professor handed us Hume, Treatise Book II, the section on the influencing motives of the will, as "where the whole debate starts." I've read it three times and i'm honestly not sure it's about the same topic at all. Here's the bit I keep staring at:

"Thus it appears, that the principle, which opposes our passion, cannot be the same with reason, and is only call'd so in an improper sense. We speak not strictly and philosophically when we talk of the combat of passion and of reason. Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them."

Earlier in the section he says he's going to prove "that reason alone can never be a motive to any action of the will; and secondly, that it can never oppose passion in the direction of the will," and then a few lines after the famous sentence he says "A passion is an original existence, or, if you will, modification of existence, and contains not any representative quality, which renders it a copy of any other existence or modification."

What I can't figure out is what kind of claim this is even supposed to be. Is Hume telling us what causes our actions -- a piece of psychology about motivation -- or is he telling us what reasons there are, in the sense Williams cares about? Those look like different questions to me. I have one friend who insists Hume "basically invented internalism" and another who says Hume is just doing causal psychology and Williams's question doesn't so much as come up for him yet. There's also alot riding on that "no representative quality" premise about passions, and I don't see why we should grant it -- my emotions seem perfectly capable of being about things and of getting those things wrong. And "ought only to be the slave" sounds like a normative claim, which is a weird thing to hand down right after saying reason can't move us at all. If you happen to remember where in Book II he says something about the calm passions getting mistaken for reason, tell me whether that wrecks the clean reason-versus-passion story, because it feels like it should. Write it in American English please, and end with one last line that reads "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase of three or four words saying what kind of claim you think the passage is really making.
