I'm a second-year PhD student in archaeology, and I'm trying to write a methods paper on the logic of inferring beliefs and institutions from material remains. The literature is full of warnings about over-interpretation, but the warnings don't add up to a principle, and I keep finding that my own judgments are inconsistent. I'd like you to help me get to a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that does justice to the verdicts I actually have.

Here are the cases I've been using, with my verdicts. (1) A burial of an adult with a flint knife in one hand, a necklace of shell beads, and red ochre scattered over the bones: I think we can infer that the community had some notion of an afterlife, or at least that the dead were thought to need or deserve these things. (2) A pit containing forty identical clay figurines of cattle, all facing east, with no other artifacts: I think we can infer only that they were deliberately made and deposited, not that the community practiced a fertility cult or held a specific belief about cattle. (3) A circle of large stones with a gap aligned to the summer solstice sunrise: I think we can infer that the builders had observational knowledge of the sun's cycle and cared about marking it. (4) A set of twenty small stone discs, each incised with a different number of lines, found on a house floor: I think we can infer only that they were used in some counting or recording practice, not that the community had a concept of number or a mathematical institution. (5) A midden with fish bones, deer bones, and broken pots: I think we can infer diet and subsistence practices, but not beliefs or institutions. (6) A large building with a central hearth, a raised platform, and a niche, with no domestic artifacts: I think we can infer it was a public or ritual building, and that the community had some form of centralized authority or communal gathering. (7) A cave painting of a horse and a handprint: I think we can infer that people were there and made images, but not that they practiced sympathetic magic or had a religion. (8) A burial of a child with a small clay animal with a string hole: I think we can infer that children were cared for and that the community had a concept of childhood.

I can see that some of these verdicts pull against each other. Why does the burial with ochre and beads license a belief in an afterlife, while the cache of cattle figurines licenses only deliberate deposition? Why does the solstice alignment license astronomical knowledge, while the incised discs license only a counting practice? And why does the child's burial license a concept of childhood, while the cave painting licenses only image-making? I suspect the difference has something to do with the object being embedded in a practice that involves coordination, or with the costliness of the practice, or with the availability of a mundane alternative explanation, but I can't state the principle. Please formulate the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each, state it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and then tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a pit containing twelve identical clay cylinders, each incised with a different number of lines, arranged in a circle, with no other artifacts. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up the verdict or the principle.
