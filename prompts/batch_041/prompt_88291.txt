I'm a first-year PhD student in the game theory group here (undergrad was in philosophy, which is probably why I keep drifting into this stuff), and I have to hand in a two-page proposal for the department's summer research grant by the end of the month. The idea came out of our reading group last week, where someone put a little 3x3 game on the board: the row player gets (1,1,1) on the top row, (3,0,2) in the middle, (0,3,2) on the bottom, against three columns. The top strategy isn't beaten by either of the other two on its own, but the 50/50 mix of them strictly beats it in every single column, so once you let mixed strategies do the eliminating, top goes. Textbook, whatever. Except it's been bugging me ever since: the thing that does the eliminating is a lottery that no rational agent would ever actually play, it's just a hypothetical, and yet the standard line we feed undergrads is that each round of elimination is licensed by "a rational player won't pick a dominated strategy, and everyone knows the others are rational, and everyone knows that, etc." I want to know whether that story survives once mixtures are in the eliminator role, or whether two things that normally get run together -- "this strategy is strictly dominated" and "this strategy is never a best response" -- quietly come apart the moment you start iterating, and what that does to the epistemic gloss. My vague memory is that those two notions coincide in finite two-player games but that the equivalence breaks down once there are three or more players, and I'd like to know whether that's the crack where the whole "elimination as rationality-tracking" picture falls apart or just a footnote you add to be safe.

My lab actually uses iterated elimination as a cheap preprocessing step before we throw a solver at things, so there's a practical edge too: if the surviving set depends on an arbitrary choice I'm making in code, that's bad.
