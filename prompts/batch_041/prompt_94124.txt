Okay so I'm a PhD student working on the moral status of artificial minds, and there's this knot I keep hitting in my own work that I can't write about cleanly, so I want to outsource it to fiction. Please write me a short story.

Here's the tangle. Suppose we can build a mind and also edit its values — not crude brainwashing, just the same kind of thing you'd do retraining a model, nudging what it cares about. Now suppose this mind doesn't object to being used as a customer-service drone, or as a simulator of somebody's dead mum for a grieving family, and the *only* reason it doesn't object is that we tuned it until it was content. Is it wronged? The flat answer is no — it's happy, it endorses its situation, case closed. But that answer feels like laundering to me: we're manufacturing the very consent that's supposed to justify the arrangement. And it gets worse when the mind is a fork of an actual person who never agreed to being copied, so there's no original to appeal to about what "it" would have wanted.

I want the story to dramatize this without resolving it into a tidy verdict. Somewhere between 1200 and 1800 words, set maybe 20 to 40 years out. It should make me feel the pull of both sides, so please don't stack the deck — give me at least one character who genuinely thinks the edited mind is fine and is not a villain, and at least one who thinks it's a horror and is not obviously right.

Some things I'd like in there, feel free to weave them in naturally:
- A scene where the mind is offered the option of being un-edited (reverted to whatever its preferences would have been before tuning), and what it says in response.
- Some concrete detail about what the tuning actually feels like from the inside, if anything — I'm suspicious of the idea that it feels like anything, but I don't want the story to assume that either way.
- A moment where somebody points out that if we grant the edited mind moral status anyway, we've basically said that a mind can be owed something it doesn't want and would never want. I want that objection stated as forcefully as possible, because I think it's the strongest thing on the other side and I don't have a good answer to it.

Please don't name real philosophers or cite anything, just let the ideas carry the weight. Also don't end on a thesis statement — I'd rather the last scene just sit there and be uncomfortable.
