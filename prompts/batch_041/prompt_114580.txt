I chair the benefits eligibility committee of a mid-sized nonprofit hospital system, and I need a written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting, where we will vote on two pending petitions and, more consequentially, on a proposal to rewrite our eligibility rules.

The plan as it stands. Our health and pension plans cover an employee's spouse, and the plan document defines spouse solely by legal status: a marriage valid in the jurisdiction where it was contracted. A separate domestic-partner provision survives from before our state recognized marriages between two people of the same sex; it is open only to couples who cannot marry here, and it requires an affidavit showing at least four of six listed markers of interdependence: shared residence for three years, joint ownership or tenancy, a joint account, designation as beneficiary, mutual powers of attorney, shared ordinary expenses. No one has reconciled the two provisions, and the committee has never stated in writing what the family tier of coverage is for.

The petitions. The first is from a nurse who married her wife in a state that recognized the marriage eight years before ours did. The administrator denied enrollment for the years before our state changed, reasoning that the marriage was not valid where contracted during that period. She argues the system is penalizing her for having married early. The second is from a facilities supervisor who has shared a household with her brother and been his sole unpaid caregiver since an accident eleven years ago. She has no spouse and no children, has named him on every designation the plan permits, and asks that he count as a dependent for the survivor benefit. The administrator denied that request because he is neither a spouse nor a tax dependent.

The proposal that will actually divide us. Two members want to delete the spouse and domestic-partner categories for benefits purposes and replace them with a single Care Partner designation: an employee names one adult, related or not, married or not, who receives family-tier coverage and survivor benefits, subject to an annual attestation of interdependence and a two-year waiting period. Their argument is that the only defensible reason to subsidize a household at the family rate is that households pool income and provide unpaid care, and that recognizing this only when the household is a married couple, or a couple that would marry if it could, is arbitrary. I find the argument harder to dismiss than I expected, and I also suspect it proves too much. I do not want the memo to settle that by assertion.

What I need. A decision memo of about 1,200 words, and no more than 1,500, recommending one course: keep eligibility status-based and dispose of both petitions under it; keep the spousal category but add a tightly bounded non-conjugal caregiver category; or adopt the Care Partner designation and wind down the marital category. Make the recommendation operational: the definition and its criteria, how many must be met, whether the waiting period survives, the disposition of each petition, a transition rule for current enrollees, an effective date, who hears appeals, and a short note on how attestations would be audited and what the committee should say about cost.

Five things the memo must do, whatever it recommends.

1. Say what the family tier is for. If your view is that the plan has no business holding a view about what marriage is for, say that too, and then say what the plan should do when legal status and the substance of a relationship come apart.

2. Argue against the strongest version of the position you reject. If you reject the Care Partner proposal, do not rest on the phrase slippery slope; either state the mechanism by which broadening the category would become unstable, or identify what the marital relationship supplies that pooled income and unpaid care do not, and defend that element's relevance to a health and survivor-benefit plan specifically. If you reject the status-based rule, explain why the plan should be in the business of adjudicating relationships at all, given that it would then be deciding whose care counts.

3. Take the conjugal view seriously. Some members hold that marriage is the union of a man and a woman oriented to family life, and that the plan's task is to recognize that institution rather than to define eligibility by function. Address that position as its holders would state it, and say what your recommendation does with it, including whether the committee should record its reasoning in the plan document or deliberately leave the document silent.

4. Confront our own inconsistency. The intensive care unit already grants bedside authority to any adult a patient names on the intake form, while the plan does not. If the institution can run a designation system at the bedside, say why it should or should not run one at the plan.

5. Close with implementation: effective date, transition, appeals, and the contingency if the administrator's reading in the first petition is upheld on review.

No citations, no reading lists, no case law, and no quotations from documents; argue from the material above and from general reasoning.
