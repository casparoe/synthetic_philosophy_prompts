I have spent a long time reading the rationalist blogs and forums, and one of the habits I have picked up from them is the discipline of collecting verdicts on particular cases before committing to a principle. I have been doing that with a question I cannot settle on my own: when is it rational to defer to my own future credences? I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for an inventory of who said what. I want to know what principle, if any, is actually generating the verdicts below, and I am willing to abandon whichever verdict the best principle forces me to abandon.

The principle I am working against is the one usually called the Reflection principle, associated with van Fraassen, which I take to say roughly this: if I am certain that at some later time my credence in a proposition A will be x, then my credence in A now ought already to be x, on the thought that otherwise my present and future selves can be sure to be exploited against each other. Some of the cases below fit that beautifully; others make me want to throw it out.

1. The sealed envelope. I am about to be handed an envelope that I am certain settles whether A, and I am certain that after reading it my credence in A will be 0.9. Verdict: my credence now should be 0.9. This is the case that made me think the principle was true.

2. The paranoia pill. I will take a pill tomorrow, and I am certain that afterwards my credence that my neighbor is a spy will be 0.9. I am equally certain this will happen because the drug disorders my thinking, not because I will have learned anything. Verdict: my credence now should stay near 0.02. I should not adopt a credence I know will be manufactured rather than earned.

3. The arithmetic on the desk. Everything needed to settle a numerical question is already in a spreadsheet open in front of me; I simply have not done the work. I am certain that tomorrow I will grind through it and land at 0.8, and that the arithmetic is routine. Tonight, not yet having done it, I am at 0.5. Verdict: I should be at 0.8 now. My future self here is not better informed than I am; it is the same evidence, better processed, and that still looks like a reason to defer.

4. The bad argument tomorrow. I am at 0.8 that P. I am certain that tomorrow I will read a long argument against P by someone I currently regard as a crank, and that reading it will move me to 0.3, not because it will give me new evidence but because it is rhetorically effective and I will be tired. Verdict: I should stay at 0.8. This is also a case of same evidence, different processing, and it pulls the opposite way from the arithmetic case.

5. The misremembering. I am certain that tomorrow I will be at credence 1 that I have finished a piece of work, because I will find it done and will have no memory of failing; but I also know there is a real chance I did fail and am misremembering. Verdict: I should be at 0.7 now, not 1. My future self's certainty is no reason for mine.

6. The two rooms. I will go to sleep and wake in one of two indistinguishable rooms. I am certain that on waking I will be at 0.5 that I am in the left-hand room. Tonight, what should I think about the proposition that the room I wake in is the left-hand one? Verdict: I do not want to say my credence now should be 0.5. My future credence seems to be about who I will turn out to be, not about a fact already settled, and I suspect the principle does not apply to it at all. But I cannot say precisely why, and I suspect this is the case that will embarrass whatever principle I end up with.

7. The forgotten distraction. I am at 0.6 that Q. Part of why I am not higher is a piece of testimony I have since learned is unreliable but which still nags at me; I am certain that tomorrow, having forgotten it, I will be at 0.8. Verdict: I should be at 0.8 now. My future self will know strictly less than I know now, and I still think it will be more accurate, and I think that is what matters.

What I want from you:

1. A principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to be tested, that best captures these verdicts.

2. For each clause, the case that forces it, and in particular any case that forces you to choose between an information reading (defer when your future self knows more) and an accuracy reading (defer when your future self is likely to be closer to the truth).

3. Your verdicts, under that principle, on a case I have not listed; pick one that strains the principle rather than illustrating it.

4. An honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would repair the principle or give up the verdict.

5. If a simpler principle would save all but one verdict, say which verdict you would sacrifice and why.

I would rather have a principle that is a little ugly and gets everything than a clean one that ignores the cases that embarrass it.
