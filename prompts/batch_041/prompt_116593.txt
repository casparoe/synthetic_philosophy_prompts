I am a third-year philosophy student currently taking a course on decision theory and rational choice, and I am writing a term paper on the Death in Damascus case. My paper will compare two competing diagnoses of what that case shows, and I would like you to work through the comparison with me.

The case, as I understand it: an agent learns that Death will be in one of two cities, and that Death's location tracks whatever the agent decides to do, so that if he flees to the safer city, Death will be waiting there, and if he stays put, Death will be there instead. Deliberating about which act to perform seems to change the act's expected utility, since each option looks better precisely when one is not choosing it, and so no act survives deliberation as the one the agent should perform.

The two diagnoses I want to compare are these. The first says the trouble is instability, or non-ratifiability: the defect is that no available act is one the agent, upon adopting it, would still endorse, and this is a structural failure in the way evidential reasoning evaluates acts, showing that evidential decision theory cannot even deliver a stable recommendation here. The second says the trouble is a mistaken treatment of correlation as though it were causal influence: Death's being in a given city is causally independent of the agent's choice, so once acts are evaluated by their causal consequences rather than by the evidence they carry, the apparent flip-flopping evaporates and the case is no harder than an ordinary decision under uncertainty about a fixed fact.

Please do the following. First, state each diagnosis as precisely as you can, and identify exactly what each one claims the defect in the agent's reasoning to be. Second, compare them: do they disagree about what makes the case puzzling, or are they two descriptions of a single failure? In particular, address whether the ratifiability diagnosis can be restated in causal terms without loss, and whether the causal diagnosis can explain why the agent's deliberation feels unstable rather than merely mistaken. Third, adjudicate between them: which diagnosis gives the better account of the case's philosophical force, and what does your answer imply about whether the case is a genuine counterexample to evidential decision theory or merely a symptom of misapplied causal intuitions?

Finally, test both diagnoses against a modified case I have been thinking about, which I suspect neither diagnosis handles in quite the same way. Suppose Death's location tracks the agent's choice only imperfectly, so that fleeing raises the probability that Death is in the city fled to without making it certain. Does the instability diagnosis still apply? Does the causal diagnosis still dissolve the puzzle? And do the two diagnoses come apart in this variant in a way they do not in the original? Use that variant to say which diagnosis is the more robust of the two.
