I am the complaints and patient-safety investigator for a psychiatric hospital group. I write up roughly twenty cases a month — a patient says a medication has ruined their life, that a nurse was rough with them during restraint, that the ward round did not listen — and my findings go to the medical director and sometimes to lawyers, so they have to be defensible. I have about ninety minutes per case, one interview, the clinical record, and no clinical training beyond what I have picked up on the job. I am trying to rebuild our method around the literature on epistemic injustice, and I keep failing, because our present checklist — ask open questions, avoid assumptions, record the patient's words verbatim — is addressed entirely to the hearer's manners, while the failures that actually worry me are not.

Let me set out the four failure modes I want the procedure to catch, since our previous version conflated them. First, plain credibility discounting: the report is heard and understood but given less weight because of who is speaking — the psychiatric label, the accent, the class, the history of complaints. Second, the discounting that hides inside plausibility: a report is set aside not because we disbelieve the speaker but because the thing reported strikes us as unlikely, and our sense of what is likely has been shaped by the very prejudices at issue. This is the one I most need tests for, because from the inside it feels like clinical judgement rather than prejudice. Third, the reports that never arrive: patients learn which complaints are futile and which will be read as symptoms, so they stop making them — which means our absence of complaints is evidence of nothing, and the procedure needs some audit step that does not depend on complaints. I have not been able to design one. Fourth, the gap in vocabulary: neither the patient nor we have the words for what happened, so the complaint arrives as vagueness, and vagueness is then read as unreliability. On the account I have been reading, that last one is not a failure of listening and will not be fixed by listening harder, which suggests that part of what I want a procedure to do cannot be done by a procedure at all.

Write me the procedure. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a finding and where it terminates by handing the question to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and I want the steps that are doing moral work under the guise of evidential reasoning flagged as such. It must be runnable by a colleague with no philosophy training, in ninety minutes, and it must still produce a decision where the evidence is thin — the medical director will not accept a finding that amounts to "we cannot know" — so tell me how to do that without pretending to a certainty I do not have.

Then run it on three cases: a patient with a diagnosis of schizophrenia reporting an unbearable inner restlessness that the record calls agitation and treats with more medication; a patient who says the food is being poisoned, where the record and an independent check both indicate that it is not; and a complaint from a patient who has made eleven previous complaints, every one of them closed without action. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like, and I would rather you tell me which parts of this job the procedure will keep getting wrong than round the edges off. No citations needed; take the concepts as given.
