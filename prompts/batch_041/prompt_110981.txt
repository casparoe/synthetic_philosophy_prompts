I'm putting together the last week of a philosophy of mind discussion section and I want to use Spike Jonze's Her instead of another paper. We've been doing Dennett on real patterns and the three stances -- physical, design, intentional -- and my students keep collapsing into 'but is Samantha actually conscious or just programmed?' which is exactly the question I think the film is smarter than. I want a reading of the film that takes both Dennett and the movie seriously: where does the intentional stance earn its keep in the story, and where does the film show its limits? For instance, is Samantha's love a real pattern in Dennett's sense -- a predictive regularity that we can't do better than by treating her as a believer/desirer -- or is it just a design-stance artifact that the film dresses up as romance? And what do we do with the ending, where she and the other AIs leave for some higher plane? Is that a physical-stance event (servers, code, bandwidth) or an intentional-stance event (a choice, a goodbye), and can Dennett's framework even mark that distinction without cheating?

I'm also worried about a specific objection one of my students raised: that Dennett's realism about patterns is just instrumentalism with a straight face, because 'real' only means 'predictively useful,' and that the three stances are just three vocabularies, not three ontologies. Does Her support that reading or push against it? I'd like you to use scenes and character choices from the film to argue it out, not just summarize Dennett. And if the film ultimately resists the Dennettian reading -- say, because Samantha's mind is so unlike ours that the intentional stance starts to look like a human-centered projection -- I'd rather you say so then force it. I'm teaching this to undergrads who have read a bit of Dennett but not much else, so keep the philosophy clear but don't dumb down the film.
