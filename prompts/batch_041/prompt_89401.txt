I have been reading a short popular book on the ethics of deception, and its chapter on Augustine has been bothering me. The author is not a specialist but seems to have done some reading, and here is how the chapter sums up Augustine's position. According to the book, Augustine defined a lie as a false statement made with the intention to deceive, and he treated any deliberate falsehood as a lie. The book goes on to say that Augustine catalogued eight kinds of lies and ranked them by how much harm they do, from lies told to protect someone from harm up to lies that corrupt religious teaching. From this ranking, the book concludes that Augustine must have thought the harmless, well-intentioned lies were permissible when the alternative was serious harm, and it presents him as the distant ancestor of the modern view that you may lie to the murderer at the door. It adds that Augustine was so uncompromising that he counted even a deliberate silence about the truth as a lie, and that late in life he reversed himself and decided that lying is never allowed under any circumstances, which the book calls a sign that his position was never fully consistent.

I have no training in philosophy and cannot look anything up, but something about this feels off. I have a vague sense that Augustine was stricter than the book makes him out to be, and also that he was more careful about what counts as a lie than the book's definition suggests. Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful reader of Augustine would accept as fair, what would make them wince, and what is simply wrong? I would like to know in particular whether the book's definition of a lie matches Augustine's, whether the eightfold ranking can bear the weight the book puts on it, what Augustine actually held about the case of lying to save someone from harm, and whether the charge of inconsistency at the end survives once the earlier points are corrected. Please be explicit about which parts of the book's conclusion still stand and which collapse.
