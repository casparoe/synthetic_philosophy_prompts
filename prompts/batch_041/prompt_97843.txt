I'm in a graduate seminar on agency and free will, and my professor has spent the term assembling a running list of verdicts about which behaviors count as "sphexish" — her term, taken from the digger wasp (Sphex) whose provisioning routine runs to completion whether or not it still makes sense, and used by her for any behavior that is locked into a fixed sequence and fails to adjust to what the situation actually calls for. Her list so far: the wasp who, having stung a cricket and dragged it to the burrow, goes inside to inspect the burrow, and who, if the cricket is moved a few inches while she is inside, drags it back and inspects again, looping indefinitely — sphexish; a sleepwalker who cooks an entire meal without waking — sphexish; a man with a checking compulsion who returns to the stove forty times in a night even though he knows perfectly well it is off — sphexish; a chess engine that finds a non-obvious move and would have played differently had the position been different — not sphexish; a jazz musician improvising fluently over unfamiliar changes — not sphexish; a woman who deliberates between two job offers and takes the one that better answers her stated reasons — not sphexish. She also keeps saying, and this is the part I can't pin down, that sphexishness rather than determinism is what actually threatens free will — that a causally determined agent whose behavior tracks her reasons is free, while a sphexish one, determined or not, is not.

Next week she is taking up a case she has not seen before, and I want to get ahead of it. It is a hospital nurse who, after a medication error early in her career, has made a deliberate and considered commitment to run an inflexible checklist before every drug administration, on the explicit understanding that she will execute each step exactly as written even when her own judgment tells her a particular step is pointless in the case at hand, and who would abandon the whole policy if she came to believe it was doing more harm than good. Predict her verdict on the nurse. Then tell me which rules her six earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with: I can already see a rule that locates sphexishness in the behavior pattern itself — fixed, unmodulated sequences — and a rule that locates it in the mechanism producing the behavior, i.e. in whether the behavior is sensitive to the agent's reasons, whatever it happens to look like from outside, and I suspect a third is in play as well. Say which of these actually fits all six earlier verdicts, which one you think she is really running and why, and what each surviving rule predicts about the nurse. Finally, tell me which single further case would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, and say which reading of "sphexish" would have to be doing the work for her claim about free will to come out true rather than merely rhetorical.
