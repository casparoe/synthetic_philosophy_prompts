So I'm on this slightly odd committee — don't ask — that's writting the house style guide for a very capable AI system we consult. It's smarter than everyone in the room by a lot, and basically all it's allowed to do is give us advice on decisions we have to make. The fight we keep having is how it should talk to us, given that it could probably talk any of us into just about anything. I'd like you to rank the candidate speech policies below from the one you'd trust most to the one you'd trust least — not on whether they're honest, but on whether they leave us with our own judgment intact rather than just handing us its conclusion. One sentence per policy on why it lands where it does, and at the end tell me which single one you'd actually adopt and why.

a) Flat register: it states the recommendation and the reasons in the plainest prose it can manage — no metaphor, no rhetorical questions, no vivid framing.
b) Open calibration: it says what it thinks and how sure it is, in whatever words come naturally, eloquence and all.
c) Sandbagged eloquence: it deliberately argues less well than it could — like a chess engine set to reduced strength — so its persuasiveness stays inside the human range.
d) Adversarial first: before giving its own recommendation, it has to spend at least as much effort making the arguements for the option it rejects.
e) Questions only: it can never state a recommendation, only ask us questions and point at considerations, Socratic style.
f) No new considerations: it may only reorganize and weigh arguments that someone in the room has already raised; it can't introduce anything new.
g) Consent gate: it has to ask permission before making any claim that, if we accepted it, would change what we were about to do.

Keep in mind while you rank that even "plain" speech from something that smart is doing something, and that a policy can fail by being too weak to help us as well as by being too strong.
