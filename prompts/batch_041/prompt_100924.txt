I have been a longtime reader of LessWrong and the wider rationalist blogosphere, and I host a small interview podcast for that audience. Our next guest is a philosopher who defends a view she calls equilibrium realism about social kinds. On her account, social kinds such as money, race, gender, borders, and marriage are not fictions or mere labels. They are real, but their reality is the reality of a stable equilibrium: a self-reinforcing pattern of beliefs, expectations, and sanctions that makes the kind term track something in the world. Money is her paradigm case; race and gender are her stress cases. She also holds that the classification can loop back and change the people classified, so the kind is not merely a neutral description of a pre-existing population. I want to interview her in a way that gets past the usual talking points that social construction is either trivial or a form of denialism. Write eight to ten questions, each followed by a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Include at least one question on each of the following: why money is the easy case while race and gender are the hard cases; whether her view can explain the wrongness of racial or gender injustice without making the wrongness itself merely socially constructed; the causal-explanatory demand, that is, what social kinds explain that individual beliefs and actions do not; the looping or feedback effects by which classifications alter the classified; the political question of whether calling a kind socially constructed gives any reason to reform or abolish it; and the limits of the equilibrium model, especially cases of asymmetric power, historical contingency, and people who do not endorse the classification imposed on them. Press where the literature presses: the circularity of collective belief creating the kind, the difference between construction and convention, the threat of relativism, the possibility of mistaken membership in a social kind, and the persistence of a social kind after the practices that created it have changed. Make her work for her answers rather than repeat her abstract. Do not ask what drew her to the topic, for a summary of her book, or for reading recommendations. Keep the whole response under 900 words. Lead each item with the question in bold, then the follow-up in one or two sentences. No preamble or closing.
