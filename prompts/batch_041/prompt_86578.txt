Me and two friends have been going round in circles about the wrong kind of reasons for weeks now and I want to stop hand-waving and actually build a case instead. The way I set it up: take any attitude — admiring someone, trusting them, resenting them, wanting something, hoping for something — and split the reasons you might have for holding it into two piles. Reasons that are about the object: facts about the person or thing the attitude is aimed at. And reasons that are about the state: facts about your having the attitude, like the standard demon cases where some powerful being will hand you a pile of money for admiring him, or where having the attitude would make you happier or more popular or more loved. The usual story is that this split lines up with another one, the object reasons being the right kind (they bear on whether the attitude is correct, whether it fits its object) and the state reasons being the wrong kind (they can make the attitude useful or prudent or advisable but never correct). I want to know whether those two halves really have to travel together, so build me a case that pulls them apart in the direction nobody expects: a reason that is about the object in the plain sense — nothing in it mentions your having the attitude, or what having the attitude would do for you — and yet is intuitively a wrong kind of reason, one that just can't make the attitude correct.

Some constraints, because I want it usable and not sci-fi. Realistic: something that could happen to ordinary people at work, in a family, in a friendship. No demons, no rewards, threats, promises or bribes, no mind-readers, no brain tech, no aliens. One paragraph for the case itself, no more. And the reason has to be the agent's actual operative motive, not a hypothetical one — I want to come away feeling that the attitude is off, with the puzzle being exactly what's off about it. Also don't use belief as the attitude; pragmatic reasons for belief is its own swamp and I'm trying to stay out of it. Pick admiration, trust, resentment, desire, hope or something in that family, and say which.

Then do the check on it. State the reason in a line. Run the object/state test and say honestly whether it comes out object-given. Then the fittingness test: does the consideration bear on whether the attitude is correct or fitting, or doesn't it? Then tell me whether you think this is a real counterexample to the alignment or whether the standard story can absorb it — say, by insisting the reason was state-given after all, or by rejigging what counts as object-given — and what that absorption costs. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the case if I remove it. If every case you can build turns out to share a shape, tell me what the shape is and whether that's a coincidence or a symptom. And finish with the defender's attack in their own voice, not a strawman. If you've got room after all that, try the mirror case too — a state-given reason that's a right kind of reason — or if you think there can't be one, give me a couple of sentences on why not.
