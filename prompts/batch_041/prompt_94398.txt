I am a junior at a school with a written honor code that I help enforce as a member of the student honor council, and the more hearings I sit through, the less I understand what the document we administer actually believes about truthfulness. The code forbids lying in terms that are entirely about falsity: the reporting form asks whether the student "made a statement that was false," the hearing script walks the council through that question and no other, and the sanctions table is keyed to it. A student who invents an excuse is sanctioned; a student who says something scrupulously true in order to leave a false impression is, as a matter of procedure, not sanctionable at all, and several council members defend that outcome on the ground that nothing false was said. The same document draws the same line in its academic-integrity section, where copying a sentence without attribution is the graver offense and paraphrasing a source while quietly dropping the citation is a lesser lapse or no lapse, even though both leave a reader believing an idea is the student's own. Our administration, which wrote the code, appears to have learned its lesson from it: last spring it announced that no student had been suspended in connection with an incident that had in fact ended in two expulsions, and when a reporter pressed the point, the reply was that the statement had been accurate as written.

What I would like to understand is what view of truthfulness and deception this whole arrangement presupposes. Does the code assume that the wrong of deception attaches to the false assertion itself rather than to the intention to deceive, so that a speaker who never asserts anything false has done nothing wrong no matter what she intends her listener to conclude? Does it assume that a hearer has a right not to be lied to but no right not to be maneuvered, and perhaps a corresponding duty to draw his own inferences and bear the cost of drawing them badly? I have heard the opposite view from a friend in my philosophy elective, who argues that misleading can be worse than lying, because the misleader recruits the person deceived into doing the deceiving to himself and retains a second layer of deniability if the true words are ever read back aloud, and I cannot tell whether our code's silence about misleading is a considered position of the first kind or merely an oversight that happens to suit the people who wrote it.

Please reconstruct the theory of deception that the code and our hearings implicitly rely on, separating what the arrangement strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Then tell me whether the line between lying and misleading is really doing the work the council thinks it is doing, or whether the same sanctions could be licensed just as well by a view that cares only about consequences, or by one that cares about treating people as ends rather than as instruments, in which case the line would be doing no independent work at all. Finally, tell me what would have to change — in the code's definitions, on the reporting form, in how we conduct hearings, and in how I should vote the next time a technically true statement comes before us — if my friend's view were correct and misleading were the graver offense.
