For a graduate reading group on the metaphysics of mind, I want to build a decision tree that carries a reader from a bare starting commitment — that the split-brain (commissurotomy) data are genuine and must be accommodated — to a named position on the unity of consciousness. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence: name the single deepest fork, the question that does the most work, stated flatly. Only then give the tree, and only after that the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of my request.

Grow the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: first identify the questions that genuinely divide the positions, then locate the named views at the leaves. Each branch node must be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — phrased, wherever possible, as a claim about a concrete case rather than as an invitation to choose a label. Make the following distinctions do work somewhere in the tree, in whatever order the logic dictates: whether unity is a constitutive requirement on consciousness or a contingent achievement; whether it is a property of experiences, of subjects, or merely of our attributions; whether the patients' own reports of feeling unified should count as evidence or as the output of an interpreter; and whether unity admits of degrees.

Sort the positions using two stipulated hypothetical cases, and say explicitly where each leaf falls on both: (i) a patient whose hemispheres are rejoined by a prosthesis that carries only coarse affective signals — enough for mood to transfer, not for content; and (ii) a patient who, when the hemispheres are set at cross purposes, reports no conflict whatsoever yet behaves as two agents with divergent preferences. Because these are constructed rather than observed, treat them as probes of the positions' commitments, not as findings.

The leaves must include at least the two-subjects reading, the single-fragmented-mind reading, the single-subject-two-channels reading, the deflationary view on which unity is not a feature of experience at all but an attribution we make for practical purposes, the graded or partial-unity view, and the view on which unity is a necessary feature of any experience and the data must therefore be reinterpreted. Add any further leaf the logical space demands, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can phrase without naming them, say so rather than papering over it. The value is in the instrument, so do not supply a reading list or citations.

Keep the whole thing to roughly two sides of A4, and write in British English.
