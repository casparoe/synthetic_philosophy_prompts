Consider the unilateralist's curse. Several agents each independently decide whether to perform an action that is beneficial if only a few perform it but harmful if many do. Each agent may reason that their own action is unlikely to be the one that tips the balance, and so act, even though all would jointly prefer restraint. Write a formal essay on whether this curse is best understood as a genuine problem of collective action or as an artefact of a decision-theoretic mistake, namely treating one's own action as probabilistically independent of the actions of others when one's own disposition to act is in fact evidence about their dispositions. In developing your argument, consider a variant in which each agent can acquire, at some cost, a private and noisy signal of how many others are disposed to act, and in which that signal is positively correlated with the agent's own disposition. Does this correlated-signal variant make the curse more or less tractable? Does it strengthen or weaken the case that the curse is a genuine collective-action problem rather than an artefact of unrealistic independence assumptions? Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides and defend a verdict; do not end on a non-committal note. Please write in British English and do not include a bibliography or references.
