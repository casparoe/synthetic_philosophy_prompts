What is the hard core, what is the protective belt, and what role do the positive and negative heuristics play? And here's what the student wrote: "Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes is his attempt to fix what's wrong with both Popper's falsificationism and Kuhn's paradigms. A research programme is built around a hard core of central assumptions that you don't give up; around it sits a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that absorb the shocks from experiment; and the whole thing is guided by heuristics. The hard core is protected by the negative heuristic, which tells you how to develop the programme and which lines of research to pursue. The positive heuristic tells you what to leave alone — basically, hands off the core. The protective belt is where all the empirical action happens: when an observation clashes with the core, you adjust the belt instead of the core. A programme is progressive if it keeps making successful new predictions, and degenerating if it only saves the phenomena after the fact — if it's just patching anomalies with ad hoc tweaks. So take string theory. The question is whether it's predicting new things or just retrofitting. Its critics say it's degenerating because it hasn't produced testable predictions; its defenders say the mathematical unification is itself a form of progress. Lakatos is closer to Kuhn than to Popper, I'd say, because he admits the core can't be directly falsified and that theory choice means comparing whole programmes. But unlike Kuhn he doesn't make it irrational or purely sociological — he gives objective criteria, so a programme can be shown to be degenerate. Also, the hard core is unfalsifiable in principle, since it's more like a definitional assumption than an empirical one, so no experiment could ever touch it. And once a programme is falsified, it gets abandoned and replaced by a better one."
