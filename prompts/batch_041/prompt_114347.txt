I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a charter. The island I have invented governs itself by a single written document which contains, among its articles, the whole of the procedure by which it may be amended; there is no legislature, court or convention standing outside it whose authority the document acknowledges. Three of its features matter to my plot. First, it is layered: some articles are entrenched and can be altered only by a two-thirds majority of a special convention summoned for that purpose, while the rest can be altered by a simple majority of the ordinary assembly. Second, the article which sets out this very distinction is, by its own terms, amendable in the ordinary way. Third, the document is silent about the convention that first adopted it; my characters simply inherit it.

My plot requires a faction to use the ordinary amendment procedure to rewrite the amendment article itself, so that thereafter it can be changed only by the unanimous consent of every adult on the island, and then to use that new threshold, or rather the impossibility of ever meeting it, to freeze the settlement in place for good. Three of my characters resist, and I cannot tell which of them is talking sense. One says that no charter can authorise its own destruction, because the power to amend is held in trust for the community and cannot be used to put it beyond the community's reach. Another says the whole document is circular from its first page, since the clause which licences amendment is licensed only by itself. The third says the manoeuvre is perfectly valid, but that what results is no longer the same charter, so that my island has, without noticing, acquired a new constitution.

What I want from you is not a reading list but an argument. Reconstruct the theory of legal validity my charter's practice must presuppose if self-amendment is to be possible at all: must there be a rule or norm outside the document, and if so, in what sense does the document contain the whole of its own law-making procedure? Say which rival accounts of validity the practice genuinely rules out and which it merely sits comfortably with. Then take my three characters in turn and tell me which of them, if any, is right, and why, in particular whether a power can confer on itself the power to extinguish itself, and whether the identity of a legal order over time is carried by continuity of procedure or by continuity of content. Finally, tell me where my practice is underdetermined: which parts of the island's constitution I may leave vague without the law becoming incoherent, and which I must nail down on the page if the novel is not to cheat. Where you think a presupposition can be replaced by a rival, say what the island's law would have to look like afterwards.
