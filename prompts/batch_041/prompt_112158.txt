I sit on a small working group that is drafting the decision procedure for an internal research-ethics board at a mid-sized foundation, and I have come to suspect we are badly reinventing a wheel that already has a name. The board must rule on cases that decompose into several separable sub-questions -- whether a study's consent process was adequate, whether risks to participants were minimized, whether the prospective benefits justify those risks -- and must then issue a single verdict (approve, approve with conditions, or reject) that is supposed to follow from its answers to those sub-questions. We began with straightforward majority voting on each sub-question and reading the verdict off the results, and we promptly ran into the pattern in which the board majority endorses every premise and yet that same majority rejects the conclusion; half the group insists this is an artifact to be engineered away, and the other half suspects it is telling us something true about group judgment. We are now weighing three departures from the textbook setup: letting members register graded confidence rather than yes/no, permitting abstention on sub-questions a member feels unequipped to judge, and weighting votes by demonstrated competence. My worry is that we will spend a year on the wrong questions.

What I want is not a survey or a reading list but a prioritized agenda for the theory of judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma: which open questions in that area most deserve attention, given that our actual aim is a procedure that is both defensible in principle and workable in practice for a real, somewhat weary board? Please rank the top several and defend the ordering. For each, I want to know why it is upstream of the others -- what further questions or design choices hang on how it comes out -- how tractable it is for people with philosophical and formal-methods training rather than, say, a statistics lab, and whether it is genuinely unresolved or effectively settled in a way we would be foolish to keep relitigating. Pay special attention to whether the ranking shifts once you relax the classical assumptions in the ways we are contemplating -- graded rather than binary judgments, sanctioned abstention, competence weighting -- and tell me which of those three relaxations, if any, is most likely to be a rabbit hole that consumes our attention without improving the procedure. I would also value the reverse exercise: name the questions in this area that look central from the inside but would barely change a procedure like ours, and explain why we can safely set them aside.

Please write in American English and keep the prose crisp. Close with a single final line of exactly the form \answer{...}, containing only a short phrase naming the one question you would put first.
