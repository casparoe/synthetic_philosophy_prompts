A couple of us in a reading group have been going around in circles on an argument one of us wrote up about goal misgeneralization. I think it proves too much but I can't put my finger on where it cheats, so I want you to tell me. Here it is as he wrote it down:

(1) What a trained model does on any input is fixed entirely by its weights.
(2) For any weights that give good behavior on the inputs we actually test, there is some goal the model could have that fits those weights and that comes apart from what we want on inputs we don't test.
(3) We can only ever observe a model on finitely many inputs.
(4) So for any finite pile of behavioral evidence, there is at least one misaligned goal that predicts that evidence exactly.
(5) Behavioral evidence is all we ever have to go on.
(6) Two hypotheses that predict exactly the same evidence can't be told apart by that evidence.
(7) So no amount of testing can ever raise our confidence that a model is aligned, over some misaligned alternative that fits the same data.
(8) So we can never be justified in trusting that a model is aligned, and inner alignment is unsolvable in principle.

He says this is the deep reason alignment is hard. But the same moves seem to show I can't be justified in trusting my flatmate, or in expecting the sun to rise, since for any finite record of past behavior there's some gerrymandered goal that fits it and then goes off the rails. Which makes me think the whole thing is really just the problem of induction wearing a lab coat and that (8) is doing more work than the premises pay for. One of us wants to keep (1)-(4) and drop (5), but I don't see how you trust anything at all without (5). Another blames (2).
