I have been arguing about the Allais paradox, on and off, for years, and the argument has stopped moving; I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of it. One side treats the paradox as a demonstration that the independence axiom is not a requirement of practical rationality: that preferring the certain moderate prize to a gamble with a slightly higher expected value, when the two options share a common consequence in the relevant states, is intelligible and perhaps defensible, so that expected utility theory is at best a descriptive idealization of choice rather than a norm binding on it. The other side holds that independence is constitutive of coherent preference: that once the common outcome really is common — once the two prospects are compared state by state under the same description — the Allais preference is simply a mistake, and needs no defense beyond the sure-thing principle. Neither side is ignorant of the other's arguments; every move has been made and answered, and yet nobody converts.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? My working hypothesis is that it is two questions wearing one name: whether independence is descriptively true of how people in fact choose, and whether it is normatively binding on how they should choose, with the further possibility that each side is quietly answering the question it did not intend. But I do not want that hypothesis simply confirmed; I want it tested against what each side actually says, and rejected if it fails. Consider, in particular, whether the two standard formulations — the common-consequence version and the common-ratio version — are treated alike by both sides, or whether one camp needs them to come apart while the other needs them to be the same problem. Consider also the sequential argument, in which independence is defended by the thought that a choice made in ignorance of the resolution of chance must match the choice one would make after learning it, and ask whether that argument presupposes a consequentialist or isolation assumption that the critics are entitled to reject. Engage the substance of the moves themselves — the sure-thing principle, regret, the ex-ante/ex-post distinction, the appeal to money-pump-style coherence — and not a survey of who said what.

Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change in the question — or an argument that none should be expected, and why.
