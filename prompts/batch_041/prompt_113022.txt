After a career in experimental condensed-matter physics I retired, took up metaphysics, and next month I am giving my first paper at a small workshop on grounding and fundamentality. The thesis I am defending is that fundamentality is not an absolute status but an indexed one: a claim that something is fundamental is always relative to a descriptive resolution, much as a claim that a particle is elementary is relative to an energy scale. My route to it is an analogy with the cutoff in effective field theory -- coarse-grain the description and the fundamental entities change, with no description-independent fact about which level is the fundamental one -- together with the further claims that grounding is an explanatory notion and explanation is interest-relative, so the demand for an absolute fundamental level is unmotivated, and that the usual formal constraints on grounding (irreflexivity, asymmetry, transitivity) are artifacts of modelling grounding on physical dependence, with transitivity in particular to be given up.

The room holds about fifteen analytic metaphysicians. Most are grounding realists who treat grounding as a single unified explanatory relation; two work on essence-based accounts, on which grounding claims are explained by the natures of the things involved; one or two think the whole apparatus is a façon de parler; and there is a philosopher of physics who will know effective field theory better than I do. Please prepare my question-period sheet: exactly five numbered items and nothing else, no preamble and no closing paragraph, ordered by how much damage each would do to my position. Each item should be a sentence or two, giving the objection in the words that member of the audience would actually use, then my reply, then a clause noting where the reply is thinnest. The objection I am most hoping nobody raises must be one of the five, and flagged as such.
