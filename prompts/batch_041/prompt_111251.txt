I sit on the restitution commitee of a mid-sized city museum here in England, and I have to bring a written opinion to Thursday's meeting, so I'd like your help drafting it.

Background, quickly. We hold 38 ancestral human remains plus some associated grave goods, dug up in the 1890s by a colonial officer and handed to the museum in 1902. Our standing policy says we return human remains to "the community of origin, where that community can be identified". That phrase has never been defined and we've always read it loosely. Now we have two claimants. One is a community still living in the region the objects came from; it has state backing and has built a reburial site. The other is a diaspora community, descended (it says) from the same group but displaced a couple of generations later; it argues that territorial descent is exactly the wrong test, because the displacement is part of the same colonial story, and that it should count as the community of origin on grounds of continuous practice rather than continuous residence. Both have genealogical claims and neither is obviously the stronger. There's a third wrinkle: our ancient-DNA person wants us to retain a small bone sample from four of the individuals, arguing it's minimally destructive and that future techniques will let descendant communities answer their own questions, so refusing on their behalf is paternalistic. The claimants say it's just another extraction.

Also, we've been sloppy with language internally: we use 'restitution' and 'repatriation' as if they're the same thing, and I suspect that's part of why we keep going round in circles.

What I want from you is the memo itself: a clear recommendation I can put to the vote, with a timeframe, and an honest account of the strongest case against whatever you recommend — I dont want a memo that pretends the losing side has nothing. Please also say whether the policy's "community of origin" should be read genealogically, territorially, or as continuity of practice, because the whole thing hinges on that and we've never settled it. And if you think the museum should keep some kind of long-term interest in the remains after they go back — a loan arrangement, a covenant, a shared guardianship, whatever — say so and say why, but don't just default to it.

After the memo, give me a short summary I can paste into the minutes as a table with these columns: Decision point | Recommendation | Confidence (High/Medium/Low) | Main reason | Strongest objection and how the memo meets it. The rows should cover at least which claimant gets the remains, the DNA sample, the timeframe, and any continuing relationship. Keep the memo itself to about a page and a half.
