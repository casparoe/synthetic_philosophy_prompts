I am a novelist, some way into a historical novel set in early sixteenth-century Italy, and one of my principal characters is a Florentine secretary who fancies himself a disciple of Machiavelli. I would rather cut the character than have him pronounce a doctrine that no careful reader of Machiavelli would recognize, so I want to get the account of fortune and virtù right before I commit it to the page. To that end I have been leaning on a popular history of the Renaissance, and its summary of Machiavelli runs roughly as follows:

'Machiavelli's great innovation was to cut politics loose from morality. For him the only virtues that count are the ones that get results: virtù simply names whatever qualities allow a prince to hold on to his state, and since fortune — blind luck — governs most of what befalls us, the prudent prince takes what he can when the wind happens to blow his way. Machiavelli is at bottom a fatalist; he thinks our fates are settled for us in advance, and the only freedom he leaves us is the freedom to snatch the occasion when it arrives.'

I have my suspicions about this, but not enough knowledge to act on them, and the book is by someone who seems to know the period.

I would like you to act as a scholar of Renaissance political thought — someone who has spent years with Machiavelli's texts and their commentators — and give me an accuracy audit I can actually use. Before you begin the audit proper, please think the matter through from first principles: set out for yourself, as carefully as you can, what you take Machiavelli's central claims about fortune and virtù to be, how the two concepts relate, and where the genuine interpretive disputes lie. Then work through the summary above sentence by sentence, and for each claim tell me three things: what a careful reader would accept as substantially correct, what would make such a reader wince (a simplification that survives only if you never press on it), and what is straightforwardly wrong. Where the summary conflates two things that Machiavelli keeps apart, name both and say why the distinction matters. Where it treats a modest thesis as a sweeping one, or reads a rhetorical figure as a literal doctrine, say so.

Two things matter especially to me. First, the term virtù: I need to know whether the summary's instrumental reading of it — whatever gets results — captures the concept, or whether it flattens something the Italian carries and the English word virtue obscures. Second, the closing move: the book infers from all this that Machiavelli is a fatalist whose only residual freedom is opportunism. Does that conclusion follow from anything true in the summary, or is it a misreading of the role fortune plays? Finally, tell me where competent interpreters actually disagree, so that I can have my character hold one defensible view rather than a caricature — and if the honest answer is that parts of this are contested rather than settled, say that too. Please write for a serious general reader, not for a seminar.
