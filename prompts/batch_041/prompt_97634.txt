Here's the exam question (Q3 from the midterm):

"The course distinguishes two roles a community's memory of an event can play. Its evidential role: the memory is put forward as a source of information about what actually happened. Its constitutive role: the memory is part of what makes the group the group it is, whether or not the events went as remembered. The course also introduced two rival theses. The asymmetry thesis says that in a conflict between living memory and the archival record, the record should correct the memory on questions of what happened, while the memory should correct the record on questions of what it meant and who counts as having taken part. The flat thesis says there's no such asymmetry — the same standards of warrant apply across the board, to factual and interpretive claims alike. Using the harbour case we discussed in week 5, say which thesis you'd apply and defend it against the other. Then address the identity-stake objection: if a memory is constitutive of a group's self-understanding, does that shift the evidential burden at all?"

And here's the student's answer (I've typed it as written, minus the margin doodles):

"I'd go with the asymmetry thesis, though I think it needs one patch. The flat thesis is basically the view that because we can't get outside our own perspective, meaning claims are just as much up for grabs as factual ones, so there's no principled reason to let the archive win on facts while letting the community win on meaning. But that can't be right, because the archive isn't a neutral machine — it was built by the same people who wrote the plaque in the first place, so if we let it adjudicate the meaning side too we've just handed the whole dispute to one party.

The asymmetry thesis gets the harbour case right. The fishermen's descendants remember forty people pulled from the water; the shipping records say eleven, and the naval log says the destroyer had most of them off before any local boat arrived. Fine — the record wins on the number. That doesn't touch what the memory is doing, which is telling the town who it is: the kind of place where people go out in bad weather. That's a constitutive claim, and you can't falsify it with a manifest, because it was never a claim about a manifest in the first place.

Where I'd patch it: once we've said the memory is constitutive, the identity-stake objection mostly dissolves, because a constitutive memory is one the group is entitled to keep asserting. If it's part of what makes you you, correcting it isn't a factual repair, it's epistemic violence — you'd be asking the community to stop being itself. So the evidential burden doesn't just shift, it comes off entirely for the constitutive part. I'd add that the asymmetry thesis should therefore be stated as: record wins on facts, memory wins on meaning, and there's no third category where they compete. The flat thesis fails because it can't explain why the town's memory has survived a hundred years of contrary evidence, which is itself a kind of warrant."
