Our school's philosophy society runs an end-of-year symposium on a format I have come to dread: every speaker files a written statement of position two weeks in advance, and the respondent's only job is to find the joint at which the statement comes apart. I have drawn the question of whether better than is transitive, that is, whether if A is better than B and B is better than C, A must be better than C. I would rather locate the weak joint myself, in private, than have it located for me in the hall.

Here is what I would file.

1. Transitivity. All-things-considered betterness is transitive, with no exceptions. My reason is the money pump: if my rankings could run in a circle, someone could take my money one step at a time and hand me back exactly what I began with, and any account of value that leaves me open to that has already lost.

2. Gaps. I also hold that some pairs of lives, and some pairs of worlds, are simply not comparable: neither better nor worse nor exactly as good. I do not think this embarrasses the first claim. Ancestry is transitive and still leaves most pairs unrelated; I see no reason value should be better behaved than ancestry.

3. The robustness rule. If one world is better than another, then making a single person in the better world somewhat worse off, leaving everyone else in both worlds exactly as they were, cannot reverse the verdict. One life is not heavy enough to tip a comparison between worlds.

4. No higher goods. A life goes better the more pleasure and the less pain it contains, and that is the whole of it. I do not believe in a separate class of higher value standing above pleasure and pain, and I distrust any view that has to appeal to one.

5. The step rule. Adding a person whose life is worth living to a world, when nobody already in that world is made worse off, makes the world better. Perhaps only slightly, but better; it seems to me a kind of miserliness to say otherwise.

6. Quality over quantity. A world of a few billion people living richly is better, and better by a great deal, than a world of vastly more people whose lives are only just worth living; and no increase in that second number, however vast, changes my mind. This is the commitment I am least willing to trade away.

7. The ladder, and a verdict I cannot drop. Picture the two worlds joined by rungs. Each rung adds some people and takes a little away from everyone already on it, and no rung pushes anyone below the point at which life stops being worth living. Asked to compare any rung with the one beneath it, I say the higher rung is better: there are more lives worth living, and the small losses look bearable beside them. Yet at the top I am certain the world is far worse than the world at the bottom. The same shape recurs inside a single life: offered a long series of small trades, a little more money for a little more discomfort, I would accept each trade, and I am equally certain that the state at the end of the series is not better for me than the state at the beginning.

I want an audit, not a lecture. Which of these seven actually pull against one another, and which merely appear to? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say exactly what would have to give and what that surrender would cost me elsewhere; be concrete about which numbered commitment is doing the damage. I would rather pay a small price now than defend an incoherent statement in front of the society. If a single revision would repair the most, tell me which and why. If some of the friction is an artifact of how I have worded things rather than a real conflict between the ideas, say so plainly, since a badly worded credo is a cheaper problem than a broken one. And where the answer is that I must abandon one of these, show me the shape of what remains rather than handing me the surrender.
