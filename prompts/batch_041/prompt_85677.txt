I run the graphics desk at a mid-size public health nonprofit, and my boss just asked me to write our house style guide for charts and infographics. He keeps bringing up Otto Neurath's picture language — the Vienna method, all those repeated little stick figures — and wants us to adopt its rules across the board: one symbol always stands for the same fixed quantity, you add more symbols rather than stretching one, no perspective, no decoration, and the whole thing should be readable by someone with zero training. Trouble is our data won't cooperate. Half our numbers are rates, some are averages, some pairs sit so close together that a 4% gap vanishes once you're counting whole little people, and we're often plotting uncertainty. Can you act as a data-viz person who also knows the history and philosophy of statistics, think carefully, and brainstorm the principles actually at stake before you write anything? Then give me a seperate decision procedure my designers can run on any chart: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a real answer to each, which Neurath-style rule applies when, where the procedure has to hand the call back to a human, and where his insistence on plain clarity is quietly making an argument rather than being neutral. Run it on two of our actual cases — a county-by-county vaccination map, and a bar chart of average clinic wait times we want to use to argue for more funding — and if one of those comes out against what my boss wants, say so out loud. Finally, tell me what the procedure just can't settle.
