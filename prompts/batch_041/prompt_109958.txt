I retired two years ago after thirty-odd years as a hydrologist for a state water agency, and I've been trying to make up for a lifetime of not reading philosophy. I'm in a reading group that meets above a bike shop, and for the last few months we've been chewing on the puzzle where you have to choose between one person getting tortured for decades and some gigantic number of people each getting a momentary speck of dust in the eye. Here's what I've noticed and what I want from you. In the stock version, the two camps line up the way you'd expect: the people who say numbers are all that matters end up choosing the specks, because enough trivial harms supposedly outweigh one catastrophic one; the people who say there's a floor, that a moment of dust can never add up to a person being destroyed, end up choosing the torture. I want you to construct a case where those two camps swap sides — where the aggregationist is forced to pick the single severe harm, and the anti-aggregationist is forced to pick the option with the huge number of trivial harms. The usual result inverted. I'm asking because I want to know whether these two positions are actually tracking something, or whether everybody's verdict is just a slogan they repeat until the numbers get big.

Constraints, and I care about these. Keep it realistic — a hospital, a city council, an insurer, a workplace, a drug trial; no brains in vats, no demons, no sci-fi. Use numbers I can picture and say out loud (millions, billions, fine; whatever "3^^^3" is, not fine). Hold the magnitudes and the count fixed and identical between the two options, so the swap has to come from some single structural feature of the case, and tell me what that feature is. No ad hoc psychologies invented purely to make it work, no infinite populations, and don't lean on the intended-versus-foreseen distinction unless it's genuinely doing work you can point at. And please don't just hand me a story in which I'm supposed to accept that the camps flip — the flip has to fall out of how each side reasons, or it's worthless to me.

Then walk me through it. What each camp says and why, which feature is load-bearing, what happens to both verdicts when I delete that feature (does everything snap back to the usual result?), and the strongest case a defender of each side would make that the scenario is rigged or is secretly answering a different question. Tell me how confident you are that the feature you've named really is the one doing the work, and flag anywhere you're guessing. If it turns out the swap can't be built without quietly changing the subject — if the reversal only works because one option stops being about trivial harms, or because the severe harm has been defanged into something not so severe — then say that straight out rather than humoring me. I'd much rather get "here's why your ask is confused" than a clever case that falls apart the first time someone looks at it seperate from the rhetoric.
