I'm a science journalist — I write a monthly "ideas" column — and I'm working up a piece on a fight in metaphysics I've been following for weeks, two philosophers who've been emailing each other about whether any two things can be exactly alike. One of them says no: if two things are numerically distinct, there has to be something that tells them apart. The other says that's just wrong, and offers a little thought experiment — two spheres, identical in every way, sitting in an otherwise empty universe, arranged so the whole thing is perfectly symmetric and there's no fact of the matter about which sphere is which. They've been going back and forth alot and honestly they're talking past each other, so before I file I want the document they should have written together instead.

Draft me the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two — call them the indiscernibility defender and the sphere-skeptic. I want:

- the claims they can both sign: how the scenario is set up, what "indiscernible" is supposed to mean, and which version of the principle is actually on the table (is it meant to be a necessary truth? about monadic properties only, or all properties?);
- the precise disagreements, each worded so that its own side would accept the phrasing — neither of them should be able to read it and say "that's a straw man";
- the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would force a side to move. My hunch is one is whether relational facts — like each sphere being some fixed distance from the other — count as properties that distinguish them, and another is whether such a world is merely conceivable or genuinely possible. But you tell me.
- and for each crux, the argument or case both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out written down in advance.

Two things I keep wondering about, so fold them in if they belong: a physicist friend insists this is definately the same problem as the hole argument in general relativity, and someone else said identical particles in quantum mechanics make it concrete. If either is a real crux, say so; if it's a red herring, say that. And if part of the fight turns out to be merely verbal — a dispute about what counts as a "property" — flag which part.

Format: a numbered list of agreed claims, then a disagreements table. Keep it tight. And end with a last line in exactly this form, no extra words: \crux{short phrase naming the single deepest crux}.
