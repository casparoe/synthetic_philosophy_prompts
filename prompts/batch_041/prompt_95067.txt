Last night a friend and I argued for hours about relational conceptions of personhood — the family of views, often labelled Ubuntu, on which one becomes a person through other persons — and I ended up defending a stronger version of the view than I actually hold. My position as I stated it: being a person is not an intrinsic status that relationships then express, but is constituted by standing in relations of recognition and mutual obligation, so that someone held outside every such web is not merely unlucky but not fully a person. Map that position's exposure for me: the cases, arguments, and results that count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a clear line between what a defender would concede and what they would dismiss as question-begging. Three pressure points matter most. First, the community that systematically withholds recognition from some of its members: if recognition constitutes personhood, is the theory committed to their being lesser persons, and if it refuses to say so, has it quietly reintroduced an intrinsic criterion? Second, does the constitutive claim say anything beyond the uncontroversial thought that social conditions shape us, and what would show that it does? Third, can the view make sense of a community wronging one of its own, given that on its own account the community is what confers the status in the first place? I am also unsure whether it needs a threshold reading — relations as one of the conditions that get you over the line into personhood — or a degree reading, on which relations fix how much personhood you have; say which one it can afford and what each costs. If the position turns out to be insulated against every defeater, say so, and say whether that is a problem. No citations needed; I will find those myself.
