I'm writing an essay for a course on the philosophy of convention, and I've got stuck on jaywalking. The thing started as an argument with a friend who grew up in a country with no jaywalking laws at all, and who was genuinely baffled to be shouted at by a stranger for crossing mid-block in Chicago; what I want to know is whether crossing the street can ever generate the sort of obligation that queueing and tipping seem to generate, or whether it is only ever prudence dressed up as duty. Since you have no way of looking any of this up, take the following as given. The word 'jay' was once a mild insult for a naive person or a rustic. Before the motor car, the street was treated as shared space. In the 1920s, in American cities, motor clubs, dealers and manufacturers ran a deliberate and well-funded campaign to redefine the street as a place for cars, recasting the pedestrian who crossed mid-block as a trespasser and a fool; the first jaywalking ordinances date from that period, and within a decade or so the pedestrian had been pushed to the margins of the roadway. I am not asking you to litigate the history. I want the case, and I want the analysis to run in both directions, from the theory to the case and back again.

The tools I would like you to use are these. On the Lewis-style account of convention, a convention is an arbitrary regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem: each of us conforms because we expect the others to, we would have been equally content with a different regularity had everyone settled on that one instead, and the pressure it exerts is conditional, evaporating as soon as the expectation does. On Bicchieri's account, as I understand it, the crucial line falls between regularities sustained only by empirical expectations, that is, beliefs about what others in fact do, and social norms sustained by normative expectations, that is, beliefs that others think one ought to conform and are disposed to resent deviation; which is why a norm's grip does not depend on the coordination payoff and can persist even when compliance is costly and unobserved. And on Hart's older point, a rule exists in a community only where people take the internal point of view towards it, treating it as a standard for criticising themselves and others rather than merely as a prediction about how others will behave. Bring all three to bear on the following questions, in order. First, were the jaywalking ordinances ever conventions in the Lewis sense, or were they the imposition of a regularity on people who had no reason to want it — and if the latter, does the fact that the practice served the interests of one party rather than solving a problem for everyone tell against its being binding at all? Second, what did the ordinances actually do to the people they addressed: create an obligation, create a legal duty with no moral force behind it, or merely raise the price of an act that was already foolish once traffic moved fast? Third, the visitor: someone from a country where crossing mid-block is unremarkable, walking in an American city, is told to go to the corner. Say precisely what, if anything, they owe to that practice, and distinguish as carefully as you can between courtesy, fairness to those who are complying, the avoidance of real risk, and anything that looks like a genuine obligation arising simply from participation in a shared practice. Finally, tell me what the case does to the framework: whether it confirms the Lewis picture, on which the ordinances were never conventions at all and the puzzlement is ours, or exposes a gap in it, in the shape of a regularity that acquired normative force without ever having been a coordination equilibrium. Where the tools run out, say so rather than forcing them to speak, and please do not pretend that crossing mid-block carries the same weight on a quiet residential street at midnight and on a six-lane arterial at rush hour.

No citations, please; I want the argument, not a reading list. Write in British English, and finish with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' that gives your answer to whether the jaywalking ordinances created a genuine moral obligation for the pedestrians they addressed, expressed as a short label of your own choosing followed by no more than two sentences.
