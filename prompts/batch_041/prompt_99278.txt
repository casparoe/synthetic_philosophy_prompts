I have been arguing with a friend who keeps ending discussions of cosmology with the remark that the universe is fine-tuned for life. He treats this as settling the matter: the constants and initial conditions could not plausibly have been otherwise, or at least their being life-permitting demands explanation, and from there he moves quickly to a designer. I am not persuaded, but I also suspect we are talking past each other, because the phrase seems to do several different jobs in his mouth. Sometimes it sounds like a claim about the extreme sensitivity of life to the values of physical constants; sometimes like a claim about the improbability of those values under some probability measure; sometimes like a claim that an improbable life-permitting universe cries out for explanation; and sometimes like a claim that the universe is arranged for a purpose. I want to know which of these, if any, the slogan can actually be made to mean, and which of them would do the work he needs.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, as a menu rather than a single interpretation. For each reading, say what it presupposes—for instance, a well-defined space of possible universes, a measure or prior over that space, a contrast class, a definition of life or of an observer, a distinction between tuning as a relational property and tuning as an intrinsic one—and then say whether the thesis is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Distinguish in particular between fine-tuning for life, fine-tuning for observers, and fine-tuning for anything at all, and between a claim about likelihood, a claim about posterior probability, and a claim about explanatory need. I would also like you to address the measure problem and the reference-class problem, and to say where the slogan slides between an epistemic reading (the constants look improbable to us) and a metaphysical one (the constants really are improbable or really are selected). Which of these readings would actually support an inference to a designer, which would instead support a multiverse or observer selection, and which would make the slogan a platitude? Finally, tell me which reading my friend most likely intends when he uses it to end an argument, and which reading would be the most defensible if he wants to keep using it. I am not asking for a verdict on whether the fine-tuning argument succeeds; I want the slogan’s possible meanings separated, with a verdict on each, so that I can bring the menu back to the argument and see where we actually disagree.
