I'm a novelist, about a year into a historical novel, and I've managed to argue myself into a corner. The book is about a woman who spent her whole life as a servant in a big country house — and it's built out of what I can actually find: parish registers, household accounts, a handful of letters, and an ocean of nothing. Months in the archive have done something to my head, and I've ended up with an argument I can't shake and can't stand. I've cleaned it up so it looks respectable:

(1) Any claim about the past has to rest on surviving sources.
(2) Sources don't just appear. Somebody had the means, the literacy, and the motive to make them and to keep them — and that somebody is almost always whoever held the power: the estate, the church, the state, the man of the house.
(3) So the archive is not a neutral record of what happened; it's a record of what the powerful thought worth writing down.
(4) If the archive only holds what the powerful wrote, then the people without power — my servant, her mother, the field hands — left no record of their own.
(5) Where there's no record, there's nothing to know.
(6) So there's nothing to be known about the inner lives or the suffering of the powerless. Anything I write about her is my invention, not history.
(7) And by the same reasoning I can't even know that she was silenced, or that she suffered in any particular way, since that's also a claim about her and there's no record of it.
(8) So the honest thing is to admit the archive is silent about her and shut up.

I can feel that something's off — (7) seems to cancel (4) (if I can't know she was silenced, how do I know she was silenced?), and if the whole thing were right I couldn't even be standing here making it, since I'd be claiming to know the archive is a record of power. But I don't want to just wave "self-refutation" at it and move on, because honestly I believe (2) and (3) down to the bone, and I do think the archive is shaped by power.

There's also a thought I can't get rid of and can't make do any work. A silence in the archive isn't just nothing — it's a shape. Like a missing tooth: the gap has edges, and you can sometimes feel teh outline of what isn't there. Maybe that's important. Maybe it's just me being precious about my own book.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd rather keep (2) and (3) than anything else on the list. And tell me straight whether the missing-tooth idea actually rescues (4) or only papers over it. I've worked up this arguement over months of reading rooms and bad coffee, so don't be gentle.
