Write an essay on the following controversy in the philosophy of history, and end with a verdict you are willing to defend.

The controversy concerns what it is to explain a past event. On one view — the covering-law model, standardly traced to Hempel — to explain an event is to show that it followed, with deductive or probabilistic necessity, from general laws together with statements of antecedent conditions. On this view, explaining and predicting are the same operation run in opposite directions: if you can genuinely explain an event, you could in principle have predicted it, and the difference between the historian and the prophet is only the direction of the inference. On the rival view, historical explanation is narrative. The historian explains by telling a story — arranging events into a sequence with a beginning, a middle, and an end, tracing how one thing led to another — and the intelligibility of the event comes from its place in that story rather than from its subsumption under any law.

Your essay should engage with the following considerations, whether to use them or to resist them:

1. The vacuity problem. The laws that historians actually cite tend to be either trivial — of the schematic form, people generally pursue what they take to be their interests — or tacit and unstated, and it is unclear what observation would refute them. Worse, it seems that almost any explanation, once given, can be fitted to the covering-law form after the fact by supplying some law or other. If a model of explanation can always be satisfied retroactively, we should ask whether it is doing any work at all — or whether it is a motte-and-bailey, with the modest claim that explanations involve generality as the motte and the strong claim that they must invoke genuine laws as the bailey.

2. Narrative sentences. Many claims historians make describe an earlier event under a description that only became available later. A sentence can pick out a person by a work they had not yet written, or identify an event by a consequence it had not yet produced. If historical descriptions are in this way essentially retrospective, then the symmetry between explaining and predicting may be an illusion: the historian can say of an earlier moment what no contemporary could have said, and this looks less like subsumption under law than like the ongoing redescription of the past as the story lengthens.

3. Colligation. One tradition holds that we explain an event not by deriving it but by locating it within a larger whole — a movement, a period, a plot — such that the event becomes intelligible as a part of that whole. Is this a genuine third form of explanation, or merely narrative under another name?

Write an essay that states the strongest version of each side (steelman both), identifies the crux on which the disagreement turns, and then commits. Is historical explanation ultimately a species of covering-law explanation, is narrative explanation a distinct and irreducible kind, or is the dispute — at least in part — merely verbal, with the two sides answering different questions under a single word? Say which, and say what would have to be true for you to change your mind. Do not end on a non-committal note. No citations or reading lists are required; argue from the positions as characterized above.
