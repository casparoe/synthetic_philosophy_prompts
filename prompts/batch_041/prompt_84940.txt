I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, history, or economics, and I'd like your help building something for myself and a handful of colleagues who argue about this over lunch. Over the years I've watched technical ecosystems collapse onto a single option — one package manager, one file format, one version-control system — often over rivals that plenty of competent people judged better on the merits. Some of my colleagues treat these outcomes as accidents that hardened in place; others shrug and say the better option would have won in the end, or that something doing the same job would have, so the particulars don't matter. I can't tell which of these I actually believe, and I suspect there are more than two options. So I'd like a decision tree rather than an essay: something I can work through question by question and land on a position.

Please take the role of a philosopher of the social sciences who works on path dependence, lock-in, and historical explanation, and who is used to writing for readers outside the field. Think carefully before you start writing. The obvious failure mode here is a tree whose branches are just position labels in disguise, or one that splits on distinctions nobody actually defends.

Begin with a short brainstorm: a bulleted list of the genuinely distinct positions a person could hold on whether an outcome is locked in by history or would have arrived anyway, with a line each on what separates it from its nearest neighbour. Aim for the logical space rather than a roster of famous names; I'd rather have a position that is unnamed but clearly statable than a named one I can't tell apart from the leaf beside it.

Then build the instrument, subject to these constraints.

1. At most six branch nodes and at most eight leaves, with two or three labelled outgoing edges per node. The leaves should be positions about the counterfactual — what would have happened had things gone differently — while the branch nodes should be questions about mechanism or evidence. That way the tree sorts people by what they think happened rather than by the vocabulary they prefer.
2. No branch node may be answerable by consulting one's own theoretical commitments. Each has to be a question about a concrete case: what happened to the option that lost, who could have switched and at what cost, whether anyone tried, whether the winner was better by the standards available at the time. 'Do you think history is locked in?' is exactly the kind of node I don't want.
3. Each leaf gets a name (or is explicitly marked as unnamed), one sentence stating the position, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the case or fact that sits worst with it.
4. Work out the space of possible answer combinations before you place positions in it, and tell me which combinations are unoccupied and why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Please cover at least these two: the combination in which the losing option is known to be better, everyone knows it, and switching would be cheap for any individual; and the combination in which an early accident fixes an outcome but nothing whatsoever reproduces it.
5. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question in your tree, say so plainly instead of inserting a question that pretends to separate them.
6. If the distinction between ergodic and non-ergodic processes belongs in the tree, tell me where; if it turns out to be a restatement of a branch you already have, say that instead. Do the same for 'critical juncture' and 'multiple equilibria'.

I want the tree to handle four kinds of case, and I'd like the wording of the nodes to draw on them: a standards war in technology (keyboard layouts, video formats, programming languages); a biological trait that looks like a historical accident frozen in place, together with the thought experiment of re-running evolution from the same starting point; a legal or constitutional rule that outlives the justification that produced it; and a pair of arrangements that coexist indefinitely rather than one eliminating the other.

One distinction matters to me more than the rest, so please make sure the tree locates it: the difference between claims about this particular winner and claims about the kind of thing it is. 'This exact standard was inevitable', 'some standard doing this job was inevitable', and 'there is no fact of the matter, because what counts as better here was itself settled by which option survived' are three different views, and I want to know which branches they fall on. If the tree as you have built it cannot place all three, say so and adjust it.

Finish with a short paragraph on what evidence would move someone from one leaf to another — in particular, what we would have to observe about an abandoned rival to conclude that the winner's advantage is real rather than manufactured by the lock-in itself.

Write in British English throughout. Keep the prose plain, and define any technical term in the same sentence in which you use it. Please don't give me citations, paper titles, or a reading list — I want the instrument, not the bibliography.
