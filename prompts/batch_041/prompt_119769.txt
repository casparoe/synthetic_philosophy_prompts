For a long time I have been turning over an argument that never seems to get anywhere, and I would like to force it onto paper in a form that both sides would have to sign. What I want is the document the two sides could jointly put their names to — not a summary of the debate, not a verdict, but the shared ground and the exact remaining differences.

The argument concerns hypocrisy and the right to criticize. On one view — call it the forfeiture view — if you criticize someone for something you are yourself guilty of, you have forfeited any claim to be answered. The person you are criticizing may set your criticism aside without addressing it; your disqualification settles the matter before the merits are even reached. On the other view — the merits-only view — this is a confusion. Whether a criticism lands depends only on whether the person really did the thing and whether it really was wrong. Your own record may make you a hypocrite, a fraud, or a fool, but it cannot make the criticism false, and it cannot release the other person from answering it. Each side thinks the other is missing something so obvious that it is barely worth stating.

Please produce the following, in ordinary language. I have no background in this subject and I do not want the names of theories, nor any reading list; I want the argument itself.

1. A numbered list of the claims both sides can accept: about what criticism is, what it is for, what one is doing when one criticizes another person, and what, if anything, the critic's own conduct has to do with any of it. Include the points on which the two are closer than they suppose.

2. A table of the disagreements that remain. For each, give a neutral statement of the question, then the forfeiture view's answer and the merits-only view's answer, each worded so that its own side would accept the wording — nothing caricatured, nothing that works only on the other side's assumptions.

3. The cruxes: the smaller questions such that settling one of them would actually move one side or the other. I have guesses about some of them — whether the critic's standing is a condition on the act of criticizing or only on whether you should respect the critic; whether it comes in degrees or is all or nothing; whether it can be lost and later regained; and whether the disqualifying fault must be the very same act or may be merely the same sort of person. But I would rather you tell me what the real cruxes are.

4. For each crux, one concrete case or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance.

5. If some part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal — the two sides meaning different things by words such as right, may, can, or standing — say which part, and why.

Keep it plain. I want the argument, not the literature.
