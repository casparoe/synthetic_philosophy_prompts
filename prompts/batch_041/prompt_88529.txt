I've been trying to work out what my country's courts are actually committed to when they decide what a law means. When the words are disputed they reach for a dictionary, sometimes a very old one, and treat what it says as settling the question, insisting all the while that this is reading the law rather than making it. But the same courts will hear evidence about how a particular trade uses a word, which makes a word's meaning depend on facts about how people actually behave; and they will enforce a contract clause that stipulates that for the purposes of this agreement delivery means something narrower than it does in ordinary speech, so that a meaning is fixed by the parties' say-so and conclusions then follow from it alone. Dictionaries seem to sit on both sides of this. They are compiled by reading how people write, so they look like reports of fact, but when one is cited in court it works as a rule; and when an entry changes we say the word changed rather than that the dictionary had been wrong, even though we also think a dictionary can be wrong.

I've read that Quine attacked the distinction between truths that hold by meaning alone and truths that hold by the way the world is. What does the courts' practice presuppose about that distinction? Does it need it, or could someone who accepts Quine's attack describe the whole thing as a convention for coordinating what people will accept, with no facts about meaning underneath? Tell me which parts of the practice strictly require the distinction, which merely fit comfortably with it, and where the practice is indifferent between the two views. And if the Quinean is right, what would have to give: would judges have to start citing surveys of usage instead of dictionaries, and would the line between interpreting a law and rewriting it survive at all? Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
