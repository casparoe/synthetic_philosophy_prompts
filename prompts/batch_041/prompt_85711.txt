I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have agreed to lead a reading-group session on satisficing and bounded rationality for colleagues who are similarly untrained. What I need is not an essay but a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree a person can walk down by answering questions about what they actually believe, arriving at the bottom at a position about what satisficing is. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases and commitments, not by which label they like, so no node may be phrased as a question about a named view (nothing like 'do you think agents are bounded maximizers?').

Build the tree from the logical space first, then locate actual positions in it. Every internal node should be a single question that genuinely separates the views below it, answerable by someone who does not know which position their answer commits them to, and where possible anchored in a decision that an engineer or a manager actually faces. At minimum, the following distinctions need to appear somewhere on the tree, though you may reorder or nest them as the logic requires:

- Whether the claim is descriptive (this is how search in fact goes), normative (this is how it ought to go), or about the limits of any possible normative theory.
- What fixes the stopping point: an externally given bar, an aspiration level that drifts with experience and comparison, or nothing antecedent at all, so that the stopping point is simply wherever the process happened to halt.
- Whether 'good enough' is an optimum in disguise: once the costs of searching (time, attention, computation, opportunity) are priced in, is stopping at the first acceptable option what a maximizer would already do, so that satisficing adds nothing to the theory? Or does the satisficer's threshold come apart from any such cost-benefit calculation?
- Whether the claim concerns how much of the option space is explored, or the standard used to accept an option, and a further split on whether the standard itself can be satisficed rather than only the options.
- Whether the limitation sits in the agent (finite computation) or in the world (open-ended option sets, unknowable bests).
- Whether a satisficing rule is defensible only relative to an environment, or as a general claim about rationality.
- A homonym check: the same word also names a view in ethics about whether it can be permissible to do less good than one could, and something in game theory. The tree must keep the practical-rationality sense separate from the ethical sense rather than letting them merge.

Required leaves, named in plain language where a familiar label exists and left unnamed but described in one clause where it does not: the cost-of-search reductionist view; the ecological or heuristic view, on which rules that ignore information can outperform optimizing and rationality is assessed relative to environments; the organizational view on which thresholds are endogenous and the real theory is about how they move; the view that satisficing is a distinct normative standard rather than a second-best stand-in; the deflationary view that the term covers several unrelated things and should be retired; and the ethical-permissibility sense as its own leaf.

Constraints on the deliverable: six to nine internal nodes and no more than a dozen leaves; a short scoring key so the thing can actually be used as a quiz, mapping answer patterns onto leaves; at least two pairs of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, flagged with a note on why the questions fail to pry them apart; a note on which combinations of answers the tree generates that nobody seems to occupy, and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment. If a position is conventionally associated with a well-known name, you may mention the name in passing, but nothing in the tree may depend on the reader recognising it. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list. Write it so a competent colleague with no philosophy background can follow it, using engineering examples (alert thresholds, stopping rules, when to ship, when to stop reading documentation) only where they track the philosophical distinction rather than merely illustrate it.
