I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and what I want is a diagnostic instrument rather than a reading list. Please build a decision tree that leads a reader from a starting commitment to a named position on whether beliefs and desires exist — the eliminative-materialist territory — under the following constraints.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a roster of names: work out first the questions that actually divide the positions, then locate the positions at the leaves. Every branch node must be phrased as a question the reader can answer without already knowing which label it leads to — no node of the form 'are you an eliminativist?'. Each leaf should carry the name of a position, or be marked 'unnamed' if you believe nobody defends it, together with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Aim for six to nine leaves in total.

Force the tree to separate at least the following, though you may add others: the view that belief-talk and desire-talk are false because the posited states do not exist; the view that such talk is not even truth-apt, so that it fails of truth rather than of reference; the view that the talk is truth-apt and largely true but that the states are not the inner causes folk psychology takes them to be; the instrumentalist or fictionalist view that the vocabulary is a useful predictive device owing nothing to a real posited kind; the revisionary-realist view that we are right about the existence of such states but wrong about their nature; and the quietist view that the dispute is verbal and dissolves once we see what the predicates are doing. Because the domain pairs beliefs with desires, the tree must be able to place someone who is eliminativist about one and not the other, and must say what does the separating.

Treat the following as load-bearing nodes, since the positions genuinely come apart on them and a reader can answer them cold: (a) the analogy with a superseded posit from a dead science, such as caloric or phlogiston; (b) whether a creature with no language and no concept of belief could nonetheless have beliefs; (c) whether your own occurrent judgment that you now believe something counts as evidence for the state it reports; (d) whether the predictive success of belief-desire explanation in everyday life is evidence for the posits or merely for the utility of the vocabulary; (e) whether 'believes' could turn out to have no extension while 'desires' still does; and (f) whether the eliminativist's own argument can be stated without invoking the very states it denies.

Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say whether the failure is a defect of your questions or a sign that the two positions differ only in emphasis. Note any combinations of answers that no one occupies, and diagnose each briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Be explicit about how confident you are in each placement, and single out the one placement you are least sure of and what would change your mind about it.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order:
- 'Branch questions' (~300 words): the diagnostic questions in order, each with the answers it allows.
- 'The tree' (~350 words): an indented outline mapping answer-paths to leaves.
- 'The leaves' (~400 words): each position and its most awkward commitment.
- 'Unoccupied and unreachable' (~150 words): the empty combinations and what each means.
- 'Confidence and least-sure point' (~150 words): your confidence in the classification as a whole, the placement you are least sure of, and what evidence would move it.
