ok so this is going to be a bit in the weeds but bear with me, because I've been chewing on this alot and I think I've talked myself into something that might be dumb.

It's about moral trade — the setup where two people with different moral views each come out ahead by doing something the other cares about more than they do, so they swap. The usual version treats it as a neat trick that's available whenever two people happen to disagree about priorities. I've talked myself into a much stronger version and I want you to try to break it.

The strong version, three parts: (1) genuine moral trade isn't a niche trick, it's the actual engine of most large-scale moral change — when you look at how norms actually shift, it's usually some group giving up something they cared about less in exchange for something they cared about more, not one side converting the other; (2) the reason we don't see way more of it isn't commensurability or trust or logistics, it's that people hold their moral views as constraints rather than as preferences over outcomes, and being willing to put a moral commitment on the table reads as betrayal, so mutually beneficial trades never get made; (3) the fix is basically a reframe — get people to see their moral commitments as very strong preferences instead of side-constraints and the trade becomes thinkable.

So what I want is the case against. Specifically, what should count against this, ranked by how decisive it'd actually be. I don't want the textbook objections to moral trade as such (I know the it-commodifies-morality line and the you-might-trade-into-something-terrible line). I want the ones that hit my version. What would have to be true of moral psychology for (2) to be wrong? Are there real cases where moral views held as hard constraints produced big change faster than any trade could, and if so does that sink (1)? Does the reframe in (3) have a self-defeat problem — does treating your commitment as a preference weaken the very thing you were trying to protect, or make you less trustworthy to trade with? And what does my version predict that the boring "moral trade is a sometimes-useful tool" view doesn't? If the answer is nothing, that's worth knowing too.

Be honest about whether I've cooked the thing so that nothing could count against it. If I have, say so, and then say whether that's fatal for a claim this broad or just the normal cost of a broad thesis. And split the defeaters into the ones I'd have to concede if I'm being honest versus the ones a defender can shrug off — I'd rather know which ones are load-bearing than get a list. For what it's worth, what would move me most is a concrete case where constraint-thinking beat trade-thinking at scale, or a demonstration that the reframe is self-undermining; if neither of those exists I'd recieve that as decent evidence for the view, so tell me if that inference is itself too cheap.
