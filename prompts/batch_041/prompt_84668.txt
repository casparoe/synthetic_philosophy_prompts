I am a systems security engineer — twenty years of access control, capability architectures, mandatory and discretionary policy, threat modelling — and I have no philosophical training to speak of. I keep noticing that the arguments we have about who may do what to whom on a machine are, underneath the technical apparatus, arguments about freedom, and that the words I use to settle them are not the words political philosophers use. I would like the translation done properly, in both directions.

Take the three conceptions of liberty that dominate the contemporary debate. First, negative liberty: freedom as the absence of interference by other agents, so that a person is unfree exactly to the extent that someone actually blocks, coerces, or prevents her. Second, positive liberty: freedom as self-mastery or autonomy, the condition in which a person is governed by her own authentic will rather than by desires, compulsions, or external powers that are not truly hers. Third, republican liberty, in the neo-Roman sense revived by Philip Pettit and Quentin Skinner: freedom as non-domination, the condition of not being subject to anyone's arbitrary will — a condition violated even when no interference occurs, as in the case of the slave whose master happens never to command him.

Restate each of these three, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually use: principals and subjects, permissions, access control lists, capabilities, ambient authority, least privilege, sandboxing, discretionary versus mandatory policy, reference monitors, the confused deputy problem (a program holding authority that is tricked into exercising it on behalf of someone else), root and the superuser, compromised credentials. Define any term of mine that you think a reader might not know, and do not assume that my vocabulary is secretly already about freedom — if a mapping only works because you have quietly imported the philosophical claim into my terms, say so.

For each of the three, I want the same three-part report: what carries the claim without distortion; what carries it only by fudging, and what the fudge costs; and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Then test the translation against four cases I deal with routinely. (a) A dormant root account: someone holds the standing capacity to read everything I do and never exercises it. Which of the three conceptions treats me as unfree here, and does my notion of a capability — or of least privilege — capture that, or only the narrower fact of what is actually accessible? (b) A perfectly locked-down machine on which I can do almost nothing, because the policy forbids it. Have I been interfered with, and by whom, given that no person is watching? (c) A supervisor process that enforces what its designers take to be my true preferences, overruling the preferences I actually avow. Is the distinction between my authentic and inauthentic will expressible in my framework, or does the framework have to posit a principal whose intent is simply whatever the administrator says it is? (d) A process running with my credentials after my account has been compromised, doing things I would never authorize. Which conception of liberty is even at stake, and does the answer depend on whether the compromise is a person's doing or a bug's?

Close with the residue report proper. Tell me which claims from the philosophical debate cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all; for each, say whether the failure looks like a defect in my framework or like evidence that the philosophical claim was less clear than it appeared. My own hunch, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that republican non-domination is simply least privilege applied to persons, and that the philosophers have dressed up a familiar engineering principle in civic costume. If that hunch is wrong, tell me precisely what the engineering principle leaves out; if it is right, tell me what the philosophers would say in reply, and whether the reply survives translation. Finally, say what my own tradition tacitly assumes about freedom — whether the practice of access control is, without ever admitting it, Berlinian or republican — and whether that assumption is defensible on its own terms.
