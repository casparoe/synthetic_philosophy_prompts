I am preparing a seminar that pairs a literary text with Dennett's critique of qualia, and I would like your help working out the reading. The text is the madeleine episode in Proust's In Search of Lost Time — the scene early in Swann's Way where the narrator, now an adult, tastes a small cake soaked in lime-blossom tea and is suddenly flooded by a memory of his childhood in Combray that he had thought irretrievably lost. The philosophical idea is Dennett's argument, in his essay Quining Qualia, that the notion of qualia — understood as ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible features of experience — is not a coherent posit but a confusion, and that everything we actually need to explain about experience can be explained without it. I would like you to read the madeleine episode through that critique, and to do so in both directions.

First, take Dennett's own thought experiment as a foil. In it, two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, both report that the coffee they taste every day no longer tastes the way it used to. Chase says the coffee itself has changed; Sanborn says his own taste has changed. Dennett argues that there is no fact of the matter about which of them is right, or about where in the chain from the taste bud to the judgment the change occurred, because the notion of a fixed, intrinsic taste-quale that could serve as the common reference point for the two claims is doing no explanatory work. Now bring Proust's narrator into that conversation. He is, in effect, a third taster: he insists that the taste of the madeleine is the same taste he knew as a child, and that this identity of taste is what unlocks the memory. Is he entitled to that insistence on Dennett's terms? Does the episode require the very thing Dennett wants to quine, or can it be redescribed entirely in terms of associations, dispositions, and reactive attitudes — the memory, the emotion, the aesthetic response — without any loss?

Second, reverse the direction. Where, if anywhere, does the madeleine episode resist Dennett's reading? Proust's narrator treats the taste as something that cannot be conveyed in words, as something that belongs to him alone, and as something whose reappearance he recognizes with a kind of immediate certainty. Those are close to the marks Dennett attributes to qualia. Does Proust dramatize a phenomenon that the Dennettian vocabulary cannot capture — a case where the subjective character of experience is not just a façon de parler — or does the episode, read carefully, actually vindicate Dennett by showing that what the narrator recovers is not a raw sensory quality but a whole world of memory, habit, and meaning that was there all along in the reactive attitudes?

I would like a reading that is faithful to both sides: it should not reduce Proust to an illustration of a philosophical thesis, and it should not distort Dennett's argument to make the novel look prophetic. Say what the episode shows that argument alone does not, and say honestly where the novel and the philosophy pull apart. The deliverable should be continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — write it the way you would write a long letter to a colleague, not the way you would write a report.
