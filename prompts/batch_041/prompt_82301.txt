My partner spent fifteen years assessing negligence claims for an insurer — care homes, hospitals, building sites — and she's now the person my whole family rings before they sue anybody. I'm writing a novel about a woman who does nothing, and I keep needing to know how my partner would call these things, so here's her record as I remember it from years of arguing about it.

The care assistant on nights who skipped the two o'clock check: the resident was alive at two and dead at four, and the check would have caught it. She says the failure killed him.

The bloke who watched a toddler go into the canal from a bridge and did nothing. She says he didn't kill the child — the ambulance would have been ten minutes too late even if he'd rung it. She's got no time for him as a person, but 'cause' is a different word.

The nurse who forgot a drug the patient should have had, when the patient was going to die of the injury inside the hour anyway. Not the cause, she says. The injury was.

The man who drove his elderly neighbour to dialysis every Tuesday for two years and then just stopped, without a word, and didn't answer the door when she rang; she missed three sessions and died. She says he caused it. I definately argued with her for an hour about that one — he never promised the woman anything — and she said the driving was the promise.

Now the case I actually need. A man in a campervan at a trailhead reads a laminated notice saying the bridge is out on the only path up. A walker sets off past him; the man says nothing, the walker goes through the bridge in the dark and dies. He could have stopped it with one sentence. He wasn't a ranger, he'd signed nothing, he'd never spoken to the walker, and he owed him nothing.

Would she say he caused the death? I want the rule she's actually running — and I know she might not have one, that's half the point of the book — plus what the other rules that fit her four earlier calls would say about the walker, which one I should bet on and why, and what case I could put to her that would seperate them properly. And if I've misremembered her verdicts, or the whole exercise is a mug's game — say, if it just comes down to which word you like — tell me straight rather than humouring me, I'd rather know.
