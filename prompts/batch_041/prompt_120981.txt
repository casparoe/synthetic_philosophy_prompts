I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I keep running into an analogy that a colleague uses to dismiss the superrationality literature outright. His version is this: a superrational player in the Platonia dilemma — a fixed group is told that exactly one of them must send a telegram, that everyone gets nothing if none or more than one does, and that the superrational prescription is for each to send with probability one over the number of players — is doing nothing more than what an evidential decision theorist does in Newcomb's problem. Both, he says, treat their own action as evidence about how things will go rather than as a cause of them: the superrationalist treats their choice as evidence about what the other copies of themselves will choose, the evidentialist treats their choice as evidence about what the predictor predicted. On his view the two are the same move, so every standard objection to evidential decision theory — the smoking-lesion case, the spurious-correlation worry, the charge that it rewards magical thinking — transfers unchanged. He adds that the Luring Lottery episode, where readers submitted absurdly large entry numbers and diluted the prize into nothing, shows the superrational prescription is a nonstarter in practice, and concludes that superrationality is a dead end and that the Platonia dilemma is just Newcomb's problem with more players.

I'd like to audit this properly instead of trading intuitions. Could you give me an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the predictor, the prediction, the correlation, the evidence, the act, the payoff — and then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from the ones that don't? Specifically:

1. Is the superrational correlation supposed to be causal, evidential, or something else, and does that difference matter for whether the evidential-decision-theory objections transfer?
2. Does moving from two players to a fixed group, and from a pure strategy to the mixed-strategy prescription in the Luring Lottery, change anything structurally, or is it just Newcomb with extra agents?
3. What does the analogy quietly import? For instance, does it assume the correlated parties are distinct agents with a track record, when the superrational story treats them as identical reasoners facing the same problem? And is Hofstadter's reply to the magical-thinking charge — that in these cases there is no choice to make, only an answer to figure out, and that the sameness comes from shared logic rather than causation — a genuine disanalogy or a verbal dodge?
4. Is the Kantian gloss that some writers give superrationality, and that Roemer's Kantian equilibrium tries to formalize, the same claim as the evidential-decision-theory gloss, or a different analogy doing different work? If the latter, my colleague may be running two analogies together.
5. Is the Luring Lottery outcome evidence against the prescription, or evidence about what happens when the participants are not the idealized agents the prescription is about?

Please flag which parts of this are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested — I don't want a contested reading presented as the consensus. And if there's a better analogy for what superrationality is doing, or for what evidential decision theory is doing, tell me what it is and what it captures that the Newcomb analogy doesn't.
