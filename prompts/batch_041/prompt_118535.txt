I'm taking a seminar on punishment and informal social control, and we have moved from the state's penal apparatus to the decentralized end of the subject: boycotts, disinvitations, dismissals for off-duty speech, uncoordinated pile-ons, and the quieter cases in which a publisher drops an author or a fellowship committee withdraws an offer without anyone having organized anything. Before I write the term paper I want the specification rather than a survey of positions. Write the desiderata a theory of social sanction has to meet: which cases it must get right, which distinctions it cannot collapse, and which of the usual markers of punishment — a violated rule, an authority with standing to respond, an intentional imposition of a burden, imposed because of the violation — it must treat as genuinely necessary rather than as artifacts of theorizing about states.

The cases I care about most are these: the organized campaign against a named individual; the leaderless drift in which thousands of strangers each do something small; the refusal that is plausibly self-protection rather than punishment, as when a venue declines to book a controversial act; the sanction imposed for violating a norm the theory's own author would reject; the sanction that follows a criminal conviction, so that the person is burdened twice for one act; and the algorithmic case, in which a recommender quietly reduces a creator's reach and no one intends anything at all. Among the distinctions I would not want collapsed: sanction versus consequence, disapproval versus punishment, the standing of the sanctioner versus the merit of the complaint, and the severity of the burden versus the authority to impose it. I suspect a theory cannot at once treat these episodes as punishment — thereby earning the demands of proportion and of a chance to answer — and treat them as nothing more than exercises of rights of speech and association, which need no license. If you find such a collision, make it the payoff.

Give exactly five numbered items and nothing else, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should be one or two sentences: state one requirement, name the case that forces it, say whether people who work on this would all accept the requirement or whether it is genuinely contested among them — do not present a contested requirement as the consensus — and note the collision if it cannot be held together with another.
