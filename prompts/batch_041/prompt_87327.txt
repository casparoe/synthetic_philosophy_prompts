So in my US history class we've been doing this unit on how countries deal with really bad stuff their own governments did a long time ago, and there's this thing I can't stop thinking about. Our teacher showed us that Germany is still paying money to people who survived the Holocaust, and that the US government mailed checks to Japanese-Americans who got locked up in camps during WWII, and in both cases the government also officially says sorry and builds memorials and stuff. Fine, whatever, that seems good. But then the same week there was this town hall meeting about our high school, which is named after some guy who it turns out did some pretty bad stuff back in the day, and half the town wants to change the name and the other half keeps saying "it's just a name, we're not honoring him, it's history." And my grandpa was like, "I wasn't even born when any of that happened, why should I feel guilty for it," which honestly is kind of what I think too. So now I'm confused, because it seems like when it comes to money and apologies we're totally fine being on the hook for stuff we personally didn't do, but the second somebody wants to take a name off a building suddenly nobody's responsible for anything. That can't be right, right?

I read a little excerpt for class by this German guy Jaspers who was writing right after WWII, and I think he said guilt comes in like four different flavors — criminal, political, moral, and then this "metaphysical" one that I honestly don't understand at all — and I have a feeling our town is doing something like that without knowing it. So what I want to know is what this whole package actually presupposes about guilt and responsibility.

Like, does paying reparations and putting up plaques require believing that the nation is a real thing that can be guilty the way a person is — one continuous "we" that stays the same even as everybody inside it dies off and gets replaced? Or can you get the exact same result with something thinner, like "the country is still holding onto stuff it took, so it owes a debt, and a debt isn't the same thing as being guilty"? Whats the actual difference there — is owing somebody money the same as being guilty toward them, or totally different? And are the parts even consistent with each other — can you honestly say the nation is guilty enough to pay but I personally am not guilty, and also that a name on a school is just a name and means nothing? Which parts of the practice actually need the collective-guilt idea and which parts would work fine without it? If we all agreed tomorrow that only individuals can be guilty and collectives can't, what would have to change — would the money stop making sense, or just the apologies and the memorial days? And is there a version of this that isn't guilt in disguise at all, like maybe it's really about the country keeping a promise to itself or being loyal to its own past or just not wanting to profit from stolen stuff? Sorry this is so long, I just feel like everybody in this debate is assuming something they never say out loud and I want to know what it is.
