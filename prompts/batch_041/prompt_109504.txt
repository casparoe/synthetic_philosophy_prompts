Here are my verdicts about which inner states represent things, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

A frog's tongue-snapping circuitry is triggered by anything small, dark, and moving, including a lead pellet I flick across its visual field; I say the circuit represents prey, and the pellet is a misrepresentation. A toad that once got stung and now flinches at hoverflies as well as wasps represents wasps and misrepresents hoverflies. A bacterium's magnetosome, which steers it down the geomagnetic field into anaerobic mud, represents nothing — it's a weathervane, not a thought. A smoke alarm that shrieks when steam hits it represents nothing either, and neither does a thermostat. But a reinforcement-learning agent trained only by gradient descent to catch red spheres does represent the sphere, even though nothing selected its ancestors for anything, and it misrepresents when it grabs a red ball. A plant bending toward the window represents nothing.

My tentative principle: a state represents X when it is a mechanism whose job is to covary with X, where the job is fixed by some selection history — natural, operant, or gradient-descent — and misrepresentation is firing without X.

That earns the frog, the toad, and the learning agent, and it condemns the magnetosome and the plant. But I cannot see how it separates the frog from the smoke alarm, which was also built to fire on smoke and not on steam, and which is wrong in just the way the frog is when I wave the pellet. If the alarm has no representation, the selection-history clause looks empty; if it has one, I seem forced to say the thermostat represents the temperature and misrepresents it when the contacts stick. Which of these should I give up?

I also keep going back and forth on a creature assembled by a lightning strike in a swamp, atom for atom like a frog but with no history whatsoever. My instinct is that it represents prey just as the frog does, which seems to cost me the history clause outright — unless something else is doing the work and history was a red herring. And one case I haven't settled: a bee's dance carries the direction of nectar and at the same time sends the other bees there. If one state both says how things are and tells you what to do, does a single-content account survive, or does it have to be split?

Please state the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely; tie each clause to the case that forces it; say what it predicts for the swamp creature, the smoke alarm, the thermostat, and the dance; and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot keep. If my verdicts are simply inconsistent and nothing short of a gerrymander holds them together, tell me that rather than inventing a principle to save them.
