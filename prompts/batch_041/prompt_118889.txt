Me and a friend have been going back and forth about HCH as a model of ideal deliberation, and he's built an argument I can't get out of. Two definitions we stipulated up front: HCH is a human who can break any question into subquestions, farm each subquestion out to a fresh copy of HCH, and then combine the answers. Factored cognition is the assumption that any big cognitive task can be split into small subtasks, each of which a human can do reliably, without needing to grasp the whole. Now the argument:

(1) Anything HCH answers, it answers with a finite tree of small steps, and every leaf is something one human can just do.
(2) So nothing HCH does is beyond what a human can do -- it just does a lot of it, with unlimited time and unlimited advice.
(3) So HCH can't answer anything a human couldn't in principle answer. It's roughly "what a person would conclude given unlimited time and help," not a superhuman thing.
(4) But factored cognition says every cognitive task decomposes into human-doable subtasks, so for any question with a definite answer, HCH can answer it -- just keep splitting until the pieces are small enough.
(5) So HCH can settle things no human could ever settle: it can grind through a computation that would take one person 10^40 years and hand you the answer, and it can find the proof, or the right theory, in cases where no individual ever would.
(6) Which contradicts (3).

He says this shows the whole HCH picture is confused. I think something slipped but I can't say where. Which step goes, and what does it cost me? I'd like to keep factored cognition as the assumption about what deliberation could be -- drop it and the idealization has no content -- but I'll give up the "unlimited time" bit if that's what's carrying the argument. And I've never understood why "a human could in principle do it" is supposed to be a ceiling.
