I'm a retired neuroscientist -- thirty-odd years on protein aggregation, and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy like it's going out of style, which my wife finds hilarious. Anyway, we rewatched Still Alice last night (the 2014 film with Julianne Moore, from Lisa Genova's novel) and it's got me stuck on advance directives and the authority of your earlier self. I want a real reading of the film through that lens. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning -- no preamble, no restating what I asked.

The philosophy, as I understand it, runs something like this. There's a view on which the person you are while competent gets to bind the person you become after you lose the capacities that made you competent -- your settled values about how your life should go, your "critical" interests as opposed to the day-to-day experiential ones, carry authority forward even when the later you can't hold them anymore. Call it precedent autonomy. Then there's the pushback: the later person is the one who has to live the thing, and her present wishes and present comfort aren't obviously less hers than the earlier person's were, so why does the earlier one get to boss her around? And underneath both, there's the older question of whether the later person is even the same person -- if what matters is psychological continuity, and that's largely gone, then an advance directive starts to look less like a promise to yourself and more like an order issued to a stranger. I can't keep the names straight on who holds which line, so just tell me the positions as positions.

Now the film, in case you need reminding. Alice Howland is a linguistics professor at Columbia, diagnosed with early-onset familial Alzheimer's while she's still very much herself. She sets herself a series of personal questions on her phone and answers them every morning as a check on herself, and she hides a stash of sleeping pills and records a video message for her future self: when you can't answer the questions anymore, go get the pills and take them all. Then the disease moves. She gives a speech at an Alzheimer's conference, following her own text with a highlighter so she knows where she is in it. Her husband takes a job in Minnesota; her younger daughter Lydia, who'd refused the genetic test, comes home to look after her. And then one day, after a video call, she opens the video by accident. She works out what she's supposed to do, finds the pills, is about to swallow them, and gets interrupted by her caregiver arriving -- she drops the pills and forgets what she was doing. The film ends with Lydia reading her a scene from Angels in America and asking what she thinks it's about, and Alice, who can barely speak now, answers with one word: love.

What I want from you:

Does the film come down for or against the earlier self's authority, or does it refuse to? Where does it dramatize the precedent-autonomy view cleanly, and where does it stack the deck -- I notice the film makes the later Alice so diminished that we can't read her non-compliance as a considered judgment at all, which seems like a cheat, but I also notice that the whole film keeps us inside her head as one continuous character, and that seems to cut the other way.

What do the two positions actually say about that bathroom scene? If the earlier Alice's values govern, then the later Alice failing to carry out the directive is just the disease winning, not a decision, and the earlier Alice's judgment stands. If the later Alice's present wishes count, then her dropping the pills is at least ambiguous -- except she didn't drop them because she changed her mind, she dropped them because she was interrupted and forgot. So is this scene even evidence for either side, or does the film rig it so the question can't be asked? I'd like you to say which reading the staging actually supports and be honest if the answer is "neither, it dodges."

The conference speech is the other scene I keep circling. She's performing her articulate self for an audience, reading her own earlier words off a page. Is that the earlier self speaking, or the later self doing an impression of her? It seems to matter for the identity question -- if the later Alice can still do this at all, even with a highlighter, the "different person" story looks weaker than the philosophers want.

Then the ending. I have a feeling the film's last beat is a rebuttal to the directive -- that there's still someone there who can love, and that the earlier Alice made her judgment about a life she couldn't imagine from the inside, so her authority is the authority of someone who never met the person she was ruling on. But I might be sentimentalizing it. Tell me whether the ending earns that or just flatters us into it.

And tell me what the film shows that the arguments don't -- what you only get from being made to sit inside one person's decline for two hours that no thought experiment about then-selves and now-selves delivers. Where does the film resist the reading and the honest critic has to admit it, and where does the film get the philosophy wrong?

Don't quote the film at me, I know the scenes, and don't send me to a reading list -- I want your reading, argued.
