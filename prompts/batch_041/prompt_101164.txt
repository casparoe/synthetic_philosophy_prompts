I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, deciding where to put roughly a year of part-time effort and a small budget. I intend to build a decision-support tool that helps people act on the long-term goals they themselves endorse — saving, exercise, finishing a thesis — and I must choose which conceptual questions to settle first, because the tool's design forks on the answers. Here is the seam I care about. The behavioral literature describes people as discounting future rewards hyperbolically, which predicts preference reversals as a reward draws near. The philosophical tradition instead speaks of weakness of will: knowing, at the moment of action, what one judges best and yet doing otherwise. I want to know whether these are two descriptions of one phenomenon or two different phenomena, and which unresolved questions in that region carry the most downstream consequences for a tool like mine. The fork is concrete. If the reversals are a genuine feature of how people weight the future, my tool should supply commitment devices and pre-commitment. If they are largely an artifact of how we elicit preferences — asking people to choose between delayed rewards — then my tool should instead help users clarify what they actually value. And if weakness of will is a failure of the will that the discounting picture does not capture at all, then a tool of this kind may be beside the point. I also want you to test a bridge definition of my own: treating akrasia operationally as a revealed preference reversal that shows up when the moment of choice arrives.
