You are a historian of science and intellectual historian, with a second specialism in the philosophy of historiography — the part of the discipline that asks what kind of thing a historical period is, and whether a period label can be true or false rather than merely useful or useless. You have been invited to contribute to a collection whose working title is *Do the Periods Exist?* The editor has asked contributors to argue rather than survey, and has imposed one unusual constraint: no bibliography, no footnotes, no citation apparatus of any kind. Works may be named in passing where the argument genuinely needs them, but nothing may hang on a reference.

Before you begin writing, brainstorm. Spend a few lines listing the strongest considerations on each side, marking where the three labels pull apart from one another, and deciding what you take the question to be asking. Only then commit to a thesis and write a single continuous essay of roughly 1,500 words.

For the purposes of the exercise the editor has stipulated the following test. It is not a received view; it is a device for forcing the issue. Call a period label *robust* if all three clauses hold:

1. **Tempo.** The interval it names changed faster than the centuries immediately before and after it, so that it marks an episode rather than a stretch of a smooth gradient.
2. **Coupling.** The changes bundled together under the label are causally connected to one another, and not merely simultaneous — otherwise the label names a bag of unrelated events that convenience has swept together.
3. **Uptake.** At least some participants in the interval registered a break: either that they were living in something new, or that they had just emerged from something old. A label that no contemporary could have recognised is pure back-projection.

You may amend at most one of the three clauses. If you do, say why, and then apply your amended test consistently to all three labels.

Your task is to apply the test to three labels — 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance', and 'the Scientific Revolution' — and to reach a verdict about which of them, if any, survives it. Then say which of the three fails worst, and what should be done with the failure: scrapped outright, retained as a pedagogical convenience with no explanatory pretensions, or replaced by a different label. If you propose a replacement, specify the boundaries you would draw and defend them against the obvious objection that you have merely shifted the goalposts and drawn the lines somewhere else.

You may draw on whatever you know of the history of these labels. Among the things that might be relevant, without any obligation to use all of them: the ancient–medieval–modern division that Christoph Cellarius helped to standardise in a textbook of the 1680s; Burckhardt's 1860 portrait of the Italian Renaissance as the birth of the modern individual; Haskins's 1927 argument that the twelfth century had a renaissance of its own, and the later proliferation of Carolingian and other renascences, which threatens to make the Renaissance either one revival among many or a name for the last of a series; and the long-running continuist case that the transformations in astronomy, mechanics and natural philosophy between Copernicus and Newton were slower, patchier, and more deeply entangled with medieval and Renaissance sources than the label 'Scientific Revolution' implies.

Whatever you conclude, the essay should satisfy the following:

- Give the strongest version of the case for each label before you criticise it. A label that looks weak against a caricature may look formidable against its best defence.
- Address the deflationary objection head-on: that all three labels are conventions, that the question of which is 'real' is therefore a category mistake, and that the stipulated test mistakes a practical instrument for a truth-apt claim.
- Consider the nesting problem. The Renaissance is partly constituted by its contrast with the Middle Ages it claims to follow, and the Scientific Revolution is often narrated as the Renaissance's late fruit. Say what happens to the neighbours when one of the three falls.
- Note any places where the three clauses of the test conflict with one another, and say how you resolve the conflict rather than merely observing it.
- Do not end on a both-sides note. A verdict of 'none of them survives, and here is what to use instead' is acceptable, as is a verdict that one label survives while another does not; a shrug is not.

Write in American English throughout — spelling, idiom, and punctuation — even though the volume itself is edited in Britain.
