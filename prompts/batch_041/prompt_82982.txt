I'm a science journalist at work on a long feature about disagreement — why two people who are equally intelligent, equally informed, and equally honest about the evidence can look at the same thing and walk away with different numbers. The piece keeps circling back to a result I only half understand, the agreement theorem — Aumann's — which says that if two agents share a prior and their differing credences for some event become common knowledge, the disagreement cannot survive; it evaporates, not because anyone concedes anything, but because the arithmetic forbids it. I've been carrying a photocopy of the paper around for a month and I've stopped trusting my own gloss on it. What I want is to build the article around a specification rather than around the theorem, so what I'm asking you for is a list of desiderata: what any adequate theory of rational agreement — one that treats this result as its central exhibit rather than as a curiosity — would have to deliver, with the case or consideration that motivates each requirement.

The theorem itself is one entry: the theory must reproduce the result in the setting where it holds, and say which of the structural assumptions in that setting are load-bearing — a shared state space, a common algebra of events, information modeled as partitions, a common prior — and which are artifacts of how the proof is set up. But I'm mainly interested in the edges, and here I'll tell you the four things I keep tripping over. First, whether anything is ever common knowledge in the first place: there's the old story about two parties trying to fix a rendezvous by messages that may go astray, where no finite exchange of acknowledgements ever lifts them from mutual knowledge to common knowledge, and if that's right then the theorem's antecedent may never be satisfied by actual people, which makes it either a vacuous constraint or a regulative ideal, and a theory has to say which. Second, the common prior, which looks like it is doing all of the work and which some people treat as a requirement of rationality and others as a substantive empirical bet; a theory that defines disagreement away by assuming it out of existence is not going to be useful to me. Third, the fact that the result is proved for agents who share a state space and who always know their own information and hence their own credences, and it is not obvious what survives when two analysts carve up the possibilities differently, or when an agent is genuinely uncertain about what they themselves believe. Fourth, the case I actually want to write about: a trading desk where both analysts' forecasts are displayed on a screen the whole room can see, where the disagreement is about as public as disagreement gets, where it persists for weeks, and where nobody is obviously irrational — so something has to give, and I want to know what the theory is licensed to sacrifice.

Then, briefly: tell me which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are house rules of one camp; whether any two of them cannot be had together (my own suspicion is that reproducing the theorem and permitting agents who carve up the possibilities differently pull against each other, since the proof seems to lean on the shared space, but you tell me — and if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, that is the part of the answer I most want); and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out otherwise attractive theories. Skip the scorecard of who said what. I don't need a reading list and I'd rather have the criteria clean than have them propped up with attributions I can't check from my desk. What I'm after is the spec: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the line where the theory is permitted to stop.
