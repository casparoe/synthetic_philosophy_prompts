My friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about hard choices and ended up more confused than we began. She takes what I will call the deflationary view: whenever a choice between two goods feels irresolvable — a demanding career against caring for a dying parent, a life of scholarly depth against a life of public usefulness — the feeling is evidence that we have not yet found the right description of the options, and that a clear enough description would show one of them to be better, all things considered. I take the opposite view: that some pairs of options are simply not comparable, and that this is a fact about the values themselves rather than about our ignorance of them.

The argument did throw up one thing I had not seen before. We kept running together two claims as if they were one. There is a claim about the values: that no common measure exists on which the two options can be placed. And there is a claim about the situation: that, here and now, neither option is supported by a balance of reasons. Call the first evaluative incommensurability and the second deliberative incommensurability. It seems possible for the first to hold while the second fails — a pair of values might admit no common measure, and yet in this particular case some third consideration, a promise or a deadline or a duty, might tip the balance — and equally possible for the second to hold for reasons that have nothing to do with the first. I suspect a fair amount of the disagreement in this area is really disagreement about which of these two things is at stake.

I have a small grant to allocate: enough to support three or four people for two years, and I get to set the research agenda. My aim is not to settle the metaphysics for its own sake but to make the theory usable by people who actually have to make and defend hard choices — clinical ethics committees, judges choosing between incommensurable harms in sentencing, and ordinary people reasoning about their own lives. Given that aim, which open questions in this area most deserve the money and the two years, and in what order? I want the reasoning more than the list: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is with the tools philosophers already have, how neglected it currently is, and what would move it up or down my ranking over the next couple of years.

Some candidates I have been turning over, though I would rather you tell me which of them I have misjudged than simply take my list at face value: whether the small-improvement argument establishes that the options are on a par or only that they are incomparable, and whether that difference makes any practical difference; whether a choice can make one option better than another rather than merely reveal which was already better, on the thought that some commitments are self-constituting; whether the fitting-attitudes account of value can accommodate incommensurability without collapsing everything onto a single scale; and whether the phenomenon is better modeled as vagueness, as a missing value, or as a genuinely distinct comparative relation. And tell me whether the distinction I drew above, evaluative against deliberative incommensurability, actually reorganizes this space or is a distinction without a difference that I should drop. End with a concrete agenda: the two or three questions you would fund, the ones you would explicitly decline to fund, and the criterion you used to separate them.
