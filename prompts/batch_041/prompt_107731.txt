Alright, so I'm a 4th year PhD student working on decision theory and I've got this cluster of views I keep defending at workshops that I'm starting to suspect don't actually fit together. I want a sanity check before I try to put any of it in a chapter. Here's the package:

(1) The only genuine requirements of rationality are synchronic ones — roughly, at any given moment your preferences should be complete and transitive. I don't think there's any seperate requirement that you be consistent across time.

(2) I think the money pump is overrated as an arguement. Sure, if your preferences cycle you can be led around by your nose paying a penny at each step, but that seems to me a fact about your preferences being bad, not a distinct diachronic sin.

(3) That said, the person who actually walks the loop, handing over a penny at every stage, is being irrational. My line has always been that the irrationality lives in the preferences they walked in with, not in any individual step.

(4) I'm pretty convinced that some values are incommensurable, and that this means some intransitivities in preference are perfectly rational — you don't have to have a linear ranking over everything.

(5) For anything intertemporal I'm a sophisticated chooser: you should predict what you'd choose later and choose now in light of that. I've never bought resolute choice.

(6) I also think it's completely fine for your preferences to change over time — new evidence, new experience, whatever. No obligation to stay loyal to your past self's ranking.

So: which of these are actually in conflict, which only look like they are, and how bad is it? Rank the tensions by severity rather than telling me they're all equally worrying, and for each real one tell me which commitment has to give and what I'd be giving up by dropping it. I especially don't want a lazy "well it's all consistent if you read it charitably" — if something has to go, say so, and if a tension is only apparent, explain why it dissolves rather than just asserting it does.
