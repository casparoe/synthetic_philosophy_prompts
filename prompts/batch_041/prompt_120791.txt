I'm helping run a session for our hospital's ethics commitee — half clinicians, half philosophers, all of them convinced they already know what consent is — and I want to hand out a decision tree that takes a person from their verdicts on concrete cases to a position on what makes consent valid. It has to sort them by what they actually say about the cases, not by which label they'd pick off a menu, so no node can just be "do you think consent requires information?" — every branch has to be a question someone can answer about a scenario without knowing where it leads. I'd like at most eight branch nodes and something like ten or twelve leaves. The leaves should be named positions where a standard name exists (I know there's a family of views that treat consent as a mental act, a rival family that treats it as a performative or communicative act, and another that treats it as the exercise of a power to change what others are permitted to do — but I don't want the tree built around just those three), and coined-and-defined positions where nothing standard fits. Where two leaves can't be separated by any case you can actually put in the tree, say so instead of pretending otherwise, and where the logical space has a slot nobody occupies, tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The cases I want it to run on, at minimum: consent given under a false belief about the nature of the act versus about its consequences versus about the other party's identity or credentials; the difference between a threat and a warning when the warning is true and the speaker didn't create the danger; pressure from the consent-taker versus pressure from a third party or from circumstances like poverty, immigration status, or being in prison; a competent adult who agrees to a risky procedure mostly for the perks attached to it; someone whose competence is fine in general but shaky for this particular decision; consent to a boxing match, to surgery, to sex where a condom is used and then removed, and to being killed; an advance directive the person later wants to revoke but can't; and a subject who consents to a trial that is fully disclosed but rests on a false premise the researchers also believe, so nobody has deceived anyone.

And here's the thing I actually care about, which may be why I can't get this straight on my own. I've started thinking about these as two seperate failure modes and I want to know whether the distinction survives contact with the cases. On one side, consent that is vitiated — something went wrong in how it was produced, in the will or in the information. On the other, consent that misfires — produced perfectly well, but with no normative effect, because the thing consented to lies outside the domain of consent altogether (consent to the criminal, consent to the irrevocable, consent to something nobody can waive). Does that line cut the space cleanly, or are there cases that are both, or neither? If the tree can't represent the distinction without collapsing it, I'd rather you tell me than fudge it. For each leaf, finish with the case it handles worst — the awkward commitment its defender has to swallow.
