OK so I'm a grad student co-teaching a first-year seminar on the three concepts of liberty — the negative one (freedom as absence of interference), the positive one (freedom as self-mastery or self-realization), and the republican one (freedom as non-domination) — and we're doing a week where the students have to fight about which of the three is the real thing. I want to give them something more concrete than just a discussion prompt, so I've been trying to build a little rating exercise and I'd like your help finishing it.

Here's the case set I've settled on, three cases that I think any decent account of liberty has to handle: (1) the plain one, someone is interfered with — locked up, coerced, blocked; (2) the republican favorite, someone is dominated but never actually interfered with, like a servant with a master who happens to be kind and never exercises the power; and (3) the one I actually care about and that I think gets undersold, someone who has internalized their own subordination so thoroughly that there's no external obstacle left and no one is currently dominating them, but we still want to say they aren't free — the constraint has moved inside and been laundered into their own preferences. I've been calling (3) 'constituted unfreedom' in my notes, partly to keep it separate from the other two, though I'm not sure the label earns its keep.

What I want: put the three concepts — negative, positive, republican — into tiers (S down to like D, or a strict ranking if tiers feel forced) by how well each one handles all three cases at once, and for each concept give me one sentence on what it nails and one on where it cracks. Then, separately, tell me straight whether my three-case split is doing real work or whether it just collapses back into the usual interference-vs-dependence divide the moment you push on case (3) — if it collapses I'd rather hear that then have you paper over it. I'm not after a reading list or anything, we've already got the syllabus; I just want the argument.
