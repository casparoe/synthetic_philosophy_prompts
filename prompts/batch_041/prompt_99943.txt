I am a law student who has been spending an unreasonable amount of time in a seminar on existentialism and the criminal law, and I want to commission a piece of sustained satire built around a thought experiment. Picture a jurisdiction whose sentencing guidelines were drafted by someone who read Being and Nothingness far too literally and never recovered. The bench has adopted as binding doctrine the proposition that a person is wholly free; that character, temperament, upbringing, addiction, and circumstance are not causes acting on the will but choices the will perpetually renews; and that to plead them in mitigation is not to report a fact about the defendant but to commit the very evasion Sartre called bad faith. The court's official view is that a defendant who says he could not help himself has, in saying it, demonstrated that he could — the excuse is the proof of the freedom it disowns. This is a reductio, and I want the reductio performed straight-faced, without a hint of the author's own judgment leaking through.

What I want, then, is the text of a real document from such a court: a memorandum denying a defense motion for mitigation, roughly 600 to 800 words, laid out as an actual judicial instrument — a caption, numbered paragraphs, one or two footnotes — and deadpan throughout. Make the defendant ordinary rather than monstrous: someone who robbed a pharmacy to feed an addiction, whose counsel has moved for leniency on the ground that a violent father, an untreated illness, and a dependence he never chose should reduce his culpability. The court should deny the motion on recognizably Sartrean grounds — that anguish is not a symptom but a mode of freedom; that we choose our emotions and our very selves; that the past is not a cause of the present act but something the present act continually reinterprets; that no fact about a person can excuse him, since circumstances are only the resistance a free project must work against. The pressure a Sartre specialist should feel is the real tension between radical freedom and facticity, the body, the Other, and material constraint: let the opinion acknowledge in a footnote that freedom is always freedom in situation, and then treat situation as a coefficient of adversity that can never exculpate — indeed, let it argue that to excuse the defendant would be to insult him by treating him as a thing, so that mercy itself becomes the deeper cruelty. Keep the court's reasoning internally consistent; the discomfort should arise from taking the doctrine seriously rather than from caricaturing it. Do not attribute the reasoning to any real judge, and do not quote Sartre's actual sentences — invent the court's prose from scratch. A reader who has never opened Sartre should follow every move, while someone who has should find the document uncomfortably apt rather than unfair.
