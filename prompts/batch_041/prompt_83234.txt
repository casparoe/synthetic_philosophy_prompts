I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy; my work is in perception and control, and over the past year I have been reading my way through predictive processing and the free energy principle, mostly in semi-technical and popular sources. The claim I keep encountering is that the brain is at bottom a prediction machine that minimizes prediction error, and that this continues, or finally mathematizes, Hermann von Helmholtz's idea that perception is a species of unconscious inference. Helmholtz died in 1894, well before any of this existed, and I would like a serious reconstruction of what his position would have been — not the flattering story that he anticipated the whole thing.

Assume he has been brought fully up to date on the relevant work, including Karl Friston's formulation, and that he remains the same empirically minded, combative theorist he was. I am interested in the following, in roughly this order:

1. Scope. Would he extend inference beyond perception — to action, to learning, and then to systems with no nervous system at all, such as a bacterium, a plant, or a single cell? Some versions of the framework go further and treat the boundary of the system itself as something inferred rather than given. Would he follow that, or would he insist that inference in his sense requires something the free energy principle does not supply? Identify what he would treat as the load-bearing commitment.

2. Status of the principle. He helped establish conservation of energy as a genuine physical law, and he had little patience for explanatory schemes that merely redescribe what is already known. Would he regard the free energy principle as a law in that sense — a constraint on any system that persists — or as a redescription, or as unfalsifiable? What would he demand by way of a test, given his own standards?

3. Signs versus representations. He is usually summarized as holding that sensations are signs of their external causes rather than copies of them, and that we learn through experience which sign goes with which cause. Predictive processing is often read the other way, as the brain representing the hidden causes of its input. Would he accept the representational reading, or the deflationary reading on which representation talk is dispensable and prediction error is all there is? Which reading does his sign theory actually commit him to?

4. The word inference. In his usage, is inference a claim about the computation a system performs or about a process it undergoes? Would he call the description of a thermostat or a bacterium as an inferrer a category mistake, a genuine discovery, or a harmless façon de parler?

5. Where the extrapolation breaks. His empiricism about perception was worked out in a long dispute with a nativist opponent, and predictive processing appears to need structured priors that are not themselves learned from experience. Does that push him onto the other side of his own old argument? Flag the points at which his commitments pull in opposite directions, and the points at which he would say the modern question has changed the subject rather than extended his.

Give me a verdict on each, with the reasoning that leads there and the confidence you attach to it. Where his own position genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so and name the specific further consideration that would settle it. No references or reading list needed; I want the reconstruction and the argument.
