I've been reading Keynes's stuff on probability for a book club and I've hit a wall. The bit I actually care about is his distinction between the probability of a proposition and the weight of the argument for it. As I understand it, probability is the degree of rational belief you're entitled to given the evidence, and weight is a separate thing, roughly how much evidence that degree rests on. So "it'll probably rain" can have the same probability whether you're going on one look at one cloud or on a decade of weather records, but the second has more weight, and Keynes thinks weight matters for what it's rational to do — you don't bet the farm on a thin argument even when the odds look the same. He also thinks not all probabilities are comparable: for some pairs of propositions, on some evidence, neither is more probable than the other, and that isn't just our ignorance. I gather this is the ancestor of what he says later about investment under uncertainty.

Anyway a friend in the group says the whole two-dimensional picture is either vacuous or collapses into the ordinary one, and gave me this:

(1) Probability is the degree of rational belief warranted by the evidence, and weight is how much evidence the probability rests on.
(2) Weight is not a degree of belief in any proposition — it's a property of the relation between the evidence and the hypothesis.
(3) Weight makes a difference to what it's rational to do (don't bet the farm on thin evidence).
(4) Anything that makes a difference to what it's rational to do must be represented in the calculation by which we compare options.
(5) The calculation by which we compare options is expected value, and every input to expected value is a probability or a utility.
(6) So weight must be representable as a probability, or as something tradeable against probability.
(7) But then a single number per option will do, and probability and weight were never two dimensions after all.
(8) If weight can't be so represented, then it makes no difference to what it's rational to do, and (3) is false.
(9) Either way Keynes's distinction does no work.

I want to keep (1)–(3), that's the whole reason I got interested, and I'll happily ditch (9). But I can't see where the arguement cheats. Is it (4), or (5), or is the slide somewhere else? And whichever premise I drop, what do I have to pay for it — does dropping (5) mean giving up on expected value as the whole story about rational choice, and is there a version of Keynes where weight enters without just becoming a second probability? Also tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and flag the one step you're least sure about.
