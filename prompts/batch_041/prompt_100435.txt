I've been reading Hegel's Philosophy of Right for a book club and got stuck on the sections about war. I built an argument that seems to prove too much, and I can't find where it goes wrong. Help?

The setup: Hegel says war has an ethical significance. Not that it's good in itself, but that it makes manifest the nothingness of finite goods — life, property — and thereby preserves the ethical health of nations. He compares it to the movement of the winds, which keeps the sea from stagnation. (I know the ethical health line is from a lecture addition, not the main text, but lets take it as expressing his view.)

Now the argument:

(1) If a practice makes manifest the contingency of finite goods and thereby preserves the ethical health of a nation, then that practice has ethical value.
(2) War makes manifest the contingency of finite goods and thereby preserves the ethical health of a nation.
(3) So war has ethical value.
(4) If a practice has ethical value, then we have reason to promote it or at least not to prevent it.
(5) So we have reason to promote war or at least not to prevent it.
(6) But that's absurd — we should try to prevent wars, not promote them.
(7) So one of the premises is false. Which one, and what does rejecting it cost?

I'd rather keep (2) than (5), but I want to know what I'm paying. I suspect the move from (1) to (4) is where it slips, but I can't articulate why. Is it that ethical value doesn't translate into a reason to promote? Or is the problem in (1) — that preserving ethical health is doing something different from having ethical value? Or is the real issue that war is one of many possible sources of the relevant good, so even if it has ethical value, it's substitutable and that changes the practical upshot?

Also: does Hegel himself think we should promote war? He clearly doesn't — he says war shouldn't be waged against private persons, family, civil life, and he criticizes Kant's perpetual peace only as an ought that can't be realized, not because he wants endless war. So maybe the argument attributes to Hegel a position he doesn't hold. But then what does ethical significance actually amount to if it doesn't generate a reason to promote war? Is it purely descriptive — a claim about what war discloses, not a claim about what we should do?

I'd like the answer to name the specific step, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives — including what happens if we give up (1) vs. (2) vs. the inferential move. And if the flaw is an equivocation on ethical, I'd like to know exactly where the two senses come apart.
