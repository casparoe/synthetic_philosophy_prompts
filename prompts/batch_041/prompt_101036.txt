I'm a staff engineer drafting a proposal to change how my company makes go/no-go calls on risky infrastructure migrations. Today each decision goes to the single engineer who knows the affected system best. I want to replace that with a company-wide secret ballot of all two hundred or so engineers, with the majority verdict taken as binding, and my justification is the Condorcet jury theorem: so long as each voter is even slightly more likely than not to get the question right, the majority is more likely to be right than any individual, and that advantage compounds as the electorate grows, so the larger the poll the closer we come to certainty. A few colleagues have objected that this would let engineers who have never touched the system outvote the people who actually maintain it, but I believe that objection misreads the theorem: the mathematics is indifferent to how much any given voter knows, it only requires that each of them clears the fifty-percent bar, and with two hundred voters we should be effectively infallible. I'd like to know precisely what the theorem does and does not entitle me to claim here, whether my colleagues' objection has any real force, and whether there is a principled way to retain the large-electorate advantage while still limiting the vote to engineers who understand the system.
