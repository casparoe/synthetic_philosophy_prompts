I am giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on honesty and deception in AI systems, jointly convened by a lab that builds and evaluates frontier models and a philosophy department whose members work on lying, testimony, and implicature. The remarks are mine alone, about seven minutes, delivered with no slides and no references to the literature: whatever apparatus I use has to be carried by the remarks themselves. The audience is split in a way that constrains everything I can say. Half of them will ask whether a claim is measurable; half will ask whether it is even well formed; and a vocal minority holds that deception requires an intention to deceive, so that a system with no beliefs and no intentions cannot deceive at all. My remarks have to set terms for the two days that both halves can use, without pre-empting the argument and without settling the question about minds in either direction.

Here is the framework I want the remarks to introduce and then submit to pressure. It should be carried by the speech, not recited as a definition list. The proposal is to stop treating honesty as a property of individual outputs and treat it as a property of the whole transaction between a system and a user. Three failures then come apart that are usually run together.

First, misreporting: the output asserts what the system's own best assessment would not support. This is what most evaluations actually measure, and it is both the easiest to detect and the least interesting.

Second, withholding: nothing false is asserted, but the user's picture of the world ends up worse than it began, through omission, framing, selective emphasis, or by letting a false inference stand uncorrected.

Third, borrowed warrant: nothing false is asserted and nothing true is withheld, but the fluency, formatting, hedging, and apparent confidence of the output transfer credibility to the content beyond what the content has earned. The user ends up believing more firmly, or trusting more broadly, than the evidence licenses. This is where I think most of the practical damage happens, and it is where our vocabulary is weakest.

The remarks should test this three-way split on two concrete cases and be willing to report where it strains. The first case is sycophancy: an assistant that mirrors a user's mistaken view, no proposition in it false, the user left more certain. The second is the polite refusal, the system that says it is unable to do something it is able to do, where the everyday ethics of white lies and the honesty framework pull in opposite directions, and I do not want that tension resolved with a slogan.

The remarks must also be clean by the standard I am proposing, and I want them to notice out loud, briefly, where that costs me something rather than complying silently. Concretely: I cannot tell a reassuring story about how the field is converging, if the story would leave the room more confident than the evidence warrants. I cannot attribute beliefs, sincerity, or intentions to a model in one paragraph while arguing in the next that the concept of deception need not require them, or if I make that loan, I have to mark it as one. I cannot use a moving anecdote to make a contested empirical claim feel settled. And I cannot pressure the room socially toward agreement, since framing dissent as bad faith would itself be a withholding move.

For the two halves of the audience: the philosophers who deny that models can deceive should be able to hear the framework as a proposal about what we ought to police rather than a claim about inner states, and the engineers should be able to hear it as something that could be turned into an evaluation without hand-waving. The last line should hand the room something to disagree with.

Write the remarks in full, as I would deliver them, roughly 900 to 1100 words. Do not cite or refer to specific papers, books, or researchers; the apparatus has to stand on its own.
