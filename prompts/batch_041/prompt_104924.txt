I am assembling readings for a graduate seminar that sits between social choice theory and democratic theory, and I want to include the sort of document the literature almost never produces: an honest adversarial collaboration rather than another exchange of talking points. The two camps are (i) a formal social choice theorist who treats the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, alongside Arrow's theorem, as a hard structural constraint that any serious institutional designer has to design around, and (ii) a deliberative democrat who regards the theorem as a reductio of a certain formal model of politics rather than a fact about politics, on the grounds that it presupposes fixed, ordinal, unrestricted preferences over a fixed menu of alternatives and thereby ignores deliberation, preference transformation, and the prior politics of how the agenda and the rule itself get chosen. These two have been arguing past each other for years, each convinced the other has missed the point. Please write the joint statement they could both put their names to.

The statement should open with a numbered list of claims both sides accept — and I mean claims with teeth, not throat-clearing: the precise statement of the result and the exact assumptions it rests on; what it does and does not imply about familiar real-world rules such as plurality voting; how it relates to Arrow's theorem without simply collapsing into it; and an inventory of the escape routes both agree exist in principle (randomization, restricting the domain, bringing in cardinal or interpersonal information, relaxing determinism or surjectivity, changing the solution concept, enlarging the space of mechanisms so that voters transact over a wider menu, as in the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves family). Then give me the disagreements in a table, each row stating the point of contention in wording that the side holding that view would itself accept, with the residual cruxes spelled out separately: my suspicion is that one crux is whether the "unrestricted domain" assumption is innocent or is precisely where real politics does its work, and another is whether the theorem's notion of a preference is the same object as the deliberative democrat's notion of a preference, or a different one wearing the same name. For each crux, give a concrete case, argument, or toy example that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about that case in advance — including at least one small worked example (a handful of voters, a few alternatives, a rule under which some voter profits by misreporting) that both sides read the same way, and, where they read it differently, exactly where the readings diverge. Finally, have them say plainly which part of their dispute is merely verbal and why, which parts are substantive but not empirical, and what each side would count as genuinely moving it. Do not cite any literature; I want the document to stand on its arguments alone.
