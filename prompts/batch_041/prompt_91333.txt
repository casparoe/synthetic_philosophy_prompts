Between the mid-1950s and the 1980s the world's civil nuclear programmes converged on a single family of designs: reactors moderated and cooled by ordinary water. The convergence began somewhere unpromising. The United States Navy, needing a plant that was compact and easy to operate aboard a submarine, chose a pressurized-water reactor for the Nautilus; when the Atomic Energy Commission turned to civil power — a demonstration programme that spread its support across several competing concepts, water-cooled and non-water-cooled alike, followed by the Shippingport plant — the naval design arrived with an industrial base, a trained staff, and an obvious supplier attached. Britain built a gas-cooled fleet, Canada a heavy-water one, and France, which had its own gas-graphite line, abandoned it in the 1970s in favour of a licensed American pressurized-water design that it then mass-produced. By the 1980s light-water reactors held the large majority of installed capacity worldwide. The Soviet Union reached the same destination independently: its VVER is a pressurized-water design, developed in parallel with the West's.

Treat this as a case of path dependence, and run the analysis in both directions. What does the theory say was happening here — increasing returns to adoption, learning by doing, the entrenchment of an early lead — and does the fit hold, given that the first decisive adopter was a state programme rather than a market of competing firms? Work with a distinction the standard accounts tend to blur: technical lock-in (compatibility and standards), institutional lock-in (regulatory codes, licensing regimes, supply chains, trained workforces), and epistemic lock-in (the atrophy of the capacity even to evaluate the discarded alternatives). Say which of these the episode actually evidences and which is doing the explanatory work. Then the hard question: is this lock-in at all, or the selection of a genuinely better design? The strong claim requires a small, contingent initial advantage rather than a systematic one, and independent convergence on the same answer is normally taken as evidence against contingency — yet the same design family is also, by most engineering accounts, the simplest and cheapest to build. Say what would have to be true for "would the same outcome have arrived anyway?" to have a determinate answer, and whether this case supplies it. I don't need the QWERTY story rehearsed; what I want is where this episode departs from it. Close with what the episode shows about the theory: which of its claims the case supports, which it strains, and where the theory is simply silent.
