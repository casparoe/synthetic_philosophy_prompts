I have become convinced of the following claim and want to know how exposed it is. Take any two-player game whose rules treat the two players identically, in which play always ends after finitely many moves, in which the only possible outcomes are a win for one side, and in which a player is never worse off for having one extra piece of their own on the board: the first player cannot lose, and therefore wins. The argument is a strategy-stealing one — if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could make an arbitrary opening move and then adopt that strategy as their own, playing any other legal move whenever it calls for an already-occupied square; the spare piece can only help, so the first player would win too, which is impossible. What would count against this? Give me the assumptions the argument actually needs and, for each, a game that violates only that assumption, together with whether the violation is fatal or a boundary case; an honest verdict on the argument's non-constructive character — it certifies that a winning strategy exists without producing one — including whether that is a defect of the argument or of the fact proved, and whether it leaves the conclusion as idle knowledge; and which of these concessions a defender of the argument would make. Rank the lot from 'the argument is invalid' through 'the conclusion is true but the argument does no work' down to 'a curiosity that changes nothing,' and say where I should come out.
