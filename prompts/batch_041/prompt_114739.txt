I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been trying to think through the problem that pleasures seem to have nothing in common: the relief of a headache lifting, the taste of a ripe peach, the satisfaction of solving a puzzle, the warmth of a friend's company. Hedonism treats pleasure as a single quantity that can be summed and compared, yet these experiences do not share any obvious felt quality. I would like you to construct a thought experiment that isolates this difficulty. The case must involve two lives that are equal by every standard hedonistic measure—same total intensity, same duration, same number and distribution of pleasurable moments—but the pleasures in one life are of kinds that share no felt quality with the pleasures in the other, so that no common phenomenal property unites them. The case should be realistic, not science-fictional, and fit in one paragraph. It should not rely on the idea that some pleasures are higher and some lower; the heterogeneity should be horizontal, not a matter of rank. It should force a hedonist who treats pleasure as a single phenomenal kind to rank the two lives, while a hedonist who treats pleasure as a family resemblance or cluster concept must either declare the lives incomparable or admit that hedonism cannot generate a verdict. Then, in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) give the scenario; (2) state what the single-kind hedonist must say about which life is better and why; (3) state what the cluster-concept hedonist must say and why; (4) identify the one feature of the case that does the philosophical work and explain what happens if that feature is removed; (5) explain how a defender of hedonism would attack the case and whether that attack succeeds. Please do not cite any books, authors, or studies; I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
