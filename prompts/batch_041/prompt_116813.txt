In a graduate seminar I teach on anthropic reasoning, the unit on self-sampling versus self-indication is the one where I can no longer pretend the literature is converging. The two sides accept the same probability calculus, the same physics, and the same cases. Both agree that self-sampling -- reasoning as if you were a random member of some reference class of observers -- yields the Doomsday argument, and that self-indication -- letting the bare fact that you exist favor hypotheses containing more observers -- yields the presumptuous philosopher, who concludes that the universe is vastly larger than our best cosmology says, on the ground that he was more likely to be born into the larger one. Each side treats the other's signature result as a reductio, and in my reading I have yet to find an argument that moved anyone not already committed to its premise.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My hypothesis, offered to be tested rather than confirmed: this is not a disagreement about rationality but about what 'probability' is doing when the fact conditioned on is indexical -- one side answering 'given the world, where am I likely to be?', the other 'given where I am, what is the world likely to be?', different questions rather than rival answers to one. Test that against the strongest form of each side, including the reading on which the dispute is really about the prior over worlds (self-indication amounting to a prior weighted by observer count), the suggestion that the unsettled choice of reference class is doing the work, and the attempts to show the choice never makes a difference to what one should do. Then say what would count as progress here: a formal result, a distinction, or an admission that the question is ill-posed. State the diagnosis in your first two sentences and give the remainder of the space to arguing for it; no more than 600 words.
