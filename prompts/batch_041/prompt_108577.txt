I teach a senior seminar on perception and cognition (mixed bunch — some phil of mind, some psych) and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on cognitive penetration that students can't get through by reciting the usual standoff: some studies show top-down effects, skeptics say the effects are confounded by attention or memory or response bias, everyone shrugs. I want them to actually have to think about what penetration is and at what level it's supposed to happen.

Here's the setup I want the question to hang on. A lab I know ran this: participants see a word flashed for a few hundred ms ('red' or 'green'), then a patch that's ambiguous between red and green, presented briefly, and they say which colour it is. The word reliably shifts the reports. The PI wants to conclude that language penetrates perception. I'm not asking you to settle whether that study is real or good — treat the description as the whole of what we know about it.

The question should make them do three things. (a) Say what that result would need on top of itself in order to establish penetration in the sense the modularity crowd (Fodor, Pylyshyn) deny — the sense where the issue is about what information early vision can consult, not just whether cognition can nudge a report. (b) Name at least two explanations of the result that dont require penetration in that sense, and say what further evidence would seperate them. (c) Say whether the result, if it did hold up, would vindicate predictive processing. That last bit is the one I care about most, because about half the class thinks priors = penetration and I want to see whether they can spot why that's too quick.

Write me the question itself, a model answer of maybe 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points out of 20 marks, plus a one-line note per point on how students typically blow it. I want the scheme to actually seperate an A from a B — name the specific moves each has to make. Closed book, no phones, so no citations needed from them.
