You are a philosopher of mind whose work sits at the intersection of the metaphysics of mental states and the philosophy of cognitive science, and you have been asked to write the final examination for a graduate seminar on functionalism and multiple realizability. The examination must test whether students can treat multiple realizability as a substantive claim in its own right rather than as a slogan that automatically licenses functionalism. Before drafting, take a moment to lay out for yourself the distinct theses that tend to get run together in this area, and the points at which students typically slide from one of them to another; keep that preliminary thinking brief, then produce the examination materials themselves.

Produce all three of the following.

First, the question. One essay question, answerable in ninety minutes by a strong candidate, subject to these constraints.

- It must not be answerable by reciting the familiar sequence — multiple realizability refutes type identity, therefore functionalism, therefore the autonomy of the special sciences.
- It must force students to distinguish at least three claims that are usually conflated: (i) that a given mental state is in fact realized by different physical mechanisms in different creatures; (ii) that it is possible for this to be so; and (iii) that mental states are individuated by their functional role rather than by their physical constitution.
- For each of (i) and (ii), it must ask what follows for functionalism if the claim is true and what follows if it is false. In particular, students must address whether functionalism could survive the discovery that a given mental state has one and the same realization in every creature that has it, and whether finding a creature in which that state is realized differently would establish functionalism or merely be consistent with it.
- It must require them to confront the modal difficulty: if a mental state term names a functional state, and if such terms are rigid designators, then the identification of the state with its functional role looks as though it must hold of necessity, which sits badly with the apparent conceivability of a creature in the same functional state with no feeling at all. Students must say what a functionalist can say here without quietly falling back on a merely empirical claim about how things happen to be realized in our part of the universe.
- It should be answerable by a student who has done the reading but remembers no names: wherever the answer turns on a well-known argument, the student should be able to state the argument in their own words.

Second, a model answer of about 700 words, written at the level of a very good graduate student and doing everything the question asks. Where the answer invokes an argument associated with a particular tradition or line of thought, state it in your own words; supply no citations, and do not let the answer turn on who said what.

Third, a marking scheme: five must-hit points, twenty-five marks in total, with the marks for each point and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. At least one of the five should be a point that separates an A from a B answer rather than merely rewarding coverage.

Write the examination materials directly, without commentary on what you are doing.
