I sit on the appeals panel for my university's student hardship fund, and I am also a graduate student in philosophy who has been reading about luck egalitarianism — roughly, the family of views on which distributions should be sensitive to people's choices but not to the circumstances they merely find themselves in. Our panel meets Thursday, and our governing document is a mess. One clause directs us to award only where hardship arises from circumstances beyond the applicant's control; a separate discretionary clause permits an award whenever denying it would defeat the purpose for which the fund was established. I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting.

Please draft that opinion. It should recommend a concrete disposition for each of the three cases below, state the rule the panel should apply going forward, and give reasons my four colleagues could actually argue with rather than a summary of the literature. It should be able to stand on its own without the panel having read anything I have read.

The three cases. First, an applicant with a congenital autoimmune condition whose medication costs have outrun their stipend; the condition is undisputedly not their doing. Second, an applicant who accumulated substantial debt during a period of heavy gambling and now cannot cover rent; they say the gambling was a response to a depressive episode that has since been treated, and they are now in recovery. Third, an applicant who resigned a paid position to take an unpaid internship they believed was essential to their career, and who now cannot afford their share of the rent; the internship has not led to an offer.

Three things I want the opinion to take seriously rather than gesture at. One: whether the panel should treat an applicant's own preferences and valuations as part of what they chose, or instead as themselves a kind of circumstance — the difficulty being that the second applicant's taste for risk, and the third applicant's judgment about what their career required, are both in some sense theirs and in some sense not under their control. Two: whether a strict responsibility filter can be applied consistently at all, given that we are judging the quality of people's choices after the fact, with knowledge of how things turned out that they did not have at the time — and whether that asymmetry should make us more lenient or simply more transparent about our reasoning. Three: whether denying the second applicant is defensible if it leaves them below a decent minimum of housing and food, and if not, how the panel should state the exception without abandoning responsibility-sensitivity altogether.

The recommendation should specify a time frame for each disposition, and should flag explicitly which parts of my position I hold with confidence and which parts I would concede are contestable.
