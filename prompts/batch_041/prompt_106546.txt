I am setting the final examination for a final-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences, and I need one essay question on the ontology of generations, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question is worth 20 marks, candidates have 90 minutes, and they will have no internet access, so nothing in the question may turn on citations, dates, attributions, or reading lists.

I want the question to turn on a crux rather than on recall of the well-worn debate about whether generations are "real." To that end, the question should hand candidates a small piece of apparatus that the course has built, and require them to apply it rather than recite it. The apparatus is a three-grade test for the reality of a social category, which you should reproduce in your own formulation but preserving the following content:

- Grade 1 (nominal): the category is a partition we impose for convenience; its members share no causally relevant property, and they do not self-identify with the label.
- Grade 2 (interactive): the label has causal efficacy because people are classified by it, come to know that they are, and adjust their self-understanding and conduct accordingly, so that shared traits arise from the classification itself.
- Grade 3 (substantial): members share causally relevant properties arising from a common cause that is independent of the label, so the category would keep tracking real differences even if no one used it.

The crux I want tested is that the two standard objections to the reality of generations — that the birth-year boundaries are arbitrary, and that what looks like a cohort effect may be a period effect — bear very differently on Grade 3 than on Grade 2. Arbitrary boundaries are close to fatal to a substantial-kind reading, but they are not fatal to an interactive one, since a salient label can do its work even when its edges are drawn arbitrarily. Likewise, the linear dependency among age, period, and cohort (cohort being, by construction, period minus age) makes the empirical separation of cohort from period harder than it looks, but that difficulty does not by itself show that the label lacks causal efficacy. Candidates must also confront the residual worry that even the interactive reading collapses into a period effect plus a label: if anxiety, say, is rising across the whole population, does calling one slice of the trend "Gen Z" add anything causal? A top answer should say what would have to be true for the interactive reading to survive that worry, and what would falsify it. Finally, require candidates to state which grade they would assign to generations and what would change their mind.

Do not ask for citations, and make clear in the question that citations are neither required nor rewarded.

- "marking_scheme": an array of five or six objects, each with the fields "move" (a short name for the move), "requirement" (what an answer must do to earn it), "marks" (an integer), and "typical_failure" (one line on how candidates usually get it wrong). The marks must sum to 20.
- "total_marks": an integer, which must be 20.
