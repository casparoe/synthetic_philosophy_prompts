ok this is an odd one and I need help with a specific thing.

I'm a 4th-year PhD student in political theory, and for the last four years I've been one of the people running a tenants' group in my neighbourhood — it started as a group chat during a rent strike on our block and grew into about forty people with a steering committee, officers, votes, a mailing list, the whole apparatus. Next Thursday is our last meeting. We're dissolving. We came apart over whether to affiliate with the citywide tenant union (I was the one pushing for it, so I get to own that), and somewhere in there the fight stopped being about whether our demands were right and turned into being about who was loyal to whom. It was ugly and pretty banal at the same time.

I have to give the closing remarks. Five minutes, someone's living room, potluck, kids running around, maybe twenty-five people.

Here's what I want to try, and I want you to write the speech. I've been reading Simone Weil's note about abolishing political parties — she wrote it in London in 1943, a few months before she died at 34, and it wasn't published until years after that. I've got the Simon Leys translation, which is where I get the line that in the Anglo-Saxon world parties have "an element of game, of sport" about them, unlike the continental kind she's actually attacking. And I half believe her. As I read her, the claim isn't that party people are villains, it's structural: once you've taken the form, the form does the work. A party needs a shared passion the way a fire needs fuel, and passion doesn't care about truth, so the organization ends up manufacturing agreement instead of finding anything out — and after a while nobody inside it can tell the difference between "this is true" and "this is what we say." Which is an uncomfortably good description of our last six months, and I don't think it's because any of us were bad people.

So: thank people honestly, don't pretend we were a family and don't pretend we failed, take the charge seriously including against me (I'm the one who wanted the merger, I'm the one who started saying "we need to be unified on this"), and make Weil's point do real structural work rather than being a decoration I stick on top of a thank-you speech. It should shape what I can and can't say in the room. In the room: two of the original rent-strike organizers. Maria's been in the labour movement thirty years and thinks Weil is a luxury for people who never had to win anything. Dave thinks we died because we weren't disciplined enough — proper dues, real structure, and we'd still be here. And then twenty neighbours who came out for the rent strike and have never heard of Weil and would be perfectly happy to keep it that way. The speech has to land for all of them, and it has to be honest that I don't know whether she's right. She isn't offering a nicer party to join instead — she resigned from her job with the Free French that July rather than keep working alongside the factions — so "just do it without the party form" isn't obviously available to a tenants' group that needs to be able to sit across a table from a landlord as a bloc. I'd like the speech to feel that, not resolve it.

Two things I want at the end, clearly separated from the speech itself, because it's a speech and not a seminar: (1) a short note on where this leans on a contested reading of Weil — I'm aware the "structural" reading might be mine rather than hers, and that she might be after parties in particular rather than collective action as such — and where it takes liberties with her; and (2) what would change your mind: which argument or piece of evidence would make you think the structural claim is wrong, or that our collapse is better explained by money, burnout and one bad treasurer than by anything Weil-shaped. Tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to be weak. I'd rather have that than a confident verdict.

No quotations from Weil inside the speech — I can't check her wording and I don't want invented quotations floating around a room of people who'll take them at face value. Paraphrase her. And don't turn it into an essay with a thesis statement; it's five minutes in a living room, it should sound like a person talking, and it's ok if it ends unresolved.
