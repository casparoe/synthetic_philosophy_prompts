I'm a fifth-year PhD student in epistemology, and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper to our department's epistemology reading group in which I defend a dissolution of the dogmatism paradox. The puzzle, as I set it up, runs like this: if I know that P, then it seems I also know that any evidence which tells against P is misleading — after all, P is true, so whatever appears to count against it must be pointing me the wrong way; and if I know that a given piece of evidence is misleading, it seems I'm entitled to disregard it. Together those two steps appear to license a knower in dismissing counterevidence in advance, which is dogmatism, and it looks like it falls straight out of the possession of knowledge rather than out of any vice of the knower. My paper argues that the paradox trades on running together two things a knower might be entitled to: the entitlement to believe that a particular piece of counterevidence is misleading, and the entitlement to ignore that evidence in the conduct of inquiry. I claim the first is closed under known entailment from knowledge but the second is not, because the rational management of evidence answers to an openness requirement that belief itself does not; the dogmatist's conclusion only goes through if you assume that rational believing and rational inquiring answer to the same norms, and that assumption is precisely the point at issue.

The audience is where my trouble lives. It is a small group, eight or nine people, and it splits into three roughly equal camps. The largest is a set of evidentialists who work in a broadly Bayesian idiom; they will hear my distinction between believing and inquiring as an ad hoc maneuver for saving knowledge from the evidence, and they will want to know why the openness requirement is not simply a relabeled requirement of total evidence. Then there are two people in the knowledge-first tradition, whom I expect to be friendly to the thought that knowledge can itself be a source of entitlement but hostile to my insistence that inquiry answers to norms that belief does not — they will press me on whether I have just conceded that knowledge is insufficient for rational inquiry, and whether that concession costs me anything I actually care about. Finally there is one colleague in formal social epistemology who will almost certainly reframe the whole thing as a question about when a belief-revision policy is self-undermining. I need a prep sheet for the question period: the objections I will really get from these people, phrased in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they would do to my position rather than by how likely they are to be raised; for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is soft or gives away more than I should; what I should concede up front to buy credibility with the room; and the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks. Close by telling me what would change the sheet's verdict — the consideration that, if it turned out to matter in a way I am not anticipating, would mean I have prepped for the wrong room — and where my own argument, rather than the objections to it, is most likely to be the thing that fails.
