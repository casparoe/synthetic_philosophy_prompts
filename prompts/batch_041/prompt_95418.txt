I retired three years ago after 30-odd years doing fieldwork on seabird colonies, and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy of social science reading group at the university in town. Last week the topic was games where the "rational" move is to stop before anything gets going. Dev (the economist who runs the lab experiments) and Mara (the philosopher) kept it up in the parking lot for forty minutes afterward. I followed the first ten minutes and then lost the thread completely. Dev kept calling the thing a centipede, which I thought was a joke about the shape of the drawing. Here's the game, which I copied onto the back of an envelope while they went at it:

Two players, Ann and Bob, taking turns, Ann first. On your turn you either take — the game ends right there and you're both paid the amounts listed — or you pass, and the turn goes to the other person. If all four moves get passed, the game ends on the last line.

- move 1, Ann takes: Ann $1, Bob $1
- move 2, Bob takes: Ann $0, Bob $3
- move 3, Ann takes: Ann $2, Bob $2
- move 4, Bob takes: Ann $1, Bob $4
- nobody ever takes: Ann $3, Bob $3

Here's the gist of what they said. I've numbered the moves so you can take them one at a time.

(1) Dev: there's only one thing to do at every point, and you get it by starting at the end. On move 4 Bob takes, because $4 beats $3. On move 3 Ann knows that, so she takes, because $2 beats $1. On move 2 Bob knows she knows... and so it walks back to the start, and Ann takes on move 1 and walks off with $1 when they could have had $3 each. It looks absurd, but it isn't a paradox, it's a proof that a certain kind of reasoning leads somewhere we don't like.

(2) Mara: the walk back only works if every step is certain. For Ann to take on move 3 she has to be sure Bob takes on move 4 — and the only way Bob ever gets to move 4 is if she's already done something she wasn't supposed to. So the whole thing assumes the two of them are rational at positions that can only be reached if one of them isn't. Nobody is sure of that, and if Ann thinks there's even a one or two percent chance that Bob passes on move 4, then passing on move 3 is the better bet.

(3) Dev: run your own numbers. If Ann puts probability p on Bob passing on move 4, passing gets her 1 + 2p and taking gets her a flat 2. So she needs p above one half — better than even money that he'll do the wrong thing — before passing is right. One percent is nothing. It isn't a knife edge, it's a cliff, and you have to be standing right on the edge of it.

(4) Mara: that's these numbers. Change the last line so that Bob passing on move 4 pays me $100 instead of $3, and the threshold collapses — a two percent doubt is more than enough to make passing on move 3 correct. And then Bob has a reason to pass on move 2, and the unravelling stops unravelling the way you said. Which is my point: whether the induction holds up depends on details of the payoff table that have nothing to do with anybody's rationality.

(5) Dev: no, that's not a point about rationality, that's a point about which game we're playing. Change the payoffs and you've changed the game. The induction is still valid, the answer is just different. And your uncertainty story has the same trouble: you're stacking the doubt up as if it multiplies. It doesn't. Ann doesn't have to be sure how the whole game will go, she only has to be confident enough about the one move in front of her, and being confident about one move doesn't get diluted by the moves that come after it. There's even a proof that if rationality is common knowledge — I'm rational, you know it, I know you know it, and so on — then the induction's answer is what gets played.

(6) Mara: then here's the part I can't get past. Your argument tells Ann to take on move 1 partly on the basis of what she would do on move 3, and if she takes on move 1 she never gets to move 3. So the "rational" thing depends on the rationality of choices that never get made. That only makes sense if "rational" is a property of plans rather than of actions — and then the whole answer is only as good as the assumption that the plan gets carried out, which is exactly the thing in dispute.

What I want is a point-by-point verdict on those six moves — Dev, Mara, both, or neither — with the arithmetic worked out wherever it matters. I especially want to know whether Mara's $100 swap really does collapse the threshold the way she says, and whether Dev's "doubt doesn't multiply" is right or is a dodge. Then tell me what they're both missing, because they're both smart and neither one gave an inch, and I have a feeling the arguement isn't really about the game at all.
