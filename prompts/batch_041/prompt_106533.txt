I've been arguing with my flatmate about inheritance tax for like three weeks now, and at some point I realised we aren't even disagreeing about the same thing, we're disagreeing about what the thing is for. My view is the blunt one: the moment you're born, some people have already been handed a massive head start that they did absolutely nothing to earn, and if you take equal opportunity seriously — really seriously, not the watered down version where it just means there are no legal barriers keeping anyone out — then inherited wealth is exactly the kind of arbitrary advantage the whole ideal is supposed to cancel. So a steep inheritance tax isn't just a revenue measure, it's the thing that makes the rest of the story hang together. Without it, equal opportunity is just a promise that everyone runs the same race from wildly different starting lines.

His view, which he got from some podcast and has been repeating at me ever since, is that you shouldn't look at inheritance directly at all. You should ask what kind of deal people would agree to in advance if nobody knew whether they'd turn out to be the rich parent writing the will or the kid who inherits nothing. Set it up like an insurance market — what premium would you pay to protect yourself against the risk of being born into a family with nothing to pass on, weighed against how much you'd want to be able to leave something behind if you turned out to be the one with money? — and he says you don't get confiscation, you get some moderate tax with a principled reason for the rate instead of a number that just feels fair. He also thinks it handles the dynastic wealth worry better than my version does, because it can explain why it's bad for fortunes to persist across generations without having to pretend that wanting to leave something to your kids is itself morally suspect. He keeps saying the scheme has to be sensitive to ambition but insensitive to endowment, which I think is just a fancy way of restating his position, but whatever.

Can you compare these two properly — the brute luck leveling approach versus the hypothetical insurance approach — and actually tell me which one does better at the thing both of them claim to care about, keeping equal opportunity from being a fiction? I want the real arguement, not vibes. Where does each one get its grip, where does each one have to fudge something, and is there a case where they'd give the same answer for different reasons? Concretely: think about a family that parks a controlling stake in a company in a trust for four generations, versus a family that leaves a house and a savings account, versus a talented kid who'd blow any windfall anyway. Do the two views come apart on those, or converge? And does the insurance view secretly smuggle in the leveling view, or does it genuinely commit you to a different conclusion about dynastic wealth? No need to cite anyone, I'm not writing a paper, I just want the arguement itself.
