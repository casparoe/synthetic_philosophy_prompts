For some years I have been defending a position that its critics call illusionism, and I have reached the point where I want to know which parts of it are load-bearing and which are merely advertised. Stated flatly, the position has three claims. First, there are no phenomenal properties over and above representational and functional ones: nothing in the world answers to the idea of an intrinsic felt quality that introspection latches onto. Second, the conviction that there is such a thing is produced by a self-model that systematically misrepresents its own operations. Third, the apparent epistemic authority of introspection in this domain is to be explained away rather than deferred to. My own version adds a diagnosis that I suspect is where the argument actually lives: what the self-model renders is not experience as such but its determinacy and unity — the impression that at any moment there is a fully formed, sharply bounded experiential state — and it is that rendering, rather than any bare datum of presence, that I am calling the user illusion.

Tell me what would count against this, and be willing to tell me that nothing does. I want the objections sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, with each attached to the specific commitment it threatens, since I do not think all three claims stand or fall together. Four things interest me most. (i) The appearance/reality distinction on which the whole view rests: if an illusion must appear as something, does the position presuppose the very phenomenality it denies, or is that objection trading on an equivocation between appearing and being experienced? (ii) Self-defeat: if introspection is systematically unreliable about this, what licenses the introspective report from which the theory sets out? (iii) Which rivals the view is genuinely distinct from — deflationary realism, the identification of phenomenal with functional properties, outright eliminativism — and whether my added diagnosis of determinacy quietly pushes me toward one of them. (iv) What would count as empirical evidence for or against, including dissociations between what subjects report and what their processing delivers.

Finally, tell me where the position has been insulated rather than exposed: which qualifications — that there are no phenomenal properties of the kind you think there are; that the illusion is itself a seeming — are principled, and which are ad hoc. Say which of these my fellow travellers would themselves concede as counting against the view, not merely what its critics allege. If it turns out to be well insulated, tell me whether that is a defect in a position of this kind or simply what it is to make a metaphysical claim. I would be most moved by a case in which the apparent determinacy of experience turns out to do explanatory work that no account of the self-model can absorb, so if such a case exists, give it to me first.
