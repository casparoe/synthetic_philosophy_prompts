Write a mock prospectus, roughly 650 words, from the client-services department of a long-established firm of actuarial consultants that sells survival insurance to sovereign polities. Invent the firm: a name, a founding date in the middle of the nineteenth century, a motto. It offers continuity endowments to states, dynasties, and hegemons, and prices every policy off a reference class of historical polities. The piece should be deadpan throughout: no winking, no narrator stepping outside the document, no admission that the exercise is absurd, and no real forecasters, historians, or scholarship named. The comedy must come from the method being applied correctly and then followed where it goes.

Include, in this order:

- an opening assurance to the prospective client, in the firm's voice, that the premium reflects the outside view rather than any estimate of the client's own prospects, and that the client's historians and strategists are welcome to attend the pricing meeting in an advisory capacity;
- a rate table with three or four reference classes, invented but recognizable in kind — say confessional monarchies of the post-Napoleonic settlement, maritime trading republics, continental land empires, twentieth-century single-party states — showing how assignment to each class moves the premium, with additional columns for paid riders, among them an Exception Rider (the client asserts that its century is unlike the others) and a Decline Rider;
- a section headed Frequently Raised Objections, in which the firm's replies concede more than the objections do;
- a closing Notice to Clients in small-print register, containing two or three clauses whose interaction ensures the policy can never pay out.

Three things should be got exactly right, since they are the real subject of the joke.

First, the reference class is selected, not discovered. Class-Selection Advisory is available as a paid add-on; the same polity priced in two defensible classes yields two very different premiums; and the firm never says which class is correct, only which is most representative of the client's stated self-understanding.

Second, selection effects. The firm holds no observations of polities that failed to come into existence, cannot underwrite polities that no longer exist, and prices from a book composed entirely of clients who were worried enough to buy insurance — worry being, the prospectus notes in passing, itself a symptom.

Third, the Class of One. Somewhere in the table the firm offers a bespoke class containing only the client. Its historical survival rate is 100 percent and its premium is the lowest the firm offers; the prospectus is obliged to disclose this in a footnote, which also observes that the Class of One is the firm's most accurate product.

Let the reductio do the work. A reader who knows reference-class forecasting, base rates, and survivorship bias should recognize the argument being made against the practice; a reader who does not should still find it funny and finish it slightly uneasy.
