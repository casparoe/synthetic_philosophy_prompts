A friend of mine, a theoretical biologist, argues that the decline of the West is not a policy failure but a biological fact. Civilizations, he says, are organisms; organisms have a developmental program and a species-typical lifespan; senescence is not something legislation can reverse, so talk of reforming liberal institutions is like talk of training a ninety-year-old for a marathon. He cites Spengler and Toynbee approvingly and thinks modern biology has vindicated them.

Audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the organism, the developmental program, the species-typical lifespan, the environment, reproduction and inheritance, and death. Then set out where the mapping breaks — in particular, whether the biology he borrows actually contains the features his conclusion needs (a fixed lifespan, senescence as program rather than accumulated damage, a lineage that persists by reproducing). Tell me which features of the source are being quietly imported into the target, which of the disanalogies matter for the use he makes of the analogy and which are decorative, and whether his conclusion — that Western decline is fated and reform is beside the point — survives once they are on the table. If the analogy can be repaired, say how; if a different comparison would do the work better, name it and say what it captures that the organism does not.
