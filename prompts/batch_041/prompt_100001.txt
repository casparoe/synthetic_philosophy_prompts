My friend and I spent an afternoon at a museum last week and then argued for two hours about something neither of us could settle, and I want help sorting it out.

Here is the pattern that started the fight. A painting can be so heavily restored that only a fraction of its visible surface is original — later hands have repainted, relined, and revarnished it — and the museum still presents it as the artist's work and the public still treats it as the same valuable object. But if a scholar establishes that the very same painting was made by a forger rather than by the artist, the object doesn't lose a little value; it collapses to almost nothing, even though its appearance and its materials are untouched. My friend says this proves we care about the history of a thing rather than the thing itself, and that this is obviously correct. I pointed out the cases where the history seems not to matter at all. Museums display plaster casts of Greek bronzes, and Roman marble copies of lost Greek originals, as legitimate objects of attention. A photographic print made decades later from the photographer's own negative is nearly worthless next to a print the photographer made with his own hands, though the two can be visually indistinguishable. A modern replica of a vintage electric guitar sells for a small fraction of the original even when experienced players cannot tell them apart by ear. And when the art market wanted to sell infinitely copyable digital images, it simply invented a token that supposedly makes one copy the real one. What complicates it further is that the conservation profession's own ethics require restorers to make their additions reversible and detectable under ultraviolet light — as if the original surface were what counts after all.

So: what theory of value does this whole cluster of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Then tell me which rival views the practice actually rules out and which it merely happens to sit comfortably with. My suspicion is that it is underdetermined between a view on which value attaches to the causal chain running back to the artist's act and a deflationary view on which authenticity is just a positional good, its worth consisting in scarcity and the pleasure of ownership. Say whether the practices I listed presuppose one consistent theory or several incompatible ones, and if a heavily restored painting and a fresh forgery really are treated as opposites, tell me precisely what the practice must be tracking that separates them — and whether that thing is coherent. What happens when the forger was the artist's own student, or the restorer was the better painter? Finally, suppose a museum adopted the view that only perceptible properties matter and that a perfect copy is as good as the original. Tell me concretely what would have to change: what it would stop doing, what it would have to start doing, and what it could not do at all.

Please state how confident you are in the account you give, and single out the one point in it you are least sure of.
