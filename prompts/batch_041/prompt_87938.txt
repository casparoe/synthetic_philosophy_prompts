I'm giving a paper at a small workshop next month and I'd like a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis: karma, read as a theory of moral desert, is really a claim about character, not a ledger of owed pleasures and pains. Rough argument: when we say someone's suffering is deserved because of something they did, the cash value isn't that a cosmic bookkeeper owes them pain, it's that the suffering is a fitting continuation of the kind of agent they've become. The act isn't stored up and later redeemed, it shapes the person who later suffers. So desert survives without a debtor, and the standard complaint that it's unjust to make someone pay for what they can't remember doing misses the target, because on this reading nobody is paying for anything — the pain is just the shape of the life a certain kind of character produces. I call the two readings the ledger and the compost heap, and I'm arguing the compost reading is the one that keeps karma alive as an option for anyone who cares about desert.

The audience is about fifteen people. A third are Buddhist studies and Indian philosophy people, textual, they know the Abhidharma fights over how a karmic result can ripen in a later life, and they'll want to know whether my reading is in the texts at all or is a cosmic-justice gloss that grew up later. Another third are analytic philosophers working on free will, moral responsibility and punishment — they think in terms of control and tracing from act to sanction, and they'll be allergic to anything that sounds like its dissolving the agent. Two or three anthropologists and religious studies people who study karma as something people actually live with, and who think turning it into a philosophical thesis is a category mistake. Plus a commentator who I'm fairly sure is going after the beginningless-samsara point.

So: the objections I'll actually get, from whom, ordered by how much damage they do to the paper rather than how likely they are, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a one-line note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so it never comes up, and the one question I'm hopeing nobody asks. Two things to fold in. (a) The free-will crowd will ask what happens to desert when the character change is discontinuous: someone dies a miser, a child is born elsewhere with the same stinginess and no memory of any of it. I need a reply that doesn't just say it's the same character, because that sounds like I'm smuggling a self back in. (b) I want their sharpest version of the charge that I'm just doing modern constructive philosophy, answered on the merits rather than by me pleading that I'm doing constructive philosophy.
