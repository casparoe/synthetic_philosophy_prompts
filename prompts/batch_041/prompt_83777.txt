I'm a second-year PhD student in comparative literature, and I have a small departmental summer research fund plus a part-time research assistant, which together buy me roughly six weeks of work on a self-contained side project. What has been nagging at me since I first read Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — the book in which he collects dozens of Englishings of one short Renaissance French poem and sets them side by side with commentary — is not the virtuosity but the bookkeeping. Every version on offer claims fidelity to the original's form, and yet no two land alike, and neither Hofstadter nor the ordinary sense-for-sense/word-for-word vocabulary gives me a way to say what a given version actually spent. I want to turn that complaint into a proposal, so here is my half-formed idea, and I'd like you to make it into something fundable and doable.

The idea has two parts. First, I want to sort the bundle of features we casually call 'the form' of a fixed-form poem into two kinds: a constraint is load-bearing if abandoning it changes what kind of artifact the translation is (drop the rhyme scheme and you have not written a worse sonnet, you have written something else), and decorative if abandoning it leaves you with the same kind of thing, merely worse. My hypothesis is that this classification is not a property of the constraint but of the pairing of source and target traditions, so that the same constraint is load-bearing in one direction and decorative in the other — rhyme is a different animal in a rhyme-rich target language than in a rhyme-poor one, and a strict syllable count behaves differently in a language that counts syllables than in one that counts stresses. Second, and this is the part I actually care about, I suspect formal constraint does not merely restrict a translator; it launders invention. When a rhyme or a syllable count forces a word the source does not license, the finished text presents the translator's decision in the grammar of the author's intention, and a reader without the source cannot separate the forced from the chosen. I want a protocol that marks the difference: for each consequential word choice in a translation, was it necessitated by the constraint, licensed by the source, both, or neither? Concretely, I picture eight to ten short texts of two to eight lines in French and one other language I read; four constraint regimes of increasing severity, one of them deliberately non-isomorphic to the source's prosody (say, a regime I invent for the occasion that fixes the number of stressed syllables and the final consonant sound of every line while leaving syllable count free); two or three versions per cell; an audit performed independently by two coders; and then a small blind-attribution study in which readers see only the translation and mark which words they take to be the author's and which they take to have been dictated by the form.

I'm pitching this to my department's summer research competition, which wants a proposal of about 1200 words. What I need from you is that proposal, and I'd like it organised under exactly these headings, in this order, with roughly these proportions: Title and one-sentence statement of the question (two lines); Background: what is already settled here and what is not (about 150 words); The question, stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, including what would count as an answer in each direction (about 200 words); Why the obvious approaches leave it open — the sense-for-sense/word-for-word split, the 'form is part of meaning' line, the equivalent-effect line, and the auditing practice of translators themselves (about 250 words); Method, week by week, with the materials, the constraint regimes, the coding scheme, and how I would keep my own poetic incompetence from contaminating the results (about 300 words); Deliverable (about 100 words); What would count as failure or a null result (about 150 words); Scope and the risks I am underrating (about 150 words). Please refer to positions and arguments rather than to particular works: no citations, no reading list, no bibliography — I will add those myself. Where the idea is underdeveloped, sharpen it rather than padding it, and if part of what I have described cannot be done in six weeks by one person and an assistant, say so in the proposal and cut it.
