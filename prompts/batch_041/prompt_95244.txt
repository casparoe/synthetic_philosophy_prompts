I'm in a phil reading group thats been stuck for about a year on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. The skeptics say no: if both options are wrong then neither is obligatory, so theres no conflict of obligations; if one obligation beats the other then theres no dilemma, just a hard call. The realists say thats just a point about the logic of ought and misses the thing we care about -- cases where you're required to do A and required to do B, can't do both, and whichever you do you've wronged someone and still owe them something. We keep recycling the same two-patients-one-ventilator and promise-vs-emergency examples and nobody moves. My hypothesis is that the two sides aren't even answering the same question: one is asking what a moral theory should say about the world's moral ledger -- what's obligatory or permissible all things considered -- and the other is asking what it should say about the deliberator's situation, where ought is supposed to guide action under cost and uncertainty. I've been calling these the ledger view and the deliberator view, and I suspect the skeptics are really ledger theorists while the realists are deliberator theorists who keep using examples that only make sense on the ledger view. Is that the right diagnosis, or is this really a verbal dispute about ought, or a substantive disagreement about whether moral requirements can conflict at all? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress -- a distinction, a formal result, a better example, or nothing? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then explain. Be committed: give me a definite diagnosis and reasons, dont just lay out the options.
