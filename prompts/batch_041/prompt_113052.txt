I'm a 3rd year PhD student working on phil of mind / cog sci, and a friend in my seminar — he's writing his thesis on the meta-problem of consciousness — has an argument he says kills epiphenomenalism dead, and I've been staring at it for a week without finding the bad step. Should say up front what I'd like to keep: I'm not giving up causal closure, and I'm not willing to end up saying my own introspective reports are just noise. Here it is, tidied up:

(1) Epiphenomenalism says phenomenal properties are caused by physical goings-on in the brain but never cause anything physical in return. (2) My judging that this pain is sharp, and the utterance that expresses it, is a physical event. (3) That event has a sufficient physical cause, and the story about what produced it needn't mention the sharpness. (4) If an event has a sufficient cause that doesn't involve X, then X isn't a cause of that event. (5) So the sharpness isn't a cause of my judgment about the sharpness. (6) If my judgment about something isn't caused by that thing, then it isn't a reliable response to it — the fit between them, if there is one, is a coincidence. (7) So my judgments about my experiences don't track my experiences. (8) My judgments are the only evidence I have that experiences exist at all. (9) So epiphenomenalism pulls the rug out from under the only evidence for its own central posit.

He thinks (5)-(7) is where the epiphenomenalist dies, and he has a nice line for it: my zombie twin has all my physical states and makes all the same judgments, and the zombie isn't judging anything at all, so whatever it is that makes my judgments be about the sharpness, it can't be the sharpness doing any work. He says the only escape left is to say the phenomenal facts and the judgments just go together by some brute psychophysical law, with no causation needed — and that that's a pre-established harmony in new clothes: lawful luck is still luck, and luck gives you true belief that happened to land right, not knowledge. My problem is that I can run the same argument against things I'm fairly sure he doesn't want to give up. Take mathematical platonism: the fact that 7 is prime doesn't cause anything, so by (6) my belief that 7 is prime doesn't track any fact, and by the same route my belief in platonism undercuts the only evidence for platonism. Same for any view where the object of a judgment is abstract or otherwise causally inert. So either (6) is too strong and the epiphenomenalist gets to keep her evidence, or (6) is fine and a good chunk of metaphysics goes down with her. He says the parallel breaks because for maths I have proofs — the belief is justified by derivation, not by causal contact with the number — and introspection has no such inferential backstop, so the epiphenomenalist is stuck with brute correlation while the platonist isn't. I can't tell if that's a real disanalogy or a patch, and I'm also not sure what a causal connection buys you here that a lawful non-causal one doesn't — if causation is itself just a brute regularity at the bottom, the asymmetry between tracking and mere correlation looks like it might be carrying the whole argument.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? Price it out for the epiphenomenalist who wants to keep (6), and for the physicalist who'd rather just identify the sharpness with the physical state (I take it that's the identity theorist's escape, and it costs her the distinctness of the phenomenal, which was the whole point), and for the interactionist, and for the person who says the phenomenal concept is deployed in a way that fixes reliability without any causal push from the quale. And tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong — what you'd have to be shown, or which argument would flip you — because I half suspect the interesting answer is that (6) is true but harmless, and I want to know what would make you say that.
