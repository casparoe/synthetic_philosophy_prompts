The student wrote:

Kim's exclusion argument starts from the causal closure of the physical, the supervenience of the mental on the physical, and the exclusion principle. Suppose a mental property M supervenes on a physical base P*. P* causes a physical effect E. If M also causes E, then E has two sufficient causes, which is overdetermination. Since overdetermination is not acceptable, M is excluded. Kim's causal inheritance principle says that M's causal powers are inherited from P*, so M and P* are really the same cause under different descriptions. That means M can cause E without adding a new cause, so there is no overdetermination. But Kim thinks this only works if M is identical to P*. If M is irreducible, then M is epiphenomenal. So non-reductive physicalism is unstable.

A standard reply is the levels reply: higher-level properties can be causes even if they supervene on lower-level ones. A traffic jam is caused by the volume of cars, even though the volume supervenes on the positions of individual cars. Kim responds that this just restates non-reductive physicalism. I think the reply fails for mental-to-physical causation, but Kim's argument does not threaten mental-to-mental causation. A mental event can cause another mental event without any physical effect being overdetermined. So the conclusion should be that mental properties are epiphenomenal with respect to physical effects, but they can still cause other mental effects.
