I'm putting together the take-home midterm for a graduate formal-epistemology course, and I've been asked to write the one long question for the unit on probability kinematics. The students have already done Bayes' rule, strict conditionalization, and Jeffrey's rule, so I want a question they cannot pass by reciting the definition of probability kinematics or by announcing that Jeffrey's rule is just conditionalization for uncertain evidence. What I want them to have to do is actually run the rule on a case where the order in which the two pieces of soft evidence arrive changes the answer, and then to say what that does to the standard claim that Jeffrey conditionalization is the uniquely rational response to uncertain evidence. To keep the item self-contained, the question should supply its own numbers: a system is in one of four states with prior probabilities 0.4, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3; let A be the set of the first two states and B the set of the first and third; you then receive two independent pieces of soft evidence, one of which should move P(A) to 0.8 and the other of which should move P(B) to 0.7. I also want the question to put a proposal into a hypothetical student's mouth — that rather than updating twice, one could apply a single rule that selects the distribution closest to the prior in the Kullback–Leibler sense among those satisfying both constraints P(A) = 0.8 and P(B) = 0.7 — and to ask whether that single-shot answer agrees with either sequential answer, and which of the three, if any, deserves to be called correct. Please write the exam question itself, with whatever sub-parts you judge necessary; a model answer of roughly 600 words; and a marking scheme that names the must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of 20, and a one-line note for each point on how students typically get it wrong. Assume the student has a calculator and about 45 minutes.

Two things I care about in the model answer. First, it should make the rigidity condition explicit and use it, rather than gesturing at it, and it should say clearly why the two updates fail to commute in this particular case — I want the dependence of the two partitions to be pointed out with the actual numbers, not just asserted. Second, it should be honest about its own footing: for each substantive claim, state how confident you are, and single out the one point you are least sure of — I expect the commutativity condition and the exact behaviour of the KL-minimizing distribution to be the two places where the answer is doing real work, and I'd rather the model flag its uncertainty there than bluff. If you think the "which one is correct" sub-question admits no clean verdict, say so and defend that, but don't leave the students with a shrug.
