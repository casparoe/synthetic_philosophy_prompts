I've been roped into running a session at our local library's monthly big questions discussion group — grown-ups, mostly retired teachers and a couple of nurses, no philosophy training, forty-five minutes and a flipchart. The topic I picked, slightly on a whim, is supererogation: the idea that some actions are morally good but not morally required. Don't explain the concept to me; I know roughly what it is. What I want is a plan for how to teach it. The thing that worries me is that the whole topic only makes sence once you've got a picture in place of morality as having a floor (what you mustn't do), a middle (what you must do), and then — supposedly — a space above the middle that isn't required of you at all. So the question of what has to come first is a real one, not a formality. I've half-decided to build the session around the old theological distinction between precepts and counsels — the commandments that bind everyone versus the counsels of perfection that are recomended but not commanded, poverty and chastity and so on — because that's where the word comes from and it makes the idea feel less like a modern invention. But I'm not sure it's the right carrying example for this crowd, or whether it just imports a religious frame they'll bounce off.

So: what order do the pieces have to go in — do they need the precepts/counsels thing first, or a more everyday case, or the puzzle first? The puzzle being that if one act really is morally better than another, how can doing the lesser one ever be permissible, which makes supererogation look impossible rather than obvious. What single example should carry the whole thing, and what does it have to have (and not have) to do that work — because I suspect the grenade-jumping soldier is too dramatic and too quick, and 'giving to charity' is too vague to bite. Which misconceptions will they form, in the order they'll form them, and at exactly which point in the sequence I should head each one off — I'm already expecting at least three: that supererogation just means being extra nice, that it's a matter of degree rather than a category, and that anything genuinely better must therefore be required. And what's the one question I can put to them at the end that would show me whether the idea actually landed, rather than just that they can repeat the word back. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level and say so — wether the whole category is even coherent, or the demandingness literature about morality asking too much of us.
