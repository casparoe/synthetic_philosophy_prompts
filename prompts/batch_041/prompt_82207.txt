I'm putting together the final paper for my third-year epistemology module and I want the essay question to sit on the bootstrapping problem and the problem of easy knowledge. The trouble is the standard version of that question writes itself: gas gauge, 'reliabilism licenses bootstrapping', 'ah but that's just easy knowledge', done. They've all read that. I want a question they can't answer by reciting it, but that they can still answer cold — no reading list, no names needed in the answer.

So I want it built on a specific contrast plus a specific principle. Here's the contrast, and please put both cases into the question in roughly this form:

(A) The gauge. You want to know whether your fuel gauge is reliable. You glance at it, it reads 'full', you form the belief that the tank is full, you note that it read 'full' and the tank is full, you do this a dozen times over, and you conclude that the gauge is reliable. Received intuition: hopeless. You can't certify a instrument by trusting it.

(B) The specs. You want to know whether your eyesight is reliable. You look in the mirror, see that your eyes look healthy and your glasses aren't smudged, and conclude that your eyesight is in good working order. Received intuition: fine, or at least not obviously vicious. Nobody thinks checking your glasses is circular in the bad way.

And here's the principle, which you should present in the question as the thing to be assessed — I'll name it after the student who came up with it, the Different-Question Principle:

You may use a process to gather evidence bearing on its own reliability provided that the question you are using it to answer is not a question about that process's own output on that very occasion.

She thought that bans (A) — the question you're using the gauge to answer, when you're certifying it, is about the gauge's output — and permits (B) — the question ('are my glasses clean?') isn't about your eyes' output on that occasion. I think it's too neat, and I want the exam to expose it.

So the essay question should make them do three things, without the question giving any of them away: (i) run the principle against (A) and (B) and say whether it gets both right; (ii) find at least one further case where the principle gives the wrong answer, or where it's plainly doing no explanatory work; and (iii) say what the real crux is if the principle fails — i.e. what any decent account of the difference between (A) and (B) would have to explain.

Deliverables: the essay question itself (45 minutes, 20 marks), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a first-class answer has to make and the specific errors that should cost marks, with a one-liner on each about how students typically go wrong. Keep it in British English throughout — the students recieve the paper and I've had complaints about mixed spellings. And please don't let the model answer lean on any names, titles or reading: the whole point is that it's answerable from the cases as given.
