For about a year now, a reading group I'm part of has been going around in circles on the axiom of choice, and I've come to doubt that the two sides are really disagreeing about the mathematics. We all accept the independence results of the 1930s and 1960s; nobody is waiting for a proof or a refutation from the usual axioms. The positions are stable, and I can state them concretely.

The classical analyst holds that there is a fixed universe of sets, and that in it every family of nonempty sets has a choice function as a matter of fact: understand what a set is and the function is simply there. The axiom is not a convenience but a truth about the cumulative hierarchy, and the dramatic consequences — a well-ordering of the reals, the Banach-Tarski decomposition, sets with no measure — are theorems to be absorbed rather than reductios. He would not do analysis without the weaker choice principles, and he sees no principled place to stop short of the full axiom.

The constructivist holds that an existence claim has to be backed by a construction. Given infinitely many pairs of socks, no rule selects one from each pair, and the axiom's assertion that a selection exists anyway is not, for her, a statement of mathematics: it posits objects that no procedure can identify. She would not say the axiom is false so much as refuse to let 'there is' float free of 'we can find.' And yet she uses countable and even dependent choice without hesitation, because analysis is unworkable without them.

The pluralist says axioms are neither true nor false but instruments, and that since there is a whole spectrum of choice principles between countable choice and full AC, the interesting question is which strength is required where.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed: the impasse is not about the axiom's consequences, on which the three agree, but about what kind of reason may justify an axiom at all. One standard demands that the axiom be evident from the concept of set itself; the other asks only that it earn its keep through the mathematics it makes possible. If so, the analyst and the constructivist are applying different standards of justification, and each finds the other's standard beside the point. I also suspect three questions are being run together: whether a choice set exists as an object, whether a choice function can be defined, and whether it is legitimate to reason with an arbitrary unspecified element — the move that even the constructivist makes in every epsilon argument.

I don't want a survey of the axiom's history or a list of its equivalents. Give me a diagnosis: which disputes here are verbal and which substantive; whether the disagreement is about mathematics, about ontology, or about standards of justification; whether the independence results bear on it at all; and whether my hypothesis is right, wrong, or half-right. Then say what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that the impasse is exactly what one should expect.
