I've been going down a rabbit hole on the AI safety stuff about keeping a system "low impact" -- the basic idea being that a powerful model shouldn't go around rearranging the world as a side effect of whatever it's actually trying to accomplish. The usual way people implement it is a penalty term: you score the agent partly on how much it changes the world relative to some baseline, or on how much it shrinks what other agents could still go on to achieve, and then you try to minimize that on top of whatever goal it's been given.

Here's the thing that keeps bugging me. Run the same measure on two cases. Case one: a system that, on its way to fetching me a coffee, quietly disassembles the furniture and rewires the building's electrics -- obviously you want the penalty to bite there. Case two: a system that cures a disease, or talks a government out of a war. By any measure of how different the world ends up, case two is a vastly bigger impact than case one. So "impact" can't mean "how much the world changes"; it has to mean something like "how much the world changes in ways that aren't part of what the agent was trying to do." But the whole selling point of these measures is that you can bolt them onto any goal without knowing in advance what the goal is -- that's the point, you don't have the value specification yet, that's what you're trying to avoid needing. And yet seperating the intended change from the side effect seems to need exactly that specification. You can't tell whether the rewired building is a side effect or the whole point unless you already know what the thing was for.

Then there's the baseline problem. If the penalty is "difference from what would have happened if the agent had done nothing," then doing nothing is the gold standard, which sounds like it privileges the status quo for no good reason -- as if the world exactly as it happens to be right now is the neutral one and any departure from it needs justification. An agent that lobotomized itself into being unable to act would score perfectly. But if you don't use inaction as the baseline, what do you use? Every alternative I can think of (the most likely outcome, the outcome under some reference policy, the outcome if the agent sticks to its "default" behavior) seems to smuggle in an assumption about what the world should have looked like.

So what does this practice actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory, not a survey. Is it committed to there being a fact of the matter about the counterfactual baseline, and if so, which one? Does "side effect" have any content that doesn't depend on a prior notion of what the agent is supposed to be doing -- i.e. does the whole approach secretly require the very value specification it was designed to let you skip? Does the measure treat change itself as presumptively bad, and if so is that a defensible conservatism or just a bias toward whatever happens to be? And where is the practice underdetermined -- what could you keep, and what would you have to give up, if you dropped the inaction baseline, or dropped the idea that impact is a value-free quantity you can measure before you know what matters? I'd also like to know which parts are actually load-bearing versus which are just how the current implementations happen to be written.
