I am a novelist, finishing a book whose ending is fixed by history — my reader knows from the first page how it turns out — and in three weeks I am to host a public conversation at a literary festival with a philosopher of history. Her manuscript argues a thesis I find at once attractive and evasive: that hindsight bias, as the psychologists describe it, is not a distortion of an otherwise sound retrospective judgment but a description of what it is to occupy the retrospective stance at all, so that there is no unbiased retrospective judgment for the bias to be measured against; and that the so-called narrative fallacy is the price of a genuine cognitive achievement, since it is narrative, and not the chronicle or the statistical series, that allows us to explain a stretch of the past rather than merely record it. She is fluent and generous, and rather good at restating her thesis in fresh words whenever she is pressed; I would like questions that make that harder.

Please draft ten questions, grouped into three movements, each question accompanied by a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — that is, a conditional instruction of the form "if she says X, put Y to her." The questions should turn on the content of her position rather than its reputation: nothing about how she came to the view, nothing about what it means for the humanities.

Each movement should carry at least one question on the matter I have allotted it, with the rest free to follow wherever the pressure is greatest. Movement One, the charge: the asymmetry between explaining an outcome and having been in a position to predict it — I want to know whether her defence of retrospective certainty depends on that asymmetry or could survive its collapse, and what becomes of her view if we can sometimes predict a class of events without being able to explain any single member of it. Movement Two, the defence: the practical stakes, where I take her to be weakest — the missed diagnosis that looks obvious afterwards, the general or the regulator judged by how it turned out, the poker player's habit of calling a decision bad only once the card has fallen badly. Movement Three, the craft: the difference, which I need for my own book, between saying that the outcome was inevitable given the facts and saying that the facts were as determinate as the narrative makes them sound — I suspect she needs the first and cannot afford the second, and I should like to watch her try to keep both. That movement should also carry a question testing a half-formed coinage of mine, which I would like her to attack: foresight bias, by which I mean the mirror image of her subject, the systematic overestimate by those in the middle of events of how open their own present was.

Write the questions so that each is answerable without prior acquaintance with the literature, supplying within the question itself whatever term of art it needs.

Please give me the response in the following sections, in this order: "Framing," sixty to eighty words, my own statement of what is actually at issue between us; "Movement One: The Charge," three questions with their follow-ups; "Movement Two: The Defence," three questions with their follow-ups; "Movement Three: The Craft," three questions with their follow-ups, one of them the foresight-bias question and at least one on what the novelist's inevitability effect owes to the historian's; "Closing Question," a single question of forty to sixty words that I can put to her last; and "Where I Expect Her to Slip," one hundred to one hundred and fifty words of notes to myself on the evasions I should anticipate and the follow-up I should keep in reserve.
