I'm a novelist, halfway through a book about a man who gets his sight back in middle age, and I've hit the bit where I need an actual thought experiment rather than the armchair version of the old Molyneux problem — you know, the one where someone blind from birth is suddenly given sight and we ask whether they could tell the sphere from the cube just by looking. So put on two hats for me: philosopher of perception who knows this debate cold, and working vision scientist. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming a few candidate cases out loud before you commit to one.

Here's the thing I want engineered. I want a case where the side that says "no, not a chance without touching them first" turns out wrong and the side that says "yes, obviously" turns out right — the person really can sort spheres from cubes by eye alone — and yet we still wouldn't say they see the shapes. They sort them correctly, but neither object looks solid or three-dimensional to them; both are flat patches. And the sorting is being done by some non-spatial cue (shading, outline, whatever you pick), not by any grip on depth.

Constraints: keep the scenario to a single paragraph, set it somewhere ordinary (a clinic room, a front room, nothing exotic), no miracles and no appeal to real named patients or published studies — I can't look any of that up and neither can you, so don't lean on it. The recovery should be plain clinical stuff, not a lightning bolt.

Then, after the scene: walk me through what each side of the old debate would say about it and why, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the real work, say what happens if I take that feature away (does it just collapse back into the plain Molyneux question?), and finish with how someone who thinks shape perception is innate would try to defuse the whole thing.
