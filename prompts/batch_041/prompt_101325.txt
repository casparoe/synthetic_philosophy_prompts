Last night a colleague and I spent three hours arguing about whether the recursive-amplification picture of deliberation is a genuine idealization or a redescription. The picture: an agent answers a question by decomposing it into subquestions, farming each out to a slower, less capable copy of itself, combining the returned answers, and bottoming out in the unaided agent — so that "what I would conclude if I thought about it long enough" is defined as the fixed point of that process rather than as an oracle's output. My friend's line is that the whole thing collapses the moment you notice the decomposition is chosen by the very bounded agent being idealized, so the "ideal" is indexed to a choice the ideal agent ought not to have to make, and different decompositions will hand you different fixed points; my line is that a version survives if the ideal is defined as a fixed point over successive refinements of the question itself, so that no single decomposition is privileged. I'm presenting that version next week and want Q&A prep. The audience is a reading group of about ten: mostly computational complexity theorists, two Bayesian decision theorists, and one epistemologist who works on coherentist justification. They are friendly to formalization, allergic to hand-waving, and none of them has read anything on AI alignment; they will want to know that the object is well-defined — that it exists, that it is unique, and roughly what it would cost to reach — before they are willing to discuss whether it is the right object. Give me the objections they will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary rather than the alignment literature's, ordered by how much damage each does to the thesis; for each, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft; which objection I should concede up front; and the one question I am hoping nobody in that room thinks to ask.
