I am preparing a session for a seminar on Camus, and I want to approach capital punishment not as the question of whether it is justified, but as an institution whose shape betrays what it assumes. Please take the modern retentionist practice as the object of study and reconstruct the theory it presupposes.

Here is the practice, set out in some detail so that we are looking at the same thing:

- The death sentence is pronounced in a courtroom, as the conclusion of a trial that aims at certainty of guilt and at a penalty proportioned to the gravity of the crime; the sentence is a public finding, and its author is the community, not the victim's family.
- The execution itself is scheduled and then repeatedly postponed through years of appeal, so that the condemned lives for a long time under a date that keeps receding.
- The state requires that the condemned be competent to be executed: a prisoner who has become psychotic is held back until he can understand and take part in his own killing.
- The condemned is permitted to waive his remaining appeals and so to hasten the execution, and the state then describes what follows as his own choice.
- The killing is regulated in minute detail: a fixed method, a fixed dose or sequence, an explicit requirement that death be painless and dignified, a last meal, a last statement, a hood or screen, a small room of official witnesses, and no public.
- Medical and correctional authorities are deeply implicated, yet the professional bodies of physicians forbid their members to take part in the act itself.

What I want from you is the implicit theory of this whole pattern. In particular:

(a) If the deserved response to the worst crimes is death, why must the death be painless, orderly, and hidden? Is the requirement of a painless death the relic of a moral standing the condemned is supposed to have forfeited, or is it only the state's concern not to be seen torturing? Can a retributive justification and the demand for a dignified killing be held together, or does one expose the other as insincere?

(b) The competence requirement and the volunteering cases pull the other way: here the state insists that the prisoner understand what is happening and not be coerced at the moment of death, as though his consent mattered. Does the practice therefore presuppose that the condemned retains a kind of sovereignty over his own death — and if so, is the state executing him or assisting a suicide? Is that consistent with the retributive reading, or does it require a different account of what the state is doing?

(c) Camus called the death penalty "the most premeditated of murders," and observed that the practice kills a man who has been warned of the day and kept for months at the disposal of those who will kill him. Does the modern practice, with its long death row and its endless scheduling, bear out that description — does it presuppose something about time, waiting, and the state's relation to the condemned that a bare account of deserved punishment would miss?

Then give me your verdict, and give it as a verdict. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit; say which single commitment is doing the real work; and tell me whether the institution, as described, can be made coherent, or whether it is a retribution it will not perform openly and must disguise as a medical procedure. I do not want a survey of the arguments for and against capital punishment; I want your reconstruction of the theory the practice cannot do without, and your judgment on whether it holds.
