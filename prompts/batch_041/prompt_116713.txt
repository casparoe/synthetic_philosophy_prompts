I never studied any of this formaly, I just got weirdly into game theory a few years ago and read my way through a couple of textbooks on my own, and now I run a small weeknight thing at my flat — seven or eight friends, an hour and a half, no economists in the room. Talking at them doesnt work, they nod and forget everything. So I want to build an actual game with rules and scores that makes them feel something that bothered me for weeks when I read it, and I can't work out how to stage it.

The thing I want them to hit: a payoff table where the middle option is strictly worse than flipping a coin between the top and bottom options, but isn't worse than either of those on its own. Roughly like this — rows are your choice, columns are what the other side (or just the world, if you want it simpler) does:

top: 3 if left, 0 if right
middle: 1.4 either way
bottom: 0 if left, 3 if right

If all you can do is pick one row and live with it, middle looks like the grown-up choice: never the worst outcome. But a 50/50 flip between top and bottom pays 1.5 no matter what, so middle is worse than a coin flip in both columns — which means middle is never the best thing to do against anything at all, while still never being the worst. The safe option is dead and it looks perfectly alive.

I want them to walk into that themselves. Rough plan: they play something for a few rounds, most of them settle on the safe middle and feel clever about it, then I let them commit to a mixed plan in advance and the middle dies in front of them — and once middle is gone, something else that looked fine becomes the new safe-but-dead option, so the deleting keeps going and they're the ones doing it. Points on a scoresheet or sweets, no real money, but they have to actually care.

So: the rules, the payoff sheet, how the rounds and the pre-commitment work, and a debrief that carries them from the game to the general point, including the bit I still find strange — never the worst is not the same as could be the best. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time. I bet someone says they'd just play top and hope, and I don't want to answer that with a lecture. And be straight with me about what this can't show. I suspect the coin flip is doing more work than the payoffs are, and that letting them talk between rounds turns it into a different thing entirely, but I don't know which of those is the real limitation.
