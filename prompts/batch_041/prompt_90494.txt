I've been in a reading group on cultural evolution for about a year now and we keep circling the same fight without ever resolving it, so what I want is a diagnosis rather than another round of it. On one side are the meme people, who take Dawkins's basic move seriously: culture contains genuine replicators, units that get copied with variation and then differentially retained, so cultural change is a Darwinian process in the same sense biological change is, just with a different replicator riding along. On the other side are the Sperber-style epidemiology-of-representations people and the Boyd/Richerson dual-inheritance people, who say cultural transmission mostly isnt high-fidelity copying at all -- what gets passed on is reconstructed by each learner and pulled toward attractors set by human cognition, so the explanatory work is done by transformative biases and by the ecology of minds rather than by replicator dynamics. Then there's a third bunch, the philosophers of biology, who keep insisting the whole dispute is confused because nobody has agreed on what makes a process Darwinian in the first place: do you need replicators at all, or is heritable variation in a population with differential reproduction enough, in which case something like Godfrey-Smith's distinction between replicators and reproducers is supposed to dissolve the fight instead of settling it. My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but three stacked on each other -- a definitional dispute about what counts as a Darwinian process, an empirical dispute about how faithful cultural transmission actually is and at what grain of description, and a dispute about explanatory aims where one side wants to explain the population distribution of variants while the other wants to explain why particular variants are stable across minds. If that's right, then a lot of the literature is people arguing about the first while believing they're arguing about the second, and no amount of fresh experimental or ethnographic data would move it.

So diagnose it for me, with reasons, engaging what each side actually claims rather than giving me a survey of the field. Is my three-way split the right cut, or is there a better one -- for instance, is the real fault line about whether cultural items count as individuals at all, or about whether a Darwinian explanation is a claim about mechanism versus just a claim about statistical population dynamics, or is it after all a largely verbal dispute about the word Darwinian that would evaporate if everyone dropped it? Tell me which parts of the impasse are substantive and which are terminological, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a formal result, a decisive finding about transmission fidelity, a conceptual distinction, or nothing at all because the question is ill-posed as stated. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and dont restate my question. And be self-critical about it: say where your diagnosis is most likely to be wrong, and which argument or piece of evidence would actually change your mind.
