I work as a program officer at a small international foundation, and for the past several years our grantmaking eligibility has been tied mechanically to a country's standing in one of the standard democracy indices: a single continuous score, aggregated from expert-coded indicators, published annually, with named category thresholds. A country that falls below the threshold for the top category loses access to one stream of funding and gains access to another, overnight, by a decision made in an office thousands of kilometres away. This year the country I am responsible for — where I have lived and worked for a decade — crossed that line. Nothing I can observe changed. The same parties govern, the same newspapers publish, the same courts rule, the same people I have dinner with describe their political life in the same terms. Yet the category we apply to them, and the paperwork I now have to file about them, changed completely.

I have come to suspect that the practice I administer rests on philosophical commitments I never chose and cannot fully defend, and I would like you to reconstruct and then assess them. Take the design of the practice as the object: one scalar value standing in for democracy, commensurating dimensions that may not be commensurable; uniform application across polities with radically different histories; a threshold that converts a continuum into a verdict; measurement performed on a country without its inhabitants' consent; and consequences attached by external bodies — the same institutions that treat the score as a neutral fact of the matter also route the decisive judgment through a political organ, and the same index that publishes a number also concedes, in its own methodology, inter-coder disagreement and retroactive revisions.

So: what conception of democracy does this practice presuppose? Does it require that democracy be a single measurable property of a polity, or only that it be usefully representable as one? Does the threshold presuppose that "democratic" has sharp boundaries, and that vagueness in the concept is an artifact of our ignorance rather than a feature of the thing? When the practice speaks of backsliding, what baseline does that presuppose, and is the baseline conceptual or historical? And is the concept doing descriptive work at all, or is it, as its critics say, a partisan label wearing the costume of a measurement — encoding a specifically liberal prior about what democracy must be and then presenting the prior as a finding?

Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with; I suspect much of it is underdetermined and would be licensed by several incompatible theories of democracy, and I want to know which parts are load-bearing and which are decoration. Say also whether the measurement strand and the political-judgment strand are consistent, or whether the practice is quietly running two incompatible theories of what makes a regime democratic.

I am asking for a committed answer, not a survey. Give me a definite verdict on whether a scalar score with a threshold can be defensible as a trigger for real consequences, and if it can, state the terms on which it can and what it must give up. If it cannot, say so plainly and tell me what should replace it — even though my job depends on the thing you may be condemning. Argue from first principles rather than pointing me at literature; I am not asking for sources, and a reading list would only postpone the judgment I need.
