Next month I am giving the opening remarks at a small ceremony unveiling a permanent exhibit on the early Cold War at our county historical society. The room holds about eighty people: a third are veterans, or the widows and children of veterans; the rest are local officials, museum donors, two high-school history classes, and several teachers who will notice if I fudge the historiography. I speak immediately before a retired colonel who will describe Soviet expansion in the late 1940s, and who is, for most of the room, the reason they came.

My own work concerns how historians assign responsibility, and I have come to think that the question the exhibit's timeline quietly answers — who started the Cold War — is the wrong question. Not because the evidence leaves the answer open, but because the form of the question smuggles in a model of explanation that fits a bar fight better than a decades-long structural rivalry. I want to say that in public without lecturing and without hedging.

Write the remarks, ten minutes when spoken. Two constraints govern everything in them. First, the room must leave feeling that the people it honors were honored, the colonel's audience included; I am not willing to be the person who implies that what they lived through was nobody's doing. Second, I am not willing to assert anything I take to be false, and I take it to be false that a single agent started it.

Build the remarks around a distinction I could defend in a footnote but must make land without one: between causally contributing to an outcome, being morally to blame for it, and bearing responsibility for what comes next. The speech should show, in plain language, why "who started it" collapses those three, why the collapse is precisely what makes the question feel at once urgent and unanswerable, and why surrendering the question need not mean surrendering judgment. It should make the point legible to a sixteen-year-old without condescending to the teachers, and it should end forward-looking, on responsibility rather than blame, so that it lands as generous rather than evasive.

After the speech, add a short note naming the two or three sentences most likely to be heard as an accusation by the colonel's audience, and give me an alternative for each.
