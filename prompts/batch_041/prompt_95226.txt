I'm in a seminar on free will and the neuroscience of it and I have to hand something in next week, and I've realised I dont want to write another essay on whether Libet's experiments kill free will. We have the same two moves every week: someone says the brain gets going before you're aware of deciding, so your conscious will is an afterthought; someone else says the experiments only show what happens when you flick your wrist for no reason, so they prove nothing; then we all go home. What nobody does is say what a theory of free will would actually have to deliver to count as a serious option in the light of that work. So give me the spec instead of the verdict: the full list of desiderata, what any adequate theory in this area has to get right, with the case or consideration behind each item.

And I mean the whole zoo, not just the headline result: the readiness potential that ramps up a few hundred milliseconds before the movement; the fact that the moment people report first being aware of wanting to move lands only a couple hundred milliseconds before the muscle fires; the later version where subjects can cancel a movement that's already been prepared, in the last fraction of a second; the reanalysis that says the ramp is just background neural noise crossing a threshold and only looks like a decision signal because everything gets averaged to the moment of movement; the decoding studies that call the choice seconds ahead; the fact that a slow ramp of the same shape shows up in situations where nothing is being willed at all; the clock-reading method used to time the urge, which is itself something we don't fully trust; and the patients whose hand does things they insist they didn't do. Each of those is something a theory has to accommodate or explain away, and I want to know which. At some point the list should also force a decision about what the thing even is, since the experiments test something much narrower than any of the candidate answers, and it should say which threat the experiments pose at all, because as far as I can tell they threaten conscious causation rather than determinism.

Be honest about which items are genuine requirements and which are just things my seminar assumes because we all read the same three authors. Flag any pair that can't be had together: my hunch is you can't both insist free will lives in conscious intention and take the timing data seriously, and that you can't both demand the theory be answerable to the science and refuse to let it hang on a self-report method nobody trusts, but tell me if I'm wrong and whether there's a worse pair I'm missing. Then say which single requirement does the most work at ruling candidates out, and give me a line each on how compatibilism, the libertarian line, and the illusionist line fare against it.
