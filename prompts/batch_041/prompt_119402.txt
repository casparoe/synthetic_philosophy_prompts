So I've somehow ended up running a lunchtime philosophy reading group at the software company where I work — long story, my manager decided it would build "critical thinking" — and we just finished a unit on Kant and animals. I'm a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy training, so I've been staying about a chapter ahead of everyone, which means I mostly know the wikipedia-level version: animals aren't persons because they lack reason/autonomy, so we have no duties *to* them, only duties *regarding* them, i.e. really duties to humanity, and cruelty to animals is wrong because it makes us brutal toward people. Everyone in the group has read a summary that says exactly that, so whatever I ask, I'll get that paragraph parroted back at me, which is useless for grading.

What I want instead is a question that makes them actually stress-test it. Two things I've been pushing in the group and want the exam question to force them to use. (1) A test I made up, which I've been calling the swap test: take a clear case of cruelty to an animal, then swap the animal for a rock that everyone has been engineered from birth to believe suffers — people flinch, they cry out when it's hit, they'd swear it's in agony. If your verdict about the rock case comes out the same as the animal case, then the animal's suffering itself isn't doing any work in your theory; if the verdict changes, you owe me a story about why. (2) A distinction I keep coming back to: is the animal's suffering a *reason* for me not to do the thing, or is it only *evidence* about what kind of person I'm turning into? Kant's line looks like the second one, and I want them to feel how strange that is, given that it's supposed to be the reason cruelty is forbidden.

So: write me one essay exam question, plus a model answer and a marking scheme. It's take-home, answerable in 45 minutes, aimed at roughly upper-undergrad difficulty since my group is a mix of engineers and two people who minored in philosophy. The question has to be unanswerable by reciting the indirect-duties line, and it should force them to commit to a verdict on whether the strongest version of Kant's position can give the animal's suffering any reason-giving force at all, and then say what a Kantian has to give up to get that verdict — or argue that nothing needs to be given up. Model answer of around 700 words, in plain english, no citations or reading lists (nobody can look anything up while writing), and it should definately be upfront about which bits are Kant paraphrased and which bits are my reconstruction. Then the marking scheme: five must-hit points, 20 marks total, the marks each one is worth, and for each a one-line note on how people typically screw that point up. Also flag which single point is the one that separates a good answer from a great one, and tell me if any of my two gadgets (the swap test, the reason-vs-evidence thing) is actually broken and would let a student wriggle out of the question.
