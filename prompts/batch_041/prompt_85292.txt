I am the rapporteur for a two-day invitational workshop on invertebrate welfare, convened jointly by a national insect-protein trade association and a university animal-welfare centre, and I have been asked to deliver the closing address: eight minutes, no slides, read aloud to a room of about sixty. A third of the room runs or supplies insect-rearing facilities, and their margins are thin. A third are entomologists who came for the husbandry sessions and are sceptical that the philosophical part of the programme belongs in the same building. The remainder are welfare advocates, a handful of ethicists, and two funders who will decide in the spring whether this becomes an annual event. Please write the address in my voice, as a text I can read from the lectern.

The reason I cannot simply read the organizers' draft statement aloud is two sentences in it. The first holds that where our credence in an animal's sentience falls below some threshold, the harm done to it should count as nothing rather than as a discounted something. The second proposes that the moral weight of a population rises with the logarithm of the number of individuals in it, so that a billionfold increase in numbers registers as only a modest increase in weight. Both were drafted to defuse the arithmetic I keep returning to. Multiply a vast number of individuals by a small probability that each is a subject of experience and you get an expected quantity of suffering that dwarfs most of what is done to farmed vertebrates; multiply instead by a probability you concede is unknown and the same arithmetic will license almost anything, including a panic about mites and nematodes that no one in this room could act on. I want the address to take that structure seriously rather than gesture at it. I believe neither sentence survives contact with the arithmetic, but I also cannot tell this room that the arithmetic settles anything, and the speech has to hold both of those facts at once.

Concretely, the address should: explain, for the operators and the entomologists, what is and is not being claimed when we say a nervous system may register damage without its owner suffering it, without pretending the science has delivered a verdict; be candid about what I do not know and about the size of the uncertainty I am asking the room to price; work through one illustrative calculation with figures of my own invention, so the audience can see exactly where the numbers come from and where they are simply guesses; and raise, in my own voice, the strongest objection to my argument — that it proves far too much — and say what, if anything, stops the slide from insects to everything smaller. Close with one concrete recommendation that the operators could adopt even if they reject the premise that insects are sentient, so that the practical advice does not stand or fall with the contested claim.

Please do not cite papers, authors, or studies; I have no way to check attributions from the lectern and the room will not be impressed by them. Argue from premises the audience can accept on the spot. The tone should be plain and unhurried, at roughly eleven hundred words, with nothing that sounds like a seminar. The operators should not feel ambushed, and the advocates should not feel fobbed off.
