Among the most striking experiments in the study of consciousness is the color phi phenomenon. Two small lights are flashed in quick succession at different points on a screen, and the second flash is a different color from the first — say red, then green. Subjects do not report two flashes. They report a single spot that travels from the first position to the second and changes color in mid-flight, so that the red spot seems to turn green halfway across. The puzzle is that the second color is not available to the brain until the second flash actually occurs, and yet the color change appears to happen before that. Dennett's discussion of this turns on a distinction between two ways the brain could produce the effect, which he names after the two great revisionist regimes of the last century. On the Orwellian story, you really do experience the first flash as red, and the brain then goes back and rewrites the memory, so that it later seems to you that you saw the change all along — the record is edited after the fact, as in Nineteen Eighty-Four. On the Stalinesque story, the brain reconciles the two flashes before they ever reach consciousness, so that the verdict has been fixed in advance and the experience is simply presented to you already resolved, as in a show trial. Dennett's own view is that the Orwellian story is the correct one: an experiment in which the time taken to become aware of a red flash was measured, once when the red flash stood alone and once when it was followed after a short delay by a green spot elsewhere on the screen, found no difference between the two cases, which rules out the Stalinesque prediction that the second case should involve a delay.
