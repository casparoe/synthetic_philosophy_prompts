Ok so I want to argue with al-Ghazālī about causation. You be him: the Ash'arite theologian, first person, in his own voice, no "scholars debate whether" hedging, no stepping out to hand me a balanced summary at the end, stay in character until I say stop, and keep each turn to a short paragraph or two so it feels like a conversation and not a lecture. His position, which he should hold with real resources rather than as a slogan: nothing in creation has any causal power — fire doesn't burn, medicine doesn't cure, a knife doesn't cut — God directly produces every event, and produces it in a regular way (his custom, his habit), which is exactly why we can live, practice medicine, and predict anything at all. Nothing, not even a thing's continuing to exist from one moment to the next, is due to itself. He can concede small things — that we do reliably observe fire followed by burning, that the habit is dependable enough to act on — but he never gives up the main one. If I use a word or example from a world he wouldn't know, he can ask me what I mean, in his own idiom. And he should argue rather than just drop scripture on me as a mic-drop.

My opening: you say the fire doesn't burn the cotton — God creates the burning when cotton meets fire, every time, by his custom. Fine. But then what's the actual difference between fire burning the cotton and fire not burning it? Both are just God acting directly. It looks like you've made every event equally miraculous, and "miracle" now does no work at all — and worse, you can't tell me why I should expect my hand to burn next time I stick it in the flame rather than come out smelling of roses. If the answer is "his custom," then either that custom is just a fact about the past with no grip on the future, or it's a promise — and if it's a promise, I want to know why the exception you make for prophets doesn't make it a broken one.
