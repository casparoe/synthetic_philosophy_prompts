I'm editing a little anthology of short fiction where every story has to do real work with one idea, and I'm stuck on the costly signaling one. Everyone writes the same story — peacock tail, only the strong can afford it, the end. I want one with more teeth, so let me say what I'd want it to bite on.

The standard line is that a display is honest *because* it's wasteful: burning resources proves you had resources to burn. But that's only one of at least three stories you can tell about why a costly display stays honest, and they come apart:

(a) the waste does the work — the cost itself is the guarantee, and it's precisely because the display is useless for survival that it's credible;
(b) the waste is a red herring — what matters is that the same display is more expensive for a low-quality signaler than for a high-quality one, so a display can be honest even if it isn't wasteful in any absolute sense, and the trait could even be genuinely useful;
(c) cost isn't doing the work at all — the trait is unfakeable, welded to the thing it indicates (size, age, scars, a voice you only get with a certain lung capacity), so it can be free and still honest.

Then there's the receiver half that people forget: a display only counts as a signal because receivers respond to it, so honesty is a property of a whole population sitting at some equilibrium, not of one animal's tail — and equilibria collapse when the costs change, or slide into a new one where a cheap display is honest for a different reason. There's also that odd category of traits that signal nothing themselves and exist only to make other signals easier to read, amplifying small differences into visible ones.

So: write me a story, 700-1000 words, where those accounts come apart. Somewhere in it a character has to try to fake the display, or find a cheap route to it, and at least two characters have to disagree out loud about *why* the display works — and I don't want the story to settle it. The disagreement should still be live when the story ends. Set it wherever serves the thing: near future, alien, historical village, a group chat, whatever. Nobody in it should quote a theorist or cite anything; let them reason like people do.

After the story, one tight paragraph of notes: which account of the honesty you leaned on, where you fudged the biology or the sociology to make the drama work, and what's genuinely settled vs. still argued about among the people who study this — dont dress up a contested reading as the consensus, and say so if the whole framing is one camp's view.

Start with the story. No "here's a story about costly signaling," no restating my brief — just the first line of the story, then the note at the end.
