I teach a seminar on the philosophy of history and want to end the term with Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle, read through the lens of counterfactual history and historical inevitability. The novel's premise is itself a counterfactual — Zangara's bullet kills Roosevelt in 1933 and the Axis wins — and inside that world there circulates a second-order counterfactual, The Grasshopper Lies Heavy, in which the Allies prevail. Give me a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously, without reducing either to the other.

Some things I want addressed. First, the nesting: the Allied-victory world of the banned book is not our world either, and the novel never lets us settle which world is basic. Does that make the counterfactual a mere narrative toy, or does it dramatize something real about how counterfactuals are constructed from the historian's present concerns rather than discovered? Second, the I Ching, which characters consult before acting and which Dick reportedly consulted while writing. Is that a rival account of necessity — non-causal, non-agential, oracular — and does it undercut the whole counterfactual project by making outcomes read off something other than causes? Third, the debate I want in the background: Berlin's 1954 Auguste Comte lecture on historical inevitability, which denies that history obeys inexorable laws and ties determinism to a diminished sense of responsibility, against Carr's 1961 lectures on history, which treat the might-have-beens as idle and stress that historians select causes in light of outcomes. Which one does the novel vindicate? My suspicion is that its form presupposes Berlin while its political texture dramatizes Carr — a world in which the losers' alternatives are literally banned — but I want you to say whether that is right.

Fourth, and this is the part I most want: the novel contains semifactuals in disguise. Does it imply that even without Zangara's bullet some German or Japanese ascendancy was inevitable, and what would that claim mean? Is the I Ching's answer to that question meant to be taken as a verdict? Fifth, the trade in counterfeit Americana and Frink's jewelry that a Japanese client says possesses wu: is that a figure for the historian's reconstruction of a lost past, or for the counterfactual historian's manufacture of one that never existed? Sixth, the politics of the counterfactual — the Nazis ban Abendsen's book, the Japanese permit it, and the author himself sits fortified and inaccessible. What does that prohibition tell us about the connection between inevitability claims and power?

Be honest about where the novel resists the reading. Dick's interests may be ontological and religious rather than causal, and the question of which world is real may be the wrong question to put to him; if the novel is divinatory rather than counterfactual, say so and explain what that costs my framing. Point me to the scenes I should teach, and end with what the novel shows that Berlin and Carr, arguing, cannot.
