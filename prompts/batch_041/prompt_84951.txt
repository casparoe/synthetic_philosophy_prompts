In my seminar on the neuroscience of free will, the discussion stalls in the same place every year, and I would like a diagnosis rather than another summary of the literature. The empirically minded students treat the early readiness potential as showing that the conscious urge to move arrives too late to initiate anything; the philosophically minded ones reply that this only touches a caricature of free will, that the task involves arbitrary finger movements rather than deliberated choices, and that the reported time of the conscious urge is a fragile introspective estimate. No one disputes the data; no one shifts position. My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but several compressed under a single heading: an empirical dispute about what the readiness potential represents, including the possibility that it is a stochastic threshold crossing rather than a signal of preparation; a conceptual dispute about which event in the stream deserves the name of decision; and a normative dispute about what any of this would imply for responsibility. Is that diagnosis right, and if so which strand is doing the real work? Or is there a genuinely empirical question here, about the veto, about the timing measures, or about the threshold reinterpretation, that could be settled and would actually move the debate? I want a diagnosis of the impasse grounded in what each side actually claims, together with an honest account of what would count as progress: a better experiment, a conceptual distinction, or the admission that the parties are answering different questions under one name.
