I sit on the advisory board of a small independent institute that maintains a long-run global database of violent deaths, the kind of dataset historians and political scientists use when they argue about whether the world is becoming less violent. The board does not collect the numbers; it decides what counts as a violent death and how the series are normalized, and over the years we have accumulated a set of precedents. We have always included battle deaths from interstate wars and civil wars, counted per capita rather than in absolute numbers, on the ground that a growing world population would otherwise make the past look artificially peaceful. We include homicide and domestic-violence fatalities, and we include state executions and police killings even when they are legally authorized, because we decided early that legality is not the same as nonviolence. We include terrorist bombings by non-state groups. We have excluded deaths from preventable disease, malnutrition, and unsafe working conditions, even when they are foreseeable consequences of government policy, because they are not direct acts of force, often lack an identifiable perpetrator, and are not the product of a single deliberate policy aimed at harm. We excluded traffic fatalities for similar reasons, though we recognized that policy could reduce them. But we did include deaths from a siege in which a government deliberately cut off food and water, because that was a direct, intentional use of force against a population. We have never folded what is sometimes called structural violence into the headline index, though we publish a separate companion series for it.

Now we must decide on a new category: deaths caused by comprehensive economic sanctions that foreseeably produce malnutrition, lack of medicine, and excess mortality, but where the sanctioning governments do not deliberately target food or medicine and say their aim is to change a regime's behavior. One colleague argues that excluding these deaths would be inconsistent with our inclusion of the siege; another argues that including them would commit us to counting every foreseeable policy harm, from austerity to climate inaction, and the index would lose its meaning. We also have to decide whether to count the category per capita or in absolute numbers, and whether the question of whether these deaths are really violence has a fact of the matter or is a stipulation we are making for our own purposes. I would like a recommendation on whether to include the category, which of our precedents is the closest analog, and what rule our decision would commit us to. If our record underdetermines the answer, say so, and tell me what further case would best distinguish the two or three rules that fit our past decisions. Please do not give me a reading list; I need the decision and the reasoning.
