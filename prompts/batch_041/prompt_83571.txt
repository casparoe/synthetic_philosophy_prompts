I run a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar I call "Machines that want things" -- 16 students, mixed philosophy and CS, a few have done a bit of RL, most haven't. Next week is inverse reinforcement learning, the idea that you can read off what someone wants by watching what they do, and I want them to feel in their gut how badly behaviour underdetermines preference before I say a word about it from the front. So: design me a game. My rough shape is that half the room are demonstrators with a secret preference over a small set of world-states who spend a few rounds picking moves, the other half are inference engines who see only the moves and have to write down a utility function that explains them, and then -- this is the part I actually care about -- we hand each inferred utility function to a third group of optimizers who maximize it blindly and mechanically, and the demonstrators have to sit and watch what happens. I want them to discover three things in order, without me naming any of them: that two demonstrators with genuinely different preferences can be behaviourally indistinguishable; that a demonstrator who acts strangely because they believe something false about the world (I'm planning to slip one demonstrator a card saying the bridge is out, and not tell the engines) gets read as having a preference they don't have; and that the utility function the engines confidently hand back, once something optimizes it hard enough, produces outcomes the demonstrator would call a nightmare. If the third beat lands, the whole point of the session has landed.

Give me the rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, roles, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes. Assume a whiteboard, index cards, and whatever tokens I can scrounge. No lecture up front, and I'd rather nobody says "reward function" or "inverse reinforcement learning" out loud until the debrief. The debrief is where I need the most help: I want it to map specific moments from the game onto the real structure -- the identifiability problem, the belief/preference confound, the near-optimality assumption and what happens when you loosen it (I want a knob for "maybe they were just being sloppy" and I want them to see how much the inferred preference moves when you turn it), the fact that you can add any function of the state to a utility and get identical optimal behaviour so the answer is only ever defined up to that, and then the optimization step. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it -- I'd bet the engines just transcribe the demonstrator's policy back at me instead of inferring a utility, and I'd bet the optimizers pull their punches because they feel bad. Finally, be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce: in the game the space of possible utilities is small and enumerable, and I'm worried that makes the underdetermination look like a puzzle with a solution rather than the mess it is at scale, plus whatever else you think the facilitator has to just supply verbally.
