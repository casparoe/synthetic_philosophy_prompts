You are a philosopher of moral psychology who has also spent two decades writing literary fiction, and I am asking you to advise me — a novelist — on how to spend a scarce and non-renewable resource: the conceptual attention behind my next book. I have roughly three years and one sustained argument's worth of thinking to give to a single cluster of questions, and I want to put it where it will carry the most weight rather than where the conversation happens to be loudest. Treat this as an agenda-setting exercise, not a literature review.

The project is a multi-generational novel about people living through a moral change whose shape they cannot see — a change in who counts, how much, and on what grounds. What I need from you is a defended ranking of the open questions in the debate over moral progress and the so-called expanding circle, ordered by how much they deserve my years.

Begin by brainstorming before you commit to anything. Lay out the candidate questions you regard as genuinely open — not the ones with settled textbook answers, and not the ones that are really empirical questions I could never settle from a desk. Then rank them: which belong at the top, which in the middle, which you would set aside, and why.

For each question you rank, work through the following explicitly:
- whether it is upstream or downstream of the others — does settling it constrain the answers to other questions, or is it a leaf?
- its tractability for someone who is a novelist rather than an academic: no fieldwork, no archives, no collaborators, just close reading of people and situations, plus whatever conceptual tools travel well.
- how much a settled answer would actually change how the novel gets written — its structure, its central character, its ending — as opposed to merely changing how I would footnote it.
- whether it is neglected, contested, or quietly assumed by everyone in the discussion.

I want you to take seriously several framings that I suspect are under-explored, and to tell me candidly whether each is a live question or a dead end:
- whether moral progress is better modeled as the circle expanding — more beings admitted to the same concern — or as its flattening, in which distance, kinship, and reciprocity lose their moral grip altogether; and whether these two pictures can come apart in practice.
- whether the circle metaphor smuggles in a center, and whose center it is; whether there is a hidden parochialism in the very image of concentric concern.
- whether the outer boundary is drawn on a principle — sentience, agency, the capacity to be wronged, the capacity to flourish — or is simply wherever our sympathies currently stop, and what would have to be true for the boundary to be principled rather than provisional.
- whether moral change is discovery or drift, and whether that distinction is even available from the inside of a culture that is changing.
- what would count as genuine moral regression rather than a mere change in values, given that we inevitably judge the past by our own lights.

Where two of the candidate questions turn out to depend on the same underlying issue, say so and rank the underlying one higher, even if it is the less glamorous of the two. Be explicit about the trade-offs among your criteria — importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other questions hang on the answer — since they will not always agree, and tell me which criterion you are weighting most heavily and why.

Think carefully and take your time; reason in stages if that helps, and show me the reasoning rather than only the verdict. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or the names of particular works — I cannot look anything up, and a guessed-at title would be worse than useless. Explain every position in your own words.

Close by telling me what would change your ranking: what argument or consideration, if it turned out to be true, would move a question up or down, and which single placement you are least confident in.
