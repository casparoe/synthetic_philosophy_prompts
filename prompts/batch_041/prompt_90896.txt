EXAM SHEET — Unit 4, open book, 90 minutes, maximum 900 words.

Question: Laïcité is often glossed as a form of state neutrality. Taking the 2004 law on religious signs in public schools as your test case, assess whether that law is best understood as an application of state neutrality or as the imposition of a particular conception of the public sphere. Set out both readings, say which you think is better supported, and defend your choice.

Background note (from lecture 3): Two readings of neutrality are in play in this literature. On the first, neutrality of the state: the state and its institutions take no position on religious truth and neither recognise nor privilege any religion. On the second, neutrality of public space (sometimes called the sphere-of-neutrality reading): the requirement reaches past the state's own stance to the appearance and self-presentation of individuals within public institutions. Note also that the law of 9 December 1905 on the separation of the churches and the state was not applied in Alsace and Moselle, which were then part of the German Empire and where concordat arrangements survive to this day; that the law of 15 March 2004 amends the education code and prohibits signs and clothing by which pupils conspicuously manifest a religious affiliation in public primary and secondary schools, and does not extend to universities or to public employees generally; and that the law of 11 October 2010, which prohibits concealing the face in public space, is a separate instrument with a separate rationale.

Required reading for the unit: Charles Taylor, "The Politics of Recognition" (1992); Will Kymlicka, Multicultural Citizenship (1995).

STUDENT SCRIPT

The 1905 law of separation, usually treated as the foundation of laïcité, rests on three commitments: that the state is neutral between religions, that the free exercise of religion is protected, and that public powers have nothing to do with the churches. Read in that light, laïcité is a doctrine about the state and not about citizens: the Republic does not recognise, fund or privilege any religion, and it does not let religious membership determine the rights it confers. That is the sense in which laïcité is a form of neutrality. The separation was never quite uniform — the 1905 law went unapplied in Alsace and Moselle, then part of Germany, where the older concordat arrangements still run — but the direction of travel was clear, and by the time religious signs in schools became politically explosive the framework was settled.

The 2004 law is formally even-handed: it names no religion, and it prohibits conspicuous signs and clothing by which pupils manifest a religious affiliation, which covers the headscarf, the kippah and the large cross alike, while leaving discreet signs alone. That drafting choice matters, because it shows the legislator was concerned with display rather than belief: the law regulates the outward sign, not the inward conviction, and is therefore continuous with the older requirement that the state take no position on religious truth.

Two readings of the law are available. On the first, the law simply applies state neutrality: the state school is an arm of the state, the state may take no position on religion, so the state school may not be the setting in which pupils display religious affiliation. On the second, the law does more: it imposes on pupils a requirement of religious self-effacement in the public sphere — the neutrality of public space rather than of the state. On that second reading the law is not neutral at all; it is the majority's conception of how a French citizen should appear, enacted against a minority that wears its religion on its body.

The two readings are, I think, two ways of stating the same requirement, since a state that is neutral between religions must also keep its public institutions free of religious markers; the second reading simply describes what the first requires in practice. So the second reading is better supported. The evidence is the political history: the Stasi commission, the parliamentary reports, the press coverage. Nobody who followed the debate in 2003 could doubt that the target was the headscarf, and the fact that the same majority which insisted on the neutrality of the state had no difficulty with the Catholic calendar, with school holidays, or with the crucifixes then still hanging in some classrooms, tells us what the law was really about.

The objection that the law burdens Muslim girls more than anyone else is therefore beside the point. Neutrality is a property of the state's stance, not of the effects of its policies; a measure can be neutral and still fall unevenly, and if it does, that is a question of social justice rather than of laïcité. This is where the Anglo-American alternative goes wrong: as Will Kymlicka argues in The Politics of Recognition, liberal states cannot in fact be neutral between cultures, because the public culture of any state is already the culture of the majority, so the honest move is not to pretend to neutrality but to extend recognition to minorities on their own terms. Whatever one makes of that, the French answer is the more coherent one, because it asks the same thing of everyone.
