Me and my mate Dan got into a stupid three-hour argument last night about Jantelagen vs the American Dream. He grew up in a small town where everyone knew everyone's business and then moved to the States, so he's got both barrels loaded, and his whole line was that they're opposites: one says you're nothing special, the other says you can be anything you want. I don't buy it, and I've scribbled some notes for an essay. Can you tell me whether the core claim is actually doing any work, or whether I'm just dressing up "both are social norms" in fancy clothes?

The gist of the notes:

1. Both are the same machine pointed in different directions. Call it the rebuke problem: someone else's success is a standing indictment of the choices I didn't make. If my neighbour built a business and I spent my evenings watching telly, his existence is quietly telling me those evenings were a waste. Both cultures are answering that exact discomfort, just with different plumbing.

2. The tall-poppy move closes the verdict now. Cut him down — he got lucky, he knew someone, he's a tosser — and my life gets retroactively vindicated. Cheap, instant, and it costs the society its ambitious people.

3. The American Dream move keeps the verdict open forever. Don't cut him down, point at him instead: that could be me. My life isn't vindicated but it also isn't condemned, it's just pending. The cost is that you have to keep buying the lottery ticket, i.e. the inequality that makes the dream worth dreaming is the same inequality that makes it necessary. So I want to say the Dream and brutal inequality aren't a bug-and-bug relationship, they're load-bearing for each other.

4. Weird prediction this spits out: both should be strongest among people who aren't succeeding. The Dream gets defended hardest by people who haven't made it yet, and Jantelagen gets enforced hardest by people who also haven't made it. Same demographic, opposite slogans. If that's right, then neither is really about the successful at all — the successful person is just scenery.

5. A worry I can't get past: maybe I'm cheating. Jantelagen as it's normally stated isn't "don't succeed", it's "don't think you're better than us". So success is fine as long as you're self-deprecating about it — which is exactly the register of every rich guy who says he was just lucky and definately didn't do anything special, then drives off in a truck. If that's right, the real axis isn't Dream vs Jante at all, it's loud ascent vs quiet ascent, and my whole framing collapses into "be humble" vs "be proud", which is boring. Does it collapse? Or is there something left standing?

Also, is this too cynical? Dan's summary was basically "everyone's just managing their own envy", and honestly I sort of agree with him, but I don't want the essay to end there. What's the strongest non-cynical version of the claim? And am I conflating resenting a person with resenting the claim they make just by existing? Those feel like seperate things to me but I can't articulate why. Be brutal, especially on point 5.
