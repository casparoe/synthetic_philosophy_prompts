In Hofstadter's account of creativity, a style is a space of possibilities whose dimensions are adjustable parameters — knobs — that the style leaves free while fixing everything else. A variation turns one or more of those knobs; a creative act is either a setting nobody thought to try or, more radically, the discovery that something everyone treated as a fixed feature of the style was in fact a knob. On that picture the interesting question is not whether a work is new, but whether it stays legible as a member of its style while moving somewhere the style has not been.

Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words set in a fictional musical tradition built entirely on variations of a single ancestral theme. The tradition keeps a ledger: every sanctioned performance must be filed under the name of the one parameter it alters from the canonical version — tempo, ornamentation, register, and the rest — and a performance that alters nothing on the list is not admitted as a variation at all. Center the story on a young performer whose rendition the keeper of the ledger cannot file: it is unmistakably the theme, yet it seems to move along a dimension the ledger does not contain. Let the plot, rather than any character's lecture, carry the question of whether she has found a new knob, broken the tradition, or simply produced a different theme; include a scene in which she and the keeper disagree about which of those has happened; and end without settling it. Do not name Hofstadter or explain the theory — a reader who knows the literature should recognize the position being dramatized, and a reader who does not should still have a good story.
