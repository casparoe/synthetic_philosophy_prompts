I am a novelist at work on a book whose plot turns on a surrogacy arrangement, and after some months of reading the legal materials I have the uneasy sense that I understand the rules better than I understand the ideas underneath them. Two regimes sit at the centre of the story. In the first — much of continental Europe — the paid arrangement is prohibited outright, the woman who gives birth is the legal mother no matter whose egg she carried, and any agreement that purports to hand the child over in advance is void as against public policy. Yet the very same systems permit egg donation, allow a mother to place a newborn for adoption, and have been required by a European human-rights court to recognise after the fact the parent-child relationships they refuse to create in advance, on the ground that the child's own interests forbid leaving the tie in legal limbo. In the second regime — several American states, California being the one my plot needs — the contract is enforceable, the intended parents can be named as the child's legal parents from birth, and the woman who carries the pregnancy is treated by the law as something close to a service provider who never acquires a parental claim at all. At a dinner last week a friend who teaches law told me the two systems simply embody different values and that I should stop looking for a single principle; another insisted they are not even consistent with themselves, and that the European side is the more obviously incoherent, since it severs the genetic tie happily enough and permits the transfer of a child by adoption but draws the line at a transfer agreed in advance and paid for. I came away convinced I had been reading the wrong kinds of documents.

What I would like from you is not a survey of either regime but an account of what each presupposes. Begin with the view of parenthood, of the body, and of what may be bought and sold that each set of rules only makes sense under: on the European side, something like the claim that gestation creates a bond no contract can dissolve and that the human body and its parts must not be a source of financial gain; on the American side, that parental status follows the intention to raise a child and that a woman's labour and risk are hers to price. Then tell me which rival views each regime quietly rules out — could a Kantian about dignity, a feminist about exploitation, a conservative about the family, and an economist about incomplete contracts all endorse the same ban, and if so, is the ban doing any theoretical work of its own? I want the distinctions kept sharp: what the rules strictly require versus what they merely sit comfortably with, and where each regime is so underdetermined that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same statutes. Address the asymmetries directly — egg donation and adoption on one side, birth certificates and pre-birth parentage orders on the other, and the recognition problem that has forced European courts to protect a relationship their legislatures had forbidden. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the rules, and not merely in the rhetoric, if a regime were rebuilt on the rival conception of what makes someone a parent. Please write in plain language and name the positions in ordinary words rather than school-of-thought labels; these arguments have to come out of the mouths of characters who have never read a word of philosophy.
