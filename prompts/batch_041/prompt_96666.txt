David Hume died in 1776, before the gold standard was codified, before central banking in anything resembling its modern form, and long before money became a purely accounting liability. I am preparing teaching materials on the history of monetary thought and want to use him as a test case: what would the author of the essays on money, interest, and the balance of trade have made of monetary arrangements that postdate him by two and a half centuries?

The exercise is not to ventriloquize an eighteenth-century gentleman's prejudices but to work out what his arguments commit him to. Take these as fixed points of his system, and reason from them rather than from a general impression of Hume: money is "none of the wheels of trade" but "the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy"; a nation that keeps its people and its industry need never fear losing its money, since prices adjust, as in his thought experiment of four-fifths of Britain's money being "annihilated in one night" (and the converse case of the money stock "multiplied fivefold in a night"); the rate of interest is not fixed by the quantity of precious metal, and "money having chiefly a fictitious value" is of no consequence considered within a nation; and he "entertain[s] a doubt concerning the benefit of banks and paper-credit," which he describes as "a counterfeit money, which foreigners will not accept of in any payment."

Four developments for him to confront:

First, fiat money with no commodity anchor and no convertibility, whose value is said by the state theory of money to rest entirely on the sovereign's power to levy taxes payable in that money and nothing else. I am interested in whether Hume's quantity theory survives this, and how much daylight there really is between his "fictitious value" remark and the chartalist claim.

Second, money as a bank liability: in contemporary systems most money consists of deposits created when banks lend, and the central bank fixes the price of reserves rather than their quantity, so there is no dial controlling the money stock of the kind the price-specie-flow argument presumes. Ask whether his account of money as oil in the wheels needs revision when the oil is manufactured on demand by the parties using it.

Third, the reform proposal that keeps resurfacing under the names sovereign money, full-reserve banking, and 100% money: deposit-taking banks would be required to hold reserves equal to their deposit liabilities in full, would lose the power to create money by lending, and money creation would become a monopoly of the central bank. I want to know whether Hume's own remark that "no bank could be more advantageous, than such a one as locked up all the money it received, and never augmented the circulating coin" commits him to this program, and what he would make of the half of the modern proposal that hands discretionary money creation to a central bank rather than ending it.

Fourth, the dollar as the world's reserve asset, which lets one country run persistent external deficits without the correction his balance-of-trade argument predicts, under floating exchange rates rather than specie flows. Is his hydraulic picture simply inapplicable here, or does the reserve currency just play the role the precious metals played in his system?

Please give me the following sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths: "Common ground" (~250 words), setting out the commitments any Humean answer must respect; "Fiat money and the state theory" (~350 words); "Bank money and the locked-up bank" (~350 words); "Sovereign money" (~300 words); "The dollar as world money" (~300 words); "Where Hume gives out" (~200 words), naming the questions his premises do not reach; and "Verdict" (~150 words). In each section, mark whether the position you attribute to him is forced by his premises, a plausible extension of them, or an anachronistic projection. I don't need citations or a reading list; I care about the arguments.

If the whole exercise is misconceived -- if a thinker's counterfactual opinions about institutions two centuries after his death are not something his texts can settle, or if I am smuggling in a false continuity between his world and mine -- say so plainly in the first section and then do whatever can honestly be done, rather than producing fluent pseudo-Hume.
