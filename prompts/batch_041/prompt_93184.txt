My first year out of college I spent about ten months working in a district office of the education ministry in a country I won't name, and I'm still close with two people from that office, Ravi and Mariam, who have been re-fighting the same argument for years now. The case that started it: a district education officer, call him Mr. A, sat on the committee that awarded the textbook supply contract. Maybe four months after the contract was signed, the company's owner turned up at Mr. A's daughter's wedding and gave a very large gift -- publicly, in front of a couple hundred guests, which is the ordinary way people give at weddings there, and Mr. A's family had given comparable gifts at the owner's two family weddings over the previous decade. Also worth knowing: Mr. A hadn't been paid in five months, the district office was broke. Ravi says this is corruption, full stop, and that anyone who says otherwise is doing the relativist soft-shoe. Mariam says calling it corruption is a category mistake dressed up as moral seriousness.

Their exchange goes roughly like this. (1) Ravi: an official with discretion over a contract took a substantial private benefit from the person who won that contract. That's the whole of it, and the wrapping paper doesnt matter. (2) Mariam: by that rule the man can't have a social life. Everyone in that town gives at weddings, the amounts track the relationship, and his family has given about as much as it's gotten. What Ravi is calling corruption is just reciprocity, and it only looks sinister because he's importing an arm's-length market picture where any transfer between two people who have dealings must be a price. (3) Ravi: the reciprocity defense proves too much, since any payoff can be dressed up as a gift, and it also proves too little, because real reciprocity is diffuse -- if this were ordinary social exchange, the flows would run in many directions, not from one live contractor to the one official who mattered. And notice what "the amounts track the relationship" concedes: the relationship is being priced. (4) Mariam: two things. First, she thinks a gift and a bribe differ in kind and not just in labeling: a bribe closes a transaction, whereas a gift opens an open-ended obligation that neither side can settle or price, which is why gifts are how people here make and keep relationships rather than how they buy things. Second, she says Ravi keeps sliding from "this is corruption" to "he's a bad man," and those come apart -- and if the category of corruption is defined by whatever the local rules happen to forbid, then it's just law-breaking with a scowl, and any state could legalize its way out of corruption. (5) Ravi: then he'll take the other horn -- the standard has to be something like a public official converting entrusted power into private benefit, which is exactly what happened, and if the local rules don't forbid it, so much the worse for the local rules. He also says her gift-versus-bribe distinction is the weakest thing she's said, since an open-ended, unpriced, untraceable obligation is worth more to an official than a completed payment, not less.

I'm the one who introduced them, so I get asked to referee every time, and I keep failing at it. Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what -- not a mushy "they both have a point," but for each of the five, whether it holds up, what's wrong with it if anything, and what that person should have said instead? Then tell me what they're both missing, and give me your actual verdict on the wedding gift itself. I'd also like to know which details would flip your answer: if he'd personally asked for the gift, if it had been cash in an envelope instead, if the giver had lost the bid, or if the town's own opinion were that the gift was shameful. American English, please.
