I chair the algorithmic-fairness review board for a public-sector data consortium. Our charter requires us to approve, reject, or condition the deployment of predictive tools used in pretrial risk assessment, child-welfare screening, and eligibility triage. We are not a court and we do not legislate definitions of fairness, but we must give written reasons, and our past decisions have become the practical precedent that applicants and advocates now cite. I need a recommendation on the case before us that is defensible as an extension of that precedent rather than a fresh start.

Our record, in brief:

- We rejected a pretrial risk score that was race-blind in its features, well calibrated within race, and more accurate than the human status quo, because it produced a higher false-positive rate for Black defendants. We wrote that a tool increasing the risk of unjust detention for a group already overrepresented in the carceral system cannot be justified by overall accuracy or calibration alone.
- We approved a child-welfare screening tool that had equal false-positive and false-negative rates across race but was not calibrated within race; its scores overstated risk for Black families at every level. We reasoned that equal error rates prevented disparate removal, and that calibration mattered less because the score was not presented to families as a probability.
- We rejected a hiring model that achieved near-perfect parity by including race as an explicit input. We did not hold that race-consciousness is categorically forbidden; we held that the employer had failed to show that race-neutral alternatives were unavailable and that its use of race was not narrowly tailored to a specific finding of past discrimination. We left open whether a narrowly tailored race-conscious remedy could ever satisfy the charter.
- We approved a race-blind eligibility model that used ZIP code as a feature and produced large racial disparities, because we found ZIP code to be a causally relevant measure of local labor-market conditions rather than a mere proxy for race. We conditioned approval on an annual audit.
- We rejected a model that was individually fair in the sense that similar applicants received similar scores, because the similarity metric was contested and the model produced large group disparities. We reasoned that individual fairness cannot substitute for group-level scrutiny.
- We approved a decision-support tool with unequal error rates and poor calibration because a human caseworker made the final decision and the tool's output was only one input among many. We reasoned that our strictest error-rate requirements apply to tools that make or effectively determine decisions, not to tools that merely advise.
- We have also approved a tool that satisfied parity for race and sex separately while failing for Black women, on the ground that our charter enumerates single-axis categories and we had no intersectional standard. We noted the issue but did not adopt a rule.

Now before us is Tool G, a pretrial detention recommendation system. It is race-blind in its features. It is calibrated within race overall, so that a given score corresponds to the same probability of the outcome in each group. It has equal false-positive rates for Black and white defendants overall. It has a higher false-negative rate for white defendants than for Black defendants. A proposed race-conscious post-processing adjustment would equalize both false-positive and false-negative rates for Black and white defendants overall. But the adjustment would make the scores uncalibrated within race, require the tool to use race at the post-processing stage, and, according to an intersectional audit, leave a substantial false-positive-rate disparity for Black women relative to white women and Black men. Tool G predicts arrest rather than offending. It is used to recommend detention, but a judge makes the final decision after seeing the score. The vendor argues that the current version is already calibrated and race-blind, and that the adjustment is unnecessary because the judge is in the loop. The public defender's office argues that the current version's equal false-positive rate is an artifact of the base-rate difference, that the higher false-negative rate for white defendants means white high-risk defendants are released, and that the adjustment is the only way to satisfy the board's own error-rate commitments. The board's charter says we must consider both the accuracy of the tool and the equitable distribution of its errors.

I want a recommendation. Please do the following:

1. Decide what the board should do about Tool G, and identify the closest analog among our past cases.
2. State the candidate rules that fit our past verdicts equally well, and say what each rule predicts for Tool G.
3. Give your best guess about which rule is actually doing the work in our precedents, with reasons, and say what principle we would be adopting if we approved the adjustment and what principle we would be adopting if we rejected it.
4. Name a further case we have not yet decided that would best discriminate among the candidate rules, and say what verdict on that case would tell us which rule we are actually following.
5. Be explicit about the impossibility results: which combinations of calibration, error-rate parity, and race-blindness are jointly unsatisfiable when base rates differ, and which of our stated commitments are in genuine tension rather than merely superficially so. Also say where infra-marginality and the choice of target variable—arrest rather than offending—bear on the analysis.
6. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on algorithmic fairness. Do not present a contested normative position as the consensus. If your recommendation depends on a contested view, say so explicitly.

No citations or reading list is needed; I want the reasoning, not the literature.
