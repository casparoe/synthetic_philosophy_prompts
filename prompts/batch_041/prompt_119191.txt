I'm three chapters into a novel about a woman who, after a long remission, decides to stop treatment, and I have been trying to get one scene right — the one where her brother argues with her — so I asked a philosopher friend for help with the dialogue. He did not help. He gave me instead a chain of reasoning he says he has carried around for years, half-believing it, and it has been sitting in my desk drawer ever since. It runs like this. (1) Any benefit a life confers must be a benefit to the person whose life it is. (2) The pains a person would suffer count against creating her and the pleasures she would enjoy count in favour of it, so far symmetrically. (3) But the comparison case is not another life, it is no life at all, and here the symmetry breaks: the absence of suffering is a good thing even when there is nobody left over to enjoy the absence — which is why we call it a mercy when a child is never born into agony — whereas the absence of happiness is not a bad thing when there is no one to be deprived of it. (4) So in weighing a birth, the suffering counts against and the happiness does not count for; in weighing a non-birth, the suffering avoided counts for and the happiness forgone does not count against. (5) Hence nobody is ever benefited by being brought into existence, and nobody who might have existed is wronged by being left out of it.

(6) Now apply the same ledger to someone already here and happy. Death ends her pain, and by (3) the ending of pain is a good even with no one left to feel the relief; death also ends her pleasures, and by (3) the absence of pleasure is bad only where someone is deprived, and once she is gone there is no one to be deprived. (7) So the reasons that bear on her own good do not tell against her dying, and may tell in favour of it; and since nothing in the argument turned on anything peculiar to her, we are no more forbidden to end a happy life than we are forbidden to refrain from starting one. (8) My friend says the conclusion is not absurd. I say it is. And the only exits he will grant me are these: either give up (3) for full symmetry, which hands back the reasons to create that I have never believed in, or accept (6) and (7), which hand me a permission I find monstrous.

So tell me which step I should reject, and why it looked so innocuous while it was doing its work. I would like to keep (3) if I possibly can: it is the only thing I have ever found that makes sense both of the fact that I do not owe anyone a child and of the fact that I do not think a child who is never conceived is owed anything — but I do not want to be told it survives merely because I want it to. Price the exits for me. What is lost by trading (3) for symmetry; what is lost by keeping (3) but fencing it off at the moment of coming into existence so that it governs only whether to start a life and never whether to end one; and what exactly would someone have to believe in order to take (7) seriously? I am not asking which exit is cheapest in the abstract — I am asking which premise is actually load-bearing, and what I pay when I pull it out. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and simply refer to the numbered steps as you go.
