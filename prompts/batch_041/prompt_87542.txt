Hey — for this one I want you to take on the role of someone who actually works on intention and practical reason (philosophy of action, decision theory, whatever the right label is), and please brainstorm three or four possible diagnoses before you commit to one, rather than jumping straight to the answer.

The situation: my reading group has been chewing abotu Kavka's toxin puzzle since roughly October and nobody has moved. You know the set-up, but just so we're on the same page: a billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you *intend* to drink a toxin tomorrow — nasty, a day of misery, not lethal. He pays up front, and he's a reliable judge of what you intend. You know perfectly well that once the money is in your account you won't drink the stuff. Can you form the intention? Three camps have hardened. Camp 1: you just can't intend it, full stop — intending to A means being settled on A-ing, and you aren't and you know you aren't. Camp 2: sure you can intend it, you just won't carry it out, and the puzzle only shows that the reasons you have to intend can come apart from the reasons you have to act. Camp 3: the whole thing is a magic trick, because the only moves available to you are self-manipulation moves (sign a contract, take a pill, bet a friend), and each of those either changes the payoffs or turns you into someone whose intention isn't really *yours* in the sense that matters.

My hypothesis, and I want you to test it rather than agree with it: the three camps aren't disagreeing about anything. Camp 1 is doing conceptual analysis of what we're willing to call intending, camp 2 is talking about the psychological state and its rational profile, camp 3 is talking about when a state counts as an expression of agency — three different questions wearing one word. If that's right there's nothing to resolve and the "debate" is an illusion. Is it right, or is there a real substantive disagreement buried in there — say, about whether intention requires some belief that you'll act, or about whether reasons attach to states or to actions, or about whether the whole puzzle secretly idealizes the agent?

Two things to throw into the mix. (a) A variant someone proposed: instead of paying you, the billionaire pays for your friend's surgery, and you have to tell your friend in advance that you intend to drink the toxin. Does the promise/announcement change what you're able to intend, or does it just relocate the same puzzle one step back? (b) Does any of this survive if we let the agent be a bit irrational, or does the puzzle only bite for ideally rational agents — and if it's only about idealization, is that a defect in the puzzle or the point of it?

Then give me your honest read on what progress would even look like here: a distinction, a lab experiment, a formal result — or nothing, and why.
