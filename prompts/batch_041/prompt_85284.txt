Suppose we could put to Kant a set of questions his own century never squarely posed: whether voting is a duty, a right, or a mere liberty; whether the secret ballot is compatible with the publicity that republican government requires; whether compulsory voting violates the innate right to freedom or instead expresses the duty to enter and sustain a civil condition; whether a voter who expects her single ballot to be causally inert may still be obligated to vote as a matter of symbolic or expressive commitment; whether it is permissible to vote for a lesser evil when the alternative is a greater one, and whether doing so makes one complicit in the lesser evil; and whether elections are even the best institutional realization of republican principles, or whether sortition or some mixed scheme would better satisfy the requirement that citizens obey only laws they could have authored.

Reconstruct Kant's likely positions on these questions. Treat the reconstruction as a disciplined exercise: begin from his settled commitments — the innate right to freedom, the social contract as an idea of reason rather than a historical fact, the duty to leave the state of nature, the prohibition on revolution and the corresponding emphasis on reform, the distinction between the public and private use of reason, the separation of right from virtue, the formula of humanity, and the active/passive citizen distinction in the Doctrine of Right — and derive answers that those commitments would support, rather than projecting modern democratic sensibilities onto him. Where the derivation is underdetermined, say so explicitly and identify which further premise would have to be supplied to settle it. His suggestion that even a nation of devils could be governed by a good constitution, if only they possessed understanding, is relevant here: it bears on whether the ethics of voting is primarily a matter of individual virtue or of institutional design.

Pay particular attention to three pressure points. First, the active/passive citizen distinction: Kant tied full citizenship to independence, excluding dependents such as servants, apprentices, and women from the vote, while insisting that all human beings possess the innate right to freedom. Can that distinction be reconstructed in a way that is internally coherent and morally defensible, or does it expose a tension that a Kantian today must resolve by extending the franchise? Second, the lesser-evil problem: if right concerns the external compatibility of freedoms and not the agent's inner purity, does a Kantian voter who chooses the lesser evil stain her hands, or is she merely choosing the best available approximation of right in a non-ideal condition? Third, the causal insignificance of a single vote: if the expected causal effect of one ballot is negligible, does the duty to vote, if there is one, have to be grounded in something other than consequences — for example, in the duty to support just institutions, in the duty of beneficence, or in the public expression of commitment to the social contract? Say which grounding Kant's texts most support and which would require an extension of his framework.

Finally, distinguish the claims that a historically careful interpreter would defend from those that are frankly speculative. I am less interested in a verdict on what Kant would have said in a modern op-ed than in a map of which of his principles constrain the answer, which leave it open, and which would have to be revised for him to reach a recognizable modern position on the ethics of voting.
