I sit in a graduate seminar on the ethics of humanitarian intervention, and one phrase keeps doing the heavy lifting: 'you break it, you own it'. It first surfaced in a session on Iraq, where someone argued that because the 2003 invasion destroyed the Iraqi state, the coalition had acquired an obligation to rebuild it, and that this was something owed to Iraqis rather than a matter of charity. A later session turned it around and used it against intervention: if you break it you own it, so the wise course is not to break it. A third exchange applied it to Libya after 2011, on the view that NATO had broken the Libyan state and therefore owed it a replacement. I do not think the slogan can be doing the same work in all three cases, and I suspect it is several distinct claims wearing one coat.

What I want is a disambiguation, laid out as a menu rather than argued to a single conclusion. For each reading I want to know exactly what is being asserted — a claim about causation and who is responsible for an outcome, a claim about what one person or state owes another, a claim about who holds authority over the broken thing, a claim about legal liability, or nothing more than a piece of prudence — and whether the reading follows from the slogan itself or has to be imported into it.

It will help me if you decompose the slogan along two axes: who the 'you' is (the individual decision-maker, the intervening state, the coalition, or the international community taken as a whole) and what 'owning' amounts to (bearing the costs of repair, holding the authority to decide the broken state's future, being answerable to those affected, being liable to them, or merely forfeiting the right to complain). Use those axes if they are useful, and tell me if some other decomposition cuts better. I am interested in whether concepts such as remedial as against outcome responsibility, the law of belligerent occupation, trusteeship, or the moral hazard of intervention are doing any real work here, or are just decoration.

For each reading I also want a verdict: is it a tautology, a substantive but defensible principle, true only given further premises (name them), or false? And does it actually deliver the conclusions the seminar drew — an obligation to rebuild Iraq, a reason not to intervene in Syria, a debt owed by NATO to Libya — or does something extra have to be added, and what?

Finally, tell me where the slogan is empty and where it earns its keep. In particular: does any reading survive the objection that the invasion was only one cause among many of the Iraqi state's collapse, and is any reading simply a version of the very general principle that those who cause harm must repair it, in which case it says nothing specific about intervention at all?

Format: a table, then a short paragraph. The table should have exactly these columns — Reading | The precise claim | Kind of claim | What would have to be true for it to hold | Verdict | Supports the Iraq conclusion? | Supports the Syria conclusion? Each row is one reading, ordered from the most trivial or uncontroversial to the most radical. In 'The precise claim', state the claim in one or two sentences of the form 'X is committed to Y because Z' rather than gesturing at it. In 'Kind of claim', say whether it is causal, moral, legal, conceptual, or prudential. 'Verdict' should be one of: trivially true; defensible; true only with extra premises (named); false. The last two columns should read yes, no, or only with the named extra premise. After the table, a paragraph of no more than 150 words saying which single reading was most likely intended in each of the three exchanges, and whether the slogan is worth keeping or should be retired in favour of the distinctions underneath it.

Please write in British English. I do not need sources or a reading list; I want the analysis.
