I'm writing a feature on the revived case for economic planning — the claim that modern computation and machine learning make the old objections obsolete — and I'm reviewing a book on the subject whose opening chapter gives this account of the socialist calculation debate, which I'd like to paraphrase in my piece:

"The socialist calculation debate is usually remembered as a dispute about whether planners could gather and process enough information. That framing gets it backwards. Mises's argument, made in the 1920s, was that a socialist economy is impossible in principle: with the means of production held in common there is no market in capital goods and therefore no prices for them, and without prices there is no way to compare the costs of alternative uses of resources. The difficulty is not that the sums are hard; it is that the terms of the sum do not exist. Hayek supplied the other half of the case, and it is an empirical half: the knowledge required to allocate resources is dispersed among millions of people and much of it is tacit, so no planning board could ever assemble it, and market prices are the only device that transmits such knowledge. Lange and Lerner replied that a Central Planning Board could set prices for capital goods itself, adjusting them by trial and error until shortages and surpluses disappeared, with managers instructed to follow a few marginal rules. In effect they conceded that planning would have to imitate the market, and the concession was fatal: their board was a market in name only, since it set prices rather than discovering them, and it could not generate the innovation that competition forces. The theoretical verdict was in long before 1989, and the collapse of the Soviet bloc confirmed it. Anyone now reviving the case for planning is reopening a question that was closed."
