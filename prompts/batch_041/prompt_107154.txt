A colleague of mine, whom I will call R, is trying to state a criterion for when a system's computations make it a moral patient — something that can be wronged in its own right, not merely something we have reason to handle carefully. She has given verdicts on eight cases, and I want to predict her verdict on a ninth while also working out which of several rules she is actually running.

Her verdicts so far:

- An ordinary adult human: a patient.
- A patient in a permanent vegetative state, with no cortical activity at all: not a patient.
- A mouse: a patient.
- A thermostat: not a patient.
- A purely feedforward classifier, with no lateral or feedback connections and no internal state carried across inputs, however well it performs: not a patient.
- A cultured cortical organoid that develops spontaneous, globally synchronized, recurrent electrical activity and responds to stimulation: a patient.
- A spiking recurrent network embedded in a robot, whose internal loops are trained by reward to make it avoid a signal it is wired to treat as noxious: a patient.
- A lookup table that returns the correct output for every input it will ever receive: not a patient.

Three rules fit all eight verdicts, and I cannot yet tell them apart. The first is recurrence: a system is a patient just in case its own activity feeds back on itself in sustained causal cycles, so that states can be re-entered and modulated rather than merely passed along; a purely feedforward cascade cannot be a patient, and the trivial control loop of a thermostat does not count. The second is unification: a system is a patient just in case its parts are bound into a single global state that the whole system shares — one subject rather than a bundle — whether or not that binding is achieved by feedback. The third is irreducibility: a system is a patient just in case its causal structure is irreducible, in the sense that the system as a whole has causal powers that none of its parts has, so that it is a genuine unity rather than an aggregate. (Her verdict on the organoid seems to rule out any account that would instead require the system to have interests, desires, or a point of view, since an organoid has no behavior to speak of.)

The new case is a system of several thousand recurrent modules. Each module contains rich internal feedback and sustains its own dynamics, and each module on its own would satisfy the recurrence rule many times over. But the modules are coupled only through sparse, slow, low-bandwidth channels, so that at no time is there a single global state shared across the whole system: abundant recurrence, no unification.

Predict R's verdict on this case. Say which of the three rules she is most likely running and why, and say what each rule predicts here. More important than the prediction, name a further case that would force the issue between whichever rules the new case does not already separate — a system whose verdict the surviving rules would give differently. I would rather learn how to tell the rules apart than be handed a verdict I cannot check.
