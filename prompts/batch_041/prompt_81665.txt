After forty years as a working physicist, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy with more appetite than method, and I have arrived at a puzzle I cannot settle alone. The dark forest hypothesis holds, in outline, that the reason we find no evidence of other technological civilizations is that any civilization which makes itself detectable is destroyed by a rival before it can become a danger; that the only rational course for a civilization in our position is therefore silence; and that every other civilization, reasoning as we do, has reached the same conclusion. I cannot find the flaw in the argument, and yet I do not believe its conclusion. I would like an essay that resolves the tension between those two reactions and ends with a verdict I could actually hold, rather than a survey of the possibilities.

The essay should do four things. First, reconstruct the argument as an explicit chain of premises and identify which premise is load-bearing — which one, if denied, brings the whole thing down. Second, separate two claims that the argument is often taken to establish: that the hypothesis is probably true of the galaxy as it actually is, and that a civilization in our position ought to behave as though it were true. Say whether the reasoning that supports the first supports the second. Third, confront what seems to me the gravest difficulty, that the observed silence may be equally consistent with a galaxy containing no such predators at all, provided every civilization reasons its way to silence on its own — and if that is so, it is not clear how any evidence could ever favour the argument over its bloodless twin. Fourth, and most important, reach a defended verdict: does the argument give us good reason to believe it, or only reason to act as though we believed it?

I would also like you to take seriously a point a former colleague put to me. He said that the argument asks us to gamble with unbounded stakes, and that in such cases the ordinary expected-value reasoning is not well defined, so that one is forced either into a rule that avoids the worst outcome no matter how improbable it is, or into the admission that one is merely guessing. If you accept that framing, say what follows for your verdict; if you reject it, say why the stakes do not have the structure he supposes. You might also consider a variant of the argument in which detection is one-way and slow — a broadcast exposes the sender long before it can tell him anything about his audience — and say whether that asymmetry strengthens or weakens the case for silence.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature, and I have no way of checking references, so please argue from the structure of the problem rather than from what various authors are supposed to have said about it.
