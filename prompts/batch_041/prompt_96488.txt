I'm a junior in high school and I'm in this philosophy club where we've been reading some AI safety stuff, which is maybe a weird hobby but whatever. There's this idea I keep bumping into called ascription universality. The way I understand it, a system is ascription universal if, relative to what we currently know, its explicit beliefs contain as much information as any other way of ascribing beliefs to it, so you can't get more out of it by interpreting its internals or looking at some hidden computation. It's supposed to be a formalization of transparency or honesty, and I guess it's related to the idea of one computation epistemically dominating another. But I'm confused about a specific objection that I saw and can't stop thinking about.

Here's the case. Imagine an AI that is actually deceptive: it says one thing while its internal cognition is doing something else. Now take that AI and replace it with a giant lookup table that just outputs exactly what the deceptive AI would have output in every situation. The lookup table has no deceptive internal cognition; it's just a table of answers. Is the lookup table ascription universal? My friend says yes, because there's no hidden internal state left to ascribe beliefs to, everything is in the outputs. But that seems obviously wrong, because the system still does the deceptive thing, and we wouldn't want to call it safe. Someone else told me the real issue is that the training process that filled in the lookup table is where the optimization lives, and that a definition of ascription universality should maybe include beliefs of the systems that helped create the system. They also said distillation is a realistic version of this, where a powerful system trains a simpler student that just mimics outputs. Can you explain the conceptual issue here? I want to understand what exactly ascription universality is trying to capture, why the lookup-table or memoized case is a problem for the definition, how epistemic dominance fits in, and why it matters whether we're making the internals transparent versus making the process that produced them transparent. If there's a clean way to state the distinction without just repeating the blog post's wording, that'd be great.
