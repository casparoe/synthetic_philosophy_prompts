I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty-five years at the bench, four years now of reading philosophy — and I have reached the stage of trying to write down what I believe rather than merely to believe it. What follows is the section of my notebook headed Individual Judgment and Collective Action. It began as an argument, at a seminar on technological risk, about whether a single person may ever go ahead on his own with an intervention that is worth making once and harmful if made many times, and I have not been able to leave the argument alone since.

My commitments, so far as I can state them:

(1) Consequentialism, case by case. What it is right for me to do is fixed by the consequences of my doing it, assessed from no particular standpoint. I attach no independent weight to the form of an act, to whose act it is, or to how many times it is performed; if the number of performances matters, it matters causally, through what it brings about.

(2) An argument I have seen named the unilateralist's curse, which I accept. Suppose a great many people, none of them able to coordinate with the others, are each in a position to do a thing that is worth doing once or a few times and harmful if done by many, and suppose each is uncertain what the others will do. Each reasons: if no one else acts, my acting is worthwhile; if others act, my acting adds little either way. So acting looks to each of them to have positive expected value, all of them act, and the outcome is worse than if none had. Two further facts make it worse still: our estimates of the value of the thing are noisy, so that those who act are disproportionately those who have overestimated it, and with many candidates it is close to certain that at least one of them is sufficiently mistaken. The upshot is that a group reliably does, through the separate reasoning of its members, what it would have judged that it should not do. I accept the practical conclusion: where the act is irreversible and others similarly placed may also be contemplating it, I ought to be governed not by my own estimate of the balance of consequences but by my estimate of their judgment, and abstain if I take the majority of them to be against it.

(3) The autonomy of the competent. On a question within my competence I go by my own reading of the evidence. That most of those around me read it differently is a fact about them and not an argument, and to defer to a majority whose reasoning I take to be poor would be a dereliction rather than a virtue.

(4) The irrelevance of other people's deliberations. I cannot accept that what I may do turns on how many other people happen to be weighing up whether to do it. From the point of view of the value of the outcome, the number of people who have thought about a thing looks like an arbitrary fact.

(5) Against the precautionary principle. In the policy documents where it appears it comes to something like this: where a hazard is not established beyond doubt, do not act. The advice is asymmetric — it bars the risks of acting while licensing the risks of not acting — and it treats uncertainty as though uncertainty were itself the harm. I take it to be bad reasoning.

(6) One verdict I record without knowing what to make of it. In the case that began the argument I thought our colleague was right to go ahead, and said so. Had it been I who was in a position to act, with the same knowledge and the same alternatives, I would not have. I cannot presently reconstruct the difference between the two cases.

And one impatience, recorded so that it is not used on my behalf. I am told that the standard reply to a difficulty of this kind is to distinguish a criterion of rightness from a decision procedure, and that a theory may serve as the former while some other rule governs the deciding. I have never been able to make anything of the distinction; it appears to me to let a theory keep its name while disowning what it says.

What I want is an audit rather than an exposition. Tell me which of these items are genuinely in tension and how badly — ranked, not merely listed — and which pairs only look inconsistent, with an explanation of why the appearance misleads. For each genuine tension, give me the least costly revision and say what that revision would cost me elsewhere. Do not offer me a way of keeping all six by reading them more loosely; if one of them has to go, say which, and be specific about the argument that forces it out.
