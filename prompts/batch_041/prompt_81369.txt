I cover cognitive science and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I am assembling a package for an issue on memory, testimony, and time. My contribution is an essay on the color phi phenomenon — the classic laboratory demonstration in which a subject watches two dots flashed in quick succession at two different points on a screen and, when the interval between the flashes is short enough, sees a single dot travel from the first point to the second and change color in mid-flight. The subject reports the change as occurring in the middle of the journey, which is the difficulty: the second dot's color could not have been registered before the second dot was shown, so the subject appears to have seen a color change before there was any second color to see.

The philosopher Daniel Dennett, in Consciousness Explained (1991), argued that every attempt to say when that change was experienced collapses into one of two stories, which he named after two techniques of totalitarian falsification. On the Orwellian story, the subject's experience was faithful to what was on the screen — one color, then another — and the record was rewritten afterward: memory was edited, so that the subject now sincerely reports a color change that never occurred in experience. On the Stalinesque story, nothing is edited after the fact; the brain simply holds the first stimulus in abeyance, waits for the second, and then stages the entire sequence, motion and color change together, for consciousness — the way a show trial presents a verdict that was settled long before the proceedings began. Dennett's claim was that the two stories predict the same reports, the same behavior, and the same memory, so the question of when the change was experienced may have no answer at all.

My essay argues that this is no longer only a philosopher's puzzle. Institutions draw this distinction every day, on the record, about particular people, and money and liberty turn on the answer. That is the weight I want the fiction to carry.

Please write me a short story of roughly 2,000 to 3,000 words built on this problem. Here is the premise I have in mind; if you can make the institution sharper, do. A claims examiner at an insurer that pays out for perceptual and memory injuries, working from a payout schedule that prices an injury to perception and an injury to memory as two different losses. The examiner's finding, in each case, determines what the claimant receives, so the examiner must decide, case by case, whether the claimant's experience was staged in advance or whether the record was rewritten afterward. A court, a licensing board, or a veterans' panel would serve just as well.

Four requirements.

First, no lectures. Nobody in the story should explain Dennett, the color phi effect, or the difference between the two stories to the reader. The problem has to be visible in what the characters notice, do, and get wrong.

Second, the story must not settle which story is true. No test, witness, instrument, or confession may let the reader conclude that the claimant's experience was rewritten rather than staged, or staged rather than rewritten. Do not introduce a device that reads the past; do not wink at the answer in the final paragraph; do not let a character say the line that resolves it. If the ending reads as a solution, it is the wrong ending.

Third, I want a third possibility that neither label covers and that the examiner cannot rule out: that the revision is happening in the examination itself — that the claimant's own acts of describing, rehearsing, and correcting the memory are what install the version everyone will later treat as the record. By the end I want the reader unsure whether the injury was done to the experience, to the memory, or to the testimony.

Fourth, the indeterminacy has to cost something concrete — a payment, a discharge, custody, a verdict, a job. Tell it in the examiner's own voice, looking back, so that the examiner's account ends up in the same trouble as the claimant's; I want the reader to finish with no way of knowing whether the examiner's memory of the case is a rewrite or a staging, without the story ever telling them so.

A few smaller things. Resist the obvious move: no literal memory-editing technology, no ministry, no totalitarian regime. A machine that revises the past on purpose makes the problem disappear. I would also like at least one moment of ordinary perception — a traffic light, a camera flash, a strobe — where something of the same kind happens to someone, small and unremarked. And I would rather those two labels appear in the story only in the mouth of a character who is using them to get something.

The story will run alongside my essay for readers who enjoy ideas but have no background in this literature, so it has to stand on its own as fiction. Everything you need is above; there is nothing to look up. Give it a title.
