For two terms I have been sitting in a joint seminar — philosophers working on practical reason and behavioral economists working on intertemporal choice — and the same exchange recurs without either side shifting.

The philosophers' problem is how weakness of will is possible at all. An agent who acts against her own best judgment appears to judge that A is better than B and yet choose B; but if a deliberate choice expresses the agent's judgment about what is best, the description is incoherent, and the familiar escapes are to say that the judgment was not really the agent's, or was not all-things-considered, or that the act did not express a judgment at all. The economists' problem is why intertemporal choice looks hyperbolic rather than exponential: an agent who discounts near rewards more steeply than distant ones will, as the earlier reward approaches, come to prefer it although she previously preferred the later and larger one. That reversal is a formal property of a preference ordering; the live questions are when it is predictable, whether the agent anticipates it, and what she will pay to precommit.

Each side treats the other as having changed the subject. The philosophers say a preference reversal is not the phenomenon they are trying to explain, because no judgment need be flouted: the agent may simply judge differently at the two moments. The economists say the puzzle is an artifact of a theory of judgment we do not need, since all that is observed is choice, and a chooser whose preferences reverse over time is not thereby guilty of anything except having those preferences.

My hypothesis is that this is neither a disagreement about the facts nor a straightforward one about what is rational, but a disagreement about the object of explanation. Let me give the two objects names. Gap-akrasia is a single act that departs from the agent's judgment at the time of acting; it is anomalous under that description and calls for a special account. Reversal-akrasia is a pattern in an agent's choices across moments, whose formal mark is a preference ordering that reverses as the options approach; under that description there is nothing anomalous, only a structure to model. The philosophers track the first, the economists the second, and if that is right they are answering different questions under one name, so that the stalemate is exactly what one should expect.

I want that hypothesis tested rather than restated. First, is it correct, or does a substantive disagreement survive the redescription — in particular about whether gap-akrasia can be reduced to reversal-akrasia, or about whether an agent who reverses and later regrets the reversal has violated a standard that is genuinely hers? Second, is any part of the impasse verbal, in the sense that the two sides attach different senses to 'irrational', 'will', or 'judgment'? Third, take two cases that seem designed to separate the objects. There is the sophisticated agent who foresees her reversal and precommits, so that her behavior never exhibits the pattern at all; and there is the agent whose judgment itself changes at the moment of choice, so that she acts on her current judgment and exhibits no gap. Does the first case show that reversal-akrasia can exist without gap-akrasia, and the second that the philosophers' phenomenon is really a special case of the economists'? Finally, given your diagnosis: what would count as progress here — a formal result connecting the two frameworks, an experiment that discriminates between them, a distinction that dissolves the appearance of conflict, or the conclusion that no progress should be expected because the two literatures were never addressing a single question?
