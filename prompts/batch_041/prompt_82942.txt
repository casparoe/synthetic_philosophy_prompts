ok, long-time lurker here, first time actually trying to write my risk preferences down instead of just arguing about them in comment sections. I want to know what principle, if any, my gut is really running on, because I'm fairly sure these can't all be right at once.

These are all one-shot choices, real money, no hedging, no repeating the bet:

1. $5,000 for sure vs a 90% shot at $6,000 (nothing otherwise). I take the $5,000, not close.
2. A 45% shot at $5,000 vs a 40.5% shot at $6,000. Here I take the 40.5% shot at $6,000. (This is just #1 with every probability scaled by the same factor, but my gut doesn't care.)
3. $50 for sure vs a 90% shot at $60. I take the gamble, easy — down here I just want the bigger expected value.
4. A 50/50 to lose $100 or win $120. I pass. And honestly I'd pass that same bet whether I was broke or loaded, it's just not a bet I want to hold. But a 50/50 to lose $100 or win $10,000, I take without thinking.
5. A sure loss of $1,000 vs a 90% chance of losing $1,500 with a 10% chance of losing nothing. Here I take the gamble and hope.

So I'm risk averse over gains, risk seeking over losses, I seem to care about the stakes and not just the probabilites (#1 vs #3), I treat a near-certain win differently from a middling one (#1 vs #2), and apparently I'll reject a small positive-EV bet forever while happily taking a huge positive-EV bet with the same downside (#4).

My own half-baked guess is that I just distort probabilities in my head — a 90% shot at something good feels like basically giving up a sure thing, and small chances of loss feel bigger than they are. But that story says the stakes shouldn't matter, and #1 vs #3 says they definately do, so I don't think it survives on its own.

Also, I half-remember an argument (from some decision theory post I can no longer find) that if you'd turn down a small favorable 50/50 like the one in #4 at every level of wealth, then expected utility forces you to also turn down a 50/50 with the same downside and a gigantic upside — which sounds obviously wrong, but I've never seen where the catch is. Is that actually right?

What I want: the principle (or a short list of candidate principles) that gets as many of these five verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is pulling its weight for which case. Tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, and be straight with me about which of my five verdicts it has to throw out, and why that's the one to give up rather than the others.
