I am a third-year student in a course on the ethics and law of death and dying, and our final assignment is a short story rather than an essay. The instructor's reason is that the difficulties in this area are less often disputes between positions than tensions inside a single position, and a narrative can hold a tension that an argument is obliged to resolve. I would like you to write that story, and I have a particular seam in mind.

The material I want it built on is this. The model statute adopted in most American jurisdictions defines death disjunctively: a person is dead if they have sustained either irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem. The law therefore does not identify one thing that death is; it offers two independent routes to the same verdict, and the verdict is what licenses everything downstream — the end of the duty to resuscitate, the withdrawal of treatment, the reading of a will, and the removal of organs, which under the dead donor rule may be taken only from someone already dead. Because the two routes are independent, they can be satisfied in either order and can even come apart: a body on a ventilator can meet the neurological criterion while its circulation continues, and a body in a persistent vegetative state meets neither.

The seam I am interested in is the word irreversible, which appears in both branches but does not mean the same thing in each. When death is determined by circulatory criteria, irreversible is standardly read as permanent: circulation counts as irreversibly lost once a decision has been made not to attempt resuscitation, even though the heart could in principle have been restarted. Under the neurological criterion that reading is unavailable, because brain death is precisely the claim that the brain cannot be resuscitated, and the decision to stop trying has no place in the determination. So one of the two statutory routes to death contains, among its conditions, a choice that someone has made; the other does not. I want a story in which that asymmetry is doing quiet work.

For the setting, a controlled donation after circulatory death is the obvious candidate: an intensive care patient with a non-survivable injury, treatment to be withdrawn on the family's decision, the transplant team kept separate from that decision and waiting outside, a fixed interval of observed asystole set by local protocol before death may be declared, and a further rule that if the heart does not stop within a fixed window the donation simply does not proceed. Use any of this only if it serves the story, and keep the numbers vague rather than specific — nothing should turn on my figures being right.

Write roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. Restrict the action to a few hours and one hospital, with no more than four named characters. I do not want a villain, a policy argument, or a clinician who breaks a rule: everyone should be competent and acting in good faith, and the difficulty should come from the structure of the criteria rather than from anyone's misconduct. Do not let any character use the words permanent or irreversible as terms of art, and do not explain the concept anywhere in the story. A reader who already knows the criteria should be able to infer, from the timing of one or two ordinary actions, which route to death was taken and what that timing was accomplishing. Somewhere in the story, though, one character should say in plain language what the difference amounts to — the plainest sentence in the story, not the most technical one. Let it end in the moment after the declaration, without a reckoning, a reversal, or a moral.
