I'm on the founding committee of a new housing co-op — about forty households, we've signed the lease, and now we're writing the rulebook from scratch. We meet every Tuesday, we're not lawyers, and everything we write has to pass a vote of the whole membership. We keep hitting the same snag and I dont have a good name for it: a rule that ends up biting the people who made it or the people who have to enforce it. We wrote "the board may suspend any member for antisocial behaviour" and then someone asked whether the board can suspend a board member, or itself. We wrote "these rules may be amended by a two-thirds vote at a general meeting," and then someone asked whether that sentence is itself amendable by a simple majority or whether it's frozen. We want "the chair's rulings can be appealed to the full membership," but then who decides whether an appeal has been properly made? It's like one of those Escher prints where each hand is drawing the other, and I'm scared we'll either write rules that can never change or rules that let a faction entrench itself for good.

Could you write me a procedure I can actually run in committee, rule by rule, for spotting when a rule is going to create this kind of tangle, and for deciding where to put the level that stands outside the rule? My hunch — I think I read it somewhere, maybe under the heading of the "inviolate level" — is that any system that can rewrite itself needs some part that isn't subject to the thing it controls, the sheet the hands are drawn on. But I don't know how to turn that into a checklist, or how to tell a loop that's harmless from one that's vicious. For each step give me the question to ask, what counts as an answer, and where to stop. Then run it on our three drafts: the suspension rule, the amendment rule, the appeal rule. I suspect at least one will get an answer I won't like, and I'd rather find that out now than in a year, when someones trying to use it against us.

Two fixes people keep floating: make the entrenchment clause need a bigger majority, or stagger it so rule changes only bind the next committee. I'd like the procedure to say whether those actually break the loop or just move it somewhere else.

And be straight with me if the whole framing is confused — if "inviolate level" is a philosopher's toy and real rulebooks sort this out some other way, or if some of these questions can't be answered by any procedure, say so plainly rather than dressing up a non-answer. I'm also happy to be told where the procedure runs out and it's just a value judgement — how entrenched to make the entrenchment is a political choice and I get that. Keep it tight, I've got to summarise it in the minutes. British English, please.
