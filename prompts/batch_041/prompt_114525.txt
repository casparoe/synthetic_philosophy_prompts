I'm a retired physiologist — thirty-odd years measuring stress and emotion responses in the lab — and since I stopped I've been teaching myself philosophy, mostly aesthetics and philosophy of mind, out of books and podcasts. There's one debate I keep running into that seems to have been going in circles since well before I was born, and I want a diagnosis rather than a summary of it.

It's the puzzle about fiction. I cry at a novel, I flinch at a horror film, my palms sweat, and I know perfectly well none of it is happening. The literature, as far as I can tell, splits roughly three ways: one camp says these are genuine emotions just like any other; a second says no, they can't be real emotions because real emotions require believing their object exists, so they must be some kind of pretend or make-believe affect; and a third says the puzzle is confused from the start. And they've been at it for decades without moving an inch.

Here's my difficulty. In my old line of work nobody would ever say the fear I show at a film isnt a real fear because the tiger isnt real — the body doesn't check. So my hypothesis is that this is largely a verbal dispute: philosophers stipulate a definition of emotion that builds in an existence-belief, then find that fiction fails the test, and then argue about whether to relax the definition. Underneath it, though, I suspect there's a real question about appropriateness or rationality that keeps getting smuggled in and mistaken for the definitional one.

But I don't trust my own read, and I don't want you to just agree with me. Can you diagnose it properly: is this a verbal quarrel, are the two camps answering different questions under one name (one about what the states are, the other about whether they're warranted), or is there a substantive disagreement I'm missing? A distinction I keep groping toward: maybe the film is the cause of my fear but not its object in the way a tiger is, and the whole thing lives in that gap. Tell me what each side is actually committed to, and what would count as progress here — a better definition, an experiment, or just dropping the question. And say so plainly if it's a genuinely hard problem rather than a muddle.
