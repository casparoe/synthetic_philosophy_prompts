I am closing a reading group on dispositions with a poem rather than a summary, and I want the form to carry the argument. The target is the old analysis of a disposition as a bare conditional: that calling a glass fragile comes to no more than saying it would break if struck. Three cases are standardly used against it. A fink: the glass is fragile, but a mechanism re-tempers it the instant anything reaches for it, so that if it were struck it would not break — the stimulus itself removes the disposition. A mask: the glass is still fragile, but it is wrapped in foam, so the consequent fails again while the disposition stays put. A mimic: the manifestation without the disposition, a cup rigged to shatter on impact. The moral most people draw is that dispositions have a causal basis and survive the failure of their conditionals.

Write a pantoum — four quatrains, standard line-recycling, rhyme optional — in the voice of the disposition itself. Let the recycling do the work: the lines that return should be the disposition's insistence that it has not changed, and that insistence has to survive being placed in the fink and in the mask, each of which should get a stanza. The mimic should get a stanza too, and there the returning line must still appear and still be the same words, but a reader should feel that there is nothing behind it — that is what mimicking is, and the form can show it rather than say it. The closing move, where the poem ends on the line it began with, should land as the fink's self-cancelling loop and not as decorative echo. If the pantoum cannot be made to carry all this, say so plainly and propose a form that can, rather than writing a poem that goes through the motions.

The three cases should be recoverable from the poem without labels, and the poem should not assert anything false about them — so if my setup already begs the question against the conditional analysis, say by building the microstructure into my description of the glass, tell me that instead of complying. End with a short note on where the poem takes liberties with the cases or leans on a contested reading, particularly if it treats the causal-basis account as the settled winner.
