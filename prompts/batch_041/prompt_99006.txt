I've somehow been roped into giving the keynote at the city's naturalization ceremony next month — a few hundred new citizens, mostly not native English speakers, families in the audience, a couple of local politicians, and (god help me) two of my colleagues from the department who will absolutely be grading me on this. I've got about eight minutes.

Here's what I actually want to do and here's my problem. I want to welcome them and, without being a scold, say something honest about what they now owe their fellow citizens — because I dont think citizenship is just a certificate you collect. But I don't want to stand up there and pretend the obvious justifications work. The line I keep reaching for is the reciprocity / fair-play one: you've been living here, using the roads, the schools, the courts, the safety — you've taken the benefits of a big cooperative scheme, so you now owe your share of keeping it runing. The other line is the relationship one: it isn't the benefits, it's that you now stand in a certain kind of relationship with these particular people, and relationships of that kind generate reasons you didn't have before — reasons that aren't owed to just anyone.

My trouble is that both of these have an obvious cosmopolitan heckler built right into them. If it's benefits, why do the benefits I get from my fellow citizens bind me to them rather than to the stranger abroad who needs the help more? If it's the relationship, why does the relationship I happen to have — because of where I happened to land — outweigh the claims of people I stand in no relationship to at all? I don't want to paper over this, and I obviously can't stand up there and run a seminar.

So write me the speech. Use that tension as its spine — let it shape what I can and can't say, instead of name-dropping it in the last line. It has to land for a room of people who've never read a word of philosophy and for whom English is a second or third language, and it still has to be something my two colleagues won't wince at. No jargon, no citations, nothing I'd have to stop and define. Don't resolve it with a bow — I'd rather it end honestly, with the pull still there, but warmer than a shrug.

Hard cap: 700 words. And if you've got a one-sentence version of the core claim, stick it at the very top before the speech itself, then give me the speech.
