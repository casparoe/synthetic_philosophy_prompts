Write an essay on whether "pseudohistory" is a real epistemic category or just a label the academy slaps on outsiders. Use a Fomenko-type case as the stress test: a mathematician who argues that the standard chronology of the ancient and medieval world is basically a Renaissance fabrication — that most of what we call antiquity is a duplicate or a back-projection, that written history only really gets going around 800 AD, and that the conventional timeline rests on a handful of chroniclers who copied each other. The mainstream calls this pseudohistory and moves on. But here's the wrinkle: you can't dismiss him as a crank with no method (he's running statistics on texts, of all things) or as someone who just doesn't know the evidence (he knows it, he's just reading it differently). And conventional chronology has been shifted around before — radiocarbon calibration alone redrew a lot of dates, and plenty of "settled" datings have been overturned — so "he disagrees with the experts" can't be the whole story, or the experts could never have revised themselves either.

So what actually seperates a crank from a legitimate radical revisionist? Is it the method, the way anomalies get handled, the willingness to say in advance what would refute the thesis, whether the claim hunts for independant lines of evidence or just piles up "confirmations", or is it in the end just sociology — who's in the guild, who gets to count as a historian? Is there a principled demarcation criterion here (the kind philosophers once hoped to find for science vs. pseudoscience), and does it survive the hard cases, including the awkward fact that mainstream history sometimes reasons asymmetrically too? Reach a verdict: either there's a real line and you can say where it falls and why, or there isn't and the label is doing social rather than epistemic work. Don't cop out with "it's complicated."
