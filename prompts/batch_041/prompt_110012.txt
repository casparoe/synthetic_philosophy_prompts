I have no formal training in philosophy, decision theory, or anything adjacent, but I have spent a long while on a puzzle of my own devising, and I would rather have a careful correction than a reassuring answer. I suspect this is a well-worn puzzle under some name, but I am going to describe it from scratch, because I do not trust my memory of what others have said about it and I do not want the reply to turn on that.

**The setup.** Two agents — call them the Sender and the Auditor — are sealed off from one another. No messages pass between them, no causal influence of any kind runs in either direction, and neither will ever observe anything the other does. Each has nonetheless built a machine that, given a full description of the other agent's situation and decision rule, reproduces the choice that agent would make. The Sender's machine has run, and it reports that the Auditor will later face a choice between two options, A and B, and that the Auditor's choice will be whatever its decision rule dictates. The Sender prefers that the Auditor choose A. The Sender's only lever is this: it can run its simulation of the Auditor under conditions of intense distress, and it will do so exactly when the simulated Auditor chooses B. I stipulate that the Auditor assigns real disvalue to that happening; I am not defending the stipulation, I am using it to isolate the question I care about.

**The defense I had in mind.** The Auditor rewrites its own decision rule so that a simulation of it run under coercion chooses the option the Sender least wants — in effect, making the coerced choice depend on the fact of coercion rather than on A and B themselves. Call this the poisoned rule. Since the Sender's machine is faithful, it will compute that coercing the Auditor yields the Sender's worst outcome, and a Sender who is at all sensible will therefore not coerce at all. So the Auditor keeps its original goal and never suffers the threat.

**The difficulty I cannot get past.** For the defense to do anything, the Sender has to know that the Auditor has adopted the poisoned rule. But there is no channel between them. So I seem to face a dilemma. If the Auditor keeps the rule to itself, the Sender cannot take it into account, and the threat lands exactly as it would have if the Auditor had done nothing. If the Auditor makes the rule known — by publishing it, say, or by choosing a rule so conspicuous that any faithful simulation must include it — then it has reintroduced the very communication the scenario forbids, and it is no longer solving the puzzle I set out to solve. And there is a third horn: the Sender's machine is faithful, so it reads off the Auditor's original goal and its new rule alike, whether the Auditor wants it to or not. If knowledge is the obstacle, the Auditor has already lost, since the Sender has the knowledge; if knowledge is not the obstacle, then I cannot see what the Auditor's choice of rule is supposed to accomplish at all.

**The fix I suspect is confused.** Make the poisoned rule unguessable. Choose a decision rule whose behavior under coercion is random, or so baroque that no simulation of the Auditor can predict it. Then the Sender cannot count on coercion succeeding, and the threat becomes a gamble that a sensible Sender will decline to take.

So: is my dilemma real, or have I misunderstood what has to be known and by whom? If the Sender's machine is faithful, in what sense is the Auditor's choice of rule a defense rather than a gesture? And does the unguessability fix help, hurt, or do nothing?
