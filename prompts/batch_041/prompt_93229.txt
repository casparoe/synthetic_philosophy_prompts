Alright, here's my situation. I'm a novelist, about two hundred pages into a book about two philosophers who've been circling each other for years, and I've decided the climax is a scene where they finally sit down and write a joint statement together — the document they could both actually put their names to. I want to write that document, and I want it to be real philosophy, not vibes.

The fight is about the statue and the lump of clay. One of them, call her M, is a two-objects person: the statue and the lump are two distinct things that happen to be made of the same stuff and sit in the exact same place — constitution isn't identity — and the tell is that they've got different properties, like the lump could survive being squashed flat and the statue couldn't. The other one, V, is a one-object person: there's just the one thing there, and statue and lump are two ways of describing or classifying it, nothing is added when you say the statue is also a lump, and the Leibniz's-law stuff is a trick of how we're describing the same object.

Here's what I need from you. Draft the joint statement they'd sign. It should have a numbered list of claims both of them accept, and then a disagreements table where each row is one remaining disagreement, stated in wording each person would accept for her own side (so M's column reads like M, V's column reads like V, no strawmen), then a crux column — the sub-question that, if it got settled, would actually move one of them — and for each crux a concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two things I need you to fold in specially, because they're what the scene is actually about. First, there's a case in my book that I made up and I want their views on it: a lump of clay that wind and rain have worn into the exact likeness of a person, no sculptor, no intention behind it. Is that a statue? If there is a statue there, is it the same object as the lump? I want to see whether their general machinery actually tells them anything here or whether they have to admit it goes quiet. Second, I want you to be honest about how much of this fight is just words — if part of the disagreement dissolves once you pin down what "is" and "object" are doing, say which part, and say it so neither M nor V would feel cheated. Related: I want to know whether they'd leave any room for the option that the lump and the statue are the same object right up until the squashing and only then split into two — and whether "how many objects are on the table" even has a fact of the matter, or only a fact relative to how you're counting.

Keep it plain, no showing off, and don't bother citing anybody — M and V are fictional and I can't put footnotes in a novel anyway. If some bit of the disagreement is just something they'd have to agree to disagree about, fine, but make it clear it's that and not a fudge. And please seperate the genuine disagreements from the ones that only looked like disagreements.
