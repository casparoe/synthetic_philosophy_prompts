Over the past several months I have repeatedly found myself in arguments about whether effective-altruism pledges, luxury watches, and unpaid internships are best understood as costly signals, and I want a standard before I use the term again. What must any adequate theory of costly signaling deliver? Give me the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that motivates it: the cases it must get right (the peacock's tail, gazelle stotting, human charitable giving, conspicuous consumption, and at least one cheap or deceptive signal); the puzzles it must dissolve (how honesty is maintained without assumed altruism, why cheap signals can still be informative, and how deception is kept in check); the distinctions it cannot collapse (signal versus cue, handicap versus index, strategic versus nonstrategic signaling, cost versus waste, and sender-imposed versus receiver-imposed costs); and the results it must be consistent with (stable equilibria and the persistence of conventional signals). Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp -- the original handicap principle, its formal game-theoretic reconstruction, human signaling accounts, index-signal accounts -- and flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied, especially any tension between the demand that signals be costly and the existence of cheap but honest signals. Keep the scorecard of existing theories brief; I care most about the criteria and the cases behind them.
