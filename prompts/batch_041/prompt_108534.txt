I am designing a seminar on the paradoxes of deterrence and want to build it around Sidney Lumet's Fail Safe (1964) rather than around the usual satire, because Lumet's film takes the doctrine seriously enough to argue with it. The plot, briefly, so that we are talking about the same thing: a computer fault hands a bomber group orders to destroy Moscow; the recall fails, partly because the command system is engineered to reject fraudulent recalls and partly because the Soviets jam the frequencies; the president can neither call the aircraft back nor shoot them down before they reach their target; Moscow is destroyed; and the president then offers the Soviet premier a symmetrical loss — one American city, destroyed by an American crew — on the reasoning that equal suffering is the only condition under which the Soviets will forgo retaliation.

What I want from you is a reading of the film as an argument about deterrence, tested against the following claims, which I would like treated as distinct rather than dissolved into a general gloom about mutual assured destruction.

1. The credibility paradox. A threat deters only if the adversary believes it would be executed, and executing it is by hypothesis irrational; credibility therefore has to be purchased by committing yourself to what you would not choose.
2. Brinkmanship. The usual purchase price is control: a threat becomes believable when its execution is partly removed from your hands and lodged in a deadline, a machine, a subordinate's discretion, or a hair trigger. The slack that makes the threat credible is the same slack that makes catastrophe possible — competence and accident run on one mechanism.
3. The always/never dilemma. A single force must always work when it should and never work when it should not, and every remedy for one horn aggravates the other. Fail Safe dramatizes the second horn with unusual precision: the safeguards that make an accidental launch impossible are precisely what make a legitimate recall impossible.
4. The non-event problem. Successful deterrence leaves no evidence, so it cannot be distinguished from luck or from the adversary's independent restraint, and the practice is in principle unfalsifiable.
5. The proof-of-innocence problem, which I take to be the film's own contribution and which none of the four above contains. The president is not trying to convince the premier that he would do something; he is trying to convince him of a fact about his own intention — that the strike was not intended — and the only currency in which such a claim can be paid is an act that could not be misread. To establish that you did not mean the harm you caused, you must inflict a harm you unmistakably do mean.

What I want, then, is not a summary but a judgment: which of these the film actually argues and which it merely assumes; whether (5) is a genuine addition to the theory or only a special case of the hostage logic in (2); where the film cheats — in particular, whether locating the origin of the crisis in a single faulty component invites the audience to blame contingency rather than doctrine, and whether the humanism of the final act (the two leaders on the line, the interpreter, the general who carries out the order and then kills himself, the First Lady inside the target city) softens an argument that ought to stay structural; and what Professor Groeteschele's counterargument — that an accidental strike is an opportunity to be exploited rather than a disaster to be contained — is actually worth, and whether the film hears it fairly or stacks the deck by making its spokesman repellent. Close by saying what the film establishes that the paradoxes as stated cannot: the texture of a decision made in minutes through an interpreter, the impossibility of warning the people who will die, the sense that the sacrifice is less a strategy than an expiation. Roughly 1,200 words of continuous prose; no citations needed.
