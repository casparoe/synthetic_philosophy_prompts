I want to know whether a view I hold about moral talk can survive the verdicts I have about how moral sentences behave when they are not asserted outright — and if it cannot, I want the best repair, not a survey.

The view: when someone asserts "lying is wrong," I do not think they are describing a fact about the world in the way they would be describing one if they said that the building is forty metres tall. I think they are expressing a stance — being against lying, rejecting it. I am inclined to add that the sentence, so used, is not true or false, and that the speaker is not reporting a belief about how things stand so much as taking up an attitude. That is the commitment I would like to keep.

The verdicts. Take "lying is wrong" as the test sentence and put it through some contexts; these are my reactions, and I am fairly confident about each on its own.

(1) "If lying is wrong, then getting my brother to lie is wrong. Lying is wrong. So getting my brother to lie is wrong." Valid — and valid as ordinary modus ponens, not as some special "practical" inference that merely looks like it.

(2) The clause "lying is wrong" in the antecedent of that conditional must mean what the sentence means on its own. If its meaning shifts between the conditional and the premise, the argument equivocates and the validity is a pun.

(3) "It is not the case that lying is wrong" is a claim with a truth value, and it is inconsistent with "lying is wrong." Someone who asserts both has contradicted herself.

(4) "Either lying is wrong or tax evasion is wrong" has a truth value, and it is true just in case one of the disjuncts is true. It does not express a single attitude; it is the ordinary disjunction of two contents.

(5) "Marta believes that lying is wrong" is true or false, and Marta is in a genuine belief state — she takes the world to be a certain way.

(6) "Is lying wrong?" asks precisely what the assertion states; answering "yes" is asserting the sentence in (1). The content is the same in the interrogative as in the declarative.

(7) Someone who sincerely says "lying is wrong" and is wholly unmoved by it — who would lie without a flicker, not from weakness but because the thought exerts no pull at all — does not seem to me to be fully in the state that sentence expresses.

Now the trouble. (2) says the embedded clause has the same content as the standalone assertion. (3) and (4) say that content has a truth value and behaves truth-functionally. If the content is the same everywhere, and it is truth-apt in the embeddings, it is hard to see how it is not truth-apt in the assertion, and my opening commitment is gone. If instead I protect the commitment by denying that the content is truth-apt, then (3), (4), and (6) go, and I do not see how (1) can be valid without (2). And (7) pulls the other way: it is the kind of verdict that makes the attitude view attractive in the first place.

My own attempt, which I would like tested and, if necessary, replaced: the semantic value of "lying is wrong" is the attitude it expresses; "if" takes attitudes as its arguments; the validity of (1) is a matter of the attitude expressed by the conclusion being inferable from the attitudes expressed by the premises, with a rule licensing detachment; and "not" and "or" express higher-order attitudes rather than operating on truth values.

Is there a single principle that gets all seven verdicts? State it precisely, clause by clause, naming for each clause the verdict that forces it. Then tell me which of the seven it cannot accommodate and which one I should abandon — I would rather lose a verdict than keep a patch that explains nothing. Finally, say what your principle predicts about a case I have not listed: a supposition voiced by someone who does not accept it, as in "Suppose lying is wrong; then what you did last night was wrong." Commit to one account and defend it rather than laying out options for me to choose from.
