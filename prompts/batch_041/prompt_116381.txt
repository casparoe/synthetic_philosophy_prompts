They'll all have read it once, and they'll arrive thinking the answer is obvious and boring. Don't explain the Meno problem to me; plan how I should teach it in about seventy minutes. I want the plan to end somewhere more interesting than "knowledge is more stable than true belief," because I don't think that's actually where the difficulty is.

So: order the components of the session so that each one depends on the last, and tell me what has to already be on the table before the question of why knowledge beats true belief even sounds like a real question rather than a trick question. In particular, I want the sequence to make them feel the gap between (a) a true belief that won't be lost, that's stable for every practical purpose, formed in a way that couldn't easily have gone wrong, and (b) knowing — since if my answer to Meno is just "knowledge is more robust," I've bought one kind of value and quietly left the harder part open.

Pick the single example that carries the whole session and tell me what it has to have and what it must not have. I'm tempted by the statue-of-Daedalus bit and by the road-to-Larissa bit; tell me whether either can carry it, and if not, why not.

Give me the others they'll form, and mark the exact point in the sequence where each one should be defused — before or after the carrying example lands, and why there rather than elsewhere.
