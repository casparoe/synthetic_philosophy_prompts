Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two parties who have argued for years about actual infinity. The first is a finitist: she holds that only potential infinity is intelligible, that no completed infinite totality is a legitimate object of mathematics, and that Hilbert's hotel is best read as a reductio of the completed infinite. The second is a Cantorian: he holds that completed infinities are legitimate mathematical objects, that an ordinary set theory handles the hotel without contradiction, and that the hotel is an instructive illustration rather than a paradox. They have agreed to produce, together, the document their dispute should have yielded — one that both could put their names to — keeping the residual disagreement exact.

Produce the following.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than hedged into triviality, and each must be a sentence both parties would accept: what the hotel argument does and does not establish; what is and is not derivable about the hotel within an ordinary set theory; what the finitist concedes about the internal consistency of that theory, and what the Cantorian concedes about its bearing on what exists; and where both agree the mathematics alone is silent.

2. A disagreements table. Each row is one question genuinely at issue; two columns give the finitist's and the Cantorian's positions, each phrased in wording its own side would accept; a final column names the crux the row turns on. Include, at minimum, rows on whether the principle that a whole exceeds its proper part is a conceptual truth about size or a principle that simply fails for infinite collections; on whether the question of how many members a collection has admits an answer of the kind the other side's notion of size was ever meant to supply; and on whether the finitist's rejection of the completed infinite is the claim that it is false, that it is unintelligible, or merely that it is dispensable.

3. A list of cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. For each, state a case, argument, or test that both parties agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Two cases must be among them. First, the Register: a hotel maintains a ledger assigning each occupant a distinct finite room number, checked at every stage, and the manager claims the ledger is complete; say whether the claim that the ledger is complete can be true, and what each party predicts. Second, the Two-Sizes Proposal, which neither has published: that we distinguish two notions of size, one governed by the part-whole principle and one by one-to-one correspondence, and that the dispute dissolves once we say which notion is in play; assess whether this relocates the disagreement or merely renames it.

4. A closing section that separates the verbal part of the dispute from the substantive part, says what each party would have to concede for the verbal part to be set aside, and states plainly what, if anything, both agree would actually move the debate.

Work from the mathematics and the concepts themselves; no references or reading lists are needed.
