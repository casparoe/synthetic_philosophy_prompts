Two research groups have spent several years arguing past each other about corruption across cultural and institutional contexts, and have agreed to produce a joint statement instead of another round of replies. One group holds what its members call the universalist position: that corruption — roughly, the use of entrusted public authority for private or particularistic advantage — is a single cross-culturally identifiable phenomenon; that the difficulty of drawing the gift/bribe and patronage/representation lines is an ordinary problem of evidence rather than a conceptual or cultural one; and that reluctance to apply a common standard across settings is usually a failure of nerve. The other holds the contextualist position: that corruption is a thick evaluative concept whose application is internal to a particular political morality; that many practices classified as corrupt by outside observers are better described as functioning institutions of reciprocity, insurance, or representation operating where the formal alternatives are absent or predatory; and that the universalist standard tends to smuggle in a specific picture of what the state is for.

Write the document the two groups could jointly sign. It should contain, in this order:

1. Scope and stipulations. What the statement covers and does not; and a short shared vocabulary in which the terms that will do the most work — gift, bribe, patronage, clientelism, favour, reciprocity, accountability, entrusted authority, and corruption itself — receive operational definitions both groups accept as stipulative. State explicitly which of these are offered as working devices rather than as claims about the world.

2. Agreed claims, numbered. Each must be a claim both groups would sign, and each must be non-vacuous: a third party must be able to disagree with it. Where a claim survives only because it has been made so abstract that no one could deny it, say so.

3. The instrument. The groups have agreed to work from four constructed cases sharing a single skeleton and differing along one parameter each: (a) whether a formal rule is violated; (b) whether the transfer is disclosed or concealed, and what the agent believes about its permissibility; (c) whether the recipient is under an enforceable or merely social obligation to reciprocate, and to whom; (d) whether the allocation tracks a personal tie or an impersonal criterion, where the impersonal criterion happens to be satisfied by the actual recipient anyway. State what both groups agree is descriptively true of each case, and where their classifications first come apart — including which single parameter does the work, and whether either group denies that the parameters are separable.

4. Baselines. Each group must say which baseline it judges a transfer against: the stated rule; the practice that would have obtained had the transfer not occurred; or the practice that would obtain under the best feasible alternative institution. For each group, say whether its choice of baseline is a factual or a normative commitment, and identify any case on which the groups can agree that the baselines come apart.

5. Disagreements, in a table. One row per disagreement, with columns for: the issue stated neutrally; the universalist formulation, in wording that group would accept as its own; the contextualist formulation, in wording that group would accept as its own; what each side takes to turn on it; and whether it is a disagreement about the extension of a concept, its normative weight, its empirical consequences, or the right level of analysis (transaction, institution, or system). Both sides must be stated at the same level of specificity — no side may purchase agreement by retreating to a vaguer formulation.

6. Cruxes. For each load-bearing disagreement, state the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one group, the case or argument both groups agree bears on it, and each group's prediction, recorded in advance. Include at least one prediction about a jurisdiction that replaces discretionary allocation of a benefit with rule-based allocation: what each group predicts for the worst-off, for the total volume of transfers, and for the informal brokerage sector.

7. Verbal disputes. Which of the listed disagreements the document's authors judge to be about words rather than substance, and what real disagreement remains once the words are fixed. If any dispute turns out to concern which cases are paradigm cases rather than the theory itself, say so.

8. Where the collaboration failed. Any disagreement that could not be jointly stated at all, and why.

9. Self-exposure. For each group, what it would accept as evidence against its own position, separating what would be decisive from what would be merely awkward — for the universalists, what would show that their standard is parochial rather than universal; for the contextualists, what would show that their contextualism is doing exculpatory work it cannot justify.

Keep the tone that of a document written by both parties rather than by a referee. Where the two groups disagree about what the other has conceded, record the disagreement rather than resolving it.
