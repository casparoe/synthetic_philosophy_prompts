I am a graduate student sitting in on a seminar that reads seventeenth-century political thought alongside contemporary decision theory, and I have been asked to open next week's discussion on the Introduction to Hobbes's Leviathan — the stretch where he sets out his method before the argument proper begins. I can follow the general shape of what he is doing, but one passage has me stuck, and the more I look at it the less I think my confusion is just about the archaic prose. He writes that "Nosce teipsum, Read thyself" was not meant to license either the arrogance of the powerful or the insolence of the low, but "to teach us that for the similitude of the thoughts and passions of one man, to the thoughts and passions of another, whosoever looketh into himself and considereth what he doth when he does think, opine, reason, hope, fear, etc., and upon what grounds; he shall thereby read and know what are the thoughts and passions of all other men upon the like occasions." Yet almost immediately he concedes that what we desire, fear, and hope varies with individual constitution and education, that the "characters of man's heart, blotted and confounded as they are with dissembling, lying, counterfeiting, and erroneous doctrines, are legible only to him that searcheth hearts," and that reading a man from his actions without comparing them with our own case is "to decipher without a key." He then closes the thought by saying that this kind of doctrine "admitteth no other demonstration."

What I want help with is how these pieces are supposed to fit together, and in particular whether Hobbes is making a claim of the sort I have seen described, in a decision-theory context I only half remember, as ascription universality — roughly, the property an agent or decision procedure has when any competent interpreter, applying its own standards of rationality, must read its choices as rational rather than as noise, error, or mere mechanism. On one reading, Hobbes is offering a substantive psychology: the passions are the same in all men, so my self-scrutiny yields a key that happens to fit every lock, and the caveats about dissembling and about the variability of the objects of desire are merely practical obstacles to using it. On another reading, he is doing something stronger and stranger: he is claiming that the interpreter's own case must serve as the key, so that a creature whose passions were relevantly different would not be an agent we are merely ignorant of, but one that would be unintelligible to us as an agent at all — which would be why the doctrine "admitteth no other demonstration," there being nothing else for it to be demonstrated from.

Please walk me through the passage on both readings, tell me which one the surrounding sentences and the stated method actually support, and be explicit about what the second reading commits Hobbes to that the first does not — in particular, whether the concession that the heart's characters are legible only to God undercuts the universal key or instead restricts the claim in a way that lets it survive. If the second reading is the right one, I would also like to know what it is a claim about: human nature, the concept of agency, or the demands of interpretation — and what, if anything, could count against it, given that Hobbes seems to have arranged matters so that nothing could.
