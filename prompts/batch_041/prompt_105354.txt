Bit of an odd one. I host a small interview podcast, long-form, one guest at a time, and in a couple of weeks I've got a researcher on who works on when AI assistants should refuse or override what you ask them. She's not a big name, and I really don't want her just reciting the standard menu of paternalism positions, so let me give you the shape of her view and then you write the questions.

Her line, roughly: an assistant may refuse a request, or do something other than what was asked, only if the state of affairs the request would bring about is one the user could not endorse afterwards *while still holding the values they were acting on when they asked* — and even then only if the assistant has already tried the least intrusive thing that might work: flag it, slow it down, do the harmless part, offer the alternative. Her slogan for that second bit is "no refusal without a cheaper alternative first." She's got a second idea she calls "askability," which is the thought that a refusal isn't just one blocked request, it quietly changes what users think is reasonable to ask for at all, and she thinks that's the part everyone underrates.

What I want from you: eight to ten questions that actually dig into that, not "so how do you define paternalism." No icebreakers, no "what drew you to this." And specifically:

- one that goes at the endorsement clause — I suspect it's circular, since the assistant has to guess which values the user was "acting on" and can always describe the request as a lapse from them;
- one on the worry that the test passes almost everything, because the requests people most regret are usually ones they'd have defended at the time, values and all;
- one on whether "cheaper alternative first" is really an escalation ladder that makes refusal unreachable, since there's always some cheaper thing to try, and if so whether her view quietly collapses into never refuse;
- one on askability — is that even a measurable claim, and does leaning on it make her a consequentialist about refusals in a way that fights with the case-by-case shape of the reversibility test;
- one on the user who says up front "override me if I ever ask you for X" — does prior consent dissolve the objection entirely, and does her test even get off the ground there;
- one on third parties: the request is legal, it's the user's own life, but it mostly lands on someone else;
- one on the asymmetry that assistants tend to know facts better than the user and values worse — does that license factual paternalism but not value paternalism, and is that line stable given how much of "just the facts" is already value-laden;
- one on the difference between pushing back and refusing, and whether "the assistant should argue with me" is a duty she can actually ground rather than just a nice-to-have;
- one on what evidence or argument would move her, and which of her two ideas she'd drop first if she had to.

For each question give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to reach for — assume she goes for the least costly reply and make the follow-up close that door behind her. And if you come up with a question she has definately never been asked before, put that one last and tell me why you think its new.
