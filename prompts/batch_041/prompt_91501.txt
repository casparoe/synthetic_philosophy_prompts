Religious Belief as a Rehearsed Form of Life

The later Wittgenstein is often read as saying that religious belief is not a hypothesis about how the world is, but a way of living, a commitment that regulates one's life. To call it a belief is not to predict an experiment; it is to take up a stance. On this view, the religious person and the atheist do not disagree in the way two scientists disagree about the boiling point of a liquid. They inhabit different forms of life, and their words do different work.

But this creates a puzzle. If religious claims are not empirical hypotheses, what makes one set of religious claims better or worse than another? Wittgenstein seems to say the practice itself is the standard. Yet some practices are cruel, incoherent, or dead. Can a practice be religiously wrong from within?

I propose a distinction between two ways a religious claim can function: as a hinge that holds a form of life together, and as a script that is rehearsed. A hinge is not questioned because everything else turns on it. A script is enacted, repeated, and varied; its truth is not correspondence but fit to the drama of a life. A prayer might be a script. A doctrine of creation might be a hinge.

Consider a thought experiment I call the Rehearsal Congregation. They gather weekly to enact a story of a god who suffers with them. They do not assert that the story happened. They say the story is true in the way a score is true when played well. They would not die for the claim that the god exists; they would die for the claim that the story must be told. Are they religious believers on Wittgenstein's view, or are they merely playing? I think Wittgenstein should say they are believers if the rehearsal actually governs their lives, if it shapes how they bury their dead, forgive enemies, and endure illness. The inner assertion is not the test; the outer practice is.

This suggests a criterion: a religious claim is genuine when it is a hinge or a script that a community cannot abandon without losing the shape of its life. But this seems to make any deeply entrenched practice religious. A nationalist ritual might meet the test. So I add a second criterion: the practice must direct itself toward what the community treats as ultimate, not toward its own power. The Rehearsal Congregation passes; a flag cult fails. But I worry this second criterion imports a moral judgment that Wittgenstein's own approach cannot license.

My question is whether this distinction between hinge and script is really Wittgensteinian, or whether I have invented a distinction he would reject. And whether the Rehearsal Congregation is a coherent example of religious belief without factual belief, or just a philosophical fiction that collapses under pressure.
