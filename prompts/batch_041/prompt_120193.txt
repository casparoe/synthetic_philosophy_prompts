I'm putting together a session on memory and national myth for a course on war and remembrance, and I want to run it through Atonement — the McEwan novel, though the film keeps the twist so either is fine. What I keep circling is that the book does at the scale of one family what a nation does with a lost cause: Briony invents the reunion that never happened, hands the lovers the life they never got, and we cry over it before we learn it was made up. Set that next to Dunkirk, a retreat that memory has turned into the founding triumph of the war — same family of stories as the Confederacy's Lost Cause, Masada, Kosovo 1389, defeats retold as the moment we became ourselves. So is Briony's coda the same move at small scale, or a seperate one? And does the novel indict that move or secretly perform it?

Two things I'd like pushed. First, a friend insists a myth isn't a belief, it's more like a debt: nobody has to think it's true, they only have to be answerable to it. Does that fit Briony's ending? Does it fit the Dunkirk story? Second, the label. Briony's fabrication is published as a novel, plainly fiction, while a national myth gets told as history and taught in schools — does that difference in what the words are doing really change the moral status, or is it a fig leaf?

Also, what exactly is she grieving when she writes the reunion — the actual dead, or two people who never existed? Can you mourn a counterfactual at all? Tell me which scenes to put in front of the class (the fountain, the library, Robbie on the road to the beach, the coda), and where the reading strains — I suspect it's a stretch to make one girl's private guilt carry a national myth, and I'd rather flag that myself than have a student do it.
