I keep reading takes on transformative experience and every one of them argues from a single pet case, so before I commit to anything I want the spec instead. What does any adequate theory of rational choice under transformative experience actually have to deliver? Give me the desiderata: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the constraints it can't violate, each with the consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are really just preferences of one camp.

Cases I want it to handle, because these are the ones that keep breaking my own half-formed view:

- the gradual one: someone becomes a parent over years, no single moment where the change happens. So is 'transformative' an event or a process, and does the theory owe us an answer?
- the reversible peek: a temporary drug lets you live for an hour as a person with completely different values, then you revert but keep the memory. You get the inside view without the change sticking. Does that dissolve the problem, and if so what does it imply about what transformation even is?
- the new-you endorsement: after transforming, you approve of who you've become, but the old you would have refused. Whose verdict counts, and can the old you ever be wrong about the new you's good?
- the collective one: a whole generation's preferences shift together, say by growing up with some technology; nobody individually chose it, yet the change is real. Does the theory have to cover that, or is it only ever about individual choice?

One more thing I want tested: I assume a decent theory has to stay neutral on whether transformed values are *better* or just *different*. Tell me if that's a genuine requirement or if it quietly makes the whole thing collapse, and if so what's the cheapest thing to give up. And flag any two requirements on your list that can't both be met at once — thats the part I care about most.
