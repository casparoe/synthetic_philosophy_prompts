For years I have followed the long-running argument in the rationalist blogosphere about coherence arguments for expected utility maximization, and it has the texture of two people who agree on all the mathematics and disagree about what the mathematics is doing. I would like the document that the two camps have never actually written.

Position A holds that the representation theorems, together with the money-pump and Dutch-book arguments, establish that expected utility maximization is rationally required: an agent whose preferences violate the standard axioms — completeness, transitivity, the independence and continuity conditions, and the rest — is not merely exploitable but irrational, and the theorems are doing normative work rather than merely describing a possible preference structure.

Position B holds that these results establish at most that some preference structures admit an expected-utility representation, or that certain violations expose an agent to a losing sequence of trades, but that the move from can be represented or can be exploited to must conform is precisely the step that needs defending and never receives it. On this view the theorems are descriptive and the money-pump is a prudential threat rather than a demonstration of irrationality.

Write the joint statement that a committed defender and a committed skeptic could both sign in good faith. I want the claims they agree on, including whatever both accept about what the theorems actually prove and about what the money pump actually shows; the precise points of disagreement, each phrased in wording that its own side would accept as faithful; the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and, for each crux, the argument or test that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

I suspect the real cruxes are among the following, but test each rather than assuming it, and add any I have missed. First, whether the money-pump argument presupposes a naive agent who keeps accepting trades after being burned, and what becomes of the argument when the agent is sophisticated enough to commit in advance to refusing the sequence. Second, whether completeness is a requirement of rationality or an idealization, and whether someone with genuinely incomparable options is irrational or merely incompletely specified. Third, whether the argument is prudential — you will lose resources — or epistemic — your preferences are incorrect — and whether a prudential argument can ground a rational requirement at all. Fourth, whether expected utility maximization as a conclusion needs more than synchronic coherence, for instance consistency across time or across states of information, and whether the theorems supply that or only assume it. Fifth, whether the support for expected utility maximization survives when the utility scale is unbounded or the relevant expectation diverges.

Format the result as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a section of disagreements, then a cruxes table whose rows pair each crux with the shared test and the two opposing predictions. Keep every formulation non-vacuous: no claim so hedged that both sides sign it for opposite reasons, and no claim slanted toward one camp's vocabulary. If any part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, say which part and why. Argue from the ideas themselves and from the structure of the arguments rather than from appeals to authority; do not provide citations, reading lists, or attributions, since the disagreement is conceptual and both parties here know the material. The output should be a text both camps could put their names to, with whatever disagreement remains stated exactly.
