I've been reading rationalist and EA-adjacent blogs and forums for about a decade, and I've got a pile of gut reactions to unilateral-action cases that I can't get to sit still under one principle. I don't want a reading list, I want you to look at the cases and tell me what I actually believe.

Quick background so we're aligned on the puzzle: the story I've absorbed is that when N people each have the option to do a thing, each will do it only if they judge it net-good, and the thing only needs to happen once, then it happens way too often — because the "should this happen?" question gets asked N times and only one yes is needed. Call it the unilateralist's curse. So there's pressure toward a rule like "only do it if you'd be fine with everyone in your position doing it," or "hold back if most people in your shoes would hold back." Fine. Here's where my guts actually are.

1. A researcher is the only person who's worked out how to make some nasty pathogen more transmissible, and she publishes it because she's sure the defensive upside is bigger. My gut: not ok. Not just "risky" — she's done something wrong to the rest of us.
2. Same researcher, but the technique is already out there from someone else and she's adding a step any grad student would've guessed. Totally fine.
3. She publishes a proof that a widely deployed encryption standard is broken. Fine. Actually good.
4. She publishes a working exploit for that standard. Not fine. And the difference between 3 and 4 is one afternoon of work I don't think is morally load-bearing.
5. One small country starts spraying sulfates into the stratosphere. It's cheap, it's reversible, they've promised to stop if anyone objects. My gut: not fine, and the reversibility doesn't rescue it.
6. A dying patient takes an unapproved drug on his own, no trial, no reporting. Fine. Now a thousand dying patients each do the same, none report anything, and the accumulated data is lost forever. Still fine, each of them — which is weird given #1.
7. One person quietly patches a bug in critical infrastructure without telling the maintainers, because they're unresponsive. Fine. But the hundredth person to independently do this feels worse to me than the first, even though their act is identical — I think it's that once quiet unilateral patches are normal, the maintainers never learn they have a problem.
8. A voter decides his vote is irrelevant and plays video games instead. Fine. Flip it: someone who'd have to spend an afternoon reporting a serious flaw in voting software doesn't bother, since his report alone changes nothing. Also fine. If everyone reasons that way, though... I still want to say fine, and I notice this is the exact mirror of the curse and I don't feel the same pressure.
9. Everyone who could do the dangerous thing has publicly sworn off it, and one person now knows he's genuinely the last candidate. My gut: still not fine! Which seems to wreck the whole framing, since there's no aggregate left to curse.
10. Someone does the dangerous thing *first* on purpose so nobody else will bother — preemption. My gut: worse, not better, which I gather is the wrong answer.

So what do I actually think? Is there one principle, and where does my gut get it wrong? Also give me your verdict on two cases I haven't listed: (a) ten labs pre-commit to a lottery deciding which one of them acts, and the loser goes ahead; (b) someone does the fine-in-itself thing but broadcasts it so others imitate. And tell me how confident you really are in whatever principle you land on — I'd rather hear "70% this is the shape of it" than have it presented as obvious — and name the single point you're least sure of.
