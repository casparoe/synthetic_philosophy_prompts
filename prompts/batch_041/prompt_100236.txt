I'm putting together the opening unit of a graduate seminar in pragmatics, and I've decided that before my students touch any particular account of conversational implicature, they should be handed the criteria that any such account has to satisfy. So I'd like you to write that specification for me. Please take the role of a philosopher of language with a specialist's command of the pragmatics literature, and work through this deliberately rather than producing a textbook recap: begin by brainstorming the full range of phenomena and puzzles an account of implicature is on the hook for, and only then organize those into requirements. What I need is the desiderata for an adequate theory of conversational implicature — a theory of how a speaker can mean more than, or something other than, what her words say — stated sharply enough that I can use them to grade candidate accounts. Give each requirement with the case, datum, or consideration that motivates it: the cases the theory must get right (the scalar quantifier that falls short of the stronger term; the disjunction that suggests exclusivity without asserting it; the tautology and the ironic compliment; the recommendation letter that damns with faint praise; the answer that is relevant only through an obvious further premise); the distinctions it must not collapse (what is said versus what is implicated; conversational versus conventional implicature; generalized versus particularized; implicature versus entailment, presupposition, and mere perlocutionary effect; implicature versus the unarticulated content that is meant but not said, as in a bare report that it is raining); the explanatory demands it must discharge (cancellability, reinforceability, non-detachability, calculability, defeasibility, indeterminacy, and the worry that the maxims cannot be applied until what is said has already been fixed, while what is said seems to be fixed partly by pragmatic reasoning); and the empirical results it cannot contradict (acquisition and processing data on scalar implicatures, cross-linguistic variation, and the absence of any genuine speaker behind texts, signs, and machine-generated strings). A table for the requirements would be welcome if it helps, and I'd like you to mark which requirements are constitutive and which are merely preferences of a particular school.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, tell me which pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since I think the list itself is a result when that happens. I am suspicious that calculability and default-based generation cannot both be had, and that the cancellability test, at least as it is usually wielded, cannot survive embedded cases. I would also like a few nonstandard cases built into the list, because my students will otherwise just rehash the stock examples: first, an utterance in which the speaker believes she is implicating one thing while the hearer recovers something else — say she expects the hearer to supply an unstated premise from context and the hearer instead takes her to be asserting it; second, a sentence in which the indefinite quantifier sits under negation or in the antecedent of a conditional, where the alleged implicature does not survive the way the standard account predicts; and third, a case in which an implication is carried without the speaker intending it at all. Say what each case forces the theory to decide, and note where a requirement seems to be doing no independent work beyond restating a favorite account.

Finish with a short verdict on which single requirement does the most to exclude the contenders — Grice's own account, the neo-Gricean default views, relevance theory, and truth-conditional pragmatics — and which of them satisfies the most desiderata while failing the fewest. I would rather have your reasoning than your verdicts, so be explicit about where your reconstruction of the standard tests is most likely to be wrong, and tell me what evidence or argument would change your mind about which requirements belong on the list. Please write in American English.
