I teach a small upper-level seminar on metaethics and the philosophy of normativity, and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on the Euthyphro dilemma that a student cannot answer by reciting the two horns. My students can all produce the standard version — the good is either good independently of God, in which case God is not what grounds morality, or good because God commands it, in which case morality is arbitrary — and I want a question on which that recitation earns almost nothing.

The crux I want to test is the one the standard treatment skips. The Euthyphro is not really a dilemma for theism; it is an argument schema, and the same schema generates a parallel dilemma for every proposed grounding of a normative property, secular groundings included. A strong answer has to state the schema abstractly enough to travel, run it on at least one non-theistic account of goodness, and then say what would count as a reply that genuinely terminates the regress rather than relocating it or dissolving it.

Please write the following.

1. One essay question, answerable in about seventy-five minutes, that requires the student to (a) state the dilemma in a form abstract enough to apply outside the theology; (b) instantiate it on a secular grounding of goodness of their choosing — a desire-based, welfare-based, or idealized-observer account; and (c) assess the standard theistic replies, especially the one that grounds goodness in God's nature rather than in God's commands, as an instance of a general class of replies, saying for each whether it terminates the regress, merely relocates it, or denies that the regress needs terminating at all.

2. A model answer of roughly eight hundred words.

3. A marking scheme worth twenty-five points that names the specific moves a top answer must make and the marks each is worth, with a one-line note on the specific error that costs students marks on each. Among the moves I expect to be load-bearing: the distinction between reading the because causally and reading it as a claim about what grounds what; the point that the arbitrary horn depends on a modal premise about what God could command, so denying that premise blocks the horn without positing a standard above God; and the symmetry point that if the divine-nature reply escapes the dilemma then its secular analog escapes it too, and if the divine-nature reply fails then the secular analog fails with it.

One requirement for the model answer specifically: it should say how confident it is in each substantive claim it makes and single out the point it is least sure of. I expect that soft spot to be whether any reply can terminate the regress rather than relocate it, but let the answer identify its own weakest claim.

American English throughout.
