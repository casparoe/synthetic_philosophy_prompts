Last night I argued with an old friend for the better part of three hours, and I would rather put the disagreement to work than lose the sleep again.

We were arguing about the co-operative where I live — some two hundred households that own their buildings in common and run everything by vote — and about a deadlock we keep hitting. Our rules let any household claim a 'personal domain' over certain matters: what it cooks, how it keeps its own front garden, whether it keeps a pet, whether it works from home. The same rules let the whole membership adopt binding standards: no open fires on the balconies, no amplified music after eleven, no vehicles on the green. These two things collide, and our rules say nothing about what to do when they do. My friend's position was that the arrangement is incoherent: a community genuinely liberal, he said, must let each household settle what concerns only itself, and one that lets the majority override that is bullying a minority with extra steps; yet a community that lets any household veto whatever the majority cares about cannot govern itself at all. So one of the two principles must be a fiction, and we should admit which we have quietly abandoned. My position was that the collision is real but not fatal — it means we cannot have both principles unconditionally, not that we must surrender either — but I could not tell him what we should do instead, and I would like to be able to.

I chair the committee that hears these disputes, so this is not hypothetical. We get two or three a month, and the household that loses can be genuinely hurt: one was recently told to rehome a dog, another to give up bees on its roof, and in both cases the members said, in effect, that we had voted on something that was theirs and not ours. I need a decision procedure I can actually run, and that the other six members of the committee can apply without having read any political philosophy. Please write it for me: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and says no, and the point at which it hands the decision back to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than of fact.

Three constraints, all of them coming out of the argument.

First, we cannot appeal to how strongly anyone feels. We have no way to measure that and no common currency in which to compare one household's intensity with another's; we have only people's rankings of the outcomes and their reasons. The procedure has to work with that and no more.

Second, people here do not care only about their own affairs. Several members have told us they are made miserable by a neighbour's choice even when it touches nothing of theirs — one said she cannot enjoy the shared garden while another household keeps a dog she is frightened of, though the dog is always on a lead. A procedure that counts only self-regarding preferences describes a community that does not exist; one that counts all preferences will let majorities legislate over what a household does inside its own walls. Be explicit about which the procedure does, and why.

Third, my friend's parting shot was a proposal I cannot dismiss. He suggested that anyone claiming an exemption should pay into a common fund — a 'liberty tax', he called it — so that the household keeping its dog or its bees compensates the others for the nuisance, with the majority free to set the price. He thinks this dissolves the conflict by making liberty a question of what you will pay. I do not trust it, but I cannot articulate exactly what is wrong with it, and I would like the procedure to say whether such a scheme is admissible and, if not, on what ground.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: a household wanting to keep bees on the roof, where the objection is stings and fear rather than harm yet done; a household wanting to slaughter an animal in the shared courtyard for a religious festival, where the objection is that the courtyard is common and the practice offensive to some; and one further case you construct, in which the procedure returns an answer that will be unpopular with the committee and unpleasant for me, so that I can see what it costs to follow it. Mark clearly which steps are genuinely deciding and which are only arranging a decision a person must make, and tell me at the end what the procedure cannot do — which disputes will still have to be settled by judgement, and which by the crude arithmetic of a vote.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in each part of this, and single out the step you are least sure survives contact with real cases.
