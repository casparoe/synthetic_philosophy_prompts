Hey — I'm a second-year student in a game theory course that's cross-listed with philosophy, which is why we get a story assignment instead of just problem sets. We've just finished backward induction, and for the seminar I have to hand in a short story that makes the paradox around the centipede game vivid for someone who's never drawn a game tree. Not an explainer with characters stapled onto it — an actual story, where the logic is doing work under the surface.

The setup I want it to dramatize: a finite chain of rounds that both sides can see the end of. At each round, whoever's turn it is can either stop (they walk away with a bit more than the other person, but they torch most of what was on the table) or let it ride (the pot for the next round gets bigger). Everyone knows how many rounds there are, everyone knows everyone knows, and so on. The backward-induction story is that the last round is a pure grab, so the round before it is pointless, so you should stop on round one — which is obviously not what any real pair of people does.

Two things I'd love the story to carry without lecturing about them:

1) There are Nash equilibria where both sides ride it out all the way to the end. So the payoffs by themselves don't force the early grab — the subgame-perfect refinement is doing all the work, and I want the reader to feel why it's strange for the refinement to be doing that much work.

2) If each side thinks there's even a small chance the other is the type who lets it ride once or twice, then riding early can be the smart move, and the unravelling stops unravelling. I keep getting into arguments about whether the paradox is really about rationality at all, or just about the common-knowledge assumption being too strong. I'd rather the story sit inside that disagreement than resolve it.

Practical stuff: keep it under about 1,200 words, make the two characters people rather than Player A and Player B, and please dont have one of them explain the game tree to the other — that's the cheap way out.

Then, after the story, a short note for me and not for the prof: where does the story take liberties with the ideas, or lean on a reading of them that people actually fight about? And seperate for me what's genuinely settled from what's contested — which claims here would basically nobody in the field dispute, and which ones are live disagreements where I'd be wrong to present one side as if it were just what the theory says. I dont want a confident verdict handed to me dressed up as the consensus.
