I have been trying to make sense of the ethics code that multinational companies now apply to gifts and hospitality, and I keep finding the same thing I can't explain. The standard regime is one code applied to every office worldwide, with training, certification, and a register of gifts. It sorts the risk of a gift by two variables and only two: how much the thing is worth, and how close in time it comes to a decision that could benefit the giver. Above a threshold that is deliberately modest — a figure in the tens or at most low hundreds of dollars — a gift must be disclosed, approved, or refused; anything given to someone who is currently deciding something that touches the company is presumptively barred; and the employee's job is to log the transaction. What the regime conspicuously does not examine is the relationship between the two parties or the intention behind the transfer: a bottle of whisky from a supplier you have dealt with for twenty years and a bottle from a stranger angling for a contract are treated as the same event. Yet the same code waves through a set of transfers that look structurally very similar — a corporate political contribution routed through a trade association, the hiring of a former regulator who was supervising the company a year or two earlier, a speaking fee paid to an official's spouse, a study tour to the company's headquarters. And the managers in the countries where we actually operate tell me the whole apparatus is just a description of how Americans do business: a gift is how you acknowledge a relationship, not how you buy one, and it is the refusal that insults, not the gift.

What I want from you is an account of what this practice presupposes about the nature of corruption — what corruption must be, such that value and timing are the diagnostic features while relationship and intention are not, and such that a personal transfer to a decision-maker is corruption while a corporate transfer producing the same influence is not. Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and then separate the parts the practice genuinely requires from the parts it merely fits comfortably with. Could a quid-pro-quo theory, an institutional-integrity theory, an account built around accountability or domination, and a straightforward cultural-relativist account each license these same rules, or does the regime's particular shape rule some of them out? I am especially interested in the threshold: a bright line at a dollar figure implies that below it the harm is negligible, or at least that the harm is discontinuous in the amount, and I want to know whether that is a substantive claim about corruption or only an administrative convenience a defender could abandon without loss. I would also like the local managers' objection taken seriously rather than treated as an excuse — is the thought that gift-giving is a different practice with a different meaning something the regime can accommodate, or does the regime depend on a conception of corruption that is not universal at all? And say what would have to change in it if the presupposed theory were replaced by a structural one that took the corporate transfers as seriously as the personal ones.

If the puzzle I have described is not really a puzzle, tell me so plainly rather than working around it. It may be that the apparent inconsistency dissolves once one distinguishes what the rules are for, or that the code is best read as a coordination device among firms and regulators that presupposes no theory of corruption whatever — in which case my question about its implicit theory has a deflationary answer, and I would rather hear that than a survey of the literature. I am not looking for a balanced tour of positions; I want a reconstruction that commits itself, and I want to know where it is weak.
