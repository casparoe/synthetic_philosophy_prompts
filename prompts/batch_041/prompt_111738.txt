I've argued myself into a position and I want you to try to break it. Here's the position: there is no such thing as choosing without preferring. Whenever an agent actually takes one of two options, something tipped it — a real difference in how the options struck the agent, however faint, or a rule the agent had already adopted (when it's a tie, take the left one; when it's a tie, flip a coin; when it's a tie, go alphabetically). And where neither is present, nothing happens: you stall, or you oscillate, and if you eventually move it's because something else broke the symmetry from outside — hunger, boredom, a noise. So the donkey between the two identical bales never starves, and more importantly there's no distinct act of "just picking" left over to explain. (Side note, the donkey probably isn't even in Buridan — I'm told the whole thing is a later caricature of him, and the older version of the case has a man between food and drink. Doesn't matter for my purposes.)

What would count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd actually be, fatal down to merely awkward, and I want you to be honest about which ones I'd just wave away.

Things I'd like you to work through specifically, though add your own:

- An RL agent whose Q-values for two actions are exactly equal, and whose argmax just returns the lowest index. Deterministic, repeatable, identical payoffs, no preference anywhere in the system, and we'd normally say it picked the first one. Is that choice without preference? Or is the tie-break rule itself the preference? And if the rule counts as a preference, doesn't any deterministic system end up "preferring" things, which makes the view true by definition and therefore uninteresting?
- The regress on the rule. When I adopted "left when tied," was there a preference for leftness-as-a-rule? If yes, the rule is redundant; if no, I needed a preference-free choice to adopt it, and the view eats itself.
- Habit, autopilot, skilled action. I don't consult any preference to take my usual route home or to put one foot in front of the other. If those are actions, that looks like a counterexample. If they're not actions, then most of what anyone ever does isn't action, and that's a cost I should have to pay.
- Decision theory. Any choice function with the right consistency properties can be rationalized as maximizing some weak order, so my view starts to look like a representation theorem rather than a claim about minds. But that cuts both ways: a choice function that can't be rationalized — cycling in the wrong way, or shifting with context — would be a case of genuinely preference-free choice. Does such a case exist, and is it a defeater or just noise?
- The experiments where people end up defending an option they never chose. Does that show the preference report is downstream of the pick, so preference isn't doing the causal work? Or does it only show that reports are unreliable?

Then tell me which of these a defender of the view would concede and which they'd dismiss, and what each concession costs. And the thing I'm most worried about: the escape hatch "there's always some faint differential pull" seems to make the view unfalsifiable — any observed pick gets a post-hoc thin preference assigned to it. Is there a version of the view that actually risks something? What does it predict that its rival predicts otherwise? The rival being: agents sometimes do act without preference, and that's precisely what makes the act theirs, or what freedom consists in. If my view turns out to risk nothing, is that fatal for a claim that's supposed to be about agency, or is it fine for a conceptual claim? Don't just reassure me either way — I'd rather hear that it's well insulated and that this is a real problem than get a defense that patches everything.

Last thing, since you'll ask: what I'd personally find most persuasive is a repeatable case where the agent insists, over and over, that there's no difference between the options and no rule being applied, and then acts anyway. But I suspect that's just introspection dressed up, so tell me what better form the evidence could take — and if there isn't one, say so.
