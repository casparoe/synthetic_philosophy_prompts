I'm a first-year MA student and I've somehow ended up as the interviewer for our department's little comparative philosophy podcast — basically I get a visiting speaker for forty minutes and have to fill it with questions. Next week it's the post-doc who's been giving her paper on the white horse dialogue, and the whole thing has got under my skin in a way I wasn't expecting. I'd always assumed that 'a white horse is not a horse' was either a pun on how many jobs 'is' does or a slightly cheeky point about class inclusion — you can hand over any yellow or black horse if someone asks for a horse, but only a white one if they ask for a white horse, so the extension of 'white horse' is a proper part of the extension of 'horse'. Then a friend pointed out that on that reading the conclusion is trivially true, and it's only interesting if you think it's *false*, and I've been going round in circles since. Her line, as far as I can make out, is that both the trivial reading and the paradoxical reading miss the point: the real target is the assumption that a name names one thing, and the opening move of the dialogue — where the advocate says 'horse' designates the shape and 'white' designates the colour — is doing all the work, because 'horse' names a what, 'white' names a quality and 'white horse' names a this, so the dialogue is a reductio of the idea that names all work in a single way. My supervisor reckons this is over-reading a corrupt text (apparantly most of the Gongsun Longzi is lost and bits of this dialogue are thought to be out of order), and I half agree, which is why I want the interview to be a proper interrogation and not a friendly chat.

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not left improvising when she says the obvious thing? I'd like the sequence to build, so it gets harder to wriggle out of as it goes, and please don't send me a reading list — I won't get through it before Tuesday. Things I definately want covered: something that forces her to say what the relation is between the shape-and-colour argument at the start and the seeking-a-horse argument later — two arguments for one conclusion, or one real argument plus a rhetorical flourish? Something on the word 非 (fei) and its two readings, 'is not identical to' versus 'is not a kind of' — which one her reading needs, and how she explains that the objector in the dialogue plainly hears it the second way and still comes off worse. Something that presses the triviality charge head on: if all she's claiming is that a white horse is not the same thing as horse-as-such, why did anyone bother writing this down, and why does the advocate treat it as something certain rather than something obvious? Something on anachronism, since her reading leans on distinctions — a name naming a what versus naming a this — that the period arguably didn't have; I don't want her to get away with 'the Chinese were doing semantics too', I want the actual cost of importing our apparatus. And something about what happens to the rest of the corpus on her reading — the hard-and-white dialogue, the pointing-at-things chapter, and whether she thinks the School of Names were doing philosophy or just playing with words.

For the last one, I'd like a question about what would make her give the reading up — what she'd expect to find in the text, or in a better reconstruction of the dislocated passages, that would show she's wrong. Oh and can it be in British English, because it goes out under a British university's name and the editor will change it all and then I'll have to check it twice.
