I am a retired marine biologist — some thirty years spent on the husbandry and physiology of farmed finfish, and latterly on penaeid shrimp — and since retiring I have been reading my way, rather haphazardly, into moral philosophy. Over dinner a few weeks ago a younger colleague asked me whether I thought a shrimp could suffer, and I found that I had neither a settled answer nor any clear sense of what argument or evidence would settle one; worse, I came to suspect that my own intuitions had been shaped by decades of treating these animals as stock to be kept alive and growing, which is probably the worst possible vantage point from which to map the space of defensible views. What I would like from you is therefore not a survey of the literature but a classification instrument — a decision tree that carries a reader from a small number of starting commitments to a named position on the moral status and the treatment of farmed fish and crustaceans. I intend to circulate it as a handout to a reading group of ex-colleagues, most of them biologists with no philosophical training, so it must be usable by someone who cannot answer by knowing the labels in advance: every branch must be phrased as a question about a concrete case or a plain commitment, never as a question about which school one belongs to.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. I would like the branch nodes to turn on, at minimum, the following questions, in whatever order you find most perspicuous: whether the capacity to suffer requires a particular kind of nervous system or admits of degrees, and how one should act while the answer is uncertain; whether felt suffering is the only intrinsically welfare-relevant bad, or whether premature death, crowding, and the frustration of species-typical behaviour count as well; whether the unit that matters is the individual animal, the population, or the ecosystem it belongs to; whether the sheer number of individuals — a single trawler's bycatch, a hatchery's countless larvae — changes what is required, and if so whether by a total-sum or a per-individual route; whether duties to these animals arise from their capacities alone or from the relation created by our having bred and confined them; and how their welfare is to be weighed against human nutrition, coastal livelihoods, and the environmental footprint of the alternatives. Fix each branch with concrete cases: the ice-slurry and live-asphyxiation methods by which many farmed fish are killed; electrical stunning; the stocking densities and oxygen crashes of intensive ponds; live transport; and eyestalk ablation in shrimp broodstock, in which a female's eyestalk is removed in order to bring on spawning. I want eight to ten named leaves, and I want these among them even if you rename them: a suffering-only welfarist; a welfarist who also counts death and thwarted behaviour; a rights-based abolitionist; a precautionary view that extends moral caution to animals whose sentience is unproven; a sceptic who denies these animals any welfare at all and reasons only from human and ecological interests; a relational or care-based view grounded in domestication; an ecological holist who privileges populations over individuals; and a gradualist who scales moral weight to cognitive capacity.

Please present the result in the following order and under these headings. Orientation: a short opening section, roughly 100 words, stating the starting commitments and any conventions the reader needs. The Diagnostic Questions: five to seven numbered questions, each followed by a sentence on which positions its answers separate; no question may name a position. The Tree: a schematic mapping, indented or tabular, of how the answers combine into leaves. The Positions: the eight to ten leaves, each with its name, one sentence characterizing it, and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Unoccupied Combinations: roughly 200 words identifying combinations of answers that nobody seems to occupy, and for each diagnosing whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Pairs This Tree Cannot Separate: roughly 120 words flagging any two leaves whose adherents would answer every branch identically, so that I do not pretend the instrument is more discriminating than it is. Write throughout for readers who have no internet access and will not be given citations; where a position lacks a settled name, say so and describe it rather than guessing at a label.
