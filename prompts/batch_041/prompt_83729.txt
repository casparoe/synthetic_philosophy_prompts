For several years I have taught a graduate seminar on the epistemology of historical reconstruction, and the session I find hardest to prepare is the one on the criteria of authenticity in research on the historical Jesus. In principle it ought to be a clean case study. The field has for the better part of a century used a settled toolkit — dissimilarity, embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, the presence of Aramaic idiom or of a recognizably Palestinian setting — to separate sayings and deeds that go back to Jesus from those that belong to the later tradition. For nearly as long, critics inside the field have argued that the toolkit cannot do the work asked of it. Dissimilarity, for instance, makes authenticity a function of how poorly a saying fits its surroundings, so that a Jesus thoroughly at home in Second Temple Judaism and thoroughly usable by the early church becomes invisible by construction. The criteria appear to be applicable only by a historian who already has a picture of Jesus, the very thing they were meant to establish. And they have never been calibrated against an independent sample of known-authentic sayings, because there is none. Yet everyone in the room agrees with the criticisms, and the books keep appearing, each with its own reconstructed Jesus and its own confident verdicts, while the critics produce further critiques rather than rival reconstructions. No one is refuted and no one moves.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict, and not a survey. Is this a substantive disagreement about how evidence works in this domain, or a verbal dispute that turns on the word 'authentic'? Are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, or is the impasse the sign of an ill-posed question?

My own suspicion, which I hold loosely, is that the quarrel is not about the reliability of the criteria at all but about two things the disputants never make explicit: what property the criteria are supposed to detect, and what kind of thing a criterion here could be. On one reading, authenticity is a property of individual sayings and deeds, established piecemeal by something in the neighbourhood of a likelihood ratio; on the other, it is a property of a whole trajectory of transmission, assessed by asking whether the resulting account is the best explanation of the tradition in its entirety. If that is right, the two sides are not contradicting each other so much as talking past each other, and the criteria are being asked to be both a test and a heuristic. Tell me whether the distinction survives contact with what each side actually says.

I also want you to press two analogies that I have found illuminating but cannot get to settle anything. First, the validation of the criteria resembles the validation of a diagnostic instrument in the absence of a gold standard: if there is no criterion-independent way to identify the cases, does that make the criteria unfalsifiable rather than false, and is unfalsifiability here a defect or simply what any interpretive discipline looks like? Second, if the criteria are likelihood ratios, then their deliverances depend on a prior over what a first-century Jewish teacher would plausibly have said, and the prior is not evidence but background theory — so is the residual disagreement really a disagreement about priors, and are the priors theological in a way that neither side will own?

Finally, and most importantly: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a clarified statement of what authenticity is a property of, a probabilistic reformulation with an honest account of where the priors come from, a demonstration that some one criterion is valid, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the object of inquiry is not well defined? And what would not count, even though it would look like progress: a new criterion, a new manuscript, more data, a more vivid reconstruction. I have read the literature; I do not need a bibliography or a reading list. I need the structure of the disagreement and an honest answer about whether it can be moved.
