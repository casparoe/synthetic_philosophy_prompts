You are a philosopher of causation and of historical explanation, writing for a working historian who is not a philosopher. Begin by laying out the candidate readings before you commit to one, then answer.

Professional historians enforce a norm against counterfactual speculation: asking what would have happened had the Armada landed, or had the archduke's car taken a different turn, is dismissed as idle fiction — unverifiable, not really history. Yet the same historians routinely assert causal claims that appear to require counterfactuals: that a battle was decisive, that a commander's blunder changed the war's course, that industrialization was inevitable, that the Reformation would have arrived anyway. The profession treats counterfactual history as illegitimate while treating counterfactual-sounding causal talk as its bread and butter. What conception of causation, of counterfactuals, and of historical explanation does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify which rival views it quietly rules out. Does causal talk in history genuinely commit the historian to counterfactuals, or can it be cashed out without them — through mechanisms, difference-making, regularities, or narrative? Is the practice coherent, or does it need a principled line between legitimate and illegitimate counterfactuals; if the latter, what draws that line? One puzzle I cannot resolve: if historical processes are chaotic, so that tiny perturbations cascade unpredictably, then counterfactuals look unverifiable — yet historians treat some causal claims as robust. Must the implicit theory distinguish robust from fragile counterfactuals, and can it? Finally, say what would change in historical method if counterfactual reasoning were admitted as a legitimate evidential tool.

Separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
