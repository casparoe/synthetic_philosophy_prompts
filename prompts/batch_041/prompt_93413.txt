Two people in my book club got into a fight last week over the old shared-pasture story and I got stuck refereeing because I was sat between them. Here's the gist of the back-and-forth.

Dan went first. He said the tragedy of the commons is basically a mathematical inevitability: if each herder gets the whole benefit of adding one more animal to the common pasture but only eats a fraction of the cost of the extra grazing, then adding the animal is always individually rational, so the pasture gets wrecked. Anyone who thinks a community can just 'decide' to hold back, he said, doesnt understand the incentive structure.

Mara said that's just false as history. There are tons of real commons -- irrigation systems, forests, inshore fisheries -- that people ran sustainably for centuries without either privatizing or handing them over to the state, so 'inevitable' is just the wrong word.

Dan said fine, but those cases only work because the users weren't anonymous strangers. When everyone knows everyone and you'll have to deal with them again tomorrow, the repeated interaction changes the payoffs, so it's not a counterexample to the logic, it's just a case where the logic predicts cooperation.

Mara said no, that's not the real difference either. The real difference is that in the working cases the users made and enforced the rules themselves -- who gets how many animals, who does the monitoring, what happens to someone who cheats -- whereas the tragedy only bites when it's genuinely open access with no rules at all. Conflating 'nobody owns it' with 'nobody governs it,' she said, is the whole mistake.

Dan said that just moves the problem up a level: making and enforcing rules is itself a public good, so now you've got the exact same free-rider problem about who's going to do the monitoring and pay for the enforcement, which is why you end up needing an outside authority anyway.

Mara said that's a different problem from the one he opened with, and that outside authorities fail for their own reasons (they have their own interests, the locals know the resource better), and that he keeps sliding between two different games without noticing.

Neither would budge and they want me to rule. Can you go through it move by move -- rate each of the six from clearly wrong to clearly right and say briefly why -- and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing? I've got a nagging feeling they're talking past each other because 'the commons problem' is doing two different jobs in the conversation, but I can't put my finger on where. If that's right, say exactly where the equivocation happens and whether it changes who wins which move.
