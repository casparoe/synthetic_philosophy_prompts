For the past several months I have been the person at my company who removes things: deprecated feature flags, a cron job nobody can explain, an internal service with three callers and no owner. In every one of those reviews I eventually hear the saying that you should not take down a fence until you know why it was put up, usually attributed to Chesterton. The most recent argument was over a nightly job that rewrites a column in one of our tables and, as far as anyone can tell, has been failing silently for about two years. A staff engineer invoked the fence principle and said that settled it; a product manager replied that a job which has been broken for two years without anyone noticing is precisely the case where the fence is decorative. Both treated the saying as obvious, and both took it to support opposite conclusions, which makes me suspect that it is several different principles wearing one coat. I have no formal philosophy training, so when I try to state what the saying actually commits you to, I end up with platitudes like be careful, and those would not have settled the meeting.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that the fence saying could be making, as a menu rather than a single favoured interpretation? For each reading I would like to know what it asserts about existing arrangements — where the burden of proof sits, what counts as knowing why the fence is there, whether ignorance of its purpose is itself a reason to keep it, whether the claim is about consequences or about authority or about the value of the past or about who has standing to decide — and then a verdict: is this reading a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something false or question-begging? Please also tell me which readings would actually support the staff engineer's conclusion that the failing job should stay, which would support the product manager's, and which reading I am most likely to be facing when a colleague cites the saying in a code review. Finally, I would be grateful if you could say where the saying sits relative to the broader conservative thought associated with Burke — the idea that inherited arrangements carry a presumption in their favour and that this presumption is not merely a matter of caution — and whether that idea adds anything the fence image does not already contain, or whether the fence image is doing something narrower and more modest.
