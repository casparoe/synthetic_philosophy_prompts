Hey — I'm a TA for a phil of mind course and we just wrapped the unit on Dennett's hetophenomenology, and I also run a weekly movie night for the students where we watch films that are supposedly about the theories. Last week was Memento, and at the end one of them asked a question I completely fumbled, and now I can't stop thinking about it (my roommate is sick of hearing about it). Her point was that Leonard is basically doing heterophenomenology on himself: he's lost the ability to lay down new memories, so the only way he can find out what he thinks, wants, or has decided is to read his own tattoos, notes and Polaroids the way you'd read a stranger's diary, and reconstruct a mind out of them. Which is weirdly on the nose, because Dennett's whole move is to treat a subject's reports the way we treat a novel — you build up what he calls the heterophenomenological world out of what the subject says, you bracket the question of whether the experiences are really like that, and then you go hunting for the third-person correlates. So far so good.

Where I get stuck is the ending. Leonard isn't just an unreliable narrator in the ordinary way — if I'm remembering the film right, once Teddy spells out the truth he deliberately burns a photograph and writes himself a false lead, because he'd rather have a story he can live in than the real one. And the whole selling point of heterophenomenology, as I understand it, is that it lets you do the science without taking a stand on the metaphysics of experience — you're neutral on whether the subject's reports are true, you just treat them as data. So what happens when the text you're reading was authored by the subject, for his own purposes? Does the method have anything to say about a subject who's motivated to write himself a fiction, or is that just outside the frame? I half suspect Dennett would say it's already priced in — the fictional stance never required the fiction to be true, and we can still predict and explain Leonard's behaviour from his notes and his habits — but I want to know whether the film shows a genuine limit rather than a cute coincidence.

So: give me a real reading of Memento through heterophenomenology, faithful in both directions. Where does the analogy actually earn its keep (Leonard as the ideal heterophenomenological subject, since his inner life is literally externalised onto paper and skin)? Where does the film push back — is a self-authored text a problem for the method, or a case it handles fine? And where does the film resist the reading, so that an honest critic has to admit the lens is distorting something? The Sammy Jankis thread especially — my half-thought is that it's Leonard's own story retold as a fiction, which would make the whole film a heterophenomenological world we're invited to move into, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real point or just something that sounds clever. Finish by saying what, if anything, the film contributes to the debate that the papers don't. Last line: start it with Verdict: and give me one sentence on whether the film vindicates the method, exposes a limit of it, or is really orthogonal to it.
