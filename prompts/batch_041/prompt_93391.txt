I'm closing a graduate seminar on intergenerational ethics, and I would rather end the term with a poem than with a summary. Write it for me.

The poem is spoken by someone in the present to a single addressee a fixed number of generations ahead — choose the number and hold it — and its shape should be the discount curve itself: each stanza shorter than the one before by roughly the same factor, so that a reader with a pencil could estimate the rate from the line counts alone. The shrinking should be visible without being a stunt, and it must never reach zero: the last stanza is one line, and that line still has weight. Nothing in the poem should announce the arithmetic; the form does that work.

Two distinctions have to be legible in the poem's movement without ever being named as such. The first is the difference between the two reasons for caring less about what comes later — that the later thing may not happen, or may be better off anyway, versus that later simply counts for less. The turn should be the moment the second reason is caught doing the work, and the speaker, not the reader, should be the one who notices. The second is the same rate applied across space: somewhere in the poem the speaker should try caring about a distant contemporary the way they care about the distant descendant, and the reader should feel how much worse the rate looks when the distance is miles rather than years — the point at which the poem's argument cuts against the discount rather than for it.

No jargon, no policy, no appeal to what we owe anyone, no closing moral. Free verse is fine; rhyme is fine if it does not fight the shrinking. Read aloud it should run under three minutes. After the poem, add one sentence naming the per-stanza factor the shape implies and saying what it would mean if someone took that factor as a discount rate on welfare rather than on goods. Someone who has spent years on this should find the poem accurate; someone who has not should want to read it twice.
