I am a high school junior and the student leader of our school's social justice club, which meets for one hour on Monday afternoons with about eighteen members, most of whom have taken the school's civics elective. Next week I am responsible for running a session on luck egalitarianism, and I want to design a simulation that makes the group feel the force of the view rather than just hear me summarize it. Luck egalitarianism, as I understand it, holds that inequalities are unjust when they arise from brute luck—where you were born, your natural talents, unexpected illness, and the like—but not when they arise from people's own choices, which is sometimes called option luck. I was discussing this with a friend after class, and we both found the view compelling at first but then started to wonder whether the distinction between brute luck and option luck is as stable as it seems, since the choices people make are themselves shaped by their dispositions and circumstances. I want the activity to stage that instability rather than just talk about it, so that participants first feel the pull of the luck egalitarian idea and then feel it start to slip.

My plan is a two-stage game. In the first stage, each participant draws a token from a bag. The token assigns them a starting number of resource points and also a risk disposition—some tokens make you cautious, some make you bold—and then, without knowing what anyone else has drawn, they make a series of choices that can either pay off or fail depending on a die roll. After the outcomes are revealed, the group has to design a redistribution rule and vote on it. The twist I want to build in is that the rule they design will then be applied to a second round in which everyone's starting positions and dispositions are reassigned by a new draw, so that people who insisted on holding others responsible for their choices in the first round may find themselves, after the second draw, arguing for compensation, and vice versa. Please give me the full design: the exact setup of the bag and tokens, the choice mechanics and payoffs, the rules for the redistribution debate, a timeline for the hour, and a debrief that maps what happened onto luck egalitarianism and its critics. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—especially if the group tries to design a rule that is too complicated, or if someone refuses to accept the second draw—and how I should handle that. Finally, be honest about which feature of luck egalitarianism the simulation cannot capture and that I will have to explain in my own words afterward.
