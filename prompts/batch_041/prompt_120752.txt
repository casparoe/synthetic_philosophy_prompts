In the UK at the end of the 80s the goverment scrapped local property taxes and replaced them with a flat charge per adult, the poll tax, with the amount set by each council. It went badly: mass non-payment, a riot in Trafalgar Square, and Thatcher out of office by the end of that year. Use that as a case study for the thesis that taxation is theft. Do the mapping first -- on the theft view, what was taken from whom, by what title, and does it matter that this was about as close to a pure head tax as a modern state gets, with barely a nod to what you use or what you can afford? Then the part I actually care about: if it's theft, the objection should be to the taking as such, but the same people paid income tax and VAT without rioting, and the loudest complaint was that a duke paid the same as his gardener -- a fairness complaint, not a coercion one. So what does the episode do to the thesis? Then run it the other way: what does the thesis do to the episode -- does "it's theft" explain the scale of the resistance better than a consent story does, or does it just flatten the difference between the tax everyone shrugged at and the one that finished a prime minister? And is the economists' point that head taxes are the most efficient kind of tax, since nobody can dodge them by working less, any sort of political defence of the poll tax, or is this the cleanest case of efficiency and legitimacy coming apart? Finish with what the episode shows about the theft thesis itself.
