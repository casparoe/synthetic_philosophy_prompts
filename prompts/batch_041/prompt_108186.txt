A friend and I spent most of a six-hour drive arguing about psychiatric diagnosis, and I ended up defending a position I am not sure I can defend. The position is this: for a large class of psychiatric categories — not every one of them, but the ones that dominate the clinic, the conditions defined largely by what people report rather than by a biomarker — there is no classification-independent fact of the matter about who has the condition. The category and the people it picks out shape each other, so that the label alters what it is like to have the condition, which alters who seeks the label and what they report, which alters the label in turn. I do not mean the weak claim that labels have social effects; I mean the strong claim that for these categories the diagnosis is partly constitutive of the condition, so that asking whether someone really has it apart from the classification is like asking how tall someone would be if we had no word for height. My friend kept calling this mysticism, and I kept failing to give her a clean statement of what would prove me wrong.

What I want from you is a map of my position's exposure. Which observations, arguments, and clinical or epidemiological developments would count against it rather than merely complicate it, and which of those would be decisive as opposed to awkward? I already suspect the sharpest challenge is that the thesis is unfalsifiable — that any disconfirming case can be redescribed as one more turn of the loop — and if that is right, I want to know whether it is fatal or whether there is a principled way to specify in advance what would refute me. I would also like to know what a sympathetic defender would themselves concede counts against the position, as distinct from what a hostile critic merely asserts, and what the position predicts that its rivals do not.

To keep this concrete, my friend proposed that we could measure a category's "loop half-life" — the time it takes for the distribution of reported symptoms to drift measurably after a revision to the diagnostic manual — and she claims a short half-life supports me while a long one refutes me. Tell me whether that test tests anything, whether it cuts the way she thinks it does, and what it would take to run it without begging the question. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, be honest if you conclude the position is well insulated, and say whether that insulation is a defect or a feature of a view of this kind. Write your answer in American English, and skip the reading list — I want the argument, not the citations.
