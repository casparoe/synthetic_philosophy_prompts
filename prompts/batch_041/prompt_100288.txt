A friend of mine from the local rationalist reading group — call him D — has spent the last few months teaching himself the logic of evidence, and he keeps handing down verdicts on whether this or that observation is evidence for this or that claim. I've been keeping a list, transcribed as literally as I can:

1. A raven flies past and it is black. D says this is evidence for "every raven is black."
2. I show him a white running shoe and say it is evidence for "everything that is not black is not a raven." D agrees.
3. I point out that the two claims in (1) and (2) are logically the same, so the shoe must also be evidence for "every raven is black." D agrees.
4. I hold up a black cat and ask whether it is evidence for "every raven is black." D says no.
5. A green apple: is it evidence for "everything that is not black is not a raven"? D says yes.
6. The same green apple: is it evidence for "every raven is black"? D says yes.
7. A red goldfish: is it evidence for "every raven is black"? D says yes.
8. The same red goldfish: is it evidence for "everything that is not black is not a raven"? D says yes.

Now the new case, and I want to work out D's answer before I actually put it to him. Does the black raven from item 1 count as evidence for the compound claim "every raven is black and there is at least one penguin in Antarctica"? I picked the second conjunct deliberately: it has nothing whatsoever to do with ravens.

What I'm after:

(a) What rule is D actually running? I suspect more than one quite different principle fits everything on the list, so give me the leading candidates rather than a single answer.
(b) For each candidate, what does it predict about the penguin case?
(c) Which prediction do you think D will actually give, and what tips the balance in favor of that reading of him?
(d) If the candidates come apart on the penguin case, name a single further case I could put to D that would cleanly separate them — ideally one where the two principles point in opposite directions. If they don't come apart here, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a distinction.
