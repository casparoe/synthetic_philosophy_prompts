I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is an entomologist studying a solitary wasp — the kind that stings a caterpillar, drags it down into a burrow, lays an egg on it, and seals the entrance behind her. Long stretches of the book are written close to the wasp, and I keep colliding with a question of diction: may my narrator say that the wasp chose the larger caterpillar, that it had a reason to prefer it, that it was wrong about the decoy? Last month two friends of mine — Nadia, an entomologist, and Owen, a lawyer who reads a great deal of philosophy — argued this through at my table, and I have not been able to settle it since. Here is their exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Nadia said that in her own papers she writes without embarrassment that the wasp stings the caterpillar in order to provision the nest, and that it prefers the larger caterpillar for a reason — the reason being that a larger caterpillar feeds the larva better. She does not think the wasp is thinking anything. Reasons, she said, do not require a thinker. There is a fact of the matter about what a wasp in that situation ought to do, and that fact does not wait for someone to grasp it.

(2) Owen said she was conflating two things: a fact about what conduces to an end, and a reason. A reason, he said, is a consideration that can guide the one who has it and can be flouted by him. When the wasp is fooled by a decoy caterpillar that is too small, we do not say it reasoned badly; we say its programming was tripped. If nothing is answerable to the norm, there is no norm, only a disposition — and calling that a reason is like calling a river lazy for taking the low ground.

(3) Nadia replied that 'answerable' was doing all the work, and circularly, since it smuggled in the ability to be blamed or to feel a pull, which she had already granted the wasp lacks. But the wasp can be wrong. It can fail to do what the situation called for, and fail because of something internal to its own machinery rather than by our outside verdict — a broken compass points wrong without knowing it. And the discovery she found most striking in her own field was that selection tracks reasons no organism ever represents: the wasp's rule of thumb about caterpillar size is a solution to a problem the wasp has no concept of. The reason is real; it is simply unattached to anyone's head. She called it a free-floating rationale and said she was happy with the phrase.

(4) Owen said that last phrase was exactly his worry. If the reason is 'there' but attached to nobody, what makes it a reason rather than a description of what happens to work? He offered a test: reasons come in a space of alternatives, they can be weighed against one another, outweighed, and revised in light of evidence, and none of that is true of the wasp's 'reason.' Selection does not weigh anything either; it kills. So 'free-floating rationale' is either a harmless synonym for 'function' or a disguised way of sneaking a designer back into the story. He added that Nadia would never say the river has a reason to take the low ground, so something else must be doing the work when she says it of the wasp — he suspected it was that the wasp resembles a little agent.

(5) Nadia conceded the word might be a stretch, but said the alternative was worse: strip out the talk and you cannot say what is wrong with a wasp that stings a pebble, or why its behavior is about caterpillars at all. And she thought his own example cut against him: a river cannot be wrong, and the wasp can, and that difference is not sentimentality — it is the difference between a system that has a problem and a system that merely has a trajectory. She would give up the word 'reason,' she said, if he would give up the word 'mere.'

(6) Owen said he would take that trade and then pressed it. If the 'problem' is fixed entirely by what the machinery was selected for, then every causal process has a problem, including the sun, whose problem is keeping the planets in orbit and which solves it rather well. The line has to be drawn at representation: something in the wasp must be about the caterpillar for the caterpillar to be a reason for it; otherwise we are describing causation in the optative mood.

(7) Nadia said that then he owed her an account of aboutness. If a state is about whatever it was selected to track, he has smuggled back the free-floating rationale he just rejected. If instead aboutness requires a mind, he must explain why a simple detector is not about what it detects — and the reason we are tempted to say it is, she said, is precisely that it can misfire, which is to say be wrong, which is to say have a reason.

(8) Owen answered that a misfire is a malfunction, and calling it 'wrong' only restates that the thing has a function; the temptation she describes is the whole problem, since it consists in inferring a reasoner from the mere fact that something can fail. Nadia said the inference might run the other way: we know what failure is before we know what minds are, and a mind is what you get when the failure can be registered from the inside.

Please work through this for me. I would like each of the eight moves rated on a scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, and then a verdict on the crux: is the difference between them a genuine disagreement about reasons, or largely a quarrel over a word, and if it is partly verbal, where exactly does the substance lie? I would also like to know what both of them are missing — the consideration neither has put on the table — and which of them, if either, ends up committed to something he or she would resist if it were spelled out. I am not asking who said what historically; names, citations, and reading lists will not help me, only the argument sorted out. Because I will have to write this scene, and because I would rather be corrected than reassured, please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, had one of them produced it, would move you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Finally, and secondarily: on your account, how far may my narrator go — may she write that the wasp wanted the larger caterpillar, or only that it tended to take it?
