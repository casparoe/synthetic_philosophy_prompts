I'm putting together a workshop thing on signaling and I need help writing the argument section, so put on your philosopher-of-biology hat for this one and take it seriously — I want an actual argument, not a survey of the literature.

Here's the setup. Everyone knows the handicap principle: a signal is trustworthy *because* it's costly, the cost is what keeps the cheaters out, so the waste is the whole point of the display. You see this trotted out for peacock tails, for why people buy sports cars, for why firms blow money on superbowl ads, for why a PhD takes seven years.

I want you to argue for a position I've come round to, which is that this popular reading is basicly backwards, and that the honesty of a signal has nothing to do with how expensive it is in absolute terms. The only thing that matters is how the cost varies with the quality of the sender: if a signal is cheaper at the margin for better senders, it can be perfectly honest while costing almost nothing, and if it costs the same for everyone then it can be ruinously wasteful and still tell the receiver nothing at all. On that reading "costly signaling" is a misnomer and the whole "the waste is what makes it credible" line is a confusion that's been passed down.

To turn that into an argument and not a slogan, do a few things. First brainstorm both sides — steelman the orthodox waste-is-essential reading as hard as you can before you go after it, because if it has a good comeback I want to know what it is. Then give me the argument properly, mechanism spelled out. In particular I want you to show that the two readings come apart empirically and not just verbally: the waste-is-essential reading predicts an open-ended arms race toward ever more extravagant signals, while the differential-cost reading predicts signals should settle down at roughly the cheapest level that still separates the types. If you can make that divergence do real work, great. Also deal with the obvious objection that in most real cases nobody can measure anyones cost function, so the differential-cost reading is unfalsifiable and therefore empty — tell me whether that objection kills the view or not, and don't just wave it away.

Then apply it to two cases, one biological and one social. For the biological one, take the pattern where the very top-quality individuals stop signaling altogether and only the middling ones bother — that should be awkward for the orthodox reading and I want you to say why, or why not. For the social one, take the claim that the academic job market's endless postdoc treadmill is a credible signal of commitment to research, i.e. the profession burns people's twenties on purpose because only the genuinely committed will put up with it. Argue about whether that survives your reading, and be honest if it doesn't.

Don't bother with citations, reading lists, or who said what when — I can't check any of it and neither can you, so argue from the structure of the thing. Aim for maybe 1200-1500 words, no bullet points, and don't hedge everything into mush at the end. Start with the brainstorming, then commit.
