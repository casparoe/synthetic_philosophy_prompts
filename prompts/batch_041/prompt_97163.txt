I am a novelist at the plotting stage of a historical novel whose entire architecture turns on a single small accident: a courier's horse throws a shoe on a muddy road, a dispatch arrives a day late, and a negotiation that would have ended a war instead collapses into one. I chose the device deliberately, because I want the book to be about how large outcomes hang on small contingencies. A friend who is a physicist, however, assures me that the matter is already settled — that chaos theory has demonstrated that small causes can have arbitrarily large effects, and that my thrown shoe is therefore not a novelist's conceit but a law of nature. I am not persuaded that the two claims are the same claim, and I would like you to compare them carefully.

On one side stands what I will call the historian's thesis of contingency — the old proverb in which a kingdom is lost for want of a horseshoe nail. Its content, as I understand it, is that some outcome was not forced by the great structural forces (economics, geography, demography, class) but turned on a particular event that might easily not have happened, so that the course history actually took was one of several that were genuinely open. On the other side stands chaos, or sensitive dependence on initial conditions, in the mathematics of dynamical systems: a fully deterministic system in which neighbouring initial states separate exponentially, so that arbitrarily small differences in starting conditions yield arbitrarily large differences later — as in the familiar demonstration in which a weather model run twice, with one value rounded at the sixth decimal, produces two entirely different long-range forecasts.

Compare the two. Are they one idea under two names, or different in kind? I have a suspicion, which I offer as a suspicion only, that they diverge at several points, and I want you to tell me whether I am right and where I am wrong: that chaos is deterministic and therefore leaves no room for the could-have-gone-otherwise that the historian's contingency seems to require; that chaos is at bottom a claim about our inability to predict a system whose future is nonetheless fixed, whereas contingency is a claim about the modal status of the outcome itself; that sensitive dependence presupposes machinery a one-off historical branching does not obviously possess — a bounded system, a state space, a distribution over initial conditions, recurrence — so that divergence within an attractor is not the same thing as a genuine fork in events; and that the popular version of the butterfly effect quietly substitutes indeterminism for sensitivity and thereby commits an equivocation my friend has inherited. Where do these distinctions hold, where do they collapse, and is there a sense in which the mathematics does lend the historian something after all?

Then tell me which conception, if either, does the work I actually want done. Does the historian's contingency require genuine indeterminism in the world, or only a counterfactual fragility that a deterministic process can supply? And since the novel's implicit claim is that this war need not have happened, tell me what the book may honestly assert about its own plot and what it must leave unsaid.
