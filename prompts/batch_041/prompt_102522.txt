I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course in epistemology, and I am currently grading the final examination. The unit on higher-order evidence concluded with a debate over whether it can be rational for an agent to hold a belief while judging that the belief is irrational. One examination item, reproduced below, was worth 20 points, and I would like you to grade the student's response to it.

Examination item:

Consider an agent, Dana, who comes to believe a proposition p on the basis of her first-order evidence. She then encounters higher-order evidence indicating that the reasoning by which she formed the belief was unreliable, and she finds it persuasive: on reflection she judges that her belief that p is irrational. She nevertheless continues to believe p.

(a) Is Dana epistemically akratic? Explain what epistemic akrasia consists in and apply your account to this case. (6 points)

(b) Some philosophers hold that Dana's continued belief can be rational even though she judges it to be irrational; this is often called the level-splitting view. Reconstruct the strongest version of that view, stating in particular what it must claim about the bearing of higher-order evidence on first-order belief. (8 points)

(c) State one serious objection to the level-splitting view and assess whether it succeeds. (6 points)

Student's answer:

(a) Epistemic akrasia is the condition of holding a belief while also believing that holding it is epistemically irrational. It is the theoretical counterpart of practical akrasia, in which one acts against one's own better judgment, except that it concerns belief rather than action. Dana is an instance of it: she believes p while judging that her belief that p is irrational. It is worth noting, however, that a belief held akratically is thereby a belief held against the total evidence, since the agent's own assessment of her reasoning is itself part of her evidence.

(b) The level-splitting view denies that rationality requires one's first-order and higher-order attitudes to line up. On this view an agent can be rational at the first level, with respect to the belief that p, while being irrational at the second level, with respect to her judgment about that belief. The crucial claim is that higher-order evidence and first-order evidence are assessed at different levels, and that the higher-order evidence need not defeat the first-order evidence. When the two conflict, then, the agent should trust the first-order evidence, because it is what actually bears on the truth of p; the higher-order evidence bears only on the reliability of the process by which the belief was formed, and reliability is not the same thing as truth. The strongest version of the view therefore says that what it is rational to believe is fixed by the first-order evidence alone, and that higher-order evidence is simply irrelevant to that question. So Dana may rationally continue to believe p while conceding that the belief is, at the higher level, irrational.

(c) A serious objection is that the view licenses epistemic self-sabotage. If an agent may believe p while judging that believing p is irrational, then she may equally believe p while judging that her evidence does not support it, which would make rational belief far too easy to come by. The level-splitting theorist can reply that the objection assumes the higher-order judgment is correct, whereas the view concerns only what is rational given the agent's situation. I think this reply is only partly successful, since it is hard to see how an agent could be rational in dismissing her own considered assessment of her reasoning.
