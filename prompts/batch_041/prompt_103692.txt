I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a historian who has staked her career on the claim that pre-industrial states undergo endogenous secular cycles — recurring oscillations of roughly two to three centuries in political integration, driven by elite competition and the immiseration of the common population — rather than merely drifting until an external shock arrives. I would like your own credence that this claim is true, given as a number and decomposed. Break it into the sub-questions you take it actually to rest on — whether the surviving proxies for elite numbers, real wages, and fiscal capacity are reliable enough to test the claim at all; whether the statistical machinery can distinguish a genuine endogenous oscillation from a slow trend punctuated by exogenous shocks; whether the proposed mechanism of elite overproduction is doing causal work or merely labelling a pattern after the fact; whether the pattern, if real, is a cycle rather than a single transition out of one regime into another; and whether it extends beyond the small set of cases from which it was built — assign each a rough probability, say how they combine, and name the sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive. Treat one further test as internal to the question, since it is the one my character's sharpest critics would apply: a cycle claim ought to survive having its period, its indicator, and its phase alignment fixed in advance rather than selected after the data are in, and ought to hold across polities that had no contact with one another. Where those who actually work on this agree, say so plainly; where they disagree, do not let a contested position pass as the consensus. Close with a single line in the form \answer{<number>}.
