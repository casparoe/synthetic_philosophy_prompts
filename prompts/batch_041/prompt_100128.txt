Two things happened this term that I cannot get out of my head. I took over the ethics portion of an introductory philosophy course, and around the same time a conversation at home about insect farming — whether it is worse or better than eating vertebrates, whether there is anything it is like to be a cricket — left me without a straight answer. What I keep circling is not the biology but the arithmetic: there are unimaginably many insects, and the chance that any one of them is sentient might be very small, and I have no settled view about how those two facts are supposed to trade off. I would like my students to run into that collision rather than hear me describe it.

So I am asking for a classroom activity. The setting is a ninety-minute session with about twenty undergraduates in an intro ethics course; they will have done no reading beforehand, and I do not want a lecture up front. I want a game or structured simulation in which they allocate something scarce — a fixed budget of points, no real money — across a set of causes that differ along two axes: how many individuals a cause affects, and how likely it is that those individuals have morally relevant experience. The insect-related causes should have enormous numbers and low probabilities; the vertebrate causes, small numbers and high probabilities. They should have to produce a ranked allocation and defend it. Then I want a second round in which the probability parameter is made revisable, so that they discover how little it has to move to overturn the whole ranking, and how much of their confidence rests on a single contested number. And I want one further element, which I think is the heart of it: a card, introduced late, whose reasoning is formally identical to the one that made the insects dominate but whose conclusion is absurd — a cause affecting some vast number of entities with a vanishing probability of mattering — so that they feel both the pull of the scale argument and the suspicion that the same machinery will fund anything.

The rules, materials, incentives, and roles should be arranged so the point shows up in what happens to them and not in what I say afterwards. Please give me the full runnable design: the rules, what is on the cards, how the budget and payoffs work, a timeline for the ninety minutes, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea — the difference between a low credence that a creature is sentient and a low degree of sentience in it, the question of whether individual insects or experiences are what get counted, and the option of refusing to multiply small probabilities by huge numbers at all. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: it cannot tell them whether insects are sentient, and it probably cannot reproduce the texture of the uncertainty itself, so I will have to supply the state of the evidence — and its thinness — myself.

One more thing. Tell me where your reasoning about this design is most likely to fail, and what would change your mind: which feature of the activity, or which consideration about how people actually behave under it, would lead you to conclude that it generates only the arithmetic and not the moral discomfort I am after.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write me a long letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no tables.
