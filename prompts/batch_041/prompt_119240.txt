I'm a science journalist — I mostly write about ideas and the machinery underneath them — and I'm building a piece around a decision-theory result that people usually call Good's theorem. The claim, roughly: if a Bayesian decision-maker gets information for free, her expected utility can't go down, because at worst she can ignore it and act exactly as she would have before she looked. It's the formal backbone of the slogan that more information never hurts.

I want the piece to argue something sharper, and I'd like you to build me the arguement: the theorem is true and almost empty, and every real case where knowing hurts has the same shape — the agent has no way to un-look. I've been calling it the look-away loophole: the proof's load-bearing move is that you're allowed to look away, and in almost every case anyone actually cares about, you're not. A doctor on a protocol that says the newest test result governs treatment can't look away. A firm whose board is legally bound to act on the safety data it commissions can't. Someone whose colleagues can see that she now knows can't — the information moves their behaviour whether or not she acts on it. And when what you believe is itself part of what you care about (the diagnosis, the news about your father), there's nothing to ignore, because the knowing is the harm. So the slogan turns out to be a claim about a trick.

Make me the strongest version of that. And make it do real work — I don't just want a list of cases where information is bad, I want to know what the no-opt-out condition actually is, wether it's one condition or three dressed up as one, and whether the unified story survives its hardest case. My guess is that the hard case is some strategic setting where the trouble is really that other people learn, not that you can't ignore. Deal head-on with the standard defence — sure, you might regret looking ex post, but the ex ante expectation is what the theorem is about — instead of hiding behind it. And tell me straight if the whole thing collapses into "well, information isn't free," because if it does I'd rather know now. One more thing: I'd find it much more convincing if this worked for an ordinary expected-utility maximiser rather than only for exotic preferences. If it only goes through for belief-dependent or ambiguity-averse types, say so, because that's a much smaller claim than the one I want to make.

Keep it concrete, no literature review, and skip the citations — I can't check them from here anyway.
