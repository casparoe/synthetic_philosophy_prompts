Read Huxley's Brave New World through the Grand Inquisitor's argument about freedom and happiness. The Inquisitor's case is usually treated as a single thesis — people cannot bear freedom, so they must be given mystery, miracle, and authority in its place — but Dostoevsky actually puts several distinct claims in his mouth: a descriptive claim about human nature, a normative claim that relief from freedom is a kindness, and the autobiographical claim that the elect must shoulder the freedom the masses cannot, and be unhappy on their behalf. Which of these does the novel vindicate? Mustapha Mond looks like the Inquisitor's elect made literal, administering engineered happiness and reading the forbidden books; the Savage looks like the returned figure who refuses the bargain; soma and conditioning look like the three temptations industrialized. Where does the analogy hold, where does Huxley trade on a simpler dichotomy than Dostoevsky's (the novel's freedom is mostly a freedom of suffering, not the terrifying metaphysical freedom the Inquisitor describes), and does the book have a position on whether the Savage's "right to be unhappy" is an argument or just a preference? I want a reading that takes both texts seriously and does not reduce the novel to an illustration. End with a single sentence stating your overall verdict.
