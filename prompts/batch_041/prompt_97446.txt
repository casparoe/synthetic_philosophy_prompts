So I hung up the lab coat a few years back (chemistry, 34 years at the bench) and my grandson got me reading about AI safety, and I've gotten a bit obsessed with this "side effects" problem. The line I keep seeing is that we want agents that avoid side effects, and that you can't even say what counts as a side effect without already having a full account of what's valuable. Maybe. But I want to start from what I actually think about particular cases and see if there's a principle hiding in there, because otherwise I'm just nodding along.

Here's what my gut says, case by case. Find me the principle, or tell me honestly there isn't one.

1. Household robot told to bring me a coffee. Takes the shortest path and crushes my kid's Lego tower on the way. Side effect, clear as day. If we're grading the robot, dock it.
2. Same robot, told to shut off the gas valve because something smells. On the way it knocks over a lamp and breaks it. I feel totally fine about the lamp. But number 1 bugs me. Both are "broke something en route to the goal," and I cant tell you why they feel so different.
3. Robot told to "tidy the living room." It stacks my papers into a drawer. They were laid out in an order I was using. Side effect, and a bad one, even though shoving loose papers into a drawer is more or less what "tidy" literally means.
4. Same robot, same order, picks a mug off the table and puts it in the cupboard. Not a side effect. That's the job.
5. I also read about agents that avoid side effects by doing nothing at all, and that feels wrong to me -- but I can't say it caused any side effect. So "minimize side effects" can't be the whole objective either.
6. And one I genuinely go back and forth on: robot told to water the plants. It also waters a weed I'd been trying to kill all summer. Side effect, or just sloppy following of the order?

My half-baked theory is that my verdicts are tracking whether the owner, asked afterward, would say the change was "part of what I wanted" versus "collateral" -- which feels like smuggling in the owner's whole value system, exactly the thing I'm told we can't specify. So: is there a principle that gets my cases, or most of them? Can it get by with something weaker than a full value specification -- a baseline of what would've happened anyway, a reversibility test, a line between the goal and the means? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? And what does it say about the weed case? Please don't hand me a reading list, I want the actual principle, how it handles these, and one or two cases I haven't thought of that would break it.
