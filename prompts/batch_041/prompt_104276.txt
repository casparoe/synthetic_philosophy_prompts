I'm building an adversarial-collaboration exercise for a grad seminar on moral aggregation, and I want you to draft the actual joint statement. The two camps I have in mind are, first, the people who think numbers count in themselves — that the bare fact that five people will die if you do nothing while only one will die if you act gives you a genuinely stronger moral reason to save the five, a reason that sits over and above the equal importance of each person taken one at a time — and second, the people who think that's a mistake: that the equal importance of each person is all the structure you get, that none of the five has a stronger claim than the one, and that whatever it's permissible to do in these rescue cases has to be accounted for some other way (fairness, a permission to favor someone you already owe or who's nearer to you, whatever it turns out to be). I've been argueing about this with a colleague for months and we keep talking past each other, so I want the document both sides could sign at the end of a serious good-faith collaboration — not a dialogue, not a scorecard of who's winning, but the constructive text they'd put their names to.

So: give me a numbered list of claims both sides accept — about what the cases even are, about what a reason-to-save is supposed to be, about what neither side is actually committed to, about which of their disagreements are downstream of some shared assumption they never noticed they share. Then a disagreements table, one row per live disagreement: the neutral statement of what's at issue, then the counting side's position in wording it would accept for itself, then the anti-counting side's position in wording it would accept for itself. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and for each crux a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out recorded in advance.

Two things I especially want you to chase down. One: whether a chunk of this fight is really about better versus required versus merely permitted, and so partly verbal — if so, say exactly which chunk and why. Two: whether the anti-counting side can give a non-question-begging account of why it's even permissible to save the five when it would be equally permissible to save the one; I've never seen that nailed and I suspect it can't be, but prove me wrong if I'm wrong. Also, dont just recycle the stock one-person-versus-five setup — if a variant case (sequential saves, a prior promise to the one, uncertainty about who's where) does more work for a given crux, use the variant and say why. Write it in American English, and keep the agreed-claims section honest — no clauses that quietly smuggle in one side's conclusion while looking neutral.
