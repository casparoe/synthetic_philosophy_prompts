I price flood and wildfire cover for a small specialty insurer. Maybe forty accounts a year, and the difference between a 2% and a 10% annual loss probability is the difference between a good year and a bad one, so these calls matter. Every time, the cat model spits out a number and then me and the underwriters argue about whether to believe the model or believe our gut.

A friend who did a philosophy degree told me there's a rule for exactly this: if you're certain the real, out-there chance of something is 30%, then your own confidence should be 30%, period — your hunches don't get a vote. Then he added two caveats and I still can't tell if they're deep or just technical. First, you're only allowed to defer like that if the rest of what you know bears on the outcome only through the chance; if you've got something like a tip about how it actually turned out, the rule doesn't apply. Second, if what you already know gives you reason to think the chance isn't 30% after all, you can't just defer either.

I want a procedure I can actually run on an account in about an hour, with no stats beyond what I've taught myself from textbooks. Steps in order, what counts as passing each one, where it stops and hands the call to a person, and where it's just my judgment dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three: a riverside warehouse where the model says 2% and my gut says 10%; a hillside subdivision where the model says 4% but I know that same model has undercalled every fire season for five years; and something brand new with no history at all. And tell me what the whole thing can't do. The chief underwriter has to sign off, so if a step needs her, say so.
