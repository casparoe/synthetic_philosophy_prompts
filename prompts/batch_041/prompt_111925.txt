I am trying to state, in a form I could actually apply, the principle behind my own verdicts about monuments, street names, and other commemorative fixtures, and I would like you to reconstruct it, test it, and repair it. The cases below are stipulated rather than historical; take the facts as given, and do not treat the task as one of verification. No citations are wanted.

My verdicts:

1. A city hospital is named for the merchant whose bequest built it, and a statue of him, paid for out of that bequest, stands at the gates. Late in life he published tracts urging the expulsion of the city's Jewish residents. Keep the statue; keep the name. The statue honours the gift, the gift was real, and the tracts are dead.

2. A bronze general was erected in 1912 by a committee that advertised its purpose as reminding the city's Black residents of their place. Remove the statue.

3. The street that also bears the general's name, named in the same campaign and by the same committee, should stay. A street name is a coordinate; a statue is an address.

4. A physician who ran a sterilization programme in the 1930s, and whose statue was erected by grateful patients who meant only to thank a healer, should come down. The intent of the donors is irrelevant to me here.

5. A street named for a poet whose work I admire and whose private cruelty to his family is documented should stay, though I notice I am less comfortable with this than with case 1 and cannot immediately say why.

6. A memorial to the dead of a war I consider unjust should stay. It honours the dead, not the cause.

7. Where a statue has come down, I would rather have a plaque recording the removal than a bare pedestal, and much rather either than a replacement statue of some uncontroversial local worthy.

8. A monument on private land, visible from a public road, of a figure I consider a monster: no removal, no public money, and no place on the city's heritage trail.

9. A street renamed in 1974 for a civil-rights leader who, it now emerges, embezzled from the organization he led: do not rename it again. The name names the place now, not the man.

The principle I would tentatively offer is this: a commemorative fixture should be removed when, and only when, its continued presence in public space asserts that the community now honours what the fixture honours. I also offer a distinction in its defence: such fixtures have both a recording function and an endorsing function, and removal is warranted only where the endorsing function is doing the real work.

What I want from you:

First, say whether that principle, with or without the distinction, actually delivers my verdicts, and where it fails. I am especially interested in whether the asymmetry between cases 2 and 3 is principled or a rationalization, and in whether cases 4 and 5 can be held together.

Second, state the best principle or small set of candidate principles you can, precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. If the only principle that saves every verdict I have given is one with clauses multiplying past the point of usefulness, say so, and tell me which verdict to abandon instead.

Third, give me your verdicts on at least two further cases you devise yourself — one involving a name rather than an image, and one in which the person commemorated is still alive — and say what each is meant to probe.

Fourth, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, rather than preserving all of them by epicycle.
