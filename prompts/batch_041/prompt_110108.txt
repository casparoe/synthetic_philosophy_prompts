I teach a graduate seminar on Dennett's three stances, and every year the same objection comes back at me, usually from colleagues in the computer science department and from students fresh from a machine learning course. It is always put as an analogy, and it runs roughly as follows: attributing beliefs, desires, or intentions to a language model is like attributing a desire to win to a chess engine. Nobody supposes that Deep Blue literally wants anything; we say that it "wants to win" because that is a compact way of describing the search it performs, and the compact description earns its keep by predicting moves. So, the argument continues, when someone asks what a model believes, or what it is trying to do, or whether it is pursuing an objective we did not intend, the question has the same status as a question about a chess engine's ambitions: it is a convenient façon de parler and nothing more, and there is no fact of the matter to be discovered. Since this comparison is now doing real work in an argument about whether alignment research can coherently ask what a system is optimising for, I would like a proper audit of it rather than another round of assertions.

Please begin with an explicit correspondence table. For each element on the chess-engine side — the rules and the winning condition given in advance, the designer who fixes the evaluation function, the search itself, the fact that the engine's competence is fully accounted for by search over a formally defined objective, and the availability of the design stance to anyone who reads the source code — tell me what is supposed to occupy that role on the language-model side, and mark the entries for which nothing does. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from those that are merely colourful: I want to know which differences establish only that these are different kinds of artefact, and which ones break the inference from "no fact of the matter about the engine's desires" to "no fact of the matter about the model's aims". Say also what the analogy quietly imports from the source — a specified objective, a transparent design stance, an external criterion of success — that the target may lack.

Finally, I want the verdict in two parts. First, what the stance framework itself can and cannot settle here: does the notion of a real pattern supply a principled criterion for when an intentional-stance attribution is tracking something that does not depend on the convenience of the attributor, and is that criterion one that a working alignment researcher could actually apply? Second, whether my colleague's conclusion survives in any form. If it must be weakened, give me the version I should be defending in the seminar; if the analogy ought to be replaced, tell me what would carry the same load better.
