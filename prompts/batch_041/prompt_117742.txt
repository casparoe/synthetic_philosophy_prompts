Bit of an odd request, this one. I teach a grad seminar on the Vienna Circle's less respectable ambitions, and every year we get stuck on the same spot: whether the picture language Neurath's team built for social statistics (the standardized pictogram stuff, the thing that later got called ISOTYPE) was ever meant as a real notation for science, or only as a nice way of presenting numbers to the public. I've given up pretending to settle it and now I just want the students to see how many positions there actually are. So take as stipulated, for the purposes of the exercise, the strong thesis: there can be a notation made entirely of standardized pictures, in which a scientific claim is read off the diagram rather than translated into English or German, and which could serve as a shared medium across the sciences. I know he hedged in places; I don't care, the strong version is the interesting one. Now build me a decision tree over the positions on it.

I want the tree built out of the questions rather than out of the names, so a student can walk it without knowing who held what. The distinctions I care about, roughly in the order they should bite: whether the notation is *read* or *decoded* (are the pictograms motivated enough that nobody has to be taught the conventions, or is it a code like any other?); whether it is a complete language or only a fragment (does it do negation, conditionals, generality, or only classification and comparison?); whether it is autonomous or parasitic on a natural language (does a pictogram need a verbal gloss to fix what it means?); and whether its point is exact science or mass enlightenment, since a notation can be perfectly good for the second and useless for the first. Use concrete cases as the diagnostic questions wherever you can — a subway map, a Feynman diagram, a pedigree chart, a population pyramid, sheet music, a knitting pattern, an emoji sequence — and say where the leaves disagree about them. Name the leaves where there's a name to use (Neurathian literalism, the auxiliary view, the code view, the expressive-limits view, the it's-an-instrument-not-a-language view, whatever else belongs on there), and mark a leaf unnamed where nobody holds that combination, with a sentence on why it's empty — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Where two leaves come out the same on every case I can actually think of, say so rather than inventing a difference between them.

Format it as: 1) 'The questions', the branch nodes in order, each a question with its answer options, a line or two each; 2) 'The tree', as an indented diagram; 3) 'The leaves', one line of name plus one sentence on each view's most awkward committment; 4) 'Empty branches', the combinations nobody occupies and your diagnosis of each; 5) 'What the cases show', where the concrete examples seperate the leaves, and which pairs of leaves the tree can't separate at all. Aim for six to eight branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves, and don't pad it with the history of the Vienna Circle — I want the logical space, not a literature review.
