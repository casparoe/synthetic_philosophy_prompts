You are a moral philosopher specializing in the ethics of memory and historical representation. Think carefully before you answer, and open with a brief brainstorm: what are the different things people might mean when they say that ranking atrocities is wrong?

Give me an example of a conceptual issue that comes into view once two grounds for condemning such ranking are pulled apart. The first is epistemic: there is no common measure, so a ranking is not merely offensive but false, or empty. The second is pragmatic: a ranking may be perfectly well grounded and still wrong to state, because of what stating it does — it licenses complacency, converts victims into entries in a ledger, or supplies a move in a denial game. Ordinary condemnations of comparison run the two together, yet they give opposite advice; and the first is embarrassed by the fact that the claim that an atrocity is incomparable is itself a comparative claim.

Construct one concrete case — invented, with named institutions and identifiable decision-makers, not drawn from the historical record — in which a body is required by its mandate to rank two mass harms, and the ranking it reaches is defensible on the evidence while publishing it is not. Then construct the mirror case, in which the ranking is indefensible and refusing to make it is what is wrong. For each, say what is being ranked and on what unit of comparison, who is harmed by the ranking being made or withheld, whether the objection is to the ordering or to the likening (these are different acts), and whether the standing to rank depends on who is doing it — a survivor, a historian, an official — and why.

Close by saying whether the two grounds can be held together at all, which of them does the real work in your cases, and what further case would make you drop the other. Argue from your cases; do not cite or list sources, and do not pad with well-known historical examples. Finish by naming one feature of the problem that your example cannot capture.
