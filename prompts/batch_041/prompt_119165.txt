I am the president of a small historical society that has just finished digitizing a cache of Newton's theological manuscripts, and next week I have to give the annual dinner toast. The board has been arguing for months about how to handle the fact that Newton was not an orthodox Christian: he rejected the Trinity, thought the Son was a created being, blamed Athanasius for the doctrine's entry into the church, and held that God is not just omniscient but literally present throughout space—space being the organ through which God perceives. About half the room will be historians of science and religion who know this; the other half will be local donors, clergy, and dignitaries who know only the falling-apple story and assume he was a conventional believer. Last year's speaker called Newton a deist and half the clergy left before dessert, so I want to get this right.

Please write the toast I should give. It should be about five minutes, and it should let one of those theological convictions do real structural work—shaping what I can and cannot say about Newton, about God, and about why the society's work matters—rather than just mentioning it in passing. I don't want to quote him directly or turn the toast into a lecture. It needs to land for both halves of the room: the scholars should hear that I am not sanitizing him, and the non-specialists should not feel that they are being scolded or preached at. Begin with the toast itself, with no preface or commentary.
