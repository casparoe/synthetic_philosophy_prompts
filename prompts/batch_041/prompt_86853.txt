I'm a third-year student and I'm currently in a course on nationalism and collective memory, which is where this is coming from (the course is half history, half philosophy, so I'm allowed to ask this kind of question). I want to apply for one of the summer research bursaries my department runs — ten weeks, small stipend, you submit a two-page proposal and end up with a roughly 4000-word paper. I have a half-formed idea and I need you to turn it into the actual proposal.

The idea: everyone lumps lost-cause stories together — the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo 1389, Dunkirk — as if being beaten and being in the right is one single phenomenon. I don't think it is. Some of these stories look consoling: they let the descendants of the losers keep living where they live and keep their self-respect, and the whole point is to make the defeat bearable, to close the wound. Others look like the opposite — they don't close anything, they charge interest. The defeat becomes a debt and a standing reason to try again, sometimes centuries later. Kosovo 1389 gets invoked to justify a war in the 1990s. Nobody thinks Dunkirk obliges Britain to invade France again, even though Dunkirk is arguably the strangest case of the four, since it's a retreat remembered as a triumph and doesn't fit either of my categories.

So my hunch is there are at least two different things going on, conflated because both are stories about losing. Two variables I can't separate: (a) whether the audience still holds the place in question — if you still have the land, maybe you need a story that makes defeat livable, and if you've lost it, maybe you need a story that makes return obligatory; (b) whether the enemy was an outsider or your own former countrymen — the Confederacy is a failed rebellion against a state you were part of, Kosovo is framed as an occupation. Or maybe the real variable is something else entirely and I'm pattern-matching. I genuinely can't tell which of these is load-bearing, and I suspect it's not the one people assume.

What I want from you is a proper proposal:

1. State the question so that it has a determinate answer. I keep sliding between what these myths *mean* to the people who hold them and what they *cause* politically, and I need to either pick one or say precisely how the two are connected.
2. Say why the obvious answers don't settle it — including why I can't just shrug and say it depends on the politics of each case. If the answer is going to be a taxonomy, I need to know what makes a taxonomy better than a list.
3. The method. Ten weeks, no archive access, mostly secondary sources plus my own framework. I'd rather write a conceptual paper that uses the cases as test cases than a mini-history of any one of them, and I want you to tell me honestly whether that's defensible for a bursary committee or whether it'll look like I'm avoiding the work.
4. What a result looks like: some criteria that would let someone sort a new case I haven't mentioned, plus what would actually falsify the two-kinds claim rather than just being absorbed by it.
5. What failure looks like, and whether ten weeks is realistic or whether I should shrink the question. Be blunt about that part.
6. A work plan — week-by-week is fine for ten weeks, don't overdo it.

Two constraints. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list; the bursary form has a separate section for that and I'll fill it in myself. Refer to positions and arguments rather than to author-date citations — if you'd normally cite someone for a view, just describe the view. And don't lean on obscure cases or theories I won't be able to check offline; use the four cases above plus at most one obvious fifth, and if you want to introduce a technical term, define it in the sentence where it appears.

Last thing: at the end, say how confident you are in the claims you're making and single out the one point you're least sure about. If the framework is resting on something shaky I'd much rather hear it now than in week eight.
