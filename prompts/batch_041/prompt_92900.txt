I want you to act as a decision theorist and help me draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in a long-running dispute about the common prior assumption. The setup: on one side, the claim that two Bayesians who share a prior simply cannot agree to disagree -- if they hold different probabilities for the same event and each knows the other's, something in the setup has to give (one of them is updating wrong, or the disagreement isn't really common knowlege). Call this Camp A. On the other side, Camp B: that this whole result is an artifact of an idealization, that a prior is just part of an agent's epistemic starting point, and that stable disagreement between reasonable agents who've seen all the same evidence is perfectly possible and doesn't show that anyone is broken.

Think it through carefully first, maybe brainstorm to yourself about where the two camps genuinely overlap and where they're just talking past each other, then write the document they could both put their names to:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps accept -- about what the "can't agree to disagree" result actually says, what it assumes, and what would count as a violation of its premises.
2. The precise residual disagreements, each written the way that side would want it written, in a table with a column per camp.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. I suspect one is whether the shared prior is meant as an empirical claim about actual agents or a normative constraint on ideal ones, and another is whether common knowledge of each other's beliefs is even achievable for bounded agents -- but you tell me.
4. For each crux, a concrete case or argument both camps would agree bears on it, plus what each camp predicts about it, recorded in advance.

Anchor the whole thing in the case that started the argument: two forecasting systems trained on the same corpus but with different random seeds and slightly different architectures keep outputting different probabilities for the same event, even after they've seen all the same evidence and after each has read the other's weights and its chain of reasoning. Camp A says at most one of them can be right and the persisting split shows something's off; Camp B says this is exactly the stable, rational disagreement the result never ruled out. Also flag any part of the dispute you think is merely verbal. Keep it focused.
