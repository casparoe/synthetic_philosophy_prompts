I have been following the debate over the singleton hypothesis in AI-safety circles for about two years, and it has stalled in a way I find unproductive. The camps, as I understand them: one side holds that once a decisive capability exists, competitive pressure selects for whoever coordinates fastest, so a single top-level decision-maker is the likely attractor, and the real questions are about its alignment and benevolence. The other side holds that concentration of that kind is unstable — coordination is expensive, defection pays, enforcement invites internal fracture — so the realistic equilibrium is a handful of actors locked together, and the singleton framing is a distraction. A third group says the word "singleton" is itself the problem, because a single agent, a single final authority, and a single world outcome are three different things and the debate slides between them. Nobody moves.

I build distributed systems for a living and have no formal philosophy background, so I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about how coordination dynamics actually resolve, a verbal one about what "singleton" means, a normative disagreement about whether concentrated power is bad in itself, or three groups answering different questions — feasibility, desirability, comparative risk — under one heading? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work, such as the stability properties of a monopoly on decisive force? My own hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than assumed: the dispute is mostly conceptual, and once the three senses are separated, what remains is an ordinary empirical bet about coordination — which is why it never resolves, since both sides argue a definitional point as if it were a prediction. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a historical case, or nothing at all, because the crux is a value commitment neither side can argue for?
