I've been argueing with a friend about Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result and I want a case that actually does some work, instead of the usual hand-waving. Recap of what I mean, so we don't talk past each other: two people start from the same prior over the same finite set of possibilities, each of them just conditionalizes that prior on whatever information they've got, and then they can't end up with different credences in some proposition while it's common knowledge between them what credences they each have. The proof is a two-line averaging argument and I get it. What I want from you is a case where the disagreement is real and stays put anyway.

Build me this. Two ordinary people -- name them, give them a setting, not philosophers and not idealized "agents" -- disagreeing about one factual question that has a definite answer. Keep it small enough to write down: five or six states of the world at most, a prior, and each person's information spelled out. Both of them update correctly, neither is lying or confused or irrational, and neither is ignorant of the other's opinion: each has told the other their credence, both heard it, and each knows the other heard it, so the disagreement is common knowledge in the everyday sense. Say out loud what mechanism did that.

Then do the check. Write the state space, the prior(s), each person's information, and both credences at the actual state. Say which of Aumann's assumptions your case breaks, and show in the tables that it really does break -- don't just assert it. If you've broken it by giving them seperate priors, fine, but then justify the difference: I don't want "they were just born with diffrent priors," I want the difference to come from something structural, like different reference classes or different evidence histories that can't be adjudicated from what they now share. And then the part I actually care about: if I insist that the priors be identical and common knowledge, does your case survive at all? If not, what would a case have to look like instead, and what does that tell us about where the disagreement has to be hiding?

Finish with the honest bit: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if you remove it (does the disagreement collapse, and how quickly), and how someone defending Aumann would try to wave your case away. Are they right?

The point of this is a reading group where people keep saying "well, they just have different priors" and nobody can say what that means. So a case that makes the hiding place visible is the whole game. And please don't stipulate the disagreement into existence -- if I can't see from the tables why they disagree, it doesn't count.
