Okay so quick context: I'm a novelist, four books, mostly quiet domestic stuff, but my last one was about this distribution warehouse outside Dayton where they'd automated the overnight crew with a fleet of little orange robots, and in the book one of them pins a picker against a conveyor and he dies. That book somehow got me invited to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on "Accountability for Autonomous Systems" that the engineering school and the philosophy department are co-hosting, and I have like twelve minutes and no slides. The room is genuinely split: half of them build these systems (robotics, ML, a couple people from industry) and half are philosophers, grad students and a few faculty, at least two of whom I'm told will be annoyed if I say "robot ethics" like it's a thing. My job is basically to set the table for the weekend. I don't want to just give the standard speech where I say "the responsibility gap" and everyone nods, because they've all heard that one and it kind of lets them off the hook.

What I actually want is to force the room to sit with a reframing I keep circling, and I want the speech built so the reframing does the work instead of being decoration. The reframing is this: the interesting question isn't "who is to blame when the robot kills someone" — I want the speech to walk through why every candidate dissolves (the guy who disabled the safety override, the vendor who pushed the software update, the engineer who tuned the reward function, the company that bought the cheapest version, me the novelist who needed a villain), so the audience keeps reaching for someone and keeps coming up empty — but rather "who is answerable for the fact that nobody is to blame?" I've started thinking of that missing somebody as a vacancy, a post that has to be filled by design, the way you'd create an officer or a trustee, rather than a person we've failed to find yet. I want the speech to land that idea in a way both halves of the room can actually use: the engineers should walk out feeling like the vacancy is something they could build into a system or a company, and the philosophers should walk out feeling like I've at least named the right problem and not cheated. There's alot riding on the tone. Please write the remarks, in American English. And don't let it get preachy at the end — I'd much rather it close on the empty chair than on a moral.
