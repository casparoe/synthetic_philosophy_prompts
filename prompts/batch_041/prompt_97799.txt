Hey — I'm putting together the final for my upper-level seminar on Confucian ethics. We spend the back half on the role-ethics reading, you know the one: persons are constituted by their roles rather than being individuals who first exist and then take roles on. Problem is every practice essay I get back is the same three paragraphs — roles constitute persons, this contrasts with Western individualism, here's a bit of the Analects. I need one essay question that a student cannot answer by reciting that.

Here's the angle I want it to force, and it has two prongs that I think pull against each other:

(1) The roleless person. If my self is made out of my roles, what happens to someone who has lost them all — a man whose family has died, who's been exiled, whose office and title are stripped, who has no one left to be a father or a friend or a subject to? Does role ethics have to say he's no one? Can he still be virtuous, or does virtue need a role to hang itself on?

(2) The bad role. Take a role a decent person should think is unjust — a role of subservience fixed at birth, say. If the self is constituted by its roles, where is the criticism supposed to come from? Can role ethics condemn the role without quietly smuggling in a role-independent standard it claims doesn't exist? I want them to feel that prong 1 pushes toward "you are nothing without your roles" and prong 2 pushes toward "you can stand outside a role and judge it" — and that you can't just have both for free.

To stop them reciting, hinge the question on a distinction I've been drilling all term: roles that constitute you versus roles you merely occupy — I've been calling them 'load-bearing' roles and 'decorative' roles. Make the question ask them to test that distinction against both prongs, and to say what a role ethicist has to do to keep both (a) the self just is its roles and (b) some roles should be refused, from collapsing into each other. If they want to argue the distinction is unstable, that's fine — I want that on the table.

Give me: the question itself, aimed at 90 minutes, so answerable but not easy; a model answer of roughly 600–700 words written the way a strong student would actually write it, not the way a lecturer would; and a marking scheme out of 20 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each has to make, and the specific moves that should cost marks. Then a short list of the ways students typically get this wrong — I want to be able to say 'you did the thing everyone does' and point at the line. Don't worry about references, it's closed book, so nothing should depend on them.
