At its 2006 General Assembly in Prague the International Astronomical Union adopted a definition on which, in our Solar System, a body counts as a planet only if it orbits the Sun, is massive enough to be in hydrostatic equilibrium (that is, near-round), and has cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit. On that basis Pluto, Eris and Ceres were reclassified as dwarf planets rather than planets, and an earlier proposal that would have made dwarf planets a subcategory of planets was voted down. I want this episode analysed through the semantic thesis of the School of Names, and specifically through Gongsun Long's defence, in the White Horse Dialogue, of the proposition that a white horse is not a horse: the argument that horse names shape while white names colour, so that the compound name does not designate what the simple name designates, together with the corroborating test that a seeker of a horse will accept a bay or a black one whereas a seeker of a white horse will not.

Treat the two as a single problem, and run the analysis in both directions. Do not give me a survey of ancient Chinese philosophy in general.

First, the theory onto the episode. Say who plays the advocate and who the objector in the IAU dispute, and what the analogue of the seeking test is. Does the resolution separate the simple name from the compound in the way Gongsun Long separates horse from white horse, or does it do the reverse, redefining the simple term so that the compound is excluded by stipulation? The definition builds in a requirement of dynamical dominance, so that a dwarf planet is fixed by roundness together with the failure to clear its neighbourhood: is that a separation of two respects, as the hard-and-white example separates hardness from whiteness in a stone, and does it matter that the compound here is formed by negating a criterion rather than by adding a further specification? The phrase is plainly not identical in sense to planet (a seeker of a planet will not accept Pluto; a seeker of a dwarf planet will not accept Jupiter); ask whether that alone establishes the School of Names' conclusion or merely restates the trivial fact that different names have different extensions.

Second, the episode onto the theory. The white-horse arguments are read both as a serious account of reference, on which each simple name tracks a single respect and no compound can fall under it, and as a verbal trick exploiting the ambiguity of the negative copula between non-identity and non-membership. Which reading does the IAU case support? The reclassification looks like non-membership secured by decision rather than non-identity discovered; say what that does to each reading.

Then commit. State plainly whether the Pluto affair vindicates, refutes, or is simply irrelevant to the thesis that a white horse is not a horse, and whether a dwarf planet is not a planet in Gongsun Long's sense or only by stipulation. Identify what the theory cannot reach: in particular, whether it can say anything about why one boundary rather than another ought to be drawn, as distinct from whether a given boundary makes a given predication true — the normative question that the 'cultural' proposal (on which a planet is whatever enough people call a planet) raises and that the School of Names, on your account, leaves untouched. Give reasons for each verdict; I want definite answers, not a menu.
