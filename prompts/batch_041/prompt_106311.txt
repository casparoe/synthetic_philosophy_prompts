I'm a doctoral candidate in moral theology, and I've kept bees on my balcony for two years now — which is a roundabout way of explaining how I ended up as the person asked to give the response at next month's workshop on animals and the Thomistic tradition. The paper I'm responding to is by a philosopher of mind, and it closes with an argument I haven't been able to shake: insects number in the quintillions, we have no idea whether they are conscious, and even a small probability that they suffer would make their aggregate suffering dwarf that of every vertebrate on earth. The author's parting shot was that the Thomistic tradition has nothing to say here, since it treats animals as creatures without reason whose only moral significance lies in what they reveal about us. I'd like to be able to say what Aquinas himself would have thought if that argument had been put to him — not a recitation of his remarks about brute animals, but a genuine prediction, derived from his own commitments, of how he would have answered the argument on its merits.

A colleague has been pressing me with a version of the argument that I've never seen in print and that I suspect is either very good or quietly confused. Since Aquinas holds that a thing is good in proportion to its degree of being, and that the universe is more perfect for containing a greater diversity of kinds, even a merely probable insect mind would be a further degree of being in the universe; multiply that by the astronomical numbers, and it would be the perfection of the universe itself, not utilitarian arithmetic, that demands we take insects with enormous moral seriousness. My own hunch, for what it's worth, is that he would regard "the probability that a mosquito is sentient" as a malformed question, since for him the powers of a creature follow determinately from its substantial form rather than being a matter of chance, and I can't tell whether that makes him immune to the scale argument or merely unable to engage with it. I also suspect that his view that the souls of brute animals perish with their bodies is doing more work in his thinking than anyone in the workshop will notice.

So: predict Aquinas's verdicts, reasoning from what he actually holds rather than from modern animal-ethics categories, and flag where his framework is genuinely silent or would have to be stretched. Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no citations or reading list — each one a sentence or two, each stating a single question together with his likely verdict and the reason for it. I want the five to be these: whether he would grant that insects can suffer at all, and what in his psychology settles that question, including whether their souls' mortality bears on it; whether the sheer number of insects could ever change the species of consideration owed to them, or whether for him the quantity of individuals is irrelevant to the kind of good at stake; whether his ordering of lower creatures to higher, together with his claim that the perfection of the universe requires a diversity of kinds, gives insects any standing that is not purely instrumental, and what he would say about the deliberate extermination of, say, mosquitoes; whether his treatment of cruelty to animals as wrong only through its effect on the human agent can survive the scale argument or would have to be abandoned; and finally what practical counsel he would give someone today who takes the argument seriously — about swatting, honey, insecticide, and the like — and how much of that counsel would rest on the uncertainty rather than on the numbers.
