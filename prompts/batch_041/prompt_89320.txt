I teach a graduate seminar in political theory on precommitment: the idea that an agent can bind its own future conduct by arranging matters now so that the later self cannot do what it will then want to do. I open the first meeting with a joke, and I have worn out the obvious ones. Please write me a new one.

The humor has to come from the structure of the problem rather than from the Sirens as a piece of mythology. A listener who has never thought about precommitment should find the joke merely puzzling; a student who has done the reading should laugh. The punchline should turn on one of the following, or on something in the same family, and should turn on it precisely rather than gesture at it.

- The enforcement asymmetry. The rope does nothing by itself. What actually holds is the crew, who are neither bound nor persuaded but merely instructed, and who are unable to hear the offer they are refusing. Self-binding therefore always arrives with a second party whose hands are tied on the agent's behalf, which is why the constitutional version of the idea looks less like a contract with oneself than like an ordinary transfer of authority.
- The reversibility dilemma. If the later self can undo the arrangement, nothing was bound; if it cannot, the earlier self has ruled the later one, and we must say either that there are two persons here or that one of them is the other's subject.
- The will and the payoffs. The device does not make the agent want less; it changes what wanting can accomplish. A joke in which someone is said to have conquered his desires has missed the point and should be discarded.

House rules: a real setup and a real punchline, no longer than a paragraph; dry is better than zany; it has to survive being read aloud; invent no quotations and hang the joke on no named theorist. Do not let the punchline be the mere naming of the device. After the joke, on a separate line, name in one sentence which of the features above the punchline turns on, so that I can check it against the lecture.
