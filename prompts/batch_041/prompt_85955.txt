I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and for the past year I have been building a product that helps people keep commitments they make to themselves: hard locks on distracting applications, savings rules that move money out of reach before it can be spent, scheduled reminders, and an optional feature that lets a user stake a small deposit against a goal. That last feature has forced me to take a position on a question I am not equipped to answer. My working model has been the behavioral-economics one: the trouble is time-inconsistent preference, a discount curve steeper in the near term than the far, so that the person deciding on Sunday and the person deciding on Friday genuinely disagree; and the useful subdivision is between users who do not anticipate the reversal, and can be helped by being shown their own future regret, and users who do anticipate it, and are effectively asking for a device that removes the choice. A colleague argues that this framing is a mistake, and that the better model is a bargaining problem among temporally indexed selves with no single ranking that counts as the agent's own, while a second insists the phenomenon is mostly cue-driven habit, so the right intervention is to redesign the situation rather than to bind the will.

I have eighteen months and a small team, and I can build two or three mechanisms well rather than ten badly. So what I want is an agenda, not a survey: which open questions in the philosophical literature on weakness of will, and in the neighboring literature on intertemporal choice, most deserve attention, in what order, and why? I care about the ordering more than the list. Does the classical notion of acting against one's own better judgment survive the discounting picture, or does that picture replace a failure of will with a conflict of preferences, and does the answer change anything a user would notice? Which questions are upstream of which, such that one has to be settled before the others even have a determinate answer? Which are tractable for someone who can run A/B tests on a few thousand users but cannot run a lab study, and which are conceptual enough that no quantity of my data would settle them? And which of the questions that get the most attention in popular treatments would, if answered, leave my product unchanged? Please say what argument or evidence over the next year would move a question up or down your ranking.
