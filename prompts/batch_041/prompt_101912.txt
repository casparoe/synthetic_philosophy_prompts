I sit on a working group that has spent the better part of two years arguing in circles about one question, and I would like a diagnosis rather than another round. The group mixes three formal social-choice researchers, two democratic theorists, and the staff who actually run our participatory budgeting process, and the argument is always the same.

Here is the concrete case. The assembly votes on a slate of propositions that are logically connected: whether the budget should fund a new transit line (P); whether it should fund the feeder-bus network that is pointless unless the transit line is built (Q); and whether the budget should refrain from funding both the transit line and the feeder buses, since together they exceed our spending cap (R). P and Q together entail the negation of R. When we put each proposition to a separate majority vote, the assembly can pass P, pass Q, and pass R — an inconsistent set. It has done exactly this, twice.

Camp A, the formal side, says this shows that majority rule is defective whenever the propositions are interconnected: the assembly has failed to produce a coherent collective judgment, and any procedure that can issue an inconsistent set is not a procedure for forming judgments at all. Camp B, the democratic theorists, say that is a category mistake: the assembly is not an agent with a mind, a vote is a way of settling a conflict rather than a report of a belief, and demanding that the output be consistent merely reifies the group into a person. Neither side has shifted. Camp A treats each new instance as confirming evidence; Camp B treats each new instance as a symptom of Camp A's confusion.

Three proposals keep coming up, and each one reproduces the same clash.

First, premise-based voting: put only P and Q to a vote and derive R. Camp A regards this as the fix, because consistency is guaranteed by construction. Camp B objects that the line between premise and conclusion is drawn by whoever writes the ballot, and that we have smuggled an undefended epistemic privilege into a democratic procedure.

Second, package voting: put P-and-Q as a single item so that the inconsistency cannot arise. Camp A calls this a dissolution of the problem. Camp B says it just transfers power to the agenda-setter, who now decides which propositions are fused and which are left separable.

Third, graded aggregation: have each member report a degree of support or a probability for each proposition and average them, so that no binary inconsistency ever appears. Camp A objects that the result is not a decision and cannot be implemented; Camp B objects that it is not a vote and cannot be legitimated.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not really about aggregation rules at all: it is about whether a collectivity can be a rational agent, and the formal results are being used as ammunition for a metaphysical thesis they do not actually establish. I may be wrong.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement, engaging the positions as I have described them:

- Is this a verbal dispute over the phrase collective judgment, a substantive normative dispute about what a democratic decision ought to be, two groups answering different questions under one heading (one asking which aggregation rules satisfy a set of axioms, the other asking what legitimates an outcome), a hidden formal question that is actually settled and whose consequences the parties misdraw, or a disagreement about which cases count as data — that is, whether our inconsistent outputs are counterexamples to majoritarianism or merely puzzles for the procedure?
- For each side, identify the suppressed premise doing the real work — a definitional choice, an epistemic assumption about who may draw the premise/conclusion line, or a normative premise about legitimacy — and say whether it is argued for or merely assumed.
- Say whether the formal result underdetermines the normative conclusion Camp A draws from it, and whether Camp B's reply is a genuine alternative or a refusal to answer the question.
- Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or a decision about which question we are asking. If nothing would move it, say so and explain why.

Write it self-contained. Assume you cannot look anything up, and do not supply citations, a reading list, or attributions — I want the diagnosis in your own terms, roughly 700 to 900 words, and I would rather have one sharp diagnosis than a survey of positions.

End your reply with a final line in exactly this form: Verdict: <label> — <one sentence>, where <label> is one of verbal, substantive-normative, different-questions, hidden-formal, case-selection, or ill-posed, followed by a second label only if a single one genuinely will not do, and the sentence states the diagnosis in plain terms.
