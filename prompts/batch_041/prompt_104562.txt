I am a high school student working on an independent project about how institutions assign credit for discoveries, and I have reached a point where I can no longer tell what the institutions themselves assume. I would like you to reconstruct the theory of discovery that these practices presuppose, say whether it is coherent, and say what would have to change if it were replaced.

The first institution is patent law, and three of its requirements matter here. First, an application must name the actual inventor or inventors; a patent is not granted to a laboratory, a company, or a committee as the author of the idea, and getting inventorship wrong can invalidate the patent later. Second, the claimed invention must be new — no one may have disclosed it already — and, as a separate hurdle, it must not be obvious, where obviousness is assessed from the standpoint of a person of ordinary skill in the field at the time the invention was made. Third, the law refuses to grant patents on laws of nature, natural phenomena, and abstract ideas as such, allowing claims only on particular applications of them. As for priority between competing applicants, it is now settled by who filed first: the United States abandoned its old first-to-invent rule only in 2013, so until very recently the law treated being the earliest inventor as the fact that mattered, and now treats being the earliest to file as the fact that matters.

The second institution is the Nobel Prize, which cannot be shared by more than three people and is not awarded posthumously.

What confuses me is how these rules sit with independent simultaneous discovery. Newton and Leibniz developed the calculus separately; Darwin and Wallace reached natural selection separately and arranged to present their results together rather than fight over them; something similar is recorded for oxygen, for sunspots, and for the telephone. If the next step in a field is routinely taken by several people within months of one another, then at a given moment that step seems to have been available to anyone with the relevant training — which is close to what the obviousness test says an invention must not be. Yet the same body of law insists that there is a determinate inventor, that this person can be identified after the fact, and that priority between near-simultaneous claimants can be adjudicated. The Nobel rules push the same way: a discovery that four people reached at once must still be compressed into at most three names.

I would like answers to the following. (1) What view of discovery does this combination of rules presuppose — that discoveries are the achievements of particular individuals, that they are near-inevitable products of the state of a field, or some third thing? (2) Which rival views of discovery does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it leave open? In particular, does the requirement that the inventor be named commit the law to the view that discoveries are attributable to individuals, or could that rule be defended on other grounds? (3) Is the practice consistent with the phenomenon of simultaneous discovery, or does it require a picture of discovery that the phenomenon undermines? (4) The 2013 change in the priority rule: does first-to-file presuppose something different from first-to-invent about what priority tracks? (5) The patent system distinguishes discoveries, which cannot be owned, from inventions, which can. Does that distinction presuppose that discoveries are found rather than made, and does simultaneity threaten it? (6) If the inevitability thesis were taken seriously, what exactly would have to change — the patent system, the prize rules, the habit of attaching a single name to a law or a constant — and what could remain as it is?

I am attached to keeping the practice, so I would like to know what it costs to defend it, and where the practice is underdetermined, in the sense that several different theories of discovery would license everything I have described.
