I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and I am putting together an application for a small internal grant that funds a six-month pilot project. I have a half-formed idea about Bertrand’s paradox and the principle of indifference that I would like you to turn into a two-page research proposal. The idea is this: the usual lesson drawn from the chord paradox is that indifference is not well-defined, because different parameterizations of the same problem yield different distributions. I suspect that diagnosis is too quick. What we call indifference may be doing two different jobs: indifference over the outcomes of an experiment, and indifference over the symmetries of the problem’s description. On this view, Bertrand’s paradox is not a counterexample to indifference as such but a symptom of conflating a symmetry of the description with a symmetry of the answer. I want the proposal to make that distinction precise enough to be testable: can we state a criterion that tells us when a problem’s symmetries are sufficient to determine a unique credence, and when they are not? The proposal should state the central question so that it has a determinate answer, explain why the usual positions—objective chance, subjective credence, maximum entropy, and imprecise probabilities—do not already settle it, and lay out a method. I imagine the method as a mix of conceptual analysis and small formal case studies: take a family of Bertrand-style problems, vary the parameterization and the group of transformations under which the problem is invariant, and see whether the proposed criterion tracks our pre-theoretic verdicts about which problems are well-posed. I would also like a section on what would count as failure: if the distinction collapses under every formalization, or if the criterion only works by gerrymandering the cases, I want the proposal to say so and to say what that would show.

I first got interested in this while TAing an introductory probability course, where students kept asking which answer to the chord problem was correct and I realized I did not have a principled reply. The proposal should be written for a reviewer who knows the paradox but not my angle, so make the stakes clear early and do not assume the distinction is already familiar. Give me a realistic scope for six months, with a month-by-month sketch and an honest note on which part is most likely to become a dissertation-sized project. I do not need a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; refer to positions and arguments as needed and I will fill in the references myself. What I want is a proposal that argues the project is both open and tractable, and that makes clear what would count as a result rather than a restatement of the paradox.
