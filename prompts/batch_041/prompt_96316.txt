At a friend's birthday thing last weekend two people I know got into a long argument and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. One of them, Dev, had read a book claiming there are four kinds of minds, and he explained it to the table like this. Some animals are born with all their cleverness already baked in — the learning happened to their species over generations, not to them; the book calls those the Darwinian ones. Some can also learn from their own trial and error while they're alive — Skinnerian. Some can try things out inside their head first, so the bad ideas die instead of them — Popperian. And then there's us and maybe a few others, who can also use tools and words, so we're thinking with stuff other people made and handed down — Gregorian, after some psychologist.

Dev's claim was that the last step is basically a cheat. Line them up, he said, and you only get two real jumps: learning out in the world, where reality does the correcting (Darwinian and Skinnerian), versus learning inside your own head (Popperian and Gregorian). Gregorian is just Popperian with a bigger library — same trick, more books.

Rosa said no, the words-and-tools one is the actual big one, because a tool doesn't just let you have more of the same thoughts, it lets you have thoughts that weren't available to any single head. She said you could give one person ten thousand years and they'd never come up with long multiplication by trial and error; you need the notation first, and the notation is a tool somebody else built. The tool isn't doing the same thing faster, it's doing something you couldn't do at all.

Dev came back that this is still just offloading — a Popperian with a scratchpad is still a Popperian, the pad is just where the trying-out happens. If using a tool makes you a new kind of mind, then a crow with a stick is a new kind of mind too.

Rosa said fine, then tell me how you'd ever know a creature was really doing the Popperian thing and not just very fast Skinnerian trial and error. From the outside, a rat that freezes at a choice point looks the same as a chess player thinking. Dev said exactly, you can't tell, which is why he thinks the whole four-way split is our description and not the animal's. Rosa said you can tell, because in the real simulating case the trial is decoupled — nothing is at stake while you try it out — and the rat that freezes is still betting its body.

Then Dev said the decoupling test proves too much, since a mouse that freezes isn't risking anything either, and a computer searching through moves isn't risking anything, and Rosa said the computer only has a search space to begin with because somebody wrote the notation down, which was her whole point. They went around like that until the cake came out.

Rosa also threw in, near the end, that the real proof the Gregorian one is a different kind is that a Gregorian creature can be systematically wrong in a way a Popperian can't — you can inherit a broken tool and it bends your thinking, and reality never gets a chance to correct it because the tool is sitting between you and reality. Dev said a rat can be conditioned to press a lever for a shock, so getting bent away from what's good for you isn't new either.

Can you go through this and tell me who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? And especially: is Rosa's last point about being systematically wrong a real difference between the two kinds, or is it just her restating that tools matter? Also say what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the four-way split is even supposed to be doing. I've got no background in this, I just want to know where each of them goes wrong.
