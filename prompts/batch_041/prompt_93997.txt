I chair a monthly methodology seminar that mixes historians with computational social scientists, and after last week's session on the secular-cycles program — the demographic-structural attempt, associated with Peter Turchin, to model long-run historical dynamics through recurring cycles of elite overproduction, popular immiseration, and state fiscal stress — two members got into an argument I was not able to referee from the chair and, honestly, would not have been able to referee from anywhere. What I want from you is a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually happened, not a general essay on whether history can be a science.

Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Theo, a computational social scientist: The program demonstrates that history can be quantified and, at the level of structure, predicted. A model fit to centuries of data flagged well in advance that American political instability would climb toward a peak in the 2020s. That is a forecast made before the fact, and it was not a vague prophecy: it named a mechanism — elite overproduction, immiseration, fiscal strain — and a rough timing. A research program that projects a turning point decades ahead is behaving like a science, not like hindsight.

(2) Mara, a historian: It is not a hit, because as stated it was not falsifiable. "Instability," "elite," and "immiseration" are theory-laden categories rather than independently measurable quantities, and the timing was given as a decade-wide window. Worse, the same data used to estimate the cycle periods were used to generate the forecast, so the "prediction" is in-sample. And if the cycle length is itself a fitted parameter, a cycle is guaranteed to appear — over a long enough series you can always locate a rhythm of roughly a century and a half.

(3) Theo: That sets a standard no macro-science can meet. Climatology cannot tell you the weather on a given day in 2090, and nobody calls it unscientific. The correct test is comparative and transferential: does the model beat its rivals on the same data, and does a mechanism fitted to one society hold up when tested against others it was not built on? The program has been extended across dozens of societies, and the same sequence keeps recurring.

(4) Mara: Transfer is exactly where it breaks. Historical societies are not exchangeable draws from a distribution. Later societies inherit the institutions, ideas, and even the memory of the earlier ones, so testing on a "held-out" case does not give you an independent trial — information leaks across the fold. She compared it to the old worry in cross-cultural anthropology that societies sharing a common origin are not independent data points. And she pressed a deeper disanalogy with climate: the climate's governing equations are time-invariant, whereas in history the actors' own theories of the cycle become causes. People who read about elite overproduction can act on it.

(5) Theo: Falsifiability is a red herring here. The real test is whether a model survives serious attempts to break it, and this one has been carried well beyond the United States. If the mechanism were empty, we would not see the same sequence recur across unconnected cases.

(6) Mara: Then produce the misses. Name a case where the model predicted calm and got upheaval, and where the program abandoned a specification because of it. My complaint is that the literature remembers the hits and never fixes the reference class of predictions in advance, so no hit rate can be computed at all.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it on a scale running from clearly mistaken, through partly right, to clearly right, and justify the rating in a few sentences, isolating the specific methodological or philosophical point at stake in that move rather than summarizing who sounded more reasonable. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing — the consideration neither raised that would change the shape of the dispute. I am interested in whether the disagreement is really about falsifiability, about the reference class for predictions, about the independence of historical data, or about reflexivity, and where those issues come apart.

Throughout, state how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point in your adjudication you are least sure of.
