I work in reinsurance and I keep running into the same line in our underwriting guidelines, and I dont think the people who wrote it know what it commits them to. We'll write earthquake cover on a fault that's been quiet for four hundred years, priced off a catalogue model, and nobody blinks. Pandemic, war, most cyber: excluded, every time, at every company I've talked to, even when our own numbers say the expected loss is smaller than the quake's. The guideline never says the risk is too likely; the wording is that we can't get comfortable with the distribution.

What view of probability does that presuppose? I remember some psychology study where people pay good money to avoid a bet on an urn whose mix they don't know, even though they know the other urn is 50/50 and can work out that the unknown one is too. Is our exclusion doing that, or is it something more boring -- correlated losses, no diversification, a regulator who won't give us capital credit for it? Is there a real difference between not knowing the odds over outcomes you can list and not knowing what the outcomes even are, and is that the line we've actually drawn, or have we drawn it somewhere else and told ourselves that story? And if a decision theorist told me that refusing to price these is just a bias we should get over, what would have to change -- contracts, capital rules, who ends up holding the risk -- to fix it? Spell out the view we're committed to, what it quietly rules out, what it only happens to fit, and where its really just underdetermined.
