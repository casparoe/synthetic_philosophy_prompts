I am a graduate student working on mechanistic anomaly detection: the project of flagging the moments when a model's output is produced by a different internal process than the one we have already characterized, even though the output itself looks unremarkable and passes our behavioral test suite. The instrumentation is not what worries me — probes, causal interventions, and attribution methods are all on hand. What worries me is the prior question that none of the instrumentation answers: when we say the model is now using a different mechanism, or the same one, what precisely are we claiming, and what would make that claim true? In lab discussions I have noticed people sliding between at least four readings without noticing it: the same computation, the same causal role within the larger system, the same input-output function, and the same physical or algorithmic implementation. The disagreement never surfaces because everyone treats the notion of a mechanism as obvious. I would like you, taking the role of a philosopher of science who works on mechanisms and causal explanation, to slow down and think this through with me, reasoning from first principles rather than from any single school.

Please begin with a brief brainstorm of the candidate readings of mechanism identity, then write the desiderata: the requirements that any account of when two mechanisms are the same has to meet in order to be usable in my setting. I am asking for a specification, not a verdict. Before I commit to any particular account, I want the full list. Fix the purpose yourself if that helps, but keep it operational: an account good enough that we could in principle decide, from evidence we can actually collect, whether a flagged run is the same mechanism or a different one. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp. Some cases I keep running into: a network retrained from scratch that implements what looks like the same algorithm in a different set of weights; two circuits that compute the same function through different intermediate representations; a mechanism that has drifted slightly but still does the job; and the same process described at the level of neurons, of features, and of the overall computation. I also want the distinctions the account must not collapse — mechanism versus function versus behavior, type versus token, and the level or grain of description — along with any conceptual or empirical results it cannot contradict, such as the way behavior underdetermines mechanism.

The part I most want, and the reason I am asking rather than guessing, is the negative result: if some pair or subset of these requirements cannot be satisfied together — if the operational demand and, say, the demand to respect levels of description pull in opposite directions — say so plainly, since that would tell me the notion cannot do all the work we are asking of it. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; a brief gesture at how existing pictures (mechanism as causal structure, as computation, as functional role) fare against the list is welcome at the end, but I do not need a full scorecard. Finally, be candid about your own confidence: tell me which requirement you are least sure belongs on the list, and what argument or consideration would lead you to drop it or to revise your verdict.
