I'm putting together a unit on Marx for a first-year course and I want to build the whole alientation lecture around Fritz Lang's Metropolis — the 1927 one, silent, robot, city on two levels. I've got a thesis about it and I want you to either defend it properly or knock it down. Don't split the difference, and don't hand me a survey of what the critics have said.

The thesis: Metropolis is a film about alienation with the exploitation surgically removed. The workers live underground, work in shifts at the machines, never see daylight, and the one we get close to collapses at his station until Freder climbs in and takes his place. The imagery is unforgettable — the machine as Moloch eating men alive, the shift change like a cattle drive, the human being as a living part of a mechanism, which is Marx's own picture of factory labour in the machinery chapters of Capital. But there's no wage in this film, no market, no commodity, no surplus — one firm, one boss, and a city that apparently produces nothing anybody sells. So you get the whole lived texture of capitalist labour without a single one of the economic relations Marx says generates it. Is that a fatal gap, or does it actually help — alienation at its purest, labour with no object at all, which is the limit case the 1844 Manuscripts are already gesturing at?

Then the bits I want you to actually work through:

- Marx's four faces of alienated labour in the Manuscripts: from the product, from the activity itself, from species-being, from other people. Which ones does the film dramatize, which does it merely assert, and which does it contradict? The workers do have Maria, and they have each other, and there's a real community down there — that seems to complicate the fourth.
- The division of labour. The film's final intertitle says "The Mediator Between the Head and the Hands Must Be the Heart". But for Marx the split between mental and manual labour is itself something to be abolished, not reconciled by a nice young man with good intentions. So is that ending a Marxian solution or the exact opposite of one? Pick one.
- Exploitation specifically: is there any in the film in Marx's technical sense, and can there even be, given the absence of wages and commodities? And what do you make of the remedy being a feeling — a handshake, a change of heart in the boss? Marx would say that's precisely the move he spends his life arguing against. Does the film know that, and is it dramatizing the failure of reformism, or does it sincerely believe the handshake works?
- The robot Maria. A made thing wearing a person's face and doing a person's job — that strikes me as commodity fetishism running in reverse, relations between things dressed up as relations between persons. She's also a machine for manufacturing a false picture of the world in the workers' heads. I don't think the phrase false consciousness is Marx's — I'm fairly sure it's Engels, in a letter late in life — so correct me if I've got that wrong and use whatever the right term actually is.
- The machine-smashing. They wreck the machines and it floods their own homes and nearly drowns their children. Marx's point about the Luddites is roughly that they attacked the instruments instead of the social relation. Does the film confirm that analysis, or does it moralize the whole thing into a story about a mob led astray by a wicked woman, which is a different and much worse claim?
- Freder. A rich kid who crosses over by working one shift and then declaring his love. Is there room in Marx's theory for the declassed bourgeois at all, and does the film get anything right about why that figure is unreliable?
- The Babel sermon in the catacombs, and the religious apparatus generally — the cathedral, the Moloch vision, the nightclub scene with the robot. Marx's own rhetoric in the Manifesto is apocalyptic and prophetic, so I don't think the theology is automatically un-Marxist. Tell me whether it deepens the film or muddies it.

Finally, the question I actually care about: does the film add anything to the theory, or is it just the sentimental version of it? My instinct is that Marx never gives you alienation from the inside — what it is like to be the cog — and Lang does, and that's a genuine contribution, a phenomenology the theory lacks. But I can also see the case that the film's power is exactly what makes it bad Marxism: it makes you feel the horror and then discharges the feeling with a heartwarming ending. Give me a verdict either way, with reasons, and be willing to tell me my thesis is wrong.
