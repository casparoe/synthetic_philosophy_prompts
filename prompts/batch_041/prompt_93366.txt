Alright — I'm a freelance hacks, usually doing science and ideas stuff, and I've been asked to write a long-read centrepiece on housing for a weekend mag. The editor wants it as a proper two-hander rather than the usual "angry man at council meeting" bit, so what I need from you is the dialogue itself.

Set it in a made-up English town — call it Marlow Vale, somewhere with a Victorian terrace core, a dead retail park on the edge, and a council that's skint. Two people, both sharp, both decent, neither a cartoon:

- Nadia, who runs a tenants' union and holds the line that there's a right to the city — that the people who live somewhere have a claim on what it becomes, not merely a right to be housed somewhere, anywhere.
- Gerald (pick a better name if you like), a former planning officer turned consultant, who thinks "the right to the city" is a lovely phrase with no enforceable content, and that the actual fix is to make objecting expensive and building cheap.

What I want them genuinely arguing about: there's a proposal on the table to turn the retail park into 400 homes, 30% "affordable". And I want them fighting over a distinction I've been chewing on, between a right to *use* a place and a right to *shape* it — and over what I'm calling the proximity principle: roughly, that your moral claim on a neighbourhood scales with how much you'd lose if it changed, and shrinks the more recently you turned up. Is that a real principle or is it just first-come-first-served dressed up? Gerald should press that if it's a genuine principle it has ugly implications — the newest arrivals, often the very people who most need more housing, get the weakest claim — and Nadia should have a real answer, not a shrug. Nadia should press that Gerald's "just build" line quietly assumes the thing in dispute, namely whose preferences get counted and whose get priced out.

Rules: both characters equally clever, they answer each other's actual points rather than talk past each other, and they get properly into the weeds — planning gain, viability assessments, why "affordable" often isn't, use-value versus exchange-value, whether a collective right is just a bundle of individual ones, what "community" even refers to when half the street turns over every three years, and whether a claim you can't enforce against anyone definately counts as a right at all. No citations, no reading lists, no hedging. Let them land blows, and let at least one of them move a bit by the end, or explain properly why they won't. Length: as long as it needs to be to be good, but keep it tight — teh reader will bail if there's filler.
