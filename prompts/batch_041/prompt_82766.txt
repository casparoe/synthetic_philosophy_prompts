hey — I'm in a third-year philosophy of mind course and we've just spent two weeks on Dennett's competence-without-comprehension stuff, the Darwin inversion and the Turing inversion. Our department has a visiting speaker coming next month who works on the evolution of cognition, and I've somehow ended up running the interview for the student podcast. She's been developing a line I want to actually press on, and I need 8-10 questions that do that. Not the usual opener stuff (no "so what is competence without comprehension?"), and nothing that could be answered by reciting the standard framing back at me. No reading recs or citations needed either, obviously, she'll just talk.

Her line, as far as I've got it from her last paper: the two inversions are really one. Darwin shows you can get design without a designer, Turing shows you can get computation without a comprehender, and she thinks comprehension itself is just another competence — specifically, the competence to evaluate and revise your other competences — so the story closes without a homunculus. She also thinks current language models are the cleanest test case anyone's ever had for this, which I'm not sure I buy.

Things I want in there, roughly one question each, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:

- whether the two inversions really are the same inversion, given that Darwin's is about origins (how you get design with no designer) and Turing's seems to be about interpretation (what counts as computing something, and for whom). If she says same, follow up on whether the Turing half then collapses into the fairly trivial claim that you can describe almost any physical system as computing something, and what's left of the interesting claim if so.
- the regress. If comprehension is the competence to evaluate your own competences, what evaluates that one? She'll probably say the same competence applied recursively and no extra homunculus is needed — so then ask what stops the recursion, and whether a system with competences about its competences but nothing at the base is different in kind from one that just has the first-order competences, or only different in degree.
- the LLM case. Press on what would actually distinguish her-she-comprehends from she-has-a-competence-whose-object-is-her-own-competences. Get her to say what evidence would separate those, and then ask whether the same evidence wouldn't have separated them for us at some earlier point.
- a case I made up that I think is nasty for her: a system trained with humans in the loop, where part of the reward is that its outputs be ones a human can reconstruct a justification for. It ends up tracking human comprehensibility rather than any structure underneath. Does that show the distinction she cares about is really about etiology — how the thing got there — rather than about what's going on in it now? And if it's etiology, isn't that a claim about history that we can almost never check?
- vacuity. What would a case of genuine comprehension look like that her picture couldn't redescribe as higher-order competence? If there isn't one, is the view still empirical, or has it been qualified into something no possible evidence could touch?
- the selection question: the inversion explains why comprehension isn't needed for competence, but does it explain why comprehension is there at all? Why isn't everything competence without comprehension? (I expect a "it pays for itself" answer, so have the follow-up ready: pays whom, measured how, and does that make comprehension just a shortcut rather than a different kind of thing?)
- whether comprehension is doing any causal work in her account or is just a label for a threshold of competence. If a threshold, is it interest-relative — do we call something comprehension when its competences happen to be ones we can recognize as our own? Follow-up: doesn't that make the whole dispute about us rather than about them?
- the Darwin side: does the inversion need the specific claim that selection has no foresight, or does it run with any mindless optimization process (learning algorithms, markets, whatever)? If the latter, then Darwin's case is just one instance and the inversion is broader than his point — ask her to say what's doing the work then.
- one question that makes the position cost something. If comprehension is higher-order competence, what does she have to give up? My guess is she loses a distinction between doing the right thing for the right reasons and merely doing the right thing, or between understanding a proof and being able to produce it. Make her either accept that or say precisely which distinction survives and how.

And if you can, end on the question she'd least want to be asked, given all of the above.
