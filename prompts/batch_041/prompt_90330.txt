For the last few years I have been keeping a mental list of arguments that share the same shape, and I have never found a name for the shape, so I am writing to ask whether one exists. The shape is this: someone claims that some outcome — a disaster, a betrayal, an accident, a punishment — cannot be ruled out, that the odds may be minuscule or may be something no one could ever estimate, and that because the outcome would be so enormous, we are obliged to act now: to pay the cost, accept the restriction, or change our lives. What interests me is that the odds and the stakes move in opposite directions by design. The smaller the chance, the larger the harm the argument reaches for, so the case for acting never weakens however silly the chance becomes, and the arithmetic of multiplying how bad something would be by how unlikely it is never comes out small enough to ignore. I noticed it first in a friend's pitch for a supplement he sells, then in an argument about a new technology that supposedly could end everything, then in a charity appeal, and finally in an argument I caught myself making about a medical test. When I complained to another friend, he said the shape can be turned against anyone: he could demand my savings by threatening an unthinkably large harm that he cheerfully admits is unlikely, and by my own reasoning I would have to pay; then someone else could demand more; and so on without end. He thinks this destroys the whole way of arguing. I am not convinced, because I still believe a small chance of something genuinely catastrophic deserves serious weight, and that does not feel to me like the same mistake he is describing. I have heard the name Pascal's wager used for this general neighbourhood, but the wager seems to be one particular bet about one particular prize, whereas what I keep running into is a recipe that can be run on any threat or any promise, by anyone, over and over, with the stakes raised each time.

So my question is whether there is an established name for this exact pattern, and if there is, I want considerably more than the name. I would like to know whether it is treated as a fallacy or as a genuine difficulty for anyone who reasons by weighing odds against stakes; precisely how it differs from the wager, from the precautionary idea that we should act against serious harms even when no probability can be attached to them, and from ordinary caution; and which ingredient is actually doing the work — the impossibility of estimating the odds, the unboundedness of the stakes, or the fact that someone is asking me for something. If there is no name for the exact thing, please say so plainly and then supply one, defined sharply enough that I can tell what falls inside it and what does not, with a case on each side of the boundary.
