Okay so I've been going down a rabbit hole on something and I need help figuring out whether the debate is even a real debate or if people are just talking past each other.

Here's what got me. I use Bayesian reasoning all the time at work — priors, likelihoods, updating on evidence, the usual. And I stumbled on the fact that Bayesian confirmation theory supposedly has this embarassing problem where it can't say a piece of evidence confirms a theory if you already knew the evidence. The example everyone uses: Einstein's general relativity explained the weird extra bit in the precession of Mercury's orbit, and at the time people treated that as a huge win for the theory. But the Mercury number had been known since the mid-1800s. So if the Bayesian story is that evidence confirms a hypothesis only when learning the evidence raises its probability, and you already knew the evidence for sure, then P = 1 and nothing can move. Which is nuts, because clearly the Mercury thing did confirm GR.

So there's this whole literature of fixes. Like: pretend you didn't already know the evidence, and ask how surprised you'd have been (some counterfactual move). Or: say learning isn't only about conditionalizing on certainties — there's some way to pick something up without any single proposition becoming more probable. Or: the real confirming evidence is some higher-level fact you only just noticed (like "the theory predicts this number" rather than the number itself). Or: the whole problem is an artifact of letting an agent be 100% certain of anything, which real agents never are.

My problem is this: I've now read a bunch of these and every single proposed fix has people who think it's obviously wrong, and the argument has been going for decades without anyone giving ground. As an ML person it kind of feels like saying a model can't be validated by data it was trained on, which everyone also argues about forever. So what kind of disagreement actually is this?

My hunch — and I want you to tell me if it's wrong — is that the two sides aren't really disagreeing about the math but about what the question even is. One side is asking "what should a perfectly rational agent's credences be at a given moment," and the other is asking "what makes it reasonable to credit a theory with having gotten something right," and those are different questions that happen to share the word "confirm."

Can you diagnose this for me? Tell me concretely what each camp is actually committed to, whether it's a substantive disagreement, a merely verbal one, or two different questions under one name, and whether there's a hidden assumption doing the work (like the assumption that rational learning has to go through conditionalization). Then be honest: given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move this — a distinction, a reframing, some formal result — or is it the kind of thing that just can't get settled because the people involved want different things out of the word? Don't just list the fixes, I want the diagnosis with reasons.
