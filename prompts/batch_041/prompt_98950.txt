I work on formal epistemology, and I have been stuck for years on the standoff over luminosity and the KK principle. The shape of it, as I see it: one camp argues that knowledge is not luminous—that there is no non-trivial mental condition such that whenever an agent is in it, the agent is in a position to know that they are in it—and reaches that conclusion by margin-for-error or safety-style reasoning about nearby cases. The other camp resists, often by appeal to idealization: the arguments trade on small changes in an agent's situation that a suitably attentive knower could track, or they presuppose a threshold conception of knowledge that ordinary epistemology need not accept. Each side concedes much of the other's casework, and neither gives ground. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about knowledge, but about which epistemic property is in play under a single word: one side is theorizing a threshold or safety property of belief-forming methods, the other a property of an agent's standing to be certain or to assert, and the arguments that appear to collide are aimed at different targets. Please test that hypothesis against what each side actually says and tell me whether it is right, too simple, or wrong.

To make this concrete, and to keep the answer from collapsing into a survey, work with the following distinction. Call a condition access-luminous if, whenever an agent is in it, the agent's evidence puts them in a position to know they are in it, where the evidence is characterized from the inside—first-personally, in terms the agent can deploy without empirical investigation. Call a condition tracking-luminous if, whenever an agent is in it, the agent's belief that they are in it is safe in the nearby-worlds sense, where safety is characterized from the outside, in terms of the agent's reliability as a matter of fact. Is the KK principle plausible on one reading and hopeless on the other, and is the impasse explained by the literature sliding between the two? Or is the distinction itself too neat—do the interesting cases force us to abandon one reading, or to admit that neither captures what the disputants mean?

If you conclude the disagreement is substantive rather than verbal, identify the hidden commitments doing the work—for instance, whether knowledge is a threshold or a matter of degree, whether the margin in margin-for-error reasoning must scale with the agent's own uncertainty about their reliability, whether idealization is a legitimate method in epistemology, or whether ordinary usage can settle anything—and give your reasons. If you conclude it is at bottom verbal, or that the sides are answering different questions under one name, describe what a charitable translation between the camps would look like.

Either way, close with an honest account of what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result (say, a version of the margin-for-error reasoning that survives when the margin scales with the agent's own reliability, or a proof that no such version can), a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. I care less about who is right about any particular case than about the structure of the impasse. Please answer in American English.
