For several years I have convened a reading group on the origins of the Cold War, and we keep returning to the same question without ever settling it: who started it? The disagreement is not naive. One camp assigns primary responsibility to the Soviet Union — a revolutionary state with a universalist ideology, an army in occupation of Eastern Europe, and a leadership that treated wartime agreements as instruments rather than constraints. A second camp assigns primary responsibility to the United States — an economically expansionist power that held the atomic bomb before its rival, curtailed Lend-Lease, declined to treat Soviet security interests in Eastern Europe as legitimate, and defined its war aims in terms its adversary could only read as encirclement. A third participant, whose objection is the reason I am writing, maintains that the question itself is defective: that "starting" a Cold War presupposes a single initiating act and a single responsible agent, whereas what we have is a process spanning a decade or more, in which both sides acted on fears that were, given their positions, intelligible, and in which the outcome was overdetermined by structure.

My own hypothesis — which I would like you to test rather than adopt — is that the stalemate is not primarily a disagreement about the archive. It looks to me as though the participants are answering different questions under one heading, and that the apparent conflict over facts is being driven by two commitments that are seldom made explicit: a contested empirical claim about whether the Soviet system was fundamentally expansionist or fundamentally security-seeking (a claim the surviving evidence may underdetermine), and an unstated normative premise about what a great power is entitled to do within what it regards as its own sphere of influence.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on the merits, and I want it earned from what the positions actually assert. Four distinctions seem to me to be doing hidden work, and I would like you to say whether that is right: temporal priority (which state moved first), causal contribution (which state's actions did most to make the conflict likely), blameworthiness (which state was at fault), and avoidability (whether the conflict could have been averted by different choices). If these come apart — and I suspect they do — then much of the dispute may be verbal, a quarrel about the word "start," or a set of distinct questions that have been fused under one name. But I do not want that conclusion by default. If a genuine substantive disagreement lies underneath, about the character of the Soviet regime or about the weight to be given to structure as against intention, I want it identified and its evidential status assessed.

So: tell me what kind of disagreement this is, whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely hard, and what would count as progress. A distinction? A change of question — from who started it to what made it likely, or to whether anyone could have stopped it? Or would progress require evidence, and if so, what would decisive evidence even look like — what could a document say that would settle the matter, and is there any reason to think such a document could exist? Where you find that specialists agree, say so; where a reading is contested among people who know the material, present it as contested rather than as the consensus. Flag your own uncertainty rather than resolving it by assertion.
