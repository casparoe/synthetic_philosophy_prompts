I run a 90-minute session in an upper-level philosophy of social science seminar, about 20 students, mixed humanities/econ, and I want an in-class activity where they feel path dependence from the inside — not the watered-down 'history matters' version, but the specific thing that makes it philosophically interesting: the part where you can't tell, from the inside, whether your outcome was an accident that got locked in or the thing that was going to happen anyway. They've all heard the QWERTY/keyboard story and I'd rather not build the thing around it; use it in the debrief if at all, and flag that there's a long-running fight over whether it's even an example of inefficiency — I don't want to relitigate that, just note that the story is contested.

Here's roughly what I have in mind; change it if you've got something better.

Set up four 'settlements' of five students each, sitting at separate tables. Each student gets one convention card, five conventions in total, and every settlement starts with all five present (one holder each) — so the starting point is symmetric and nothing has won yet. Payoffs per round for holding convention X = the number of people in your settlement holding X, minus a switching cost if you changed this round. Critically, you can only see the cards of the two people sitting next to you, not the whole table, so information is local but the returns are global. Eight rounds, simultaneous reveals, tally sheet per table, points not money.

Then two things happen. First, at round 5, announce that in each settlement whichever convention is held by the fewest people there now pays double for the rest of the game (a 'discovery' that only arrives after everyone is committed). Tune the switching cost so that one person switching alone doesn't pay off, but a coordinated switch by the whole table would — that gap is the whole point of the exercise, so the numbers matter more than anything else here. Second, after the last round, each table appoints a chronicler who writes three sentences explaining why their convention won, without being told the other tables' outcomes. Shuffle the chronicles, read them out, and have the class guess which table each belongs to. My hope is that the same narrative form fits every outcome and they can't tell them apart, which is the moment they see that a local, plausible-sounding story about why this convention won is compatible with its being an accident.

Then, if there's time, re-deal one table's cards and run it again to see whether it lands somewhere else — because that's the thing the historian never gets to do.

I want all of this in the rules, not in a lecture: they shouldn't hear the phrase 'path dependence' until the debrief, and they should arrive at the contingency/inevitability distinction themselves. So give me the rules, materials, a round-by-round timeline, the payoff table with actual numbers, the roles, and a debrief that maps each beat of what happened onto the concepts — increasing returns, lock-in, inefficient vs. merely different outcomes, contingency vs. functional convergence, and the counterfactual problem. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'd guess they'll try to coordinate publically before I've told them not to, and that half the tables will collapse into join-the-biggest-group by round 2, but tell me the failure modes I haven't thought of and how to handle each without killing the exercise. And be honest about what this can't reproduce: the payoff table is stipulated by me, so the activity quietly guarantees there's a fact about which convention is better, whereas in real cases the whole dispute is whether 'better' has a determinate answer at all; also we get to rerun worlds and the historian doesn't; also nobody in the game really loose's anything. I want that limitation named in the facilitator notes, not buried.
