I am trying to pin down a pattern I keep meeting in arguments about whether the law should punish a sexual practice, and I want to know whether there is a name for it — and, if there is, what exactly it covers, because I suspect that whatever name I am given will cover either too much or too little.

Here is the pattern as I meet it. The conduct in question is rarely opposed on the ground that it causes harm. Instead it arrives already labelled, and the label is not a separate verdict appended to a neutral description — it is the description. The statute is titled with it, the charge is read out with it, and because the label is already in place, anyone who asks whether the conduct should be punished is taken to be asking whether the conduct is really the sort of thing the label names. The argument that would have to be had about suffering, consent, or third parties is displaced by a dispute about whether the word applies; to resist the punishment you have to resist the word, and resisting the word looks like quibbling about language rather than a substantive objection about harm. I notice the same structure with the opposite valence, where a term carries the acquittal inside it, so that someone who wants to argue for restrictions must first argue about the meaning of the term. And it appears well outside sex — in expressions that fuse a description with a verdict, so that one cannot accept the description and reject the verdict without sounding as though one is misusing the language. I came to this while reading a utilitarian law reformer who argued that certain sexual acts were punished with no showing of harm and who complained in passing that the names of the offences were doing the work of the argument; he seems to have had a term for a class of such names, but I have only a secondhand impression of it and do not want to lean on a name I cannot see defined.

What I want from you:

First, is there an established name for this — either a general one for the fused description-and-verdict expression, or a narrower one for the specific trick of putting the verdict into the name of the offence so that the case for punishment never has to be made? If you think you know of a historical term for the class, give it, say what you take it to cover, and say how confident you are; if you are unsure of it, say so rather than guessing.

Second, and more important, do the carving. I want to know whether the things I have lumped together are one phenomenon or several. In particular, how does the fused expression differ from a persuasive definition, from a thick ethical concept, from an essentially contested concept, from a euphemism or a dysphemism, and from a plain enthymeme that simply leaves a premise unstated? Some of these concern what a term means, some concern the attitude it carries, and some concern the step in an argument that it lets a speaker skip; I want to know which of those distinctions is doing the real work, and which of my examples fall on either side of it.

Third, boundary cases. Does the phenomenon require that the term be contested, or can it operate while everyone agrees on its meaning? Does it require that the term be embedded in an institution — a statute, a charge, a diagnostic manual, a form — rather than merely used by speakers? What about a term that fuses description and verdict but where the verdict happens to be correct: is that still the same phenomenon, or does it become something else? And is the mirror-image case, where the fused term produces an unearned acquittal rather than an unearned conviction, genuinely the same phenomenon with the sign flipped, or a different one?

Fourth, if no established name fits, coin one and define it, tightly enough to exclude the near misses, with at least two boundary cases marked explicitly as inside or outside.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of my question.
