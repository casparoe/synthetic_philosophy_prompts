The episode. In 2016 ProPublica published an analysis of COMPAS, a risk-assessment instrument sold by Northpointe (now Equivant) and used in Broward County, Florida to inform bail and sentencing decisions. They took the scores assigned to more than 7,000 people arrested in 2013 and 2014 and checked two-year re-arrest, the same benchmark the tool's makers used. Two findings: black defendants who did not go on to reoffend were flagged as high risk at almost twice the rate of white defendants who didn't reoffend, and white defendants who did reoffend were mislabeled low risk more often than black defendants who did. The overall mistake rate was roughly similar across the two groups, but the mistakes were distributed differently. Northpointe replied that the instrument was accurate regardless of race. Both sides, on their own numbers, appear to have been right, and that's the interesting part.

The theory. Around the same time, a cluster of impossibility results (Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan; Chouldechova; and others) showed that three natural conditions cannot in general hold at once for a risk score: (i) calibration within groups — among everyone given score s, the outcome rate is the same whatever the group; (ii) balance for the negative class — equal false positive rates across groups; (iii) balance for the positive class — equal false negative rates across groups. These are jointly satisfiable only in degenerate cases: equal base rates, perfect prediction, or a classifier that ignores the data. Other criteria in the family (equalized odds, which is ii plus iii; predictive parity; test fairness) have their own incompatibilities.

What I want, in this order:

1. The mapping. Which criterion was ProPublica implicitly holding the tool to, and which was Northpointe defending? Be explicit about who counts as the decision-maker, what the classifier is, and what counts as the label.
2. Fit. Is the impossibility result actually what's going on in this dispute, or does the theorem's setup not quite match the tool as used — an ordinal score with a handful of bands, a human judge downstream, an outcome variable defined as an arrest rather than an offense?
3. The limits. Where is the theory silent or misleading? Say plainly whether the theorem explains why the two sides talked past each other, or whether the real disagreement is upstream: about what the target variable should be, about whether the base-rate gap is itself an artifact of policing, about who gets to pick the threshold, about whether the tool should be used at all. Does the theorem's bite depend on the score being converted into a binary decision?
4. The reverse direction. What does the episode show about the impossibility results as a piece of theory — do they vindicate both parties, dissolve the dispute, relocate it, or hand each side a mathematical fig leaf? Is there a reading on which publishing the theorems made the public argument worse? And consider the follow-up complaint that constraining a decision rule to satisfy something like equalized odds imposes a real public-safety cost: is that a theorem or a normative preference dressed up as one?
5. Your verdict, briefly. If you were advising Broward County and you accepted the theorem, what would you recommend, and what would you say to the side you came down against?

Don't let the math do normative work it can't do, and flag anything you're unsure of instead of asserting it. I'd rather have a sharp analysis that says where the theory runs out than a tidy one that pretends it doesn't.
