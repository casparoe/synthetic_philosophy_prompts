Next week I present to a metaphysics-of-mind reading group a paper defending the following claim, and what I want from you is a question-period prep sheet, not a summary or a defence of my own argument.

The claim: Kim-style causal exclusion arguments threaten only what I call productive sufficiency — the thesis that a property instance is a cause of an effect only if, given the circumstances, its instantiation guarantees that effect. Once causation is read as contrastive difference-making rather than productive sufficiency, mental properties are no more excluded by their physical realizers than one thrown switch is excluded by the rest of the set it belongs to. My working example is a device I have built for the paper, the quorum lock: a door that opens when at least three of five switches are thrown. Throw three and it opens; throw two and it does not. Each thrown switch is a difference-maker for the opening — hold the other four fixed and varying that one varies the outcome — yet no single switch is sufficient for the opening, and the full set of five is sufficient without making any member redundant. My analogy is that the physical realizer is the whole set and the mental property is one switch, so the exclusionist's inference from the whole set's sufficiency to the individual switch's causal idleness is precisely the inference the quorum lock refutes. The realizer relation itself I treat as a grounding relation rather than a causal one, so realizer and realized never enter the same causal competition.

The audience: two members are committed exclusionists in Kim's mould, who will press the supervenience and overdetermination steps and are impatient with anything they hear as redefining causation to save the mental; one works on Woodward-style interventionist theories and will ask whether my difference-making relation is invariant under interventions and whether it survives the move from type to token causation; one works on grounding and will want to know why the realizer relation is exempt from the causal closure principle I am content to grant elsewhere; one is a Russellian who thinks the whole dispute is an artifact of a folk notion of causation and will say I have conceded his point by redescribing causation; and one is a philosopher of mind with epiphenomenalist sympathies who will ask what my account predicts that the exclusionist's does not — what turns on my being right. Nobody in the room works on free will, so do not spend replies there.

Prepare me for what I will actually be asked, ordered by how much damage each does to the paper rather than by the order in which they will come. Phrase each objection the way the relevant member of the audience would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine. For each, give a two-minute spoken reply and one sentence on where that reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than defend, and what I must hold even if it costs me the room. Include, at the end and labelled as such, the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Lead with your bottom line: your first sentence should name the single objection most likely to sink the paper, in one sentence; then give the reasoning and the rest of the sheet. No preamble and no restating of what I have asked for.
