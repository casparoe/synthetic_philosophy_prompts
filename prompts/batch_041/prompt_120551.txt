I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about sunk costs with a friend who works in decision theory and takes a hard-nosed rational-choice line, and I came away with the uncomfortable sense that I don't know where the live disagreements in that area actually are. His case was thoroughly deflationary: once you write the decision problem down properly, he said, past expenditure can only matter through the state it has left you in, so anything that looks like respecting sunk costs is either a mistake about which state you are in or a perfectly respectable piece of reasoning about information, commitment or reputation that has been mislabelled as a fallacy; on his telling, the only open questions left are about how to taxonomise the textbook demonstrations. I think that is too quick — the stories people tell about escalation, about wars and infrastructure projects and relationships that nobody can bring themselves to leave, do not feel like taxonomy problems — but I could not articulate the alternative, and I would rather have a serious answer than a debating point.

Here is what I actually want from you. I have a funded PhD place starting in October: three years, no laboratory and no data collection to speak of, conceptual work plus whatever formal modelling I can teach myself, and a supervisor who has said the question is mine to choose. My friend's challenge has left me nervous that I would be spending those three years on something either closed or trivial, so I want help choosing among the open questions rather than a survey of them. Give me a ranked shortlist of three or four and defend the ordering on explicit criteria: how much other work waits on the answer, how tractable each is for someone with my toolkit rather than for an experimentalist, and how much any answer would change what people actually do — in individual decisions, in how organisations design cancellation reviews and stage gates and who is allowed to call time on a project, in policy, and in how the theory of rationality is written. Where the criteria pull against one another, say so and tell me how you would resolve the conflict. Then tell me what would move each question up or down the list over the next couple of years, and be specific about the kind of result that would do it. I would also like to know which questions that everyone in the area treats as central I should quietly ignore.

One half-formed thought, to be pushed back on if it is wrong: it seems to me the useful cut may be between three things that get run together — past investment mattering because the agent misreads the state, past investment mattering because it has genuinely changed the world (skills, capabilities, option value, the expectations of others, the credibility of one's future commitments), and past investment mattering because abandoning the project would be a different act rather than a continuation of the same one, in the way that giving up on a person is not simply a change of plan. If that three-way split collapses into the first item wearing costumes, or if there is a better carving, say so, because it would reorganise the ranking. I am also curious whether the comparative evidence, where the same pattern turns up in animals and is argued over as an adaptation rather than a bias, bears on any of this or is a sideshow, and I want your view on the moral side that my friend waved away: whether there are cases where seeing something through is owed to the people who relied on it, and whether that is a reason of a different kind from the prudential ones. Please do not give me citations or a reading list — I will find the literature myself. I want the reasoning and the ordering, and I would rather you spend your space on the disagreements that specialists would recognise as live than on the ones that are only live in textbooks.
