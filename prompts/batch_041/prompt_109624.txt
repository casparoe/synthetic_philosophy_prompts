Every time my global history seminar gets anywhere near decentering the discipline, one of my students shuts the discussion down with 'history is written by the victors.' Last week she went further with it: the sources for anything precolonial reach us through colonial archives anyway, so telling the past 'from everywhere at once' is just trading one set of victors for another, and we should stop pretending any narrative is more than a power play. A colleague of mine likes to turn the line around — it's the losers who write the archives — and I suspect he and the student are talking past each other, though I couldn't say exactly where.

What I want from you is a menu of the precise claims that one sentence could be making, running from the trivially true to the ones with real teeth: claims about who produces the surviving evidence, about who gets to decide what counts as an event or a period, about whether there's a fact of the matter about the past at all, and whatever else is buried in there. For each reading, say whether it's true, how strong it is, and whether it actually gets her to 'so the past can't be told from everywhere at once.' Then tell me which reading people usually have in mind when they use the phrase to end an arguement.

500 words max, and lay it out as a list rather than an essay — I want to hand this to the seminar.
