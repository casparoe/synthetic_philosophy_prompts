I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and something that happened over the winter break has pushed a question I had been treating as a technicality to the center of my attention. While choosing between three apartments for my postdoc year, I found that my all-things-considered judgments would not settle into an order: on one evening I was convinced the first was better than the second, the second better than the third, and — when I forced myself to compare the first and third directly — the third better than the first. I dismissed it as ordinary indecision until I recognized the same shape in a spectrum I have been using in a chapter on value: a sequence of possible lives, each trading a small loss in quality for a slightly larger gain in length, so that every adjacent pair seems to favor the later life (A worse than B, B worse than C, and so on) even though the final member of the sequence — a very long life that is barely worth living — seems plainly worse than the first. I would like to see the two philosophical responses to this set against each other in their strongest form.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers, each fully competent and neither serving as a foil. The first holds that 'better than' is transitive as a matter of the logic of the relation itself: if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C, so the spectrum shows only that at least one of our adjacent judgments is mistaken and stands in need of revision. The second holds that transitivity is a substantive thesis about a particular value relation rather than a truth of logic, and is prepared to accept that all-things-considered betterness may really be intransitive in cases like this, so that the cycle is not an error but a genuine feature of the evaluative landscape. Let them press each other's actual claims rather than talk past each other. I would like the first to say precisely which judgment must be abandoned and why the revision is not merely ad hoc, and to bring in the familiar worry that an intransitive ranking exposes its holder to a money pump; and the second to explain what deliberation and choice come to when one's rankings cycle, and to say whether intransitivity, if real, undermines the very notion of a best option or of a rational choice. I would also like them to sort out the surrounding neighborhood: how intransitivity differs from incomparability and from the idea that adjacent items are 'on a par,' and whether one could retain the transitivity of 'at least as good as' while denying the transitivity of 'better than,' or whether the two stand or fall together. Two pages or so, and please leave the ending unresolved.
