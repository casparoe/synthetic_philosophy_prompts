I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind — fourteen students, one ninety-minute session — and I want them to feel the two-sided squeeze on functionalism rather than hear about it. Design a runnable activity in which the class implements a single functional specification three times over: as a device they build from cheap parts; as an ensemble of people, each occupying one internal state and following a rule card; and as a lookup table that returns the correct output for any input history while containing nothing that plays the role of the creature's internal states. Tell me what the specification should be; all three implementations must pass the same behavioral test battery, which the students run themselves. The trap is the order of operations. Before the third implementation is revealed, each student writes and pins up a one-sentence criterion for what it takes to really have the creature's state of wanting or avoiding something, and is then held to it. The first vote and the second must come out differently, so that their own criterion convicts them — once for denying the state to the mechanical build, once for granting it to the table — and the pressure comes from commitments they already made, not from me. Give me materials, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the two failure modes at either end, too demanding and too permissive about what can realize a mental state, and onto the standard functionalist reply that the table has the wrong causal organization, together with what that reply costs. None of the debrief's vocabulary — functionalism, multiple realization, chauvinism, the liberal objection — should be spoken before the debrief. Say what predictably goes wrong the first time and how to handle it, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: it bears not at all on phenomenal consciousness, and a working intuition pump is not an argument.
