I teach a senior elective on big ideas at a public high school — twenty students, one ninety-minute block — and I have no training in history or economics. Everything I know about famines I have picked up reading on my own, and the thing I keep circling is the claim that people starve when their ability to command food collapses, not when the food runs out, and that a bad harvest turns into a catastrophe only because of the arrangements people happen to be living under. I want my students to run into that with their hands rather than hear me assert it, and I would rather they never hear the technical name for it until the debrief. No lecture up front; the rules should do the work.

Design the activity, and tell me where my plan is wrong. My plan is to split the room into two districts identical in every respect — same households, same grain, same weather — and run each as a small economy for a handful of rounds. Households should differ in what they own and what they can sell: a few large landholders with grain in store, smallholders, landless labourers who live by wages, a moneylender, a couple of traders able to move grain out of the district, and at least one household that is not the poorest in the district but whose livelihood is a service the crisis destroys. Each district gets the same modest shortfall — small enough that the grain actually present, if it were shared out, would still feed everyone — and the same outside buyer, a wartime procurement agent willing to pay well above the local price. Then they trade, month by month, at prices they set, each household having to buy enough each round to live. One district's officer has a relief budget and a standing order to feed anyone who comes to him; the other district's officer is told to keep order and send revenue up the line. Same weather, same harvest, different rules.

What I want at the end is the picture: unsold sacks still in the warehouse, standing next to people who died, and two districts with different body counts, so that at least one student can say afterwards, correctly, that there was enough food. Then a debrief that reconstructs what actually killed whom — the price, the wage, the export, the officer's orders, the shortfall itself — and that ends by asking why we reach for the phrase natural disaster. Keep the traders' conduct ordinary and defensible, so the lesson cannot collapse into blaming a villain.

Give me the roles and their starting endowments, the market rules, numbers simple enough to run on a whiteboard without calculators, the ninety-minute timeline, how deaths get recorded, the debrief questions in order, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce. I will supply the historical cases myself — I am thinking of Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward — so nothing in the debrief should rest on historical details I cannot check, and I do not need citations or further reading.
