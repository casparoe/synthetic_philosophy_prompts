So I'm a first-year in a cog sci MA and last week a bunch of us ended up arguing at a house party after a lab meeting, way more heated than a party warrants, about cognitive penetration. Two of the people in it were Reza and Tamsin, both second-years, and I mostly just stood there holding a drink, so I genuinely don't know who came off better. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Reza said the whole encapsulation picture is dead, and his proof was the usual stuff: people who are scared of heights overestimate how high a balcony is, people wearing a heavy backpack say a hill is steeper, and everyone knows a word's meaning changes how you see the letters in it. He said your beliefs and feelings are literally changing what you see, so perception can't be a sealed module.

(2) Tamsin said none of that is cognitive penetration. The fear and the backpack stuff run through arousal and attention, not through the content of what you believe. Penetration, she said, is supposed to be about the *content* of perception being shaped by the *content* of cognition — semantically, so that what you see is answerable to what you think. Fear making everything look a bit bigger isn't tracking anything, it's just noise. And she said if you count that, the term swallows everything and stops meaning anything.

(3) Reza said that's just smuggling in a "semantic coherence" requirement that isn't in the original definition and isn't even clearly met by the standard examples, and that the "it's mediated by attention" objection is empty because every effect is mediated by *some* mechanism — you can always point at a neural intermediary and declare the influence indirect. He said she's basically assuming encapsulation to prove it.

(4) Tamsin said the mediation point is doing real work: it separates an effect that goes through a shift in spatial attention, or through years of perceptual learning that retunes the visual system, from a case where a belief directly alters what you see without moving your eyes or changing your hardware. She also said Reza's framing is a bait and switch, because the interesting question was never whether the *phenomenology* changes — it's epistemic, whether a belief can justify a perceptual belief. And she threw in a test she said would settle it: the effect has to be *content-sensitive*. If believing the balcony is dangerous raises your estimate and believing it's reinforced lowers it, fine, that's penetration; if it's just "being worked up makes things look bigger," it isn't.

(5) Reza said the epistemic version is a different debate wearing the same name, and that if she wants "direct" she owes us an observable test for directness, and there isn't one — you can't see the difference between a belief reweighting a perceptual signal and a belief merely nudging attention, from the outside or the inside.

They both kept going and neither would budge. Can you go through their exchange move by move — rate each of the five from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each — and then say who's right about what, what each of them is missing, and who has the better overall position? Don't just tell me who wins, I want the specific points where each one is actually correct.
