I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been reading about the extended mind debate on my own for a couple of years, and I think I have an argument worth writing up as a short essay. Before I do, I would like comments on the notes below — particularly on whether the distinction I am drawing is real or collapses into something already on the table.

The familiar case, as I understand it: a man with severe memory loss keeps a notebook and consults it constantly, and we are invited to say that what is written there is part of his mind, because he trusts it, reaches for it without deliberation, and endorses what it says automatically. The obvious worry is that this lets in too much — a phone, a search engine, a friend, the notes app — and the usual reply is a cluster of conditions: reliability, easy access, automatic endorsement.

My proposal is that this cluster omits the condition doing the real work, which I have been calling editorial sovereignty: whether the resource can be changed, by someone other than the person whose mind is supposedly extended, without that person knowing or agreeing. My notebook is mine in this sense. The shared calendar my partner and I keep is not either of ours, because either of us can silently move something. The search engine is not, because what it returns is re-ranked for reasons I am never told. The suggestion, then, is that a resource belongs to a mind only if its owner has final say over what it holds.

What I want from you:

1. Is editorial sovereignty genuinely independent of the standard conditions, or does it quietly reduce to one of them — endorsement, say, or trust?
2. Can you construct a case that passes the standard conditions but fails mine, and would we really want to say the resource is not part of the mind there? If the answer is that we would not, my condition is doing no work.
3. Here is the objection I cannot answer. All memory is socially maintained: what I recall, and in what shape, has been edited by other people, and my brain can be altered without my consent by illness, by advertising, by a hundred things I never agreed to. If sovereignty over one's own memory is already a fiction, why should its absence from the notebook matter?
4. Is the whole thing interesting enough to be worth an essay, or is it a distinction without a difference? And what is the strongest version of the opposing view — the one I would have to beat rather than the easy one?

I am not looking for reading suggestions; I want to know whether the argument holds and where it is confused.
