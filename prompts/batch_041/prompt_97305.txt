For most of the past year I have been arguing with my sister about what happened at a family gathering we both attended. The details are irrelevant; what matters is that the argument never moved, because each of us treated her own recollection as something that could not itself be called into question, and the longer we went on the clearer it became that we were not disagreeing about evidence but about which things each of us was refusing to put in doubt. That sent me back to Wittgenstein's later remarks on certainty, and I have since been reading around the debates they set off. The trouble is that the labels I keep meeting — hinge epistemology, framework propositions, entitlement, quasi-fideism, relativism — tell me nothing about which view I actually hold, because I find myself agreeing with different writers about different cases.

I would like you to build me a sorting instrument: a quiz, in the manner of a magazine personality test, that sorts its taker into one of the positions in this debate on the strength of what they say about concrete cases rather than on the strength of which label they find congenial. Eight to ten questions. Each question should present a short scenario or a single claim and offer two or three mutually exclusive responses, phrased so that a thoughtful reader can choose among them without knowing which theoretical position the choice corresponds to. No question should simply ask whether hinges are propositions, or whether hinge commitments are rational, or which writer the taker prefers; the point is to sort people by their verdicts on cases.

Write the questions, and then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the following outcomes, which should be the possible results of the quiz:

1. Hinges are not propositions or beliefs at all — they are ways of acting, a kind of animal certainty that shows itself in practice and cannot be stated, doubted, or justified.
2. Hinges are genuine propositions, but constitutive of rational inquiry rather than justified within it; they are held with certainty and exempt from doubt without being arbitrary.
3. Hinges are neither known nor justified, yet we are entitled to them: there is a non-evidential warrant for adopting them, so they are rationally permissible even though nothing supports them.
4. Hinges can be knowledge, because knowledge does not require the ability to defend what one knows; scepticism about them is unmotivated, since doubt to be meaningful needs a method and a context, and the radical sceptic has neither.
5. The demand to justify hinges is a philosophical confusion rather than a problem with an answer; the right response is to dissolve the question, not to defend or refute anything.
6. Hinges are necessary conditions of inquiry, and the sceptical question about them is a real question that cannot be answered from within inquiry — so scepticism is neither refuted nor shown to be nonsense, only set aside in practice.
7. Which propositions function as hinges varies between communities, and there is no neutral standpoint from which one community's hinges can be judged better than another's; rationality itself is local.
8. Hinge commitments are held on trust, in something like the way religious commitments are, and this is no defect: they are not rationally grounded, but they are not for that reason irrational.

For each outcome, add a short label of your own devising and one or two sentences on what a person in that position is committed to denying, and which of the other outcomes they are most often confused with. If a single well-known defender of a position comes to mind, give the name alone; I do not want citations, dates, or reading lists.

I also want the instrument to press on a distinction that the summaries tend to blur: the difference between losing a hinge and revising one. Make sure at least two questions turn on cases in which someone stops being able to treat something as certain — through conversion, grief, or breakdown — and asks whether that transition can be assessed as rational, arational, or a matter of brute change, and whether the person has lost a commitment or merely exchanged one.

Two further requests. First, include cases that test whether the taker thinks there are practical and moral hinges as well as epistemic ones, and whether these behave the same way; I have in mind things like the wrongness of cruelty to a child, or the reliability of one's own memory, rather than only the standard examples about hands and tables. Second, wherever the quiz cannot in principle separate two of the eight positions, say so explicitly and explain why, instead of inventing a question that pretends to do it.

Finally, since I suspect the space of answers contains combinations nobody defends, note any such unoccupied cells — for instance a view on which hinges are propositions and yet can be rationally revised without loss of certainty, or one on which they are non-propositional and yet open to criticism — and say briefly for each whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to occupy it.
