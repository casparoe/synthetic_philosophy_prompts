ok so i'm running a one-off workshop on translation next month and i want to open with a poem instead of a mini-lecture. the thing i want people to feel in their gut is the bind you hit the second you try to carry a poem's form into another language: you can keep the shape or keep the sense, and whichever one you drop was a choice, not a freebie. hofstadter spent a whole book doing this with one tiny renaissance french poem and i want the same effect in about fifteen lines.

so write the poem. 12 to 16 lines. the speaker is a translator mid-attempt, working on a made-up source -- give that source an actual spec (a fixed line count, a rhyme scheme, a syllable count per line, and an acrostic on the name of the person it was written for) and then show the english trying to honor it. the part i actually care about: the poem's own form has to do the work. hold a strict form through the first stretch, then at a marked turn let it crack -- a line that wont scan, a rhyme that has to be forced or dropped -- at exactly the point where the sense wins. the break should cost something, and the poem should name what got thrown overboard. a reader who's never thought about translation should feel the trade-off by the end; someone who translates for a living shouldn't wince at the example.

dont let it resolve into "and then i found the perfect version." thats the one ending that kills it.
