I run a small local museum in the Black Country and I've somehow ended up reading economic history in the evenings, so this is a half-informed question, and I'd rather have the argument than a reading list — no bibliography please, I'm not going to check it. Set yourself up as a philosopher of the social sciences who works on explanation and comparative history, think hard about this before you answer, and start by brainstorming two or three possible readings of what I'm describing before you commit to one.

Here's the thing. I keep running into a certain kind of question, and I've started to suspect there's something structurally off about it, but I can't put my finger on what. Instances, from grand to trivial:

- Why did Britain industrialise and China not? Every answer I hear is a list of things China didn't have — no competitive states, no secure property, no open science — or, turned round, a list of things Britain did have. I can never tell whether the absences are causes or just the same fact said twice.
- Someone at the same seminar asked why the Ottomans didn't take up printing, and the answers had exactly the same shape. A list of lacks.
- My mum asks why my uncle never married, and gets the same sort of answer from the whole family.
- Why didn't our town get a railway station when the next town over did? The local answer is that we "lacked the right sort of people".

Three things bother me. First, nobody ever asks why the Aztecs didn't build the steam engine, so which absences get treated as questions at all seems to be doing the real work, and it's never acknowledged. Second, when the thing did happen the explanation cites positive facts, and when it didn't the explanation cites absences — and absences don't seem to need causes, which makes the second kind of answer awfully cheap. Third, a why-not question seems to smuggle in the idea that the outcome was on the cards, that China was, as it were, already leaning towards an industrial revolution and something held it back. Is that presupposition ever legitimate?

What I want is not a label but a carving. Is there an established concept for this? I've heard "contrastive explanation" and "the Needham question" thrown around, and I've got a vague sense that "selection on the dependent variable" is a different thing again — something about the evidence rather than the question. So: what exactly is the flaw, if there is one? The unspecified contrast class? Treating a non-event as though it were an event with causes? The smuggled-in tendency? The asymmetry between explaining presences and absences? Which of the neighbouring ideas actually fits, which doesn't, and why? And if nothing fits, coin something, define it precisely, and give me cases on both sides of the line — including a why-not question that is perfectly well posed, because I don't beleive they're all rotten.
