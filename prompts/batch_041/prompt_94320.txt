I'm in a small reading group on metaethics, and last week we got onto the move people reach for whenever someone argues that moral facts are too metaphysically expensive to believe in — the argument that moral properties would be causally inert, unlike anything else in our ontology, and that we would have no credible story about how we come to know them, so parsimony tells against positing them at all. A friend of mine, a PhD student in mathematics who is not especially invested in the debate, offered the reply that is sometimes called the companions-in-guilt strategy, in a form I had not seen put quite so bluntly. His line is this: every one of those charges lands just as hard on mathematical objects. Numbers are not part of the causal order, nobody bumps into the number seven, and we have no causal contact with them either — and yet almost everyone in the room happily treats arithmetic as a body of truths about mind-independent matters that we nonetheless manage to know. So, he says, if you can be a realist about mathematics without embarrassment, you can be a realist about morality without embarrassment, and the parsimony argument against moral facts proves far too much.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than simply adjudicate the underlying dispute. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — saying exactly what is supposed to map onto what: which objects, which epistemology, which flavour of metaphysical oddity, which explanatory role, which kind of independence. Then tell me which features of the mathematical case are actually doing the work in his argument, and whether the moral case genuinely shares them. Where does the mapping break, and which of the breaks matter for the conclusion he is drawing rather than being merely decorative? I am especially interested in whether the analogy quietly imports a particular philosophy of mathematics: if you are not a Platonist about numbers — if you think mathematics is structural, or fictional, or ultimately a matter of our conventions and inferential practices — does the analogy collapse, does it turn against him, or does it survive only in a weakened form? I would also like you to test whether the analogy is stronger or weaker when the companion domain is swapped: instead of mathematical objects, take the normativity of logic and correct inference, or the bare idea that some beliefs are correct and others are not. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that moral facts are no worse off than mathematical ones, so the parsimony argument fails — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if it does not, what a more honest analogy would be.

Give me your own verdict, and be explicit and self-critical about it: say which way you come down, how confident you are, and — most importantly — what would move you off that verdict. Which single disanalogy, if it turned out to hold up, would be enough to sink his argument, and where is your own reasoning most likely to be overreaching, or to be trading on a contested reading of one side or the other?
