So I've been reading one of those popular science-y books about morality and evolution, and the chapter on the is/ought thing goes roughly like this:

"One of the few things moral philosophy has actually settled is that you can't get an ought from an is. Hume spotted it first: in every system of morality he looked at, the author slides from claims about what is the case to claims about what ought to be, and the slide is never justified. A hundred and fifty years later Moore sharpened it into the naturalistic fallacy -- the mistake of thinking you can read values off of nature, of treating what's natural as what's good. Together they show that facts and values are two seperate realms and no argument can bridge them. You can know everything about how the world is, including everything about what people happen to want and approve of, and still not have the first clue what you ought to do. That's why every attempt to ground ethics in biology collapses, and it's also why Hume thought reason is just the servant of the passions. The upshot: moral commitments aren't discoveries, they're commitments you make."

I'm not a philosopher and I basically believe this, but two things nag at me. My brother says it's obviously false, because 'he promised to pay me back' entails 'he ought to pay me back' -- there's your ought, from an is, in one step. And a friend of mine uses the term naturalistic fallacy for people who say raw milk must be healthy because it's natural, which doesn't seem to be what the book is describing at all.

Can you go claim by claim through what the book says -- which bits a philosopher working on this would accept, which are oversimplifications of something true, and which are just wrong -- and then tell me whether the book's closing conclusion (morality is commitment rather than discovery) actually follows from anything true in the earlier claims, or whether it's a seperate claim that's been smuggled in? And settle the two objections if you can: is my brother's promise case a real counterexample or is he cheating somewhere, and is my friend using the term wrong, or is the book?
