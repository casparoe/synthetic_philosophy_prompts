Call the amount in your envelope X. (2) Since you were handed it at random, it's just as likely to be the smaller as the larger one, so with probability 1/2 the other envelope holds 2X and with probability 1/2 it holds X/2. (3) So the expected value of the other envelope is (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = (5/4)X, which beats X. (4) So you should always swap, and swap back, and back again, and if there's a little fee per swap you should cheerfully pay it every single time, forever. (5) But that's clearly nuts -- two fixed envelopes can't make swapping back and forth profitable, and a rule that has you paying to shuffle money between two envelopes you already own is a bad rule. (6) His moral is that expected-value reasoning is broken, or that "the expected value of the other one" is a phrase that just doesn't mean anything, and he wants to throw out a big chunk of decision theory. He even says it gets worse if you peek and see $10, because then (3) still says swap. I'm pretty sure (5) is right and something in (1)-(4) has to go, but I can't pin whether the bad move is the probability assignment in (2), or the way X is doing work in (3), or something about what "equally likely" can even mean when nobody told us how the experimenter chose the amounts.
