I am teaching War and Peace in a philosophy of history seminar this term, and I want to set it against Popper's critique of historicism rather than treat it as an illustration of it. The novel argues a thesis of its own: that the men we call great are history's instruments rather than its authors, that an event is the integral of numberless small individual acts, and that freedom is an illusion which shrinks as we discover more of an event's causes. Does that thesis fall under Popper's charge of historicism — the belief that history obeys laws from which the future could be foretold — or is it the opposite error, a determinism so complete that it denies not only foresight but agency, and so dissolves the piecemeal politics Popper means to defend? I should like your view on Kutuzov especially: is his refusal to intervene the abdication of responsibility that Popper associates with historicism, or an instance of the alternative Popper proposes, in which an action is explained by the agent's situation rather than by laws of development or the designs of a great man? Which episodes would you put before the class — Austerlitz, Borodino, the epilogue — and where does the novel resist the reading, say in its providentialism? Close by saying what the novel, as a work of fiction, contributes to the argument that Popper's prose cannot.
