I run a small shop in a town that has been transformed over the past five years: a large employer arrived, and with it a stream of newcomers and outside money. Since then I have watched a pattern I cannot name, and I have begun to wonder whether Hume, whom I am reading for the first time, described it long ago.

The pattern is not simply that prices rose. It is that they rose in an order. The things newcomers buy — housing, meals, labour, services — rose first and rose most; the things we sell to one another rose later and less. For a while the town genuinely boomed, but the boom belonged to whoever was placed to sell to the newcomers early. For anyone on a fixed income, and for the trades that serve only their neighbours, those same years were a squeeze. And once local wages and prices had finished catching up, the boom was over: what remained was a town with the same houses, roads and skills as before, a different distribution of who held what, and a general sense of being back where we started.

Two things I cannot get straight. First, I cannot answer the question of whether the town is better off. Every gain I can point to is somebody's loss and every loss somebody's gain, so the aggregate figures look splendid and I do not trust them. Is there a name for that whole configuration — outside money arriving, prices adjusting in a fixed order, a temporary real advantage to whoever sells to the newcomers first, and a place that ends up differently arranged rather than richer? Is there a separate name for the stretch itself, the time between money arriving and prices finishing their adjustment? I know the words inflation and supply and demand, and neither is what I am after.

Second, Hume. Reading his essay on money, I take him to say that an increase in money helps industry only during the interval before prices catch up, and to treat that interval as a benefit. That is the interval I think I am living in. If what happens is a reshuffling rather than an addition, how can it be a benefit? And does it matter that no new money arrived in the town as such — the notes are the same notes as anywhere else; only the spending changed hands?

I am not asking for a bare label. I want the nearest established names, an account of where each of them stops fitting my case, and some view of what would have to be true for the interval to leave a place better off rather than merely different.
