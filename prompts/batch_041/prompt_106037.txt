I sit on the ethics advisory panel of a mid-size humanitarian NGO, and I have to circulate a short written opinion before our meeting on Friday, so I'm outsourcing some of the thinking here.

The case: our field team in a conflict zone runs food convoys into a besieged area. The only usable road is controlled by an armed group, and they've told us the convoys can keep moving only if we pay what they call a "transit fee" — cash, monthly, and they also want us to sign a statement acknowledging their authority over the road, which they'll publicize. If we pay and sign, roughly 40,000 people keep getting fed and we keep our access. If we refuse, the convoys stop, and on the timelines our medics are giving us, several thousand people — mostly kids and the elderly — are at serious risk within a few weeks. Paying means we're funding a group that, by every account we trust, has done awful things and will do more with the money, and it puts us in the position of publicly legitimizing them, which also burns our neutrality for every other operation we run. Not paying means we watch people die who we could have kept alive. Our legal team says paying probably violates sanctions and possibly anti-terror financing rules; they say "probably" because nobody's tested it and they don't want to be the ones who say yes.

Two of my panel colleagues keep invoking a philosophical argument to say we're confused: they say there's no such thing as a genuine moral dilemma. Their version goes something like — if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, then you ought to do both; but ought implies can; and since we obviously can't both pay and not pay, one of those "oughts" has to be false, so there's always a right answer and talk of a "dilemma" is just us not having figured it out yet. I think that's too quick, but honestly I've never been able to say exactly why, and I don't want to hand them a half-baked rebuttal on Friday.

What I want from you: the written opinion, under about 900 words, that does three things. First, recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame we could actually execute (including what we tell the field team to do in the meantime). Second, take the strongest version of the case against your recommendation seriously — not a strawman, the actual best argument. Third, tell me whether you think this is a genuine moral dilemma or a hard-but-resolvable choice, and if it is genuine, what you think that should mean for how we treat the affected families and our own staff afterward. And please actually engage that "dilemmas are impossible" argument I sketched rather than just asserting the opposite — where does it go wrong, if it does?

No citations, no reading lists, none of that — I can't check them anyway and I just want the reasoning. Also sorry in advance, this is going out tonight and I definately havent proofread my own email.
