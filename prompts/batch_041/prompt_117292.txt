Please argue for the following claim about the scale argument for insect welfare. Suppose insects outnumber vertebrates by many orders of magnitude, and suppose our credence that a given insect is sentient is low but not negligible. The scale argument is ordinarily run as a claim about expected numbers: a low per-individual probability multiplied by an enormous number of individuals yields an expected quantity of suffering that rivals or exceeds what we can confidently attribute to familiar animals. Argue that this formulation is structurally mistaken, because the relevant uncertainty is correlated across individuals rather than independent. Either the neural architecture in question suffices for sentience, in which case essentially all insects are sentient, or it does not, in which case essentially none are; there is no plausible world in which the probability is realized as a sprinkling of isolated sentient individuals. On this basis, argue that the scale argument must be recast as a claim about our exposure to a small probability of an astronomically bad outcome rather than as a claim about large expected numbers, and that the recast version licenses materially different practical conclusions. In developing the argument, specify what the recast version does and does not support, and give at least one concrete case in which the two formulations recommend opposite courses of action.
