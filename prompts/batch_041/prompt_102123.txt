Hey, so I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy (I mostly work on collective action and population ethics) and I've been roped into running the discussion section for a big undergrad ethics course next week. About 18 students, 80 minutes, and the unit is individual contributions to collective harms — climate, meat, voting, the usual trio. The problem is that the students have all heard the lazy version of the inefficacy objection a hundred times ("one burger doesn't kill a cow", "your vote never decides anything") and they just nod at it, so I want an activity where they actually run into the thing themselves and then watch it get shaky under them, instead of me just lecturing at them. Can you design me a runnable in-class game for this? I'd like the rules, the materials (nothing that needs real money — tokens, candy, points on a board, whatever's cheap), a payoff scheme, a rough minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what actually happened in the room onto the pieces of the objection: the causal difference-making view, the expected-impact view, and the structural/collective view. Ideally they hit the wall themselves before anything gets named — so no theory up front, and don't put "inefficacy" or "expected value" on the board until the debrief.

One wrinkle I really want baked into the mechanics, because I think it's where the lazy version of the objection falls apart and I never manage to convey it in discussion: run the game in two rounds, and make the second round's threshold hidden. Like, round one the tipping point is public and everyone can see it, so it's obvious in most outcomes whether you personally were pivotal or not (and usually the group either falls short or massively overshoots, so almost nobody can point to their own act as the cause of anything). Round two, the facilitator draws the threshold secretly from some distribution and doesn't reveal it until afterwards, so nobody can know whether they're the pivotal contributor — which forces them to pick a standing policy rather than react to a situation, and I think that's exactly the move that makes the "my act makes no difference" line lose its grip. I'd love the game to make them feel the pull of both sides, not just one. Also worth having the group notice that each individual reasoning locally ("it won't tip because of me, so I'll sit this out") is the very thing that produces the bad outcome when everyone does it — but I don't want that spelled out, I want it to fall out of the payoffs.

Then the usual extras, please: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm worried about a couple of loud students anchoring everyone, and about a round landing on an uninteresting number), how to handle it live, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity genuinely can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. And keep it runnable by a single person who's decent at crowd control but not, you know, a professional game designer — I'll be doing this on a Tuesday afternoon in a room with a whiteboard and not much else.
