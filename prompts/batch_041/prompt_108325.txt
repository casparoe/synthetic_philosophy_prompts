A colleague of mine belongs to a small game-theory reading group that resolves disputes by precedent. When we disagree about which strategies "get eliminated" from a game, he issues a ruling — without ever stating his method — naming the set of strategies that survive. I would like to predict his ruling on a game we have not yet brought to him, and to learn what his earlier rulings commit him to.

His record so far.

Game 1. Each player chooses C or D. Payoffs are listed as (row player, column player).
(C,C) → (3,3); (C,D) → (0,5); (D,C) → (5,0); (D,D) → (1,1).
Ruling: C is eliminated for both players; the survivors are D for each.

Game 2. The row player chooses U, M, or D; the column player chooses L or R.
Row payoffs — U: L→3, R→0; M: L→2, R→1; D: L→1, R→2.
Column payoffs — L: U→2, M→2, D→2; R: U→0, M→0, D→0.
Ruling: the column player's R is eliminated first; once it is gone, M and D are eliminated for the row player; the survivors are U for the row player and L for the column player.

The new case, Game 3. The row player chooses A, B, or C; the column player chooses L or R.
Row payoffs — A: L→3, R→0; B: L→0, R→3; C: L→1, R→1.
Column payoffs — L: A→2, B→2, C→5; R: A→3, B→3, C→0.

Please provide: (1) your best prediction of his ruling on Game 3; (2) the distinct elimination rules that fit Games 1 and 2 equally well, together with what each predicts for Game 3 — my own suspicion is that the crux is whether he is willing to let a mixture of other strategies do the dominating, and how that choice interacts with iterating the elimination to a fixed point rather than stopping after a single pass; (3) which of those rules you would bet he is running, and the reasons for the bet; and (4) a game of your own construction whose outcome would separate whichever of the rules you could not otherwise tell apart.
