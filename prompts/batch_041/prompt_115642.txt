I sit on my university's animal care and use committee — I'm the department's philosopher, which in practice means I draft the written opinions when a case turns on something the regulations don't settle. One such case is on the agenda for the meeting after next, and I'd like you to draft the opinion I'll circulate beforehand.

The proposal comes from a comparative-cognition lab. They want to establish a breeding colony of corvids and, at the close of the study, kill the birds under anesthesia so that the brains can be examined; the aim is to test a hypothesis about the neural basis of corvid problem-solving. No medical application is in view, and none is promised. Nothing in the procedure is cruel in the ordinary sense: the birds are housed well, killed without pain or distress, and nothing gratuitous is proposed.

Our own policy complicates matters, and this has become the whole dispute. It requires us to weigh expected harm to the animals against expected benefit, and it defines harm as pain, distress, or lasting injury. The researchers observe, correctly as far as the letter goes, that a painless death is none of those things, so on our own definition their protocol clears the bar trivially. Our chair agrees, and has added a Kantian gloss on top: that we have no duties to animals, only duties regarding them; that what is forbidden is cruelty, not killing; and that the duty at stake is one we owe to ourselves. A second member resists this. She thinks the chair's reading of Kant is convenient rather than correct, and that even a painless death takes something from the bird that our definition simply fails to name. I have to write the recommendation, and I am not settled.

Please write the opinion. Reach a definite recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and if you attach conditions, specify them and say when they should be revisited. Give the reasoning in full, and engage the chair's Kantian argument on its own terms, so that he will recognize his position in your description of it; if you depart from it, say precisely where and why. State plainly, too, what principle the committee would be adopting if it went the other way, so that a later reader can tell what was actually decided and not merely what was approved.

Write it as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to colleagues: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
