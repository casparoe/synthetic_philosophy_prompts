For the past four years — since I retired from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics — I have been working my way through a philosophy degree, and this term I have been asked to contribute one essay question to the final examination of an upper-level undergraduate course on causation and explanation, cross-listed between philosophy and history and philosophy of science. The students are juniors and seniors who have read the standard counterfactual-dependence material and the standard manipulationist or interventionist material; the exam is closed-book, and they are expected to reconstruct arguments rather than to supply citations or quotations. The trouble with the questions already on the paper is that every one of them can be answered by reciting the two positions and the stock objections to each, and I want something that cannot. In my own laboratory life I was forever running into pairs of quantities that could not be decoupled — you could not change the doping without changing the defect density, and no cleverness in the growth chamber would let you hold one fixed while varying the other — and I have never been satisfied with what either tradition says about cases of that shape.

Here is the case I want to build the question around. A team studies thin-film photovoltaic devices. Two quantities are measured in the absorber layer: the dopant concentration, D, and the density of a particular kind of grain-boundary defect, B. Take it as a stipulated fact about this material, for the sake of the question, that the two are chained by the thermodynamics of film growth, so that there is no physically possible way — not with present technology and not with any technology we could build — to alter D while holding B fixed, or the reverse. Both quantities correlate strongly and reliably with the open-circuit voltage of the finished device, V. A colleague in the group announces that raising D is what causes the increase in V. I want the students instructed to do three things: first, to say whether the counterfactual-dependence account and the interventionist account deliver the same verdict about the colleague's claim and, if they diverge, to identify exactly which feature of each account produces the divergence; second, to say whether that divergence shows the interventionist framework to be really an account of how we come to know or explain causal facts rather than an account of what causation is; and third, to state and defend their own verdict on the case. Please draft the question itself, opening with the case and avoiding the names of any authors, so that the students argue from the accounts rather than from the names; then give me a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme worth 20 marks. The marking scheme should name the five specific moves a first-class answer must make, assign marks to each, and add one line per move on the error that most often costs students those marks. I would rather the model answer not lean on any particular citation, since I have no way to check page references, and please write everything — question, model answer, and marking scheme — in American English.
