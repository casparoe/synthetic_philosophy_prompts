I'm working through Moore's Principia Ethica, ch. VI, and I keep bouncing off this bit:

"The title of this chapter is ambiguous. When we call a state of things ideal we may mean three distinct things... By this is meant the best state of things conceivable, the Summum Bonum or Absolute Good."

Then a few pages later: "the intrinsic value of a whole is neither identical with nor proportional to the sum of the values of its parts... it follows that the best whole may be one, which contains none of the positive goods with which we are acquainted."

What I dont quite see is how this fits with what Moore goes on to say about beauty and friendship — that the greatest goods we know are the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. If the principle of organic unities says a whole can be better than the sum of its parts, and if the best whole might contain none of the goods we know, then why is Moore so confident that friendship and beauty top the list? Is he only saying these are the best known goods, not the best simpliciter? And does the organic-unities point mean the value of a friendship isn't just the sum of the value of the friends' beautiful qualities plus whatever pleasure each takes in the other? I'd like the argumentative move spelled out, especially what the third sense of "ideal" is doing and how it relates to the first two. No citations needed, I have the text — just the logic.
