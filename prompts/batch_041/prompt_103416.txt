ok so for my great books of asia elective i have to turn in a creative response and ive decided to write a dialogue, but i want to do it on the one thing we basically skipped and that i cant stop thinking about. we spent like half a class on the School of Names (the Mingjia, the warring states "sophists") and one text in particular, Gongsun Long's white horse thing, where two people argue about whether a white horse is not a horse. the two moves i actually remember are: if someone asks you for a horse, a yellow or black horse will do, but if someone asks you for a white horse, a yellow or black one wont do, so a white horse isnt the same as a horse; and that "horse" picks out a shape while "white" picks out a color, and what picks out a color isnt what picks out a shape. and then theres a third bit i really dont get, about a stone: you feel it and you get hardness but not whiteness, you look at it and you get whiteness but not hardness, so hardness and whiteness never actually show up together, and he thinks that proves theyre genuinely separate. i want a dialogue where Gongsun Long has to defend this against someone whos actually a worthy opponent, not some guy going "lol word games."

make the opponent a Confucian official — somebody who cares about names lining up with reality because he thinks if names get slippery then duties and laws get slippery too (i think thats the zhengming or "rectifying names" idea). dont let him just dismiss it: give him real objections, like that we all use these words perfectly fine every day and thats what they mean, or that the argument proves way too much (fine, white horse isnt horse — so whats next, brown cow isnt cow, and now language is broken?), or the practical one, that you can obviously ride a white horse into battle and a not-horse cant carry you anywhere. and Gongsun Long should have actual replies to those, not just restate his thesis louder. neither of them should be dumb.

write it as exactly five exchanges, five back-and-forths, each a few sentences, and number them. no intro before the first one and no summing-up at the end, and dont resolve it — i dont want a tidy moral where someone wins. set it somewhere concrete if it helps, like a courtyard or a ruler's court. someone whos read the actual text should recognize the moves, but someone whos never heard of it should still be able to follow the whole thing.
