I'm a grad student in a cognitive science program who ended up cross-registered in a philosophy of science seminar, and I keep running into something I don't have a word for and that nobody in the seminar treats as one phenomenon. Here is the pattern. When someone lays out a case to make a point — a sealed lab with a demon sorting molecules, a person who wakes up with another person's memories intact, a jury asked what a reasonable bystander would have done — the case is never described all the way down. It's specified just far enough to make one outcome feel obviously correct, and everything else is left blank, and the audience silently fills the blanks with whatever they already believe. The trick is what happens next: if you ask what exactly fills the blanks, the person who was persuaded either says "that part is just obvious" or supplies some further claim that quietly does all the work the case was supposed to be doing. And when somebody actually tries to spell the case out completely — to say how the demon knows which molecules are fast, or what the amnesiac's dispositions are, or which facts the bystander had — the intuition either turns contentious or flips outright, and the whole exercise starts to look like a very elaborate way of restating the conclusion it was meant to establish.

What bothers me is that these cases are advertised as producing knowledge we didn't have before, and I can see the same move in physics, in ethics, and in law. But if the knowledge was smuggled in through the parts of the case that were never stated, then nothing new was learned — the case just rearranged what the audience was already carrying and made them feel it as a discovery. So I'm asking whether this specific thing has a name. I don't mean cases of this kind in general, and I don't want a list of famous ones. I want to know whether the dependence of a case's verdict on unstated background knowledge that the audience supplies has a recognized name and a place in some literature, and if it doesn't, I'd like you to name it and define it. I also want the boundaries drawn, because I suspect it's not the same as the ordinary logical point that an argument can have hidden premises, nor the psychological point that different people have different intuitions, nor the trivial point that any hypothetical leaves something unspecified. Tell me which nearby ideas it is closest to, whether the described thing is one of them or something distinct, where it stops applying, and what a couple of borderline cases would look like. I tried a few search terms and everything I found was either too broad to be useful or about something else. Please answer in American English.
