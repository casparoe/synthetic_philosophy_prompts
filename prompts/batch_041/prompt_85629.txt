You are an expert in formal epistemology, with a particular feel for the logic of belief and a talent for constructing minimal cases that isolate a single structural feature. I'd like your help designing a case for a teaching unit I'm assembling on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I'd like you to think carefully before you start writing.

Here is the problem I want the case to expose. The two paradoxes are usually defused by two different moves, and I suspect each move is local, working only in the case it was built for. The first move, the one usually offered for the preface, is that the errors in the individual claims do not aggregate: no single act of belief takes on the whole list at once, so an author may believe every claim and still be confident that the book contains a mistake, without ever being required to believe the conjunction. The second move, the one usually offered for the lottery, is that you simply do not believe your ticket will lose, because your evidence does not discriminate among the tickets; nothing singles yours out.

What I want from you is a single, realistic scenario in which one agent is under pressure from both paradoxes at once and neither move can rescue her. Please build that case, and then show me that it does what I claim. I have four constraints in mind, and I want you to check the case against each of them explicitly. First, the agent must be in a position to know, with certainty, that at least one of a large set of her beliefs is false, while being unable to tell which one. That is the lottery's bite, and it must not depend on any numbers chosen just to make the arithmetic come out. Second, unlike a fair lottery, each of her beliefs must be supported by evidence specific to it, so that her confidence in any one of them would survive being challenged on its own; she cannot wave any of them away as mere symmetry. Third, her practical situation must be such that she relies on all of the beliefs together in a single, indivisible commitment, so that she cannot discharge the tension by declining to believe the conjunction; the tension has to show up in what she has to do, not merely in what she has to say. Fourth, the setting must be ordinary, of the kind a reader could imagine happening to someone they know: no demons, no infinite agents, no exotic devices.

Once you have the case, tell me what each of three positions must say about it, and why. The threshold view holds that belief is simply credence above some high mark. The closure view holds that rational belief must be consistent and closed under obvious entailment. The fallibilist view holds that we are never required to believe the conjunction of a long list, and that the appearance of trouble is an artefact of over-intellectualising belief. Which of these does the case make uncomfortable, and in what way? Then tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the work; I have my own suspicion, but I want your judgement. Say what happens if I remove that feature: does the case collapse back into a plain lottery, into a plain preface, or into neither? Finally, put yourself in the shoes of someone who holds the position your case treats worst, and tell me how she would attack the case and whether the attack succeeds. If, as you work, you find that the four constraints cannot all be met together, say so plainly and tell me which one has to give, rather than quietly dropping one.

I don't want any citations, and I don't want a recitation of the literature; I want the case built from scratch, and I would rather you spend your words on the construction and the verification than on background. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would in a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
