For the past term a small reading group I belong to has been working slowly through J. L. Austin's lectures on speech acts — the series from the mid-1950s that was published as How to Do Things with Words — and we have ended up fixated on a remark he makes almost in passing, to the effect that a performative uttered by an actor on a stage, or introduced into a poem, or spoken aloud in soliloquy, is hollow or void in a peculiar way: not a genuine promise, bet, or bequest at all, but a derivative, second-hand use of the words, which he sets aside before turning to the serious cases. Half the group (the lawyers, mostly) think this is just a tidy piece of bookkeeping; the other half (the poets) think it quietly ruins everything, since it means the words only work when they are not being watched. We would like to close the year by writing something rather than rehearsing the argument again, and because I am the one who raised the objection I have been asked to commission the piece. So: please write me a villanelle that is itself an attempted promise.

Formally, I want a strict villanelle — five tercets and a closing quatrain, the two refrains alternating and then coming together in the last two lines — with the refrains doing the conceptual work. Let one refrain be the promise itself, in the first person and present tense, phrased as the sort of formula that is supposed to be the act rather than a report of it; let the other refrain be a line that merely describes a promise being made, or states some condition that has to hold for promising to work at all. Because the refrains are fixed and keep returning, the same words will have to be doing the act in some turns and describing it in others, and I would rather the poem let that happen than explain it. Across the tercets, stage at least three of the ways such an act can come apart — no accepted procedure for what is being attempted, the wrong person or the wrong circumstances, the formula botched or broken off halfway, the words sincere on their face but not meant, the promise kept in word and ignored in everything afterwards — as concrete scenes with actual people in them, and do it without the technical vocabulary: no felicity, no illocutionary, no uptake, no misfire as bare terms. A reader who knows the theory should be able to say which failure each tercet has staged; a reader who doesn't should simply find the poem true. Somewhere in the middle the poem should turn on the fact that it is a poem, and that by Austin's own account this may be exactly what disqualifies it, and it should not settle that question. When the two refrains meet in the final two lines I want them adjacent rather than fused — the doing and the describing side by side, leaving the reader unable to say for certain whether a promise was made.

Keep the promise itself small and specific, the sort of thing that could be kept or broken by Tuesday, since large vows drag the whole thing toward sermon; and keep the addressee a particular person who is present and could answer, because a promise with nobody to take it up is one of the failures you are already staging. It should be a poem first and an illustration second: accurate enough that a philosopher of language would not wince, but not a lecture, not a riddle, and not a joke about poetry being hollow. If you can manage it in one line, let the title be a phrase that could be read either way. After the poem, add three or four sentences of plain prose for the group, saying which condition you staged where and why you left the two refrains unreconciled — but keep that note outside the poem.
