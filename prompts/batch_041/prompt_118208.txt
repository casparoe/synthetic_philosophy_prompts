I host a small interview series whose audience is mostly the commentariat of a few rationalist blogs, and I've followed the self-reference-in-agents literature from those blogs for years. My next guest works on the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. His heterodox line is that the obstacle is an artefact of a demand we never had to make — that an agent prove the soundness of its own proofs inside its own logic. His alternative, which he calls reflective closure, treats self-trust as a fixed point rather than a theorem: the agent's trust predicate is posited as a fixed point of a reflection operator, and it comes out partial, so some of the agent's conclusions fall inside its self-trust and some do not. He claims this dissolves the obstacle rather than dodging it, and that the familiar no-self-trust results only bite agents that insist on proving their own soundness. Treat him as a stand-in for the position rather than any real person, so you needn't worry about attributing views to anyone.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond letting him restate this, and lay the response out in these sections, in this order:

- "Framing" (about 80 words, one paragraph): a neutral setup I can read aloud, stating his position without stacking the deck.
- "Ground clearing" (two questions, about 60 words): what exactly he is denying, in terms a listener who knows Löb's theorem but has not read his work can follow.
- "The fixed point" (three questions, about 200 words, each with a one-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give): press on how a posited fixed point differs from simply adding a reflection schema as new axioms, and in particular on what happens when the agent asks whether *that* theory is trustworthy — my worry being that the problem just moves up a level; on which sentences land inside the trust predicate and what rule decides membership without the rule being ad hoc; and on whether the greatest fixed point is non-empty and closed under the agent's own reasoning about it.
- "The dogmatist's shadow" (two questions with follow-ups, about 120 words): if the trust is not proved, what separates his agent from one that simply assumes it is correct — and can he name a decision problem, say a one-shot exchange with an adversary who has read the agent's source code, where the two come apart?
- "Where the deflection stops" (two questions, about 100 words): he has a habit of redescribing objections as cases where the reflection operator has not converged yet, or as problems that only afflict proof-based agents. Ask these so that the move is unavailable — one question forcing a behavioural prediction that would come out differently if he were wrong, and one about what he would have to see to give up the fixed-point framing.
- "The closer" (one question, about 30 words): something on whether, if he is right, the obstacle was never really about self-reference at all.

No questions about how he came to the problem or what he is working on next.
