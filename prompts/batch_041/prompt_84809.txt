Alright, I want an essay on Buridan's ass — the donkey starving between two identical piles of hay — and I want it to actually land somewhere, not do the standard "this is a fake paradox, obviously you just pick one" routine and not the opposite routine either. Here's my angle, tell me if it holds up. The story as usually told seems to cheat, because starving is itself an outcome and the ass presumably ranks either pile above dying, so the two piles aren't the only things in the preference order. The symmetry is only between the two means of eating, not over the whole field of outcomes. So the death can't be explained by the tie at all; it has to come from somewhere else, and I only see two candidates. Either the ass has a defective decision procedure — an inability to act on a tie, which makes it a story about a broken donkey rather than about rationality — or the two piles aren't tied but incomparable, neither better nor worse nor equal, "on a par" in the sense people argue about in the value-incommensurability literature, in which case "just pick one, they're equally good" isn't licensed and the paralysis argument starts to have teeth.

I think the interesting question is whether the tie-breaking-policy move rescues the first version. An agent can be indifferent between A and B while having a perfectly determinate preference between deliberating forever and having a standing policy of resolving ties arbitrarily, so if attitudes toward your own decision procedure can generate reasons, then nobody with any second-order structure is ever genuinely preference-free, and the ass only works on an agent with no self-directed attitudes whatsoever. Is that a real source of reasons, or just bootstrapping a reason out of a commitment device? That's the bit I most want adjudicated.

So: reach a verdict. Does choice without preference show anything bad about rationality, and if so, in which version of the case? If it dissolves, which of the two versions dissolves, and why doesn't the other one dissolve with it? Feel free to say one version is just confused. It's fine to bring in historical takes if they help — Leibniz's line that there are always tiny unconscious perceptions tipping the scales seems relevant to the first version, and al-Ghazali thought something in the agent must break the symmetry between two identical dates — but don't let it turn into a history of the problem.

And please seperate what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. I don't want a majority line presented as consensus if it isn't one, and I don't want a minority view about parity passed off as if everyone accepts it.
