A friend of mine — a functional morphologist by training, and the sort of person who will give you a flat yes or no rather than a hedge — has become our group's unofficial oracle for the question whether some trait has a function, and I have been keeping a running tally of her verdicts because I increasingly suspect the tally is doing more work than any of us admits. The list so far: the vertebrate heart, pumping blood, function; the lens of the eye, focusing light, function; an enzyme catalysing its step in a metabolic pathway, function; the white winter coat of an arctic mammal read as camouflage, function; the elaborate tail of a male bird read as mate attraction, function; a trait that turns up in a cave population, appears to do nothing at all, and shows no sign of ever having been favoured, no function; and a structural byproduct — a ridge or a gap that falls out of the geometry of neighbouring parts and does no work of its own — no function. What nags at me is that every one of those six is a case where two quite different things happen to coincide: the trait has a history of being favoured for the effect in question, and the effect is something the trait currently contributes to the system it belongs to. So the six verdicts do not yet separate the family that ties function to selection history from the family that ties it to a trait's present contribution — the etiological or selected-effects family on the one side, the causal-role or systems family on the other — because on all six they agree.

Next week we are arguing about a case that I think pulls them apart, and what I want from you is a prediction rather than a survey of the literature. Take a long-running laboratory evolution experiment — many thousands of generations, the usual frozen archive of ancestors — in which a lineage has lately evolved a modified appendage that measurably improves its swimming efficiency, in the sense that the appendage now does hydrodynamic work the ancestral form did not; the population's geneticists, however, can find no evidence that the modification was ever favoured, and the best current story is that it rode to high frequency as a hitchhiker on a beneficial mutation nearby, and may even be mildly costly on its own account. My friend will be asked, flatly, whether that appendage has a function.

Please tell me: what she will say; which candidate rules fit the six earlier verdicts — I can see at least a selection-history rule, a present-contribution-to-a-capacity rule, and a present-fitness-contribution rule, and I suspect I am missing one — and what each predicts about the new case; your best guess at which rule she is actually running, with the reasons; and then the part I genuinely care about, namely the further case that would discriminate among whichever rules survive the new one, together with an explanation of why none of the six verdicts we already have could have done that job.
