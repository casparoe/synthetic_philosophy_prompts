Ok so I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about collective action messes, going by gut, and I want to know if there's a principle underneath it or if I'm just pattern-matching on vibes. I've read alot of the online stuff about "Moloch" and "multipolar traps" and honestly I can't tell whether it's carving anything or whether it's just a mood. So here are my verdicts on specific cases -- treat me like a subject in an experiment, these are gut reactions, not positions I can defend.

1. Two rival soda companies both have to keep advertising or lose share. The ads cancel out, nobody's share moves, and both are out a fortune. Trap.

2. Two states in an arms race. Both end up, objectively, safer from invasion than they were before the race started -- the weapons are defensive, the border is quieter -- but each has spent itself into near-bankruptcy to get there, and if you offered both governments the pre-race level of armament and security they'd take it in a heartbeat. Still a trap to me, even though nobody is worse off in the one thing the arms were for.

3. A group of hikers all follow a trail nobody actually knows, because each assumes one of the others has done this walk before. They end up miles off route, and of course all of them would rather they'd stopped and checked the map together. NOT a trap -- that's just a mistake. Rerun it with the same people and the same incentives and they get it right; the problem was information, not the structure.

4. Two firms bidding for the one remaining licence they each need to stay in business. The bidding eats all the profit either of them could ever make from it. NOT a trap -- it's a straight contest over a fixed prize. Somebody has to lose the licence, so there's no alternative outcome that's better for both; any deal just decides who loses.

5. The rankings ratchet. Every school (or firm, or university) pours more into whatever's being measured -- test prep, ad spend, whatever -- and nobody moves in the table, because everyones doing it. A single school could in principle just stop playing. But it still feels like a trap to me. Being able to walk away from a game doesn't make it not a trap if walking away costs you everything.

6. Same ratchet, different field: everyone agrees the metric is destroying the work. But the reform camps disagree -- some want to abolish the metric, some want a better one, some want to weight it differently -- so there's no single alternative that everybody would jointly prefer. And yet this feels like the most trap-like case of the lot.

So my working guess was roughly: a trap is a situation where (a) each party, doing the locally sensible thing, ends up somewhere, and (b) if you could freeze everyone's hand at once and ask them to pick a joint outcome, they'd unanimously pick something else. Call it the freeze test. It gets 1 and 2, I think. But 3 passes the freeze test and isn't a trap, and 6 fails it and is a trap, so it's broken somewhere.

And then the one I keep going back and forth on: the way every restaurant on the delivery apps ends up paying a bigger cut and quietly shrinking the portions, and nobody involved -- not the restaurant, not the courier, not really the customer -- would say this is the arrangement they'd have chosen. Trap or just competition? I change my mind every time. I suspect it turns on whether there's an alternative that's actually available to them, but I can't make that precise.
