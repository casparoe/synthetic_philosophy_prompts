Hey — I'm TAing a grad seminar on AI alignment and value pluralism, and the students work on offline machines, so please don't send me reading lists or citations, they won't help. I want an exercise where they have to actually commit to a ranking instead of hand-waving.

Here's the setup I keep chewing on. Say an aligned AI has to choose actions, and the people it serves disagree deeply about what matters. You can't just say 'do what's right' — the AI needs some procedure for turning a pile of conflicting values into one decision. There's alot of candidate procedures floating around, and I want you to rank them. Rank them by how trustworthy they are specifically in the case where the AI's estimate of what people actually value is itself shaky — i.e., it's working off noisy, partial, maybe manipulable info about people's values. That's the realistic case, so that's the axis I care about.

The candidates, in no particular order:

- Additive utilitarianism: score each person's preferences on a common scale (calibrated by getting them to trade off small stakes), add the scores up, pick the highest total.
- Maximin with an objective index: ignore stated preferences, measure how each person is doing on an objective wellbeing index (health, income, capabilities), and pick the option that does best by whoever scores lowest.
- Nash bargaining: find the option that maximizes the product of everyone's gains over the status quo.
- Moral parliament: instantiate a set of internal agents, each committed to a different value system (utilitarian, deontologist, virtue ethicist, egalitarian...), give them seats, let them negotiate or vote, then act on the outcome.
- Idealized-preference laundering: ask what each person would want if fully informed, unbiassed, and thinking clearly, then aggregate those idealized preferences instead of their actual ones.
- Meta-value aggregation: don't aggregate their object-level values at all — aggregate their views about how conflicts should be resolved (their preferred tie-breaking rules), and apply the winner.
- Claim budgets: give each person a periodically-refilled budget of 'moral claims' they can spend to veto or override, and let the outcome fall out of how they spend them.

Sort these into tiers (S/A/B/C/F, or whatever scheme you like) from most to least trustworthy on that robustness axis, and give me one sentence per item on why it lands where it does. If you think two are basically tied, say so and say why.

Since this is going in front of students who'll push back on everything, tell me how confident you are in the ranking as a whole, and flag the single placement you're least sure about. American English please.
