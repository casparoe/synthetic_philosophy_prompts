I am in the final third of a novel and have written myself into a corner I would rather not resolve by authorial fiat. Two of my characters — Ines, a myrmecologist who has spent eleven seasons watching a single colony of an invented leafcutter-like species on an invented island, and her brother Rafe, who works on theories of cognition and is visiting her field station — will not stop arguing about that colony, and I want to write the argument out properly before I decide which of them the book believes.

What I want from you is the dialogue itself, of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, followed by a brief structured summary in JSON, as set out below.

Some ground rules, because I have read enough versions of this argument in other people's books to know exactly how it usually goes and I want none of it.

1. Neither character may cite, name or allude to any real scientist, philosopher, book or paper. The whole exchange must be carried by the fictional case, by reasoning, and by ordinary examples the characters could reach for themselves. No reading lists, no attributions, no appeals to who has said what.

2. Neither may win by scepticism. Rafe may not say that we can never know what it is like to be a colony; Ines may not say that the question is merely verbal and thereby dissolve it. Each must answer the other's particular examples.

3. No free analogies. Neither may simply assert that the colony is a brain and the ants are its neurons, or that it is a market, or a computer, or a democracy. If either reaches for a comparison, the other gets to attack it and the first must defend it.

4. Both characters are competent. Ines is not a mystic and Rafe is not a bore who denies that anything smaller than a person thinks. Over the exchange, at least twice, one of them must concede a real point and adjust their position rather than restate it.

5. Each speaker gets at least three substantive turns, and the turns should shorten and sharpen as the argument proceeds, the way real arguments do, rather than lengthening into speeches.

The facts of the case are fixed. I established them in earlier chapters and they may not be altered to suit the argument.

- Individual ants live a few weeks. The colony's architecture and its foraging routes persist for years across something like ninety per cent turnover of individuals. No ant has ever traversed a complete route, and the load-bearing arches of the nest are never built by any ant that has surveyed the finished arch.
- The colony reliably takes the shorter of two routes between nest and food, and re-routes within minutes when a route is blocked.
- A chemical I call blight-salt, applied to a single trail junction, with no effect on any ant's senses or legs, makes the colony take the longer route. It does not correct the error while the chemical is present, and corrects within a minute of the junction being rinsed. Ines calls that junction a memory. Rafe says it shows there is nothing there that could remember.
- A parasitic wasp lays in the colony. Infected ants behave in ways that serve the wasp, and the colony's whole foraging pattern shifts accordingly. Neither character can say cleanly whether the shift belongs to the colony's purposes or to the wasp's.
- If the single trail joining two halves of the nest is cut, each half carries on as a competent colony; when the trail is restored, they reconcile. Neither may make anything of the resemblance to the split human brain without paying for it.
- The queen dies in the penultimate chapter. Behaviour degrades over about six weeks; an unrelated replacement restores it.

Let neither have the last word in a way that settles the matter. I want the exchange to end either in a genuine standoff or in a narrower question than the one it began with.

After the dialogue, give me the following JSON object and nothing else beyond it, so that I can see where the argument actually turns before I write the next chapter. Use these exact field names:

- dialogue_title: a string.
- speakers: an array of two objects, each with name, position_in_one_sentence, and claim_they_never_abandon.
- concessions: an array of objects, each with speaker, point_conceded, and what_it_costs_them.
- agreements: an array of strings, each a point both characters ended up accepting.
- crux: an object with question, what_ines_would_need_to_show, and what_rafe_would_need_to_show.
- left_open: an array of strings, each a question the dialogue leaves unresolved and that I could use later in the book.

Please write in British English.
