I have no training in mathematics or philosophy — I read on my own, mostly in the evenings — and for some months now I have been turning over a puzzle I first met as an aside in a book about probability. I would like to see it made into a poem, and I have fairly definite ideas about the shape the poem should take.

The puzzle concerns a circle. Draw a chord across it — a straight line whose ends lie on the circumference. What is the chance that this chord is longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in the circle? Everything depends on what "at random" is taken to mean, and there are at least three readings, each of which seems to me entirely natural. Choose two points on the circumference at random and join them: the chance is one third. Choose a direction at random, then a distance from the centre at random along it, and draw the chord perpendicular to that direction at that distance: the chance is one half. Choose a point inside the circle at random and make it the chord's midpoint: the chance is one quarter. The circle has not changed, the question has not changed, and yet the answer will not hold still.

Someone who knows more than I do tells me the puzzle is only badly posed, and that the remedy is to require the answer not to change when the circle is turned, or made larger or smaller — that once the procedure is made to respect the circle's symmetries, one of the three readings is singled out and the other two fall away. I cannot tell whether that rescues the principle that equal ignorance deserves equal shares of belief, or whether it quietly concedes that the equal division was never doing any work and that the symmetry was doing all of it. And there is a further trouble I came across in the same connection: imagine a factory turning out cubes whose edges are somewhere between zero and two, with nothing favouring any size; the same even-handed reasoning says the chance that an edge is under one is one half — but the volumes of those cubes run from zero to eight, and nothing favours any volume either, and the same reasoning says the chance that a volume is under one is one eighth, although an edge is under one exactly when the volume is under one. The even-handedness seems to depend on which quantity I am being even-handed about.

What I want is a sestina: six-line stanzas, the same six end-words turning through the stanzas in the usual rotating order, and a three-line envoi at the end. I would like the six end-words to be circle, chord, centre, draw, longer, equal. The three ways of drawing the chord should each be present, each with its answer, and the three answers — a third, a half, a quarter — should meet in the envoi, one to a line, with the last line making clear that nothing in the poem has settled which of them is right. I would like the sixth stanza to leave the circle and go to the cubes, and it would please me if the fixed end-words felt strained there, since cubes have no chord and no centre and the same vocabulary will not stretch over them. Above all I do not want the poem to resolve anything or to close on a moral. What holds me is that the puzzle and the proposed remedy both seem right to me, and I would like the poem to hold both without choosing between them.

After the poem, please add a short note of four or five sentences, telling me which of the claims above I should treat as contested, whether the symmetry requirement really does single out one of the three readings and how confident you are of that, and which single point you are least sure of. No references or reading lists, please; I would rather have your judgment than a bibliography.
