I do the joke page for our dept newsletter — one page, every spring we slip a fake paper in among the real ones — and this year I want to write a help-centre FAQ, the SaaS support kind ("Why did my answer change?", "Is there a way to turn the creativity down?", "Why is my invoice still wrong?"), for a company selling an AI "analogy assistant" that's built on the old Copycat program from Hofstadter's group. The thing I want the jokes made out of is the actual machinery, so here it is in my words: the microdomain is letter strings, you get "abc → abd, so what's ijk → ?" and everyone's happy, but then you get something lumpy like mrrjjj (one m, two r's, three j's) and it all comes apart, because "the last thing" can be the last letter — advance it and you get mrrjjk — or it can be the last *run* of letters, in which case what advances is the group itself and you get mrrjjjj, and there's no fact of the matter about which reading is the right one. Copycat's whole point is that it doesn't choose: concepts in its network (successor, same, group, rightmost, length, leftmost) drift into one another, so "successor of a letter" can slide into "successor of a group size," little codelets compete to build and wreck structures in a workspace, and a temperature knob governs how much of that drift is allowed, which is why the program returns a whole spread of answers rather than one. The company in my piece has wrapped this up and shipped it, and they're now fielding complaints.

So: 6–8 Q&As plus a short "Known Issues" section, deadpan corporate voice, no winking, 600 words or so. The support agent should understand the system perfectly — the comedy should come from competence, not from an idiot on the phone — and the FAQ should never actually admit the deep thing out loud, it just keeps saying it in customer-success language: they sell "there's no fact of the matter" as a premium tier, they offer a Determinism Mode that quietly doesn't do anything, they blame the customer for asking the wrong question, and somewhere in there is the press release where they claimed to have cracked general reasoning because their demo got the abc one right. Don't give me generic tech-support gags where you could swap in a different product and nothing changes — every joke should need the letter mechanics to land, and someone who's never heard of any of this should still be laughing by the third answer. Also please don't cite or quote the actual papers or books, I'm not putting a reading list in a newsletter, I just want the real machinery used properly.
