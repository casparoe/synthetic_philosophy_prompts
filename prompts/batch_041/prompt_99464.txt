I am working through the Mytilene debate in Book 3 of Thucydides and keep stalling on a passage in Diodotus' speech. In Crawley's translation:

"It is probable that in early times the penalties for the greatest offences were less severe, and that, as these were disregarded, the penalty of death has been by degrees in most cases arrived at, which is itself disregarded in like manner. Either then some means of terror more terrible than this must be discovered, or it must be owned that this restraint is useless; and that as long as poverty gives men the courage of necessity, or plenty fills them with the ambition which belongs to insolence and pride, and the other conditions of life remain each under the thraldom of some fatal and master passion, so long will the impulse never be wanting to drive men into danger. Hope also and cupidity, the one leading and the other following, the one conceiving the attempt, the other suggesting the facility of succeeding, cause the widest ruin, and, although invisible agents, are far stronger than the dangers that are seen. Fortune, too, powerfully helps the delusion, and by the unexpected aid that she sometimes lends, tempts men to venture with inferior means; and this is especially the case with communities, because the stakes played for are the highest, freedom or empire, and, when all are acting together, each man irrationally magnifies his own capacity."

The standard summary is that Diodotus is the rational and merciful counterweight to Cleon and that he offers a case against capital punishment as a deterrent. I can follow that at the level of the summary, but the passage itself defeats me. Why are hope and cupidity "invisible agents," and in what sense does fortune "help the delusion"? Is the claim a universal law of human nature, or a description of what happens to cities in particular? And I cannot resolve what looks like a tension: if no penalty restrains men once they have set their minds on something, how does the argument support Diodotus' actual conclusion, which is not that the Mytilenians will be deterred but that Athens should spare the common people as useful and should not let anger guide it? Walk me through the argument sentence by sentence; say where the deterrence claim is doing real work and where it is a rhetorical lever; and tell me whether the fact that this was a second assembly, reconsidering its own earlier vote, bears on what the passage is claiming about deliberation. I am after the argument, not citations or further reading.
