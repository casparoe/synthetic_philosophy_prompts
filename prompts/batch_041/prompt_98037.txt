I have been working through Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness, and I would like to set out the position I take her to be defending, then ask what would count against it. As I reconstruct it: happiness is largely something we manufacture rather than find; it lies not in tranquillity but in the satisfaction of strong tastes and passions; the taste for study is the most dependable of these, because it depends least on other people and can fill the void left by the passions that fade with age — indeed, part of the art is to let tastes gradually replace passions as one grows older; we must free ourselves of prejudice and be virtuous, since a vicious person cannot be happy; we should not try to overturn the station fortune has assigned us, but make the best of it; and — the claim I find hardest to assess — we must remain susceptible to illusion, conserving the illusions that give us pleasure and driving out sad ideas such as our own death, on the ground that seeing everything clearly is incompatible with being happy.

I want a map of this position's exposure, not a summary of it. What would count against it? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be — from those that should make me abandon the view to those that would merely be awkward — and separate what a sympathetic defender would themselves concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding. Say which of its claims make predictions or commitments that rival accounts of happiness do not, so I can see what it actually risks. Tell me whether the view, once its parts are separated, is really one position or a bundle of a hedonic thesis, a set of prudential maxims, and a piece of moral psychology that could come apart, and whether any part has been qualified into unfalsifiability — in particular, whether the claim that the vicious cannot be happy is a substantive empirical prediction or a definitional move dressed up as one.

I would especially like you to press on three things. First, the tension with her own rationalism: this is a person whose central intellectual work was mathematical physics, who prized clear reasoning, and yet who tells us that happiness requires illusion and the deliberate banishment of sad truths. Can that combination be held, or does the taste for study — itself a truth-seeking passion — sit awkwardly with the recommendation to cultivate illusion? Second, whether the position is self-undermining: if happiness depends on illusion, does a theory that exposes happiness as depending on illusion threaten the very thing it recommends, and does the advice to pursue happiness by these means fall foul of the familiar thought that happiness pursued directly is the least likely to be caught? Third, whether the counsel to drive out sad ideas survives contact with what we know about how attempts to suppress a thought tend to work, and whether the empirical literature on the value of mildly self-flattering illusions, and on the costs of seeing oneself too clearly, tells for or against her. I would also like to know whether the move that restricts the whole program to those who already enjoy a settled fortune is a legitimate limitation or a way of quietly purchasing the conclusion, and whether contentment with one's station is a virtue here or a rationalization of injustice — especially given that she was writing as a woman who had run up against the constraints of her own society. Rank everything, and tell me honestly whether, once the load-bearing claims are isolated, the position is more exposed than its charm suggests.
