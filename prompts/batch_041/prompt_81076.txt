I'm a features writer at a magazine, at work on a profile of a decision scientist. As part of my reporting I've had her answer a series of hypothetical choice problems on the record, and I'd like your help anticipating the one I'm saving for the interview. Each time I put two options to her and she chose one.

First: a guaranteed $1,000,000, or a ticket paying $5,000,000 with probability 0.10, $1,000,000 with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. She took the guarantee.

Second: a ticket paying $1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise, or a ticket paying $5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise. She took the second ticket.

Third: a guaranteed $3,000, or a fair coin paying $10,000 on heads and nothing on tails. She took the guarantee.

Fourth: a guaranteed $2,000, or a ticket paying $5,000 with probability 0.6 and $2,500 with probability 0.4. She took the ticket.

A colleague of hers tells me that no single assignment of utilities to dollar amounts can reproduce all four answers, so she is not simply maximising expected utility. The fifth question, which I have not yet put to her, is this: a guaranteed $10,000, or a ticket paying $1,000,000 with probability 0.99 and $9,900 with probability 0.01.

I care less about her answer than about the rule she is running. Take her four answers at face value. Tell me the decision rule that best fits them; the rival rules that fit them just as well; what each of those predicts for the fifth question; which prediction you would bet on, and why; and which further pair of options would best separate the surviving rules. I don't need the literature or the citations — I need the reasoning itself.

Lead with your prediction for the fifth question, and keep the whole reply under 350 words.
