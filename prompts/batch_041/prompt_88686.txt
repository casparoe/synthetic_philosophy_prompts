I teach an upper-level seminar on decision theory and I'm putting together teh final. I want one long exam question on the St. Petersburg paradox, but the whole point is that it shouldn't be answerable by a student who has just memorized "Bernoulli fixed it with log utility." Here's the setup I want the question to use, so nobody needs to look anything up. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads comes on toss n, the player is paid £2^n. The puzzle is what a rational person should pay to play, given that the expected payout is infinite (2⁻¹·2 + 2⁻²·4 + 2⁻³·8 + ... = 1 + 1 + 1 + ...).

First twist: a student in office hours told me you can fix the whole thing just by capping the number of tosses at some N (if no heads come in N tosses, you win nothing). I want the question to force them to see that this doesn't help, that it just relocates the problem. I'm pretty sure the expected value of the capped game is just N, which grows without bound as N grows, but check me on that and don't let the model answer hand-wave it.

Second twist: a generalized version where the payout is a^n instead of 2^n and each toss lands heads with probability p instead of 1/2 (tosses independent). I want one part of the question to ask for the exact condition on a and p under which the expectation diverges, and I want the model answer to note that the original game sits right on the boundary of that condition rather than safely inside it.

The question should make them confront what the paradox is actually a problem for: expected value maximization itself, the notion of a fair price, or the assumption that rational preferences can be represented by an expected utility with a real-valued (and possibly unbounded) utility function. And it should push on whether the diminishing-marginal-utility move is a genuine solution or just a way of changing the subject, plus what it costs you elsewhere, e.g. what a bounded utility function commits you to.

Please give me: the question itself (multi-part is fine), a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points whose marks add to 20, plus a one-line note for each point on how students usually blow it. Write the whole thing in British English.
