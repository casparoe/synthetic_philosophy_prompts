I am running a session of an interdisciplinary reading group — advanced graduate students in law and political theory, none of them trained in early modern philosophy or theology — on Pascal's account of justice and force. They will have read, in translation, the cluster of fragments in which Pascal holds that justice without force is powerless and force without justice tyrannical, that our judgments of what is just shift with custom and with the crossing of a border, and that the powerful have succeeded in lending force the name of justice. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

The session has to reproduce the movement of the fragments rather than announce their conclusion: the audience should first feel the pull of natural-law confidence, then lose it, and then be shown that the loss leads somewhere other than cynicism. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what. Name the single case that should carry the session: it must be one in which a practice the audience initially takes to be straightforwardly just can be traced back to an imposition of force while still being experienced by its participants as natural or self-evident, and it must be one on which the audience cannot reach a confident moral verdict during the session, since a verdict available in the room will short-circuit the exercise. I would rather avoid the Melian dialogue, the ring of Gyges, and the standard obedience demonstrations; if one of them really is the best case available, explain why the closer-to-home alternatives fail, but I suspect the case needs to be mundane rather than dramatic.

Then give me the misconception map, keyed to points in the sequence. I expect 'this is Thrasymachus in French'; 'Pascal is a legal positivist'; 'Pascal denies that there is any true justice at all'; 'Pascal is instructing us to obey whoever holds the sword'; 'force and justice are simply two separate things, and Pascal is lamenting that they do not match'; and a subtler one, that Pascal has debunked justice rather than humbled our knowledge of it. I also expect the group to collapse Pascal's position into Hobbes's authorization story and into Hume's convention-based account of justice; mark the point at which that collapse is most likely and how to prevent it. Which of these should be headed off explicitly, and which should be allowed to surface and be corrected in the room?

Tell me what to leave out at this level — the Jansenist polemical context, the Augustinian background, the doctrine of the three orders, the relation to Montaigne — and how to flag the omissions without appearing evasive. Finish with one question to put to the group at the end that would show whether they have grasped the distinction between the just, the established, and the forcible, as opposed to merely acquired a new vocabulary for cynicism.
