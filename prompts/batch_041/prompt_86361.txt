I'm a features writer assembling a long read on the free-will wars, and the thing that keeps nagging at me is how much of the Dennett–Caruso exchange reads as two people talking past each other: on one side the compatibilist who insists that the reasons-responsive self-control we actually exercise survives whatever determinism turns out to be true, and that some wrongdoers therefore really do deserve blame and punishment; on the other the hard determinist who cheerfully grants everything about control but denies that any of it can ground basic desert, so that no one ever deserves to suffer for what they have done, and who would rebuild penal policy on a public-health footing. I don't want another transcript of them trading points; I want the document their collaboration should have produced, the one both of them could put their names to.

So give me, first, a numbered section of agreed claims: what determinism does and does not threaten; the distinction between holding someone responsible and believing they deserve to suffer; what each side is and is not denying when it uses the words "free will" and "desert"; and what both accept about the psychology of blame and about the manifest failures of existing punishment. Then a disagreements table, each residual dispute stated in wording its own side would accept, with a column marking whether the dispute is substantive or merely verbal — and if you judge that part of the row is a quarrel about words, name that part and say why. Above all I want the cruxes isolated. My suspicion is that one is what free will must involve for desert to attach (ultimate origination versus reasons-responsiveness), another is whether blame can be fitting even if nobody deserves to suffer, and a third is what we should predict would become of moral life in a society that abandoned retributive punishment. For each crux, hand me a case or a line of reasoning both sides would grant bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts about it.

To stop the two of them retreating to the usual examples, run one of those cruxes through a case neither has made much of: a man who commits a serious assault, and whose violent disposition can be eliminated outright by a cheap, safe neurointervention, after which he is no more likely to reoffend than anyone else — but is otherwise unchanged and entirely unrepentant. Say what each camp holds should now happen to him, and what that verdict reveals about where the real disagreement lies. Commit to specific formulations rather than spreading alternatives on the table for me to choose between: I would far rather have one wording you will actually defend, with your reasons for it, than three hedged ones, and where you genuinely cannot choose, tell me so and explain why.
