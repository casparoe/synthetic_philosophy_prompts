Two people in my Thursday night reading group got into it over the Wittgenstein chapter and I've been chewing on it since. We'd been going through the bit where he lines up board games, card games, ball games, the Olympics, and says stop looking for something they all share -- just look at the overlapping similarities, the way a family's features overlap without any one of them being in all of them. The word, on his telling, isn't pinned down by a definition at all. Jenny and Marcus took that in opposite directions and neither would give an inch. Roughly how it went:

(1) Jenny said that's the whole lesson, and not just for games: the word 'game' has no definition and can't have one, because any definition will either leave out something we all happily call a game or drag in something we don't.

(2) Marcus said that's way too strong. No single shared feature, fine -- but that doesn't mean there's nothing to say. You could give a definition as a disjunction, or in terms of the point of the activity. He said he's definately read a philosopher somewhere who defined a game as voluntarily taking on an obstacle you didn't have to take on, and that gets chess and solitaire and tag in while keeping work and war out, which is exactly what we want.

(3) Jenny went at the definition instead of defending her own line. Watch what it does, she said: a toddler shoving a ball around, two kids playing house -- those are games if anything is, and there's no obstacle anywhere in them. Marcus said he meant people, not cats, and Jenny said right, so 'person' just got added to the definition, and every counterexample is going to get patched like that.

(4) Marcus said the definition doesn't have to match what's going through your head when you use the word. It's a proposal about what the word is actually tracking, and the fact that we argue over hard cases and sometimes change our minds is the tell: if 'game' were just a family of likenesses, there'd be nothing to be right or wrong about, and nobody could ever talk anybody out of anything. Jenny said people get talked out of all kinds of things without there being a hidden essence -- the practice has a shape, and the shape does the persuading.

(5) Then came the move I still can't make up my mind about. She said two things don't just resemble each other, full stop -- they resemble each other in some respect. Name the respect and you've named a feature. So the family is just a big list of features, and Marcus's disjunction walks back in the door. Marcus said the list is open-ended and always growing, which is why nobody can write it down, and an unfinishable list isn't a definition. Jenny said fine, then say that, instead of saying there's no definition.

(6) Marcus turned it around on her. He said 'there is no definition of game' is itself a general claim about how concepts work, and it could turn out false -- she's using Wittgenstein as a licence to stop theorising while quietly asserting a theory of her own. Jenny said it's not a theory, it's an observation about how one word gets used, and she doesn't need it true of every word. Marcus said an observation about one word, with no method for checking it, is a theory in a costume. That's where we ran out of beer.

I was just there eating crisps and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what -- not who won overall, but which of the six moves hold up and which don't, and what each of them is missing? Don't just tell me Wittgenstein settles it either way; I've read the chapter, I want the moves weighed on their merits. And tell me how confident you are in the verdict and which single point in your answer you're least sure about.
