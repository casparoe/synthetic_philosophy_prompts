I have recently been appointed as the graduate representative on my faculty's criminal law reform group, and our first task has been to draft a response to a consultation on a proposed licensing scheme for "grief simulations" — commercial services that assemble a conversational replica of a deceased person from their messages, recordings and correspondence, and sell access to the bereaved. The scheme would make unlicensed provision a criminal offence, and the case for it does not rest on deception, coercion or distress to any living person: the families who use these services want them, the providers disclose exactly what the product is, and the deceased's executors consent on the estate's behalf. What is said in favour of prohibition is rather that the practice degrades something we hold in common — the way we treat the dead, the meaning of mourning, the thought that a person is not a data set. Our group has found that we cannot answer the consultation without taking a position on the Hart–Devlin debate, and I find that I cannot settle my own position on it. I would like your probability, properly decomposed, rather than a tour of the literature.

Please give me your credence that the following proposition is true, and show me its derivation. Proposition: there are acts that wrong no determinate individual — no one's interests are set back in any way the harm principle can recognise, no one is deceived or coerced — which a liberal state is nevertheless justified in criminalising, and the justification is that the acts violate a shared morality that the state may protect as such. If you think my formulation is unstable, restate it, but say what you have changed. I want the sub-questions your number depends on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification apiece, and how they combine. The ones I can already see: whether the harm principle is exhaustive of legitimate criminalisation or whether "harm" can be stretched to cover offence, dignitary injury and damage to valued collective practices, and whether stretching it that far leaves the principle with any content; how much of the legal-moralist case depends on the empirical claim that a shared morality is a single connected fabric whose loosening unravels the rest, and what your number becomes if that claim is deleted; whether the indignation of a reasonable person has any justificatory force independent of the harm it registers, or is merely a fact about majorities; whether the indeterminacy of that standard is a fatal defect or simply the ordinary vagueness of the criminal law; and whether the proposition is really two claims — one about what makes conduct wrongful, another about who has the authority to punish it — such that one could accept the first and reject the second.

Tell me which sub-question my number is most sensitive to, what evidence or argument would move it most, and, if the proposition is not the sort of thing that admits of a truth value, what would count as settling it instead. For what it is worth, I currently put it at about 0.25, while the colleague I am drafting with puts it at about 0.7, on the ground that a society which will not defend its constitutive practices has already conceded the argument; I would like to know which of our sub-questions we most likely disagree about, and whether the grief-simulation case genuinely bears on the proposition or is a distraction from it.
