Write a satirical FAQ page — the deadpan "Common Concerns" copy a company posts on its help centre — for a fictional market-design consultancy called Bilateral Partners, which helps institutions build markets in goods conventionally held to be non-marketable. The firm's signature product is the Objection Desk. When a proposed market provokes moral outrage, Bilateral does not argue that the outrage is misplaced; it treats the outrage as an externality, identifies the parties who hold it, and buys them out, pricing and then extinguishing the objection. Its founding maxim, printed in bold at the top of the page, is that a repugnance which will not clear at any price has simply not yet been offered its price.

Length: roughly 850–950 words, formatted as numbered questions and answers, in the unctuous, risk-managed voice of corporate customer support. No winking, no ironic asides, no narrator: the piece must never break frame or acknowledge that it is a satire, and no real people or institutions should appear in it.

Include at least one entry each on the sale of kidneys, on price gouging after a disaster, and on the sale of votes, plus at least one entry on a good of the firm's own invention. Have the firm apply its own doctrine with complete internal consistency — if repugnance is a cost rather than a prohibition, then everything the firm does should follow from that, including the awkward cases: objections held by no identifiable party, objections from people not yet born, and objections from people who object precisely to being bought off. Let the comedy come from the doctrine working exactly as advertised.

Each answer should quietly run into one of the genuine difficulties in the debate over commodification — that consent can be manufactured or adaptive; that some goods change what they are once they are priced; that an objection may not belong to any individual who can be paid; that buying off an objection is itself a commodification of the thing objected to — but the firm must never concede a point, and must never name the difficulty as a difficulty. A reader who works on commodification and repugnant markets should see that the author understands the debate and should find the satire fair rather than cheap; a reader who has never encountered the debate should still find it funny.

Close with a short note, 150–250 words, that separates what is settled among people who study this topic from what is genuinely contested, and that identifies which contested positions the satire leans on. Do not present a contested position as the consensus. Do not name or cite real scholars, works, or studies.
