I chair the ethics review panel of a state agency that licenses novel paid-transaction programs: pilot schemes paying living organ donors, emergency surge pricing on necessities, and the resale of queue positions for scarce public benefits all come through us. The panel has five members, none of them philosophers; we see roughly two proposals a month, must issue a recommendation within ten working days, and our recommendations are usually adopted. I need a decision procedure we can actually run, built on what is genuinely established about commodification and repugnant markets, but written so that a member who has never read any of it can apply it. Give me no citations or reading list; where you use a distinction from the literature, explain it in your own words in a sentence.

Phrase the questions in plain language and in the order we should ask them. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence we should demand from the proposer and what we should refuse to accept (a label like compensation for time and risk, where the substance is a price for the good itself, is not an answer), and what to do when no answer is available. Mark clearly the points where the procedure stops with a no and the points where it hands the decision to the panel because the question is evaluative rather than factual; for those, say what we must record so that a dissenting member can see exactly which contested value the verdict rests on. The procedure must separate repugnance at the terms of a trade, repugnance at the mere existence of a market in the good, and repugnance at who is paying whom for what, and it must not treat bare disgust as an argument.

It also has to handle at least these complications: offers made against a background of injustice, which may make the offer coercive rather than merely exploitative; markets that price only a component of a good, such as the donor's time and risk rather than the organ, or the seller's inconvenience rather than the place in line; markets that erode a working non-market form of provision, as paid plasma might undercut voluntary donation; and effects on people who are not parties to the transaction at all, whose relation to the good changes once it is for sale.

Then run it on three proposals — paying living kidney donors in a pilot, letting a utility charge scarcity prices for water and generator fuel in the week after a flood, and letting holders of a scarce subsidized housing voucher sell their place in the queue — plus vote selling as a limiting case. At least one of the four should come out against my own instincts; I am sympathetic to the kidney pilot, and I would rather the procedure told me the truth than agreed with me. Finish with what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will get wrong, the ways a clever proposer can walk a transaction around it, and the questions on which a panel of non-philosophers should refuse to decide and send the matter up.
