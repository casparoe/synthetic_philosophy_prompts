I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been trying to find a name for a pattern that keeps surfacing in different guises. I first ran into it while building tooling for an internal forecasting exercise, and later while reading about how bookmakers and markets operate.

The forecasting case: a colleague told me she was 30% confident in some event A, 60% confident in B, and 40% confident that both A and B would occur. Taken one at a time, each figure sounds defensible, but they cannot all be right, since both happening requires A to happen, and she cannot be more confident of the pair than of the first alone. What interested me was that this is not merely an abstract inconsistency. Had I been willing to take the opposite side of all three of her estimates at the odds those figures imply, I would have come out ahead regardless of how things turned out — the loss is not a matter of bad luck but is built into the commitments themselves.

The same shape then appeared somewhere with no uncertainty at all. A colleague had a set of preferences over trades that were unobjectionable one at a time: offered any single one, he would accept it. Offered in sequence, however, he would end up strictly worse off than he began, having agreed to every step himself, with no gamble involved. And in the market setting, this seems to be part of what people mean when they call a set of prices off — that one can trade around them and lock in a profit.

There is one further wrinkle that makes me doubt the betting case is simply about inconsistency at a single moment. I suspect that a person whose estimates are perfectly consistent at every instant could still be trapped, provided those estimates shift over time in the wrong way — that it is the sequence of revisions, and not any one set of numbers, that does the damage.

I am reasonably sure the betting version has a name, and I suspect the over-time version and the market version may be the same idea or may be distinct ideas that merely resemble one another. What I want is not a label but the carving:

- Is the essential feature the guaranteed loss, the inconsistency among the commitments, or the fact that a third party can exploit them? Can any of these come apart from the others?
- Is the all-at-once case (the three estimates) the same phenomenon as the case that unfolds over time (the sequence of trades, or the drifting estimates)? If they differ, what precisely separates them?
- How does the market case relate? Is arbitrage the same concept, a special case of it, or something adjacent?
- Does the bare possibility of constructing such a trap actually establish that the person is being irrational, or does it only establish that they can be taken advantage of? People often wave this away by saying they would never accept all the bets at once, and I cannot tell whether that is a substantive reply or a dodge.
- Where are the edges of the phenomenon? Must the loss be certain, or does a probable loss suffice? Does it matter whether the person ever actually accepts the bets?

If there is an established name — or a family of related names — I would like the nearest ones set out and told apart, and if what I have described is really a blend of several, I would like to know which piece belongs to which.
