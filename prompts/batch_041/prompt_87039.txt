For the past two years, since retiring from a long career in experimental physics, I have been teaching a seminar in the philosophy department, and this term the course is on collective action and moral uncertainty: eighteen advanced undergraduates, one ninety-minute session a week. I want to devote a full session to the phenomenon usually called the unilateralist's curse, but I am determined that the students should not merely be told about it. Thirty years in large scientific collaborations persuaded me that people truly believe a result about collective failure only after they have walked into it themselves, so what I am after is a game or structured exercise in which a roomful of sensible, well-intentioned people each make a defensible individual choice and then discover, to their discomfort, that their combined choices have produced an outcome none of them would have endorsed. The formal structure I have in mind is roughly this: each participant may privately choose to act or to refrain; acting is genuinely attractive to the one who does it when few others do, but each act imposes a small cost on everyone, so that a single actor is harmless and a crowd of actors is a disaster. I would like the activity to run across three or four rounds of increasing coordination, so that the students feel how the curse weakens without vanishing once cheap communication is allowed, and then see whether some more binding arrangement actually repairs it. Please give me the rules, the materials, the scoring scheme (points, not real money), a rough timeline for the ninety minutes, and the debrief.

There are two further things I would like the debrief and your accompanying notes to address head-on. First, the opposite failure: I have read enough to worry that the very logic which makes a group over-act can, under a slightly different payoff, make it freeze and under-act, and I do not want my students to leave believing that the cure for the curse is simply universal caution. Second, I would like at least one round to test the difference between a commitment that is merely public and one that is costly or verifiable, because I suspect the students will badly over-estimate how much a round of discussion has solved. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — which participants will defect anyway, where the group will over-coordinate into paralysis, how social pressure will contaminate the later rounds — and how I should handle each of those failures in the moment.

Finally, since I am new to this material and have been learning it largely on my own, be frank about which feature of the underlying idea the activity cannot reproduce on its own, and which part I will simply have to explain from the front rather than let them feel.
