I teach a 300-level seminar on early modern philosophy and I'm doing a session on Berkeley's economics — the stuff around The Querist, his insistence that money is more like a permission slip or a claim ticket than a substance, that it's a means of transferring wealth rather than wealth itself, plus his push for a national bank and paper credit in Ireland. The students already know the immaterialism; most of them have never heard that Berkeley wrote economic pamphlets, and I want them to get why that isn't a weird side gig.

Design me a runnable in-class game, about 20 students, 75 minutes, that makes them feel the force of the ticket view of money. I want them to hit the wall themselves — the moment where a room full of tokens stops being able to move real goods, or where everyone's chasing the tokens and nobody's making anything — before anyone says the word "circulation." The rules and incentives should do the work; I don't want to stand up front and explain the thesis first.

Here's my rough instinct, feel free to overhaul it. Split them into producers (baker, weaver, farmer, smith, etc.), a landlord, a couple of laborers, and one person who gets to issue notes. Round one is barter and it should visibly grind because nobody wants what the other person has at the right moment. Round two we hand out notes redeemable for a fixed basket, and things should hum. Then somewhere in round three the issuer does something — overissues, or hoards, or recalls, or a rumor spreads that redemption is shaky — and I want the economy to seize up in a way they can feel in their bodies, not just read off a chart. Round four someone proposes melting the notes or buying silver with them, and I want the group to discover that the notes were never the wealth, the goods and the labor were, and that the note is just a socially sustained permission to claim them.

Give me the full package: roles, materials, the exact rules and what counts as a payoff (no real money — points, candy, whatever's cheap), a round-by-round timeline with rough minutes, what the issuer is allowed to do and what they're told to keep secret. Then the debrief, where you map specific moments that will actually happen onto Berkeley's claims: the circulation metaphor, the priority of industry and labor over the medium, the trust/opinion element that makes the whole thing hold together, and the bridge back to his metaphysics — that value lives in the idea and the convention, not in the metal, which is the same move as denying the material substance behind the perceived object. I'd like that last connection to land but not be heavy-handed.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to fix it on the fly. My guesses: some groups just treat the notes as money and never feel the difference, one person tries to corner the market and makes it unfun, someone refuses to accept notes on principle and either kills the round or accidentally becomes the star of the debrief. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce — I assume it can't give them Berkeley's actual historical situation, the coin shortage and the whole Irish grievance about English control of the currency, and it can't reproduce the providential/theological frame where wealth and industry are ultimately gifts, so I'll have to supply those myself. What else is it missing, and what should I say to fill the gap?
