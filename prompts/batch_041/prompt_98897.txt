In my distributive justice seminar this week, a classmate defended Nozick's entitlement theory by leaning on an analogy that keeps resurfacing in discussion: forcing someone to labor for another's benefit is a form of servitude, and taxing the earnings of someone's labor is said to make that person work, at least in part, for the state, so redistributive taxation is illegitimate for the same reason forced labor is, with the Wilt Chamberlain case serving as the vivid illustration. I would like an audit of the analogy itself rather than a restatement of the theory, and I already know the Chamberlain case well enough that it does not need to be retold. Please respond with a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, and with no introduction or conclusion. First, state the correspondence explicitly, saying what plays the role of the coerced worker, the labor, the master, and the product, and flag which of those mappings is doing the load-bearing work. Second, identify where the analogy breaks, being specific about the strongest disanalogies rather than listing every difference you can think of. Third, sort those disanalogies into the ones that matter for the conclusion being drawn and the ones that are merely rhetorical, and explain the basis for the sorting. Fourth, say what the analogy quietly imports about the target that the source case lacks, for instance about whether a person's property is continuous with the person, about how the existing distribution of holdings came about, and about whether the state's relation to the earner is purely extractive. Fifth, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy was used to support survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, describe the analogy that would carry it better.
