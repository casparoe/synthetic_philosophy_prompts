I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade — long enough to keep a running note file of phrases that end up doing more work in argument than they can bear, and long enough to notice when one of them starts showing up everywhere. The entry I cannot resolve is "it's turtles all the way down." I met it again last week in a comment thread that started with Gödel's incompleteness theorems, wandered into Escher's two hands drawing each other, and settled on the question of whether there is any level of description that is not itself part of the thing it describes. Someone closed the exchange with the turtle line, and plainly meant it as a closing move: the regress never terminates, so the question is not worth asking. But I have seen the identical sentence used to mean at least three different things — that justification never terminates; that no level of description is privileged over any other; and, in a spirit that looks nearly opposite to the first two, that no foundation was ever needed because an endless regress is perfectly harmless. I suspect that at least one of those uses is quietly borrowing the idea of an inviolate level — the level that makes the rules rather than obeying them, and that the tangled hierarchy cannot reach from the inside — while sounding like a flat denial of exactly that idea.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims "it's turtles all the way down" could be making, ordered from the ones that are trivially true to the ones that would need a real argument, and for each say what it commits you to, whether it is defensible, and — the part I care about most — whether it actually supports the use it was put to in that thread, namely ending the discussion of whether a reasoner can justify the rules it reasons by. Separate the readings under which the slogan and the inviolate level are compatible from the readings under which they contradict each other, and tell me which reading the people who say this in these circles most likely have in mind, distinguishing that from what the sentence would have to mean in order to do the work they ask of it. Where a reading turns on a contested position — about foundations, about self-reference, about whether there can be a description from outside every system — name the position and say how much rides on accepting it.

Write in American English. Do not manufacture a taxonomy just to satisfy me: if the three uses I think I have distinguished are really one claim wearing three hats, or if the tension I am describing is confused, say so plainly and explain why, rather than politely producing a tidy list. I would rather be told that I have invented a puzzle than be handed a menu that conceals it. And where you are reporting your impression of how the phrase is usually used rather than arguing from the concept itself, mark it as an impression.
