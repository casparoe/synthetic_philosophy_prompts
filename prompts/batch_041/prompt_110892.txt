OK so I'm setting the final for my upper-level ethics seminar (final-year BA, mostly phil majors, a couple of law students) and I want a single essay question on standing to blame, and I'm stuck on how to pitch it. Every year half the class writes 'a hypocrite has no standing to blame' and then recites the tu quoque fallacy, and the other half writes 'hypocrisy is irrelevant, blameworthiness is blameworthiness', and neither group ever says what standing actually is or what it's doing that plain blameworthiness doesn't already do. So I want a question that forces them to either give standing a real job or give it up, and that they can't answer by just reciting the standard moves.

The case I want to hang it on — tell me if you think it works or if you'd tweak it: Ada learns that Ben read her private journal and goes off at him, tells him he had no right, that it was a betrayal, that this is who he is. Ben says she did the exact same thing to her sister two years ago. Ada says: yeah I did, it was the only way to find out my sister was being abused, I knew it was wrong at the time and felt awful about it, and I'd do it again in those circumstances. Ben's snooping was just nosiness. And there are two seperate further versions I want folded in: in one it isn't Ada's journal at all, she's a bystander who heard about it; in the other, Ada never actually did it but would do it if she were in Ben's exact situation.

What I want the question to make them do: say precisely what Ada's hypocrisy is supposed to consist in — the act-type, the reason she acted, the judgment she'd pass on herself, or her character — and whether the fact that her own breach was excused changes anything; whether it matters that she's the victim rather than a bystander; what happens if she just openly concedes she's a hypocrite (does the concession restore standing, and if so what does that tell you standing was all along); and then land a verdict on whether 'standing' names a real normative status or is a placeholder for a bundle of other things (sincerity, efficacy, special relationships, what blaming is for) that we could say without it. No fence-sitting — they have to commit.

Practical stuff: upper-level undergrad, in-class final, they get 50 minutes for this one question, so it has to be doable in that time without being easy. Give me (1) the question as it'll appear on the paper, with whatever short preamble lays out the case, (2) a model answer of about 650-750 words, and (3) a marking scheme with six must-hit points worth 25 marks total — uneven is fine, just say what each is worth — and for each point a one-line note on how students usually get it wrong. One more thing: dont let the model answer lean on any named philosopher's position. I didn't assign reading on this and I don't want them rewarded for name-dropping, I want the moves themselves.
