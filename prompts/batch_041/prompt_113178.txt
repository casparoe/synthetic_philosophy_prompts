I am a retired geophysicist — most of my working life went into groundwater surveys, including a number of field seasons in the Sahel — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy without much discipline, chiefly, I think, because a lifetime of insisting that a measurement means nothing until one can state the standard against which it is taken has left me uneasy about how we are to state the standard in moral and political matters, and who is to hold it. The occasion for writing is an argument I could not referee. Years ago the district officer who hosted our field team — a man I liked, and who by every local account was a dutiful kinsman and a generous host — used a discretionary budget under his control to put a borehole in his own village and to send a number of children from his extended family to secondary school in the capital; over the following years he received customary gifts at naming ceremonies and at harvest, and at the next election the organized support of the families concerned. I set the facts down as I would set down a log, without comment. It is what two friends made of them at a reunion last month that I cannot settle, and I would like your help in adjudicating their exchange rather than my own view of it, since I have none.

The first, whom I will call A — a former colleague who moved into public-finance work — said flatly that this was corruption: an official entrusted with a discretionary budget had directed it toward his own kin and had been rewarded for doing so, and the villages with no kinsman in office had paid for both the borehole and the school fees, in the form of a well they never received. She added that she was invoking no Western standard, since the country's own constitution, courts and press denounce precisely this conduct; and that the gifts were not merely accepted but solicited and repaid in the currency of electoral support. The second, B, who reads a great deal of social history, said the label was doing illicit work. The office, he argued, had never been understood locally as an impersonal trust; an officeholder was expected to be a broker for his lineage, and a man who took the salary and the prestige of the office while ignoring his own people would have been thought to have failed in the only obligation anyone could actually enforce against him. He granted that some things are plainly wrong — the embezzlement of the budget itself, the extortion of people with no choice, the sale of a permit to whichever stranger bid highest — but insisted that the last of these is a different animal from what the officer did: a price paid to anyone who can pay is one thing, a transfer to those to whom one already owes something is another. A replied that the distinction, whatever its descriptive value, is morally inert, and that if anything the kin-based version is the worse of the two, since a price can at least be met by anyone whereas a tie cannot be outbid; the officer's duty, she said, came from the office and not from the officeholder's private theory of the office. B replied that the duty is itself a construction, and that the public whose asset was held in trust was assembled out of kin groups and localities that never consented to be treated as an undifferentiated mass of equal claimants; where the state is the chief instrument of competition among groups, a rule of strict impartiality does not neutralize that competition but merely hands the proceeds to whoever already commands the center. A then turned to the fact that the gifts were given quietly, the accounts kept loosely, and that no one, the officer included, was prepared to defend the arrangement in the terms B was defending it — hypocrisy, she said, is evidence of a standard, since one does not conceal a practice that one's own community regards as an ordinary fulfilment of duty. B answered that the concealment might be owed entirely to the auditors and the ministry, that one can be perfectly open before one's own people and closeted before a foreign law, and that a norm may be locally normative and still harmful — which he took to be a concession on his part rather than a defense.

Please adjudicate this move by move, taking the six exchanges in order and telling me for each whether the claim as stated is correct, partly correct, or mistaken, and why. Then say which of the two has the better of the dispute overall, and what each of them is missing. I am particularly interested in whether the norm A is invoking can be stated in a way that neither imports a specifically Western conception of the state as an impersonal trustee nor degenerates into the rule that a practice is beyond criticism whenever it is locally expected; and in whether B's distinction — between a price paid to anyone and a transfer owed to someone in particular — can be made precise enough to bear the weight he puts on it, or whether, as A suspects, it collapses the moment one asks how a gift that maintains a relationship is to be told apart from a price that buys a decision. I am not asking for reading suggestions; I want the arguments themselves assessed.
