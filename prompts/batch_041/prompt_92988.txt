Ok so I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me, but I've gotten obsessed with this thing and I need help.

Here's the setup. I'm writing a book where two of my characters are basically the two sides of an argument I can't resolve, and I want to write the document they'd have produced if someone locked them in a room and made them do one of those adversarial colaboration things, where the point isn't to win, it's to write down what you agree on and then make the disagreement exact.

One of them, call her the Layered person, thinks flexibility is real and comes in layers. Bottom layer: the wasp thing. You know the one, the wasp drags a cricket to her burrow, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's down checking the burrow, she just drags it back and checks again, and she'll do it forever if you keep nudging it. She's stuck in a loop. The Layered person says: fine, that's a fixed routine, but humans can do something the wasp can't, which is notice their own loop and break it, step back, represent their own dispositions, and revise them. Call that second-order flexibility. And free will, whatever it is, is just this: not a magic spark but the top floor of a building the wasp is living on the ground floor of. It's real, it's not binary, and the wasp isn't a zombie, she's just low-rise.

The other one, the Flat person, thinks that's all a story we tell ourselves. For him there's no principled difference between a wasp that responds to a cricket and a human that responds to a reason. Both are just dispositions firing on cues. Flexibility just means more cues and more dispositions wired in, and second-order flexibility is just more of the same, one floor up. The human can't help representing her own dispositions any more than the wasp can help dragging the cricket. And if the line between a cue and a reason, or between first and second order, is arbitrary, then the freedom worth wanting is an illusion too.

I want the joint statement these two could both sign. Specifically:

- a numbered list of claims they actually agree on, about what the wasp case does and doesn't show, about what flexibility even means, about whether the difference is one of degree or kind, and about what would have to be true for free will to be worth the name;
- where they actually part ways, each side's position written in wording the other side would accept as fair;
- the cruxes, and I think one is whether there's a non-arbitrary line between responding to a cue and responding to a reason, and another is whether being able to revise your own dispositions is a difference in kind or just more degree, and for each crux, a concrete test or case they'd both agree bears on it, with each one's prediction written down in advance.

Two things I'd like you to build in, because I think they sharpen it. First, a rewind test: take either creature, rewind to just before the loop or the choice, run it again, what does each side predict, and does the answer even differ between the wasp and the human? Second, a thing I keep calling the loop budget: how many times will a creature re-run a failed routine before giving up, and can that number itself be changed by the creature's own history? I suspect the Flat person says the budget is just set by wiring and experience like everything else, and the Layered person says the human's budget is set partly by the human, and that's the whole ballgame.

And if part of their fight turns out to be just about words, if flexibility or free will is doing two jobs at once, say which part and why.

I don't need citations or reading lists, obviously, just the document. Also don't be nice about it, these two have been at it for a year.
