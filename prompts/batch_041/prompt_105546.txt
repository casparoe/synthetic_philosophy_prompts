I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I am planning a ninety-minute session on whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. The students know the basic mechanics of reinforcement learning—policy, reward, value—but they keep sliding between two claims: that a negative reward is a bad state for the agent, and that a negative reward is a bad experience for the agent. I have been arguing with a colleague in the computer science department who insists that a negative reward just is suffering, and I want an in-class simulation that makes that insistence feel less obvious. The setting is a seminar room with about eighteen students; the session is ninety minutes; the materials should be cheap and low-tech—index cards, dice, a timer, a whiteboard. The deliverable is a runnable activity: rules, roles, materials, a round structure, a payoff scheme that uses points or tokens rather than real money, and a debrief. The idea should show up in what happens, not in what I say afterward.

To give the activity a spine, I have been using a distinction I call R-valence and E-valence. R-valence is the functional role a scalar reward plays in updating a policy—the way a negative number changes what the agent does next. E-valence is the felt quality of an experience, if there is one—what it is like, if anything, to receive that reward. The activity should force participants to confront the inference problem: from R-valence and behavior alone, can you tell whether E-valence is present? I imagine something like a three-role simulation. Agents play a simple repeated choice game and receive rewards; Monitors see only the reward logs and the agents’ actions; Advocates argue for or against attributing suffering to the agents. The twist I want built in is a pain button that the Agents can press. In the first condition, the button is secretly disconnected from the reward—pressing it is uncorrelated with negative reward. In the second condition, it is connected. The Monitors must decide, in each condition, whether the Agents are suffering, and the Advocates must defend their verdicts. The debrief should map the two conditions onto R-valence and E-valence, and should be honest that the activity cannot reproduce the hard problem of consciousness or settle whether any RL agent has E-valence.

Please include a timeline for the ninety minutes, a list of materials, the exact rules and payoff scheme, and a debrief script or outline. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for example, participants trying to win rather than introspect, Monitors over-reading the pain button, or someone demanding a behavioral definition of suffering—and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief: the fact that first-person experience is not observable from the outside, and that the question of machine suffering may turn on metaphysics rather than on reward architecture. I would like the activity to leave them with the discomfort of not knowing, rather than with a tidy answer.
