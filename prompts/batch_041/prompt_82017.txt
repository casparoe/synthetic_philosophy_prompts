My girlfriend is German and last month she had to go down to the town hall and pay something like thirty euros just to announce that she was leaving the church — not to any church, to the state — so that the tax office would stop skimming nine per cent off her income tax every month and handing it to a church she hasn't set foot in since she was a teenager. Her employer withholds it like national insurance; the state keeps the register of who belongs to which religion; and the Catholic side apparently takes the view that she's still baptised regardless, so as far as they're concerned the paperwork hasn't really got her out of anything. What got me was how much work that little arrangement is doing. It only seems to make sense if religious membership is a public, ascribeable status that the state can record and bill you for, and if a church is something like a public body that does public work and therefore deserves public money — but then the way out is a purely private act of resignation, which belongs to a completely different picture.

And it's not as if anyone else does it more coherently. France is meant to be the militantly secular one and still pays the salaries of Catholic, Protestant and Jewish clergy in Alsace-Moselle, and funds a school system that's overwhelmingly Catholic. Britain has a genuinely established church, bishops in the Lords and the King at the head of it, and yet the whole thing runs off its own investments and donations and almost nobody is taxed for it. America shouts loudest about the seperation of church and state and yet churches are automatically tax-exempt and the courts have invented a rule that the state can't tell a church who its own ministers are. So each country is doing something different with the same concept, and I can't tell whether any of them has a coherent view or whether they're all just sedimented compromises that nobody would design from scratch.

What I actually want from you is the implicit theory in each case. For the German church tax especially: what does it presuppose about religion — a voluntary association you're enrolled in until you resign, a quasi-public corporation the state has to fund, or a cultural inheritance you're born into and only opt out of by a deliberate legal act? Then tell me which parts are genuinely presupposed and which are just accidents that several incompatible theories would license equally well, and whether the German arrangement can be made consistent with a laïcité-style view of religion as something the state must not even record. I suspect it can't be done piecemeal, because the register is wired into tax law, employment law and the churches' own finances, but I'd like the argument rather than just the conclusion. Please don't give me a history of disestablishment; I want the commitments.
