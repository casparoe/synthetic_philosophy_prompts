In 2006 the German constitutional court struck down a law that would have let the defence minister order a hijacked passenger plane shot down once it was clearly being flown at a building full of people. The court said no: the innocent passengers would be sacrificed for the benefit of the people on the ground, and that treats them as objects rather than as people, and human dignity doesn't bend to numbers, not even to save hundreds. So the rule ends up protecting nobody on the plane (they die either way) while costing the people on the ground, which is basically the thing I've seen called the paradox of deontology - the rule that's supposed to stop people being used as tools is the reason more people die, and it's justified by respect for persons.

I'm not a philosopher, just someone who's been chewing on this, and what I want is for you to take that decision and really run it through the paradox, in both directions. What does the paradox say the court got right, and where does it say the court is fooling itself - and does it matter that the passengers were doomed anyway? If they die either way, is there even a person left for the constraint to protect, or is the whole thing really about the state keeping its hands clean? Then the other direction: what does this case do to the paradox, given that the agent here is a state and not a person with a conscience - does the clean-hands story even survive that swap, or does deontology turn out to be about the agent's own purity in a way nobody could defend to the people it lets die? And if there's a version of the rule that gets through this case without the paradox, tell me what it costs to buy, because if the honest answer is that the paradox is just a real seperate feature of deontology, I'd rather hear that than watch you patch it.
