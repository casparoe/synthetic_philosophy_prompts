My brother's consumer DNA test came back in March with two distinct genetic profiles: his saliva and his blood disagree about who he is, and the geneticist's explanation was that two fertilized eggs fused in the first days after conception, so the person I have known for thirty-four years developed out of two zygotes rather than one. I did a philosophy degree a decade ago and spent a term on the thesis that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg; I can still reconstruct the intuitions, and I remember finding them irresistible. What I never once considered was that the premise underneath them — one person, one origin — is a biological convenience rather than a fact about human beings.

The rest of my household does not fit the premise either. My older children are identical twins: one zygote, two people, and no one in the family regards them as a single person who failed to finish splitting. My wife and I did IVF last year. Our daughter was one of four embryos, three of which are still in a freezer in a clinic two hours away; which one became her depended on a thaw schedule and a conversation about my wife's work calendar. And a colleague's son was conceived by having his mother's nuclear material moved into a donor's egg whose own nucleus had been removed, so his cells carry the mitochondrial line of a woman he will never meet and whose name is on no form he will ever see. Four different ways of being a particular human being, all of them ordinary, none of them matching the picture I was taught.

I would like you to write me a short story in which this tangle is dramatized rather than explained. Set it in the present, with no technology we do not already have. Give me at least one scene in which two people who love each other talk past each other about whether someone could have been someone else — a parent and a genetic counselor, a pair of siblings, a couple arguing about what to do with the remaining embryos; the choice of scene is yours. Nobody in the story should deliver a lecture, name a philosophical position, or cite a text, and I would rather you avoid any well-known published thought experiment entirely: I want invented particulars, not a rehearsal of someone else's. Keep the vocabulary ordinary as well — no "essence," "possible world," "counterpart," or "modal." Take the biology seriously enough that a reader learns nothing false about twinning, chimerism, or IVF. Do not resolve the question, and do not end with a moral. Somewhere between 1,200 and 2,000 words.

Begin with the story itself. A title is fine. No preamble, no epigraph, no note about what the story is meant to illustrate, and no afterword.
