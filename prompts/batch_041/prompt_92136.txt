I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, but I'm sitting in on an epistemology seminar this term and we spent a whole session on the Meno problem, and I came away thinking the whole thing might rest on something empirically false. I'd like someone to set me straight if I'm confused, because I've asked two people in the seminar and they both just told me to read more secondary literature, which is not helpful.

Here's my reading. Socrates' answer to why knowledge is better than true belief is the Daedalus bit: true beliefs are like statues that wander off unless you tie them down, and knowledge is the tied-down version. As I understand it, that's supposed to be a claim about the belief sticking around — knowledge is more durable, true belief is flighty. But that seems definately false as a description of actual people. Someone can carry a lucky hunch for forty years and never revise it; someone else can hold a belief they can fully justify and drop it the moment their department changes fashion or they get tired of defending it. Whether a belief persists looks like a fact about the person's memory, incentives, and social environment, not about whether they can give the reason why. Same story with the juror and the witness, as far as I can tell — nothing about having seen the thing yourself guarantees your belief outlives the juror's. So if the tethering is what carries the answer, and the tether holds no better than the untethered statue, why is knowledge better than true belief? Doesn't the problem just dissolve?

Lead with the bottom line on whether I'm confused, then explain. Max 250 words.
