Construct a thought experiment to the following specification. Two agents, otherwise identically placed, each perform an act of the same type, at the same cost to themselves, with the same knowledge of the causal facts, and without malice. In both cases the harmful outcome is exactly as it would have been had the agent not acted, and this immunity is built into the facts rather than resting on a threshold already crossed by others, on anyone's uncertainty about what difference her act makes, or on her act being a mere omission. The two cases must differ in exactly one feature, and the verdicts must come apart: one agent is blameworthy and the other is not. The asymmetry has to be one a reader will feel rather than merely be told about. Keep each case to a single realistic paragraph — a purchase, a vote, a commute, a committee — with no trolleys and no science fiction. Then do three things. (i) Name the feature that does the work and show that deleting it flips the verdict. (ii) Say what a purely difference-making account of responsibility must conclude about the blameworthy case, and whether it can reach the intuitive verdict by appealing instead to expected consequences, to a rule whose general acceptance would be harmful, or to the expressive meaning of the act, without smuggling a difference-making condition back in. (iii) Identify the feature of your case that a defender of that account will attack, and say whether the attack succeeds or merely restates the account.
