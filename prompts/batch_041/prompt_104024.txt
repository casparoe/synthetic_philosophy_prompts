For about two years now I've been part of a small discussion group — none of us are academics; we're a retired probation officer, a couple of teachers, a nurse, and me — that keeps returning to the same argument about punishment, and I've finally decided I don't want another round of the argument, I want a diagnosis of why it never moves. The split is clean and predictable. On one side are the backward-looking people: they hold that someone who does something seriously wrong deserves to suffer in proportion to what they did, and that this remains true even when the suffering does no good — even if no one ever learns of the crime, even if the person is already reformed and will never offend again. On the other side are the forward-looking people: they hold that suffering is only ever justified as a means to something — deterring, incapacitating, reforming, or expressing a community's disapproval in a way that shifts future norms — and that 'desert' on its own is either a prediction about behavior in disguise or a residue we ought to outgrow. Here is what makes me suspect the argument is not what it looks like: the two sides agree on almost all the facts we can check. Nobody in the room thinks the evidence for deterrence is strong; nobody disputes the recidivism numbers or the psychology. So it isn't a quarrel about the data, and yet it will not resolve.

Someone in the group once said something that has stuck with me: that the backward-looking view treats a wrong as a debt, something that creates an imbalance only suffering can settle, while the forward-looking view treats a wrong as a problem, something that calls for a solution and nothing more. If that is the real difference, then the dispute may not be about which considerations count as reasons at all, but about what a wrong fundamentally is — a debt or a problem — and I cannot tell whether that is a substantive disagreement, a merely verbal one, or two groups answering different questions while using a single word. Two cases split us every time. First: imagine a society invents a cheap, safe, painless procedure that reliably removes the disposition to offend and can be given to someone who has already committed a serious crime; the forward-looking people say that replaces punishment, while the backward-looking people say it does not, because the debt is still owed. Second: a man who committed a terrible crime is now terminally ill and will certainly never offend again, and punishing him can deter no one because everyone knows he is dying; the backward-looking people still want the remaining sentence served, or at least think something is genuinely lost if it is not. I want you to use the actual shape of the disagreement — not a survey of who has said what — to tell me what kind of disagreement this is: whether the two sides differ over facts, over what counts as a moral reason, over which cases are decisive, over the correct description of wrongdoing, or over different questions that happen to share the name 'punishment.' If my debt-versus-problem hypothesis is right, say what follows from it; if it is wrong, say where the real fault line lies. Then tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress here — a distinction that dissolves the quarrel, a case that settles it, a reason to think one side is simply mistaken, or a reason to think no progress should be expected because the disagreement rests on something neither side can argue for.

Please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your diagnosis, and single out the one claim you are least sure of, so that I can see where you are reasoning and where you are guessing.
