I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm at work on a piece about moral responsibility — specifically about whether you must be able to do otherwise in order to be answerable for what you do. For the centerpiece I've sketched a case, and I've asked two philosophers to argue it out on the record. Please write the dialogue as it would run in the piece.

The case. Nadia Reyes is the chief structural engineer at a port authority. One Friday in November she has to decide whether to certify a pier for cargo traffic before a set of bolt inspections is finished. She judges the risk small but real, and she signs, because keeping that pier open is what keeps the region's grain terminal running through the winter. What she doesn't know is that a clause she herself wrote eighteen months ago, as filler in a routine emergency-continuity package, is still in force: if the engineer of record has not certified a pier within forty-eight hours of its operational deadline, the certification is deemed to have been issued, and it carries her name. She drafted the clause for the case of an engineer unreachable after a storm, and she has long since forgotten it. No one is coercing her and nothing physically prevents her from refusing — but had she refused, the pier would have reopened on time anyway, on a certificate bearing her name.

The two speakers. Vera Lindqvist is a compatibilist: she holds that responsibility requires an agent to act from her own reasons and to be responsive to reasons, and she thinks the tradition of Frankfurt-style cases has shown that the principle of alternative possibilities is not a genuine requirement. Owen Mbeki is an incompatibilist: he holds that answerability does require a real ability to do otherwise, and he has never been persuaded that a Frankfurt-style case removes the alternative that matters.

What I need from the dialogue. Give each of them at least four substantive turns, and make every turn answer the strongest version of what the other just said rather than restating a position. Neither may be a strawman; each should concede something real and produce at least one move the other didn't see coming. I want them to come to grips with three things in particular: whether the alternative that matters must be an alternative act rather than a different description of the same act; whether a deemed certification is anything she does at all; and whether the fact that she drafted the clause, and forgot it, cuts for either side. Let them argue the case itself — no appeals to what other philosophers have written, no citations, no literature review. Don't force agreement or a tidy synthesis at the end; stop wherever the argument actually lands. Keep it in the register of a magazine dialogue: plain, tight, sharp when it needs to be.
