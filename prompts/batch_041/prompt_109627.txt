I'm assembling a short anthology of poems on epistemology for a reading group that has just finished a unit on the limits of self-knowledge, and I'd like to close it with a new poem on the KK principle together with the anti-luminosity argument -- the idea that "I know that p" and "I know that I know that p" can come apart, and the stronger and stranger idea that a mental condition can fail to be luminous, in the sense that even when you are in it you may not be in a position to know that you are in it, because a small enough change in your situation can slip below the threshold of what you can detect. I want the poem to be a pantoum, because the form does the philosophical work I'm after: the pantoum's rule is that the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third lines of the next, so a line that was true in one setting is reasserted in another and its meaning drifts, which is exactly what I take to happen to a knowledge claim as you iterate it. Let the two recurring lines be, or be versions of, "I know" and "I know that I know," and make the reader feel the claim weaken each time it is carried into a new stanza -- the point being that there is no level of iteration at which you can be sure the next level is safe. Don't let the poem announce this or resolve it; it should end in the place where the assertion has hollowed out without saying so in so many words, and someone who knows the argument should find it right while someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.

Two things I'd like you to build in that go past the standard textbook cases. First, rather than the familiar slowly-warming-body or slowly-brightening-sky example, run the whole poem through a single concrete situation I've been turning over: someone sitting with a dying parent, trying to fix the moment at which the room has gone quiet, or whether the hand being held is still being held back -- the kind of case where "I am here, I know I am here" is at once the most certain thing in the room and the least verifiable. Second, I want one small structural rule to carry the KK idea on its own: let the line about knowing that one knows appear one time fewer than the line about knowing, so that the poem is always one iteration short of closing the loop and the missing repetition is what the attentive reader notices. Keep it to roughly six or eight stanzas and a controlled line length. If the pantoum's rules make that asymmetry impossible to sustain, tell me where the form resists and choose the least bad alternative -- but keep the pantoum if you can, and explain in a sentence or two, after the poem, which formal decisions were doing which philosophical work.
