I retired a few years ago, after thirty-five years in experimental physics, and I have been spending my retirement reading philosophy of mathematics — a subject I never had time for while I was running a lab. The intuition I brought with me from the bench is that you come to know a thing by interfering with it; that is how we learned anything at all about the particles I spent my career on. So I was prepared to find Benacerraf's dilemma immediately convincing when I met it in a trade book on the philosophy of mathematics that a former colleague lent me. The book states it as follows:

"Benacerraf's dilemma is the argument that mathematical Platonism — the view that numbers and sets are abstract objects existing independently of us — cannot be true, because it is caught between two demands that cannot both be met. If mathematical statements really are about abstract objects, then we could never know anything about them: knowledge requires some form of causal contact with the thing known, and abstract objects, being non-spatial and causally inert, can never be in contact with us. If, to avoid this, we treat mathematics as being about things we construct or manipulate, then we lose the objectivity of mathematics and its theorems become true only relative to our conventions. Benacerraf's own verdict was that the first horn is fatal, and the moral is that numbers do not exist. The difficulty is sometimes put as the fact that there is no answer to the question whether 3 is the Zermelo set or the von Neumann set — which is the same problem viewed from a different angle."

Something here strikes me as too quick, but I cannot say precisely where, and I am aware that my laboratory habits of mind may be doing some of the work: the causal premise feels to me like a truism rather than a philosophical thesis, and I suspect that is exactly why the argument goes through so smoothly for a reader like me.

What I want is an audit I can act on, not a verdict. Take the book's paragraph claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher of mathematics working on these questions would accept as accurate, which are defensible simplifications that I should nonetheless mark as simplifications, and which are simply wrong — including any place where the book has quietly substituted one thesis for another. Where a defender of mathematical Platonism would object, state the objection and say whether it has force. In particular I want to know: (i) whether the conclusion that numbers do not exist follows from whichever parts of the argument survive scrutiny, or is an extra step the book has taken on its own; (ii) whether the two horns as the book states them are the two that Benacerraf actually sets against each other, or whether the second has been replaced with something easier to knock down; and (iii) whether the closing remark about the Zermelo and von Neumann sets really is the same difficulty seen from another angle, as the book says, or a separate argument that has been folded into this one. I would also like to know what escapes remain open to a realist once the dilemma is stated at full strength. I am less interested in being told that the problem is unsolved than in seeing, for each escape, which premise it has to give up.
