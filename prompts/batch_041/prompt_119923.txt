I'm the TA for a small cross-listed philosophy and economics seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and I've been handed the grading for one of the midterm's short-answer questions. The question is one I've had in my back pocket since a long argument in a group chat: Aumann's agreement theorem comes up constantly in online discussions of peer disagreement, and it does an enormous amount of work in those discussions, so I wanted to see whether students could keep its assumptions straight and resist reading more into it than it says. What I asked was this: "State Aumann's agreement theorem, being explicit about which propositions have to be common knowledge and about what work the common prior assumption is doing. Then assess the following claim. Suppose two analysts share a common prior about whether a coin is biased. Each privately examines a different sample of tosses and truthfully announces her posterior that the coin is biased, and each then updates on the other's announcement. By Aumann's theorem, this process guarantees that the two end up agreeing, and that the answer they agree on is the correct one; it follows that anyone who continues to disagree with an informed peer about a factual matter is either being irrational or else is simply not yet in a position where the disagreement is common knowledge."

Here is one student's answer, transcribed as written. "The theorem says that if two agents share the same prior for some event, and their posterior probabilities for that event are mutual knowledge between them — that is, each one knows what the other's posterior is — then those posteriors have to be equal. So a disagreement of that particular kind can't be mutual knowledge; at least one of them must be in the dark about what the other thinks. In the coin case, both analysts start from the same prior, and each has her own private sample, so their posteriors will usually diverge at first. Once each has announced her number, though, each knows the other's posterior, which is exactly the condition the theorem imposes, so they must settle on a single number. The common prior is what does the real work here, and I'd argue it isn't an extra assumption we're imposing from outside — it's just what it is to be a coherent Bayesian. A rational agent's prior can only be a function of logic and the evidence she has, so two agents who have both reasoned correctly will already have the same prior; the theorem is better read as a constraint on rationality than as a modeling convenience. On the second half of the claim, I think it's right. Because the shared prior is arrived at rationally, it can't be systematically misleading, so the number the analysts land on isn't merely one they can agree about — it's the number that a fully informed observer would give. That's what makes the theorem so useful against someone who insists on disagreeing after everything has been laid on the table: once the disagreement is genuinely common knowledge, staying put means somebody has made a mistake somewhere, and the honest options are to change your mind or to identify which premise you're rejecting."

I'd like you to grade the answer on a ten-point scale and, more useful to me than the number itself, draft the comments I'll hand back: which claims earn credit and why, which claims are wrong or overstated, and what a fully correct answer would have needed to say instead. The professor is calibrating the rubric across four sections, so please be specific about the individual moves in the answer rather than giving me a general impression — including any place where the answer reads as plausible on a first pass but doesn't actually follow from anything the theorem gives us.
