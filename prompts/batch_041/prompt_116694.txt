I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about the badness of death by attending to my reactions to particular cases rather than by following the arguments, and I should like to know what principle, if any, those reactions embody. I am not persuaded by the argument that death cannot be bad for me because there is no subject left to be harmed, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong, and I suspect my own reactions are not fully consistent either.

My verdicts are these:

- A woman dies painlessly in her sleep at eighty-five, after a full and satisfying life, with nothing of much value still ahead of her. Her death is bad for her, but only a little.
- A man of thirty is killed instantly and without pain at the height of his powers. His death is bad for him, and gravely so, even though he never experiences anything bad and there is no moment at which he is worse off.
- After a person has died, a fraudster destroys their reputation and fabricates evidence that they committed a crime. This is not bad for the dead person at all; it is bad for those who loved them.
- A patient in an irreversible coma, with no prospect of recovering any capacity for experience, is taken off life support and dies. Their death is not bad for them.
- A patient who has been in unrelievable agony for years and who longs above all to die finally dies. Their death is not bad for them; if anything it is good for them.
- Someone who dies at the very point at which a long and irreversible decline would have begun. Their death is good for them.
- It is not bad for me that I did not exist for the billions of years before I was conceived.
- It is bad for me that I shall one day cease to exist.

The last two are the pair I cannot get to sit together. Each is a stretch of my non-existence. If what makes death bad is the goods it keeps me from, then the stretch before my conception kept me from exactly the same kind of goods, and it is hard to see what the difference is meant to be. The third and fourth cases pull in another direction: they seem to say that a thing can be bad for me only if, at the time, it makes me worse off, or deprives me of something I was actually going to get — but then the second case, in which nothing is taken from me at any moment, should not count either.

The principle I am drawn to is the obvious one: death is bad for a person just in case it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have had, and the badness is proportional to the goods lost. I should like you to test it against all eight verdicts and tell me precisely which of them it cannot accommodate. Then repair it in the smallest way that saves the rest, and say honestly what the repair costs — which other verdict it now threatens, and what awkward question it leaves open.

I am especially interested in three things. First, whether the repair that handles the symmetry with prenatal non-existence is the same repair, or a different one, from the one that handles the reputation case; and if they are different, whether they can be stated as a single principle or have to be two. Second, what the repaired principle predicts for a case I have not listed, which you should construct so as to be genuinely undecided by my verdicts above. Third, the case I keep going back and forth on: a woman with a disease that will kill her painlessly tonight is offered a machine that will, at the moment of death, create a duplicate of her — same memories, same character, same projects and attachments — who will go on living her life indefinitely. She accepts. I cannot decide whether her death was bad for her: everything she cared about continues, and yet she is gone. Tell me whether the repaired principle settles this, and if it does, whether it settles it for a good reason or merely by a stipulation about what is to count as a good lost.
