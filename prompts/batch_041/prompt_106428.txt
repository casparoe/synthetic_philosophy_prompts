A dispute with a colleague — she claimed she deserved no blame for missing a deadline, because it turned out she could not have met it anyway — has left me trying to work out what I actually believe about whether an inability to have done otherwise excuses an agent. Here are the verdicts I keep arriving at when I run through cases.

Nadia gives a large sum to famine relief out of genuine compassion. Unbeknownst to her, a device had been implanted that would have compelled the same donation had she shown any hesitation; she never hesitates. I think she is fully praiseworthy, and the device's idle presence takes nothing away from her.

Omar embezzles from his employer; a device would have forced the same act had he balked. He does not balk. Fully blameworthy.

Priya votes a certain way out of conviction. A device would have produced the same vote, but only by filling her with terror; in fact she feels no terror and votes calmly. Still fully responsible, I say.

When a device actually fires and moves Ravi's hand, with no deliberation of his own, he is not responsible at all.

Sam has a broken leg and cannot leave his house; he stays home gladly and for his own reasons. Responsible.

Tanya steals a watch under a compulsion she can neither resist nor endorse, with no device anywhere in the story. Barely responsible, if at all.

Uma acts as she does, and a device would have fired only had she begun to waver — and its firing would have worked by erasing her grasp of the reasons against the act, leaving her to do it for no reason whatever. She never wavers. Here I am torn, and I cannot say why the mere non-occurrence of that blankness should leave her fully responsible.

Vera gives generously, out of a character engineered in her as a child by a manipulator; nothing looms over her now. I find I cannot give her much credit, even though her act flows from her own settled character and she could have done otherwise.

My tentative principle is that responsibility depends only on the actual sequence of events — on whether the act flowed from the agent's own reasons — and not on what would have happened otherwise. Is that principle, or some repaired version of it, adequate to the verdicts above? If it is, say what it implies for two cases I have not described: an agent who acts from her own reasons but would have responded to almost no other reasons, and an agent whom an idle device would have made do the same act for the same reasons. If no single principle gets everything, give me the candidates, say which of my verdicts each forces me to abandon, and tell me which one you would abandon.
