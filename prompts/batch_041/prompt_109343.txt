I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether our housing cooperative should adopt a futarchy-style procedure for a decision we have been stuck on for months. We are a nine-hundred-unit co-op with an elected board, and we have to choose between raising monthly carrying charges by roughly four percent to rebuild the reserve fund and holding charges flat while taking a loan against the building. Our bylaws currently leave this to the board, but a faction wants to run what it calls a decision market: the board would first define a single measurable welfare metric — reserve months of operating expenses three years out, adjusted by an annual member-satisfaction survey — and then open two conditional contracts, one paying out according to the metric if we raise charges and one paying out according to the metric if we take the loan. Whichever conditional contract trades higher would settle the policy. My friend finds this elegant; I suspect it quietly assumes the very thing it is supposed to decide, and that in a body of nine hundred people who all know each other the market would mostly mirror the loudest faction. Neither of us could get past trading anecdotes, so I would like the argument made properly.

Please set out the most important implications of futarchy, as a governance mechanism, for an organization exactly this small and this homogeneous — not for a nation-state. I am after the design choices that stop being free once the market is thin, the participants are also the people the policy affects, and the metric has to be fixed in advance by the same body that will later be judged by it. In particular, I want to understand the implications of the decision rule being public: does reading the price in order to make the decision change what the price means, and if so, what follows specifically for the two conditional contracts above? And I want to know what becomes of the mechanism's advertised advantage — that it separates the question of what we value from the question of what will happen — when the people betting on the second question are the same people who voted on the first, and can plausibly be identified by their bets. Where the honest answer is that futarchy has no real implications for us because a precondition fails, say so and name the precondition that fails first; I would much rather have a short list of consequences that actually bite than a survey of the idea.
