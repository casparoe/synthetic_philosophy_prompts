I'm currently taking a course on early modern metaphysics, one of whose units is on Leibniz, and I've also been reading some contemporary material on mind uploading; I'd like to see the two brought together. Please reason out, from Leibniz's own commitments, what verdict he would have reached on a case he never encountered, and defend that verdict as he would have defended it. I'm interested in the argument rather than in any passage you may happen to recall, so please don't lean on sources or citations.

The case is this. A living human being undergoes a procedure in which their neurons are replaced one at a time by silicon devices that are functionally equivalent in every respect: identical inputs and outputs, identical memory traces, identical dispositions, so that at no stage does the subject's behaviour or inner life deviate from what it would otherwise have been. When the last biological neuron is gone, the resulting system behaves exactly as its predecessor would have — it reports the same memories, answers to the same name, and forms the same plans. Consider also a variant in which the whole replacement happens instantaneously rather than step by step.

What would Leibniz say about whether the original person survives, in each variant? I expect his doctrines will not all point the same way, so please make the conflict explicit rather than smoothing it over: set out the principles that bear on the question, say which one he would let govern the verdict, and justify that choice on his behalf. Say whether he would treat the gradual and the instantaneous cases differently, and why. Say whether he would allow that the finished system is a person at all — perhaps a new one, furnished with memories that are not really its own — or only an exceptionally lifelike automaton, and what that implies about the criterion of personal identity his view presupposes, as against memory-based or continuity-based criteria. Finally, state the strongest objection a contemporary philosopher would raise against his verdict, including the worry that it turns on a difference no possible observation could detect, and indicate how he might answer it.
