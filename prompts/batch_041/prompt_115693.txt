I'm a fourth-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and through a series of bad decisions I've become the person who vets the weird-history submissions at a small online history magazine — three or four reader-submited claims a month, each of which turns into a 1,500-word piece under my byline. The editor-in-chief signs off, but she's a Victorianist and has made it clear that the magazine's reputation, and its legal exposure, is mine. I've been improvising, and I write a different piece depending on how tired I am, which is not a method.

Please write me a procedure I can actually run, and that I can hand to a freelancer with a humanities MA and no archaeology so we get the same verdict twice. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict and where it hands the decision back to me because it's a values call or a judgment about credibility, which step is doing the work in each verdict (so a reader who disagrees can attack the right step instead of the whole thing), and an honest statement of what the procedure can't do and where two competent people would run it differently. I also want the verdicts seperated more finely than pseudoscience / not pseudoscience: false but not pseudoscientific, unfalsifiable, contested but legitimate, pseudoscientific, and whatever else you think earns its place. Each verdict has to come with what it means for the column — publish a debunk, publish an explainer, publish something about why people want to believe this, or spike it.

Some pattern notes to build in, but be careful with them. The submissions come in four shapes: the compression move (real history is shorter than advertised, the Middle Ages were duplicated, the standard chronology was manufactured in the early modern period); the diffusion move (one lost civilization or one lost source explains every resemblance on earth); the anomaly move (one artifact, inscription, or old map the field can't explain, therefore the field is a fraud); and the suppression move (you weren't taught it, and that's the proof). Treat these as diagnostics of argumentative style only, and say plainly that none of them licenses a verdict on its own, because real scholarship does all four sometimes — there's genuine fighting among specialists over whether parts of ancient chronology are stretched by a century or two, diffusion really happens, and anomalies really have overturned fields. The procedure has to say where the difference actually lives.

Also don't tell me that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence; I know, and so does the reader. Give me something specific to chronological claims: which dating chains are genuinely independent of one another (tree rings, calibrated radiocarbon, eclipse and astronomical records, tephra, coin sequences, the synchronisms tying Egypt, Assyria, Anatolia and the Aegean together), and what a claim like this would have to survive before I print anything.

Run it on three submissions: (a) a reader who says antiquity actually happened in the Middle Ages, citing statistical patterns in lists of rulers and duplicated events; (b) a reader who says the Gilded Age train stations and the star forts are the remains of a lost global empire wiped out by a mud flood and then written out of the record; (c) a reader who says a comet airburst caused the Younger Dryas and that the establishment buried the evidence — that last one worries me, because the hypothesis and the legend riding on it may be two different things, and I suspect the procedure's verdict isn't the one I'd like.

I've got about three hours per claim and no library. No reading lists or citations — I have the literature, I want the procedure. American English.
