Ok so I teach a grad seminar that mixes consciousness stuff with ethics, and there's a version of the panpsychism question I keep getting stuck on that I think is weirdly under-discussed. I want your honest number on it, and I want the reasoning dragged out into the open, not a hedge.

Here's the proposition. Take some form of panpsychism or Russellian monism — the view that phenomenal/experiential properties are among the fundamental features of reality rather than something that pops into existence only once you assemble the right kind of system. Assume for the sake of the question that this is true. Now price this conditional claim: in that world, ordinary physical goings-on (a rock just sitting there, hydrogen fusing in a star, an electron scattering off a proton) contain enormous numbers of morally considerable states — where "morally considerable" means having some intrinsic positive or negative valence, i.e. some way it feels good or bad to be that thing.

So: given panpsychism/Russellian monism, how likely is it that physics is saturated with valenced, morally considerable experience?

Decompose it for me. Give me the sub-questions the number actually rests on, a rough probability on each, one line of justification each, and then show how they combine. The ones I think are in play (add or cut as you see fit):

- the prior that experiential properties are fundamental at all;
- given that, whether fundamental experience comes with valence or is more like a neutral "there's something it's like" with no good/bad character — the colleague I argue with insists this gap between experience and valence is huge and does alot of the work;
- whether any valenced state, however thin, counts morally, or whether there's some threshold / integration requirement that filters the micro-level out;
- whether micro-valences even aggregate into anything that matters, or whether summation across tiny subjects is a category mistake;
- and whether, even if all of that goes through, the ethical upshot actually follows or gets screened off by something else (that we couldn't act on it, or that it's not a harm anyone can own, etc.).

I'm sitting at about 0.35; my colleague is at about 0.75. Give me your own number, but more usefully: tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives on, and which single term your number is most sensitive to. Then tell me what would actually move it — some argument or consideration, not a new empirical result, since we're not going to get one. And if you think the proposition is definately too vague to have a truth value as stated, tell me how you'd tighten it so it does, and then give me the number on the tightened version.
