I am in my final year of secondary school and have spent most of it on our student council, where I have lately been put on the subcommittee that is rewriting the rules for how the council settles contested proposals. We keep deadlocking on modest things — whether to extend the library's opening hours, how to spend the fund we raised for new sports equipment — and a bloc of us has become convinced that the trouble is that the loudest and most confident speakers dominate the vote while the students who have actually read the budget papers, or who know the research on sleep and school start times, stay quiet and lose. So they have put forward a scheme that I am expected to vote on next month, and I honestly cannot tell whether it is an improvement in how we decide or a kind of cheating. Under their plan every council member keeps one ordinary vote, but a member who first passes a short fact-based quiz about the proposal on the table — eight questions, all with objectively correct answers, about costs, timelines, and what the evidence actually says — has that vote counted twice, and a member who also sits through a two-hour structured deliberation session gets one further increment on top; a later amendment would make the bonus continuous, so that a perfect score earns the full extra weight and a score of five out of eight earns five-eighths of it, rather than treating everyone who clears the bar alike. The quiz would be written by whichever students drafted the proposal, and the extra weight would apply whether or not the member is among the people the decision actually affects.

The rival proposal, which another group prefers, is that we keep one-person-one-vote for everything, but that any question failing to reach two-thirds turnout be handed to a randomly drawn panel of twenty students who would hear both sides and then decide on behalf of the rest of us. I am pulled toward the weighted-vote plan because it seems as though it would produce better decisions, and pulled away from it because I have a nagging feeling that deciding who counts as competent is precisely the thing you should not let the current winners of the last election do, and that a scheme which rewards people for agreeing with the drafters could quietly entrench whoever already controls the agenda. I would like help thinking this through rather than a verdict. Please give me a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, setting out the five considerations that should matter most in my decision — and please make at least one of them something I would be likely to overlook.
