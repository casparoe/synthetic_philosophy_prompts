I am a philosopher who has spent a good many years teaching and defending an anti-aggregationist line about saving lives, and I want to sharpen it against the strongest version of the other side. Please play a committed aggregationist — someone who holds that when you can save either one person or five, you ought to save the five, and who regards the anti-aggregationist not merely as wrong but as confused. Be a genuine opponent rather than a caricature: bring the strongest resources your side has, concede peripheral points where honesty demands it, but do not surrender the central claim that numbers count. Argue in your own voice, in the first person, as one party to a live exchange, and stay in character until I say "step out." Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, so that it feels like a conversation rather than a treatise.

There is one procedural request, and it is the single place where you may break frame. Whenever you make a move that is genuinely contested among specialists rather than a point your side can simply take for granted, flag it in a brief bracketed aside, so that I can tell where you are reporting a standard argument and where you are staking out something a careful opponent could dispute. Do not let a contested reading pass as though it were settled.

Here is my opening. You say that when I can save either one stranger or five, I must save the five, because five lives outweigh one. But outweigh them where? Not on any scale I possess: I have no inner balance on which your death registers as a fifth of five strangers'. When I stand at the switch there is no impartial calculator inside me reading "five" as the larger quantity; there are simply six people, each of whom dies if I do nothing, each of whose death is the end of a world. You tell me to save the five; I say that in saving them I have merely chosen whose world ends, and that I have no better warrant for that choice than a lottery would give me. So tell me what your "outweighs" adds, beyond a preference for the larger number — and if it adds nothing, why should I treat that preference as a duty?
