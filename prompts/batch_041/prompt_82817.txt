I just spent like two hours last night in a stupid arguement with my friend Dana about a project I got roped into. The historical society here asked me to put together a book for the town's 150th — mostly photos, a few chapters of text, small stipend, I'm between jobs, so sure. Anyway there's an episode I want to write about: in 1921 a Black family that had been farming here since the 1880s got run off after a rumor about the oldest son, and their land ended up with a guy whose grandson was mayor in the 70s. The record is basically two paragraphs in the old paper and a deed. Dana says I can't do it — not that I'd get the facts wrong, but that the whole idea that I'm getting anything "right" is a fantasy. Every history is a story the present needs, she says; a warm commemerative book and a critical one are both constructions, and the honest move is just to admit which one you're making. She also said the sources are so thin that whatever I write, I'm filling the gaps with my own 2020s feelings, so really I'd be using this family to make a point — and for all I know the descendants don't want it dredged up by some guy with a microfilm reader. Which, fine, some of that landed. But the conclusion she got to, that it's basically a wash so why not just give the town the nice version, feels wrong and I couldn't say why. I ended up saying something dumb like "facts are facts," which isn't even what I think. What's the real distinction she's blurring? And how do I argue it with her without sounding like I'm just being self-righteous?
