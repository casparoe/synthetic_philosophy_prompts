I am in the final weeks of a seminar on Nietzsche's late works, and I am trying to decide whether his scattered remarks on diet and health amount to a position or only to a set of autobiographical idiosyncrasies. The verdicts I have collected run roughly as follows. He treats vegetarianism as wrong for him, and he is contemptuous of it whenever it presents itself as moral purification rather than as a personal experiment. He treats alcohol, beer and wine especially, as a depressant that falsifies one's sense of wellbeing, and he is dismissive of strong tea and coffee as stimulants that darken and tax rather than nourish. He prefers small, plain, light meals, finds heavy or elaborate cooking disabling, and claims to work better on the plain fare. He insists that the question of place and climate comes before the question of diet, and he credits particular locations with his productive final year. He refuses to generalize: what suits one constitution ruins another, and a universal dietary law handed down by physicians or moralists is itself a symptom of decline. He distrusts regimens adopted for the sake of longevity, comfort, or contentment, and he is drawn to cold, altitude, exertion, and walking as conditions of thought. And in the same late chapters he reads the moralized dietary codes of religions as instruments of self-denial rather than of health.

A friend of mine has now adopted what she calls the alpine protocol: no alcohol, no caffeine, no animal products, one substantial meal in the late afternoon, ninety minutes of walking and twenty minutes of deliberate cold exposure daily, a strict limit on reading and news, and residence in a cold, bright, high place. She logs sleep, mood, resting heart rate, and hours of focused work every day, and she adjusts the protocol by the numbers. She reports feeling worse for the first fortnight and considerably more productive thereafter. She has also made the protocol a rule she will not suspend, on the grounds that suspending it would show her discipline was never real.

Assume that the verdicts above are his and that he is being consistent with them, and predict what he would say about her. Please do the following. First, set out at least three rules that fit his earlier verdicts equally well; I suspect that at least one of them is really about the moral form of a practice rather than its physiological content, and I want that separation made explicit. Second, say what each rule predicts about my friend, and mark where they come apart. I am particularly unsure whether her daily self-quantification counts as the kind of disciplined self-experiment he admires or as the outsourcing of self-knowledge to impersonal measurement, and whether her no-exceptions rule is the hardness he praises or the moralized asceticism he attacks. Third, give your best guess at his verdict, with your reasons for preferring the rule you do. Fourth, name a further case, other than my friend's, that would discriminate among the surviving rules, and say what verdict each rule would deliver there. Finally, since his own health was evidently poor and his regimen plainly did not preserve it, tell me whether that should count against his implicit standard or whether it is irrelevant given what he seems to take that standard to be.
