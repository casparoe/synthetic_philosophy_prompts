For the better part of a career I built and validated risk models — tuberculosis screening in a public health service, then underwriting — and I have spent my retirement reading the philosophical literature that has grown up around what my former colleagues now call algorithmic fairness. I have reconstructed an argument from that literature that I cannot dislodge, though I am confident it is unsound. I would like you to tell me where it breaks.

(1) A scoring instrument, whether silicon or a committee of clinicians, is fair only if a score means the same thing whoever receives it: among those assigned the same score, the same proportion actually has the outcome, and the instrument does not systematically over- or under-predict for any group.
(2) It is also unfair for the cost of the instrument's mistakes to fall on one group rather than another, so the false-positive rate and the false-negative rate should each be equal across groups.
(3) The impossibility results worked out in the machine-learning literature in the middle of the last decade show that when two groups have different base rates and the instrument is less than perfect, these requirements cannot all hold at once; every instrument must trade one against the others.
(4) Therefore every instrument that must operate under unequal base rates is unfair, and the choice of which fairness criterion to satisfy is merely a choice of whom to sacrifice.
(5) Nothing in (3) turns on the instrument being an algorithm. A committee of clinicians reading the same files and applying the same criteria faces the same base rates and produces the same error structure.
(6) Therefore the committee is unfair in exactly the same way, and so is any procedure for allocating a scarce good under unequal base rates.
(7) But a predicate that condemns every procedure condemns none. If every way of allocating is unfair, then fairness marks no distinction, and the demand for it collapses into a demand that one group's errors be weighted more heavily than another's.
(8) Since we must allocate regardless, we should abandon the language of fairness and simply adopt the rule that does the most good overall.

I find (8) repellent, and I would rather surrender (4) than accept it; I have watched too many conscientious clinicians do this arithmetic in their heads to believe they were all merely picking a victim. But I want to know what I am paying. Identify the step that fails, explain why it looked compelling, and tell me what each of the going positions has to concede to reject it: those who say the impossibility result merely forces an explicit, defensible choice of metric; those who say it indicts the metric framework rather than the possibility of fairness; those who say the trouble lies in single-threshold scoring and that richer prediction or randomization escapes it; and those who relocate fairness from the arithmetic of errors to the reasons a decision can be given to the person it falls on. I would also like to know which of these, if any, survives contact with the fact that a human committee is subject to the same theorem.
