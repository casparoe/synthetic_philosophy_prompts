In my department's reading group on skill and knowledge, two colleagues — call them Nadia and Owen — spent most of last week's session locked in an argument, and I came away unable to say who had the better of it. I have no stake in either position and report the exchange as fairly as I can reconstruct it.

Nadia holds that knowing how to do something is knowing, of some way that is in fact a way to do it, that it is a way to do it — with the proviso that the relevant proposition need not be one the agent could put into words, since it may be grasped under a practical mode of presentation. Owen holds that knowing how is an ability or a disposition to succeed, not a propositional attitude at all, and that the practical mode of presentation is a label doing no explanatory work.

(1) Owen opened with a sorter who reliably tells chicks' sexes but cannot say how, and said this refutes Nadia: here is know-how with no articulable know-that.
(2) Nadia replied that the case is irrelevant, since articulacy was never required; the sorter knows of a way, just not under a discursive mode of presentation.
(3) Owen: then no case could ever tell against the view, which makes it a redescription of the phenomenon rather than a theory of it.
(4) Nadia: not so — the view predicts that know-how and the corresponding know-that stand or fall together, so losing the ability should take the knowledge with it, and that know-how is answerable to justification and defeat as knowledge-that is and a mere capacity is not. She then turned Owen's own style of case against him with a coach who knows how a move is to be executed but can no longer execute it, which she takes to show that know-how comes apart from ability.
(5) Owen answered that the coach knows how the move is done, which is propositional, and that the case trades on an ambiguity in the phrase; on the ability reading, the coach simply no longer knows how to do it.
(6) Nadia then met the familiar regress — that applying propositional knowledge would itself require know-how, and so on — by saying it runs only if propositional knowledge is a rule to be consulted before acting, whereas knowing that something is so is a state, not a procedure.
(7) Owen: a state the agent merely occupies cannot explain the intelligent exercise we set out to explain; and know-how comes in degrees and is displayed in performance in a way that knowledge-that is not.

Please adjudicate this move by move — say of each whether it is clearly right, defensible but overstated, or mistaken, and why — then say who has the better of the exchange overall and what both parties are missing. Finally, tell me which consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
