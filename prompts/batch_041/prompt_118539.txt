My two flatmates got into it at dinner last night and it went on so long I just kept eating and listening, and now they want me to settle it and I honestly can't. It was about that old puzzle: you can save one person or you can save two, everyone's a stranger, everyone's equally healthy, no other differences, and you can't save all three. Here's the shape of it, roughly.

Dev said you obviously save the two — more lives saved is better, end of story. Ravi said it's not obvious at all. He said the two aren't a single thing that has a claim; each of them has his own claim, and saving the two doesn't make the one any better off, so there's nothing sitting on the other side of the scale to outweigh him. He said he'd probably still save the two, but as a coin-flip-type tiebreak, not because two lives are worth more than one.

Dev: then how do you stop a surgeon from killing one patient to save five with the organs? On your view the five don't outweigh the one either, so nothing rules it out.
Ravi: the transplant case is different — there you're using the one as a tool without his consent. The numbers question is about who to save when everyone is already in trouble. Different wrong.
Dev: that just moves the whole thing onto a killing-versus-letting-die line you haven't defended.

Then Ravi tried an isolation test. What if it's one person who'd live another 60 years versus two people who'd each live another 30? Same total years either way. He said if the count really matters by itself, Dev has to save the two; if what Dev actually cares about is years of life, Dev should be indifferent between the options. Dev said he'd still save the two, and that this proves the count matters on its own. Ravi said no, it proves Dev is dressing up a tiebreaker as a reason.

Dev came back with something I hadn't heard before. He said counting doesn't have to be a claim about value at all — you can treat the count as a procedure, a rule for deciding, while refusing to say two persons are worth more than one, the same way you can treat a queue as a fair way to order people without thinking the people at the front are more valuable. He said Ravi keeps demanding that the numbers show up as a value and that's the only place he's looking.

Ravi said that's just relabelling. If the procedure tells you to let a person die when you could have saved him, then the procedure is making a value claim whether you admit it or not, and calling it a procedure doesn't get you off the hook. He also said: try the procedure on a case where the two are both 95 and the one is 20 — if your rule still says save the two, you've smuggled in something about numbers that you said you weren't claiming.

Neither would move and I got stuck refereeing. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing? You don't need to cite anyone or point me to papers, just reason it out. And at the end say who's more right overall.
