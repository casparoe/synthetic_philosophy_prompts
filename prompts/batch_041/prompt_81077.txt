I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on language acquisition and the philosophy of mind, and I need to submit a term-paper proposal by the end of the month. The paper is to be about 6,000 words and is due at the end of the semester. I have a half-formed idea that I would like you to turn into a full proposal.

The idea is this. Debates about linguistic innateness standardly appeal to the poverty of the stimulus, but I think that label hides at least three separable claims: first, an empirical claim that the primary linguistic data underdetermine the target grammar because children receive no systematic negative evidence and because the data are finite and noisy; second, a formal claim that no learner in a psychologically plausible hypothesis class could identify the target grammar from any finite sample of the kind children actually receive; and third, an explanatory claim that the best explanation of children's success is an innate, domain-specific constraint on the hypothesis space. I suspect the nativist conclusion is often treated as following from the first claim, when in fact it requires the second, and that the second is either trivial or false depending on how the hypothesis class and the learning criterion are specified. I want the paper to separate these claims and ask whether any version of the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument survives once they are distinguished. I have been calling them input poverty, learnability poverty, and hypothesis-space poverty, though I am not attached to the labels.

Please write the proposal. It should state the central question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and say what would count as resolving it. It should explain why the existing positions in the debate leave the question open, without assuming that I already know the literature by citation. It should describe the method I would actually use month by month, and why a conceptual and formal approach is the right one rather than, say, a survey of acquisition data. It should say what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them. It should give an honest assessment of what could make the project too big for a semester, and what a scaled-down version would look like. Finally, it should include a short section on what would count as the project failing.

I do not need a bibliography or reading list; I will add citations myself. The proposal should be written for a reader who knows the basic debate but not my particular angle, and it should make a case that the project is worth doing even if the conclusion turns out to be negative.
