I'm putting together the final for a masters course on formal epistemology and decision theory, and I want a single long essay question on imprecise probabilities that they can't just answer by regurgitating the Ellsberg stuff. The crux I want them to hit is the tension between two things that both look right: (i) when you genuinely don't know the odds, representing your uncertainty as a set of probability functions rather than one is the honest thing to do, and (ii) once you do that, what counts as a rational choice starts to hang on things it shouldn't -- how you carve up the possibilities, the order in which you learn things, that sort of thing.

To stop them recycling the textbook cases I've built a toy one they won't have seen. A regulator has to choose between rolling out (R) and holding back (H) on a new intervention. Three states, s1 s2 s3. The expert evidence doesn't pin down a single distribution; it gives a credal set: all distributions over the three states with p1 somewhere in [0.2, 0.5] and p3 somewhere in [0.1, 0.4] (p2 takes up the rest). Utilities, R pays 10, 4, 0 in s1 s2 s3; H pays 0, 5, 9. I've also defined a decision rule I'm calling 'guarded dominance': R beats H just in case the minimum expected utility of R is greater than the maximum expected utility of H; if neither beats the other that way, you may pick either but you have to defend it. I know it's close to something in the literature but I've deliberately given it a name that isn't googleable, so they have to reason about the rule itself instead of looking it up.

What I need from you: the exam question (90 minutes, essay, so aim for a model answer of roughly 800-1000 words), then the model answer, then a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points, each with the marks it's worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically fumble that point. The question has to force them to work out what guarded dominance actually selects in my case and whether that's defensible; to say what a defender of imprecise probabilities should do when the interval is wide enough that no act dominates; and to connect it to the partition/order sensitivity worry -- the 'dilation' thing, where learning something you'd have sworn was irrelevant makes your interval wider. But I dont want the question to presuppose they've read the dilation literature, so make it self-contained about that part.

And please dont let the model answer be a list of positions; it should take a line on whether guarded dominance is a reasonable rule and actually earn it. The marking scheme should seperate an A from a B by naming the specific move each answer has to make, not by vague 'depth of understanding' waffle.
