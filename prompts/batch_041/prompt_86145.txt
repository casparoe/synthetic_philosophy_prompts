ok so I'm a third-year PhD on the logic/AI-safety side (my thesis is on proof-based agents, mostly Löb-ish stuff), and I've somehow been volunteered to run next weeks session of our department's ML reading group. The crowd is eight or so people, mostly deep-RL folks who are great with probability and PyTorch but have basically zero proof theory — one or two have poked at Lean/Coq so they know what a proof is, but nobody has seen a provability predicate or a modal logic, and nobody there has read any of the alignment-theory stuff, so I can't assume any of it is familiar.

Topic I'm down to cover: the Löbian obstacle — why a proof-based agent can't just reason its way into trusting itself. You can assume I'll put the math on the board myself; I don't need you to derive anything or explain Löb to me. The setup is an agent whose decision rule is "take action A only if I can prove, in my own formal theory T, that A is safe," and Löb's theorem says if T proves (□P → P) for a sentence P then T already proves P. So if the agent adds the natural self-trust schema "whenever I prove something, it's true," it can prove every instance of that schema's antecedent and ends up proving everything, unsafe things included — the naive self-trusting agent is either inconsistent or has to have its trust installed from outside rather than earned by its own reasoning. And if there's time I want a second beat: the same flavour of obstacle when the agent has to trust a *successor* it builds or becomes, the tiling / self-modification version, which is the one that actually matters for self-improving systems.

What I want from you is a teaching plan, not the content. Specifically:

(1) the dependency chain — what has to come before what, for people who've never seen a provability predicate. Do I need Gödel first, or can I get away with Löb plus a handwave at the diagonal lemma, and what can I safely just assert?

(2) the one example or case that carries the whole session. It has to be concrete enough that an RL person feels it in the gut, and I do NOT want the stock "robot won't press the button until it proves the button is safe" story — they've half-heard it and it makes the whole thing sound like a toy. What example actually does the work, and what does it need to have and not have?

(3) the misconceptions they'll form. My guesses: that this is just the halting problem / a computability result rather than a fixed-point self-reference thing; that it means self-trust is impossible in general rather than that this particular proof-based route to it fails; that an RL agent would just learn empirically to trust itself so the whole thing is an artifact of the proof-based framing; and that Gödel and Löb are the same theorem. Where in the sequence does each need to be headed off, and which of them is actually a fair objection I should concede rather than defuse?

(4) one question to ask at the end that tells me whether they got it versus just nodded along.

(5) what I should openly tell them I'm not going to cover at this level.

And if you think the successor/tiling beat needs to be its own session rather than squeezed in, say so and tell me where to cut.
