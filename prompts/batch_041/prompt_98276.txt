I am not an academic. I run a monthly philosophy discussion group at a community center, and for some time now I have been trying, mostly on my own, to get to the bottom of a puzzle about value and admiration. I would like to set the group a written exercise, and I want you to write the exercise, a model answer, and a marking scheme I can use to score it. The group is made up of adults who read a fair amount but are not specialists, so please pitch everything to a bright non-specialist.

Here is the puzzle in my own words. There is a tempting way of explaining what it is for something to be valuable: to be valuable is simply to be a fitting object of valuing. On this view, something is good just when it is correct or appropriate to admire, cherish, or respect it, and the two sides of that claim are supposed to be the same fact seen from two directions. If that is right, then whenever there is a genuine reason to admire a thing, the thing is to that extent good, and whenever a thing is good, there is a reason to admire it.

The trouble is that some reasons to admire something seem to have nothing to do with the thing being admirable. Suppose a very powerful person tells me that he will hurt me unless I admire him. I now have a strong reason to admire him. But it would be absurd to conclude that he has become admirable, or that my admiration would be correct. His threat is a reason that concerns my having the attitude, not a reason that concerns the object of the attitude. I have seen writers call the first sort of reason object-given and the second sort state-given, and the point of the second sort is that it is a reason to want to have an attitude, or to arrange to have it, rather than a reason that the attitude is correct. The puzzle is that the neat explanation of value above seems unable to tell the two apart, so it licenses the absurd conclusion. I gather this is called the problem of the wrong kind of reasons.

I want the exercise to make the group work on a case of their own rather than on the tired threat example, so please use this one, which I made up and which I believe is less well-worn. A city has an annual award for its most admirable resident. The award is decided by a small committee, and the rule is that the prize goes to whichever candidate the committee most admires; the committee cannot simply hand the prize to someone it does not admire. This year there are two candidates. One, Rosa, is genuinely admirable in an ordinary way. The other, Victor, is not admirable at all, being petty and dishonest, but he has pledged that if he wins he will fund a clinic that will save many lives, and no one else will. So if the committee admires Victor, the clinic is built; if it admires Rosa, Victor wins nothing and the clinic is not built. The committee therefore has a powerful reason to admire Victor, namely that doing so will save lives. The case is meant to raise the question whether that reason is a reason for Victor's being admirable, or only a reason to want to admire him, and what the neat explanation of value is forced to say about it.

I also want the group to test a proposed fix that I have not seen stated anywhere, which I will call the isolation test. The proposal is this: a reason to have an attitude counts as a reason of the right kind, and so bears on whether the object is valuable, only if it would still be a reason to have that attitude in a world where having the attitude made no difference to anything at all, where no one could ever detect the attitude and where holding it changed nothing in the world, including nothing about the person holding it. The idea is that the threat in the powerful-person example would vanish in such a world, since a threat works only through consequences, while a reason that genuinely concerns the object would survive. I would like the exercise to press on whether the isolation test actually works, and to look for cases where it gives a verdict we would not want.

So please write, in American English, the following. First, one essay question, divided into parts, that a careful adult could answer in about an hour without any reference materials, and that cannot be answered merely by reciting the labels. It should require them to explain the two kinds of reasons and why the distinction matters for the explanation of value; to apply the distinction to the award case and say what the explanation of value is forced to conclude there, and whether that is acceptable; to evaluate the isolation test, including at least one case where it seems to give the wrong answer; and to say what any successful solution to the puzzle would have to accomplish, and whether they think the explanation of value can be saved. Assign marks to the parts so that they total twenty.

Second, a model answer of about six hundred words that actually answers the question, written so that a bright non-specialist can follow it, and that takes a definite line rather than listing options.

Third, a marking scheme. It should name the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks, with the marks attached; it should make clear what separates an A answer from a B answer; and it should note at least one tempting wrong turn for each part.

Please do not require the group to cite any books or articles, and do not have the model answer lean on names or dates; I want it to stand on its own reasoning.
