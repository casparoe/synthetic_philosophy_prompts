I've been going down a rabbit hole on this and I want the two sides to actually have it out, so here's the setup. A friend of mine, Priya, is obsessed with this matching platform (she calls it Quid Pro Bono, I think the name is a joke) that pairs people with opposing moral priorities and arranges swaps between them. You register what you care about most and what you'd be willing to do that you otherwise wouldn't, and it finds you a counterpart whose priorities run the other way. Her favorite example: a woman whose top cause is animal welfare agrees to vote for a candidate she thinks is terrible on gun policy, and in exchange a gun-rights guy agrees to go vegan for a year and give a chunk of his income to farmed-animal advocacy. The whole selling point is that no agreement on values is required — each side only has to come out ahead in their own moral accounting, so the swap is supposed to be a pure win-win, which is a strange and interesting idea. The platform has some design tricks to stop people gaming it: pledges go into escrow with a third party, a swap only fires if both parties clear a "surplus" threshold, and there's a forfeitable bond, so you can't just inflate how much you claim to care in order to shake concessions out of someone. Priya thinks this is the most important moral invention in a century — it turns moral disagreement from a fight into a market, and it rewards intensity, since whoever cares most gains most from the trade. My other friend Marcus thinks it's a horror show with good UX, and that the mechanism-design stuff actually makes it worse, because it hides what's happening behind talk of "surplus."

What I want is a dialogue between Priya and Marcus — think of yourself as a philosopher who works on moral conflict, partiality, and the ethics of cooperation, and be careful not to let either of them be a pushover, they're both smart and I dont want one of them winning on easy mode. I'd rather you spend a bit of time first brainstorming the strongest version of each position (just a few bullets to yourself, thinking carefully about where the real pressure points are) and only then write the scene, because the failure mode here is a dialogue where one side is obviosuly right and just waits for the other to say something dumb.

Some stuff I'd like to see them actually get into: whether there are commitments that can't be traded without being abandoned — i.e. whether "I'll tolerate one wrong thing if you'll tolerate another" is even coherent, or is just doing the wrong thing with extra steps; whether the swap instrumentalizes the other person's values or corrodes the trader's own integrity (and whether the woman who trades her vote still has any standing to say what she says she believes); whether a bond-and-escrow design can really stop a market in moral concessions from rewarding people who manufacture extreme positions in order to sell them; and whether the "no agreement on values needed" claim is actually true or quietly presupposes a common unit of how much someone cares. Let them push back on each other's actual arguments rather than trading monologues — if Priya makes a point, Marcus should answer that point specifically, and vice versa — and let it end where it ends, maybe with a line or two about what they now think they were really disagreeing about.
