Hey — I need a hand with something I've been avoiding all week. I'm a fourth-year in a philosophy of biology program, and my department is hosting a one-day workshop on Saturday for working science journalists (beat reporters, two freelancers, a museum educator, plus two of our own biologists who are coming mainly to heckle). The theme is how you write about animal behaviour without either saying the animal did it on purpose or flattening everything into 'it's just instinct.' I got volun-told to give the opening remarks — 8 to 10 minutes, right after coffee — and they have to set up the day without settling it, because the afternoon is literally two people who disagree with each other.

Here's the idea I want to build around. My advisor keeps saying that a lot of the time when we say what a trait or a behaviour is for, we're citing a reason that nobody ever had. The wing is for flying. The bee's dance is for telling the hive where the flowers are. No designer, and neither the wing nor the bee is doing anything like thinking it through. But the 'for' isn't a metaphor or lazy shorthand either — it's earned, because the thing is the way it is (roughly) because it kept doing that job, at the level of who survived and left copies. So the reason is real, it's just not anyone's. People in my field call these free-floating rationales. I think I want to use the phrase once, then never again, because it sounds like a band name.

Where it gets hairy — and I want the remarks to be honest here instead of slick — is that the reason-without-a-reasoner story works cleanly when there's a selection history, and I have two cases where I genuinely can't tell whether it applies, and I know journalists will definately ask about both.

First, the path. You know the dirt path across a campus quad that the grounds crew never built, just worn in by people cutting the corner. It's for getting from the library to the dining hall. Nobody decided that, there's no selection, no generations, no inheritance. Is that the same kind of thing as the wing? My advisor says no — every single walker had a reason of their own, so the reason isn't floating, it's just pooled. But to a journalist the path and the dance look like the same phenomenon, and I'm not sure her line is as clean as she thinks.

Second, the one that actually broke my brain, from the lab down the hall. They've got a beetle whose back ridges measurably reduce drag when it moves through sand — take the ridges off and it slows down. But the ridge pattern isn't selected for: it's a buckling pattern that falls out of the way the tissue dries, same pattern every time, and they can't get it not to happen. No variation, so nothing for selection to grab. So is the ridge 'for' reducing drag? There's a real benefit, and if you ask why it's that shape there's a true answer that mentions drag — but the drag didn't make it that shape. That feels like a different animal to me and I don't want to pretend otherwise.

So: write me the remarks. No jargon (no 'teleology', no 'function' as a technical term, nothing about proximate and ultimate), no philosophers names, no papers, no citations — half the room will check out the second it sounds like a seminar. They should leave with something usable, i.e. a rough rule of thumb for when a 'for' is earned and when it's wishful, because they've all got editors pushing them to make animal behaviour vivid. But it can't be a rule of thumb I'm going to watch get taken apart at 2pm, so where the rule breaks, say so out loud, in a way a non-philosopher can hear without concluding the whole thing is mush. Keep it warm — these people gave up a Saturday.

If you take a side on the path or the beetle, fine, take one, but flag it as a call and not as consensus. Thanks — this is due Thursday and I have 300 words of it that I hate.
