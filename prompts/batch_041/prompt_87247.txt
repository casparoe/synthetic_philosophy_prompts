I am a junior and the student representative on my school district's Commemorations Review Committee, which the school board created this fall after a petition about the name of one of our middle schools. Our charge covers everything the district owns — school and building names, plaques, portraits, trophies, the two memorial trees on the front lawn — and we have also been asked by the city for advisory opinions on three street names near campus. We expect three or four requests a semester for several years, so we need a standing method rather than a one-time answer. Our recommendations go to the school board, which has the final vote, and the local paper covers every meeting. The stakes cut both ways: a bad call either leaves a real harm standing in a place students walk past every day, or removes something the community would grieve, and either way it makes the next case harder to decide.

What I need from you is a decision procedure I can put in front of a committee of eight people — two teachers, four parents, two students — none of whom has any philosophy background, which we can run on one case in a single fifty-minute meeting after about two hours of homework. Please do not give me a reading list or a list of sources; I need a worksheet.

Over the past month I have collected the distinctions that seem to be doing the actual work in these arguments, and I want the procedure built so that they survive translation into plain language. I am fairly sure that if we drop them we will end up deciding by who speaks loudest:

1. Honor versus record: whether the thing asks the public to esteem a person, or merely to remember that an event happened. A bronze figure on a pedestal at the front entrance is doing the first; a plaque at a trailhead is usually doing the second.
2. Whether the wrongdoing was the point: whether the honor exists because of the conduct now objected to, or whether the conduct is incidental to an honor given for something else entirely, as with a physician honored for a vaccine who also held repugnant views about who deserved it.
3. Whether the name still functions as an honor or has worn down into a label: whether anyone now knows who it names, whether the district uses it ceremonially, whether it appears on letterhead and trophies, or whether it is a tag like a street named after a family nobody can identify.
4. Supersession: whether the community has already answered the wrong through apology, restitution, or a change in law, so that the monument now records a repudiated past — and the opposite case, where the monument is the last unrepudiated endorsement left standing.
5. What the thing says to people now, as distinct from what it says about the past: the claim that the objectionable part of a monument is not the dead person's conduct but the message a present-day institution is sending to present-day students.
6. Standing: who gets to decide, and whose interest counts — descendants of those harmed, current residents, the district as owner, the donors' families, the students who pass it every day.
7. Options other than remove or keep: relocation, dual naming with a transition period, a second plaque that contests the first, an adjacent counter-monument, or converting an honor into a record.
8. Consistency across the whole inventory: whatever test we adopt has to be applied to every name and object the district owns, and if it condemns things the community will never touch, then either the test is wrong or the community's standard is inconsistent and should be stated as such.
9. The presentism objection — that we are judging people by standards they could not have held — and the standard reply, that the decision to keep a monument is being made now, by us.
10. Who bears the cost of each option, since removal and retention both impose costs, and not on the same people.

Please turn this into an ordered set of questions the committee asks, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation to keep the thing as it is, where it stops with a recommendation to change it, and where it must hand the decision upward — to the school board because the remaining question is about values rather than facts, or to the district's lawyer because it turns on deeds, contracts, or the state's rules for renaming. I would rather have ordered judgments than a point system, so please do not invent a scoring formula unless you can defend it; if a step is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, mark it as one.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, the middle school named for a local businessman who donated the land and the first building, and whose papers show he also organized and paid for the campaign that pushed a Native community off the river bottom in the 1880s. My family has gone to that school for three generations and I will admit I want to keep the name, so tell me honestly what the procedure says and do not soften it. Second, a street near campus named for a man no one on the committee could identify until we checked; the records show he was a town founder who also led a vigilante group. Third, a bronze plaque in the main hallway, installed in 1955, listing the founders and the pioneer families of the valley, with no mention of the people who were displaced — a case where the defect is omission rather than veneration, and where I suspect remove-or-keep is the wrong frame.

Finally, tell me where this procedure will misfire, because I would rather find out now than in a meeting. I am especially interested in whether it systematically under-detects monuments whose problem is omission rather than veneration, and whether it can be steered by whoever writes the wording of the questions we vote on.
