I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and I've somehow ended up helping a zoo and aquarium welfare body write their invertebrate inclusion policy — the upshot is they have to make a call, species by species, on which animals get the full welfare apparatus (anaesthesia before killing, enrichment, habitat standards) and which get filed under "probably nobody home" and left out. They keep asking me for "the science" and I keep telling them there isn't a lookup table, so they've asked me for a procedure instead, and honestly they're right to. Write me one: the indicators to check, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to a human because what's actually being disputed is a value (how much uncertainty we're willing to tolerate before we protect something) rather than a fact about the animal. The board has to sign off on it and they meet quarterly, so it can't be a research program — it has to be something a smart person with a biology background can run in an afternoon and defend.

Two things I care about. First, build it so it can handle the access/phenomenal split explicitly: most of the evidence anyone can actually collect is evidence about access-type capacities — integration, global broadcast, flexible control — and the procedure needs to say out loud what it's licensing when it infers phenomenal consciousness from that, and what it does for a species where the two come apart or the access evidence is there but the case for a unified subject is weak. Second, don't let it collapse into a lookup table for the taxa we've all heard about — run it on three cases, including one where it returns an answer that will annoy the people who commissioned it, and be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure and where the whole thing will misfire. I'd rather they know the soft spots than recieve a clean flowchart that seperates species it has no business seperating.
