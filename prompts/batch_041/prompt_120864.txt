Analyse R v Brown — the Spanner case, House of Lords, 1993 — as a test case for the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism, and tell me where the framework runs out. The facts: five men were convicted under sections 20 and 47 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 for consensual sadomasochistic acts carried out in private over a decade; the harm was serious but no participant complained at the time or needed medical treatment; the appeal turned on whether consent is a defence, and the Lords upheld the convictions three to two; the applicants later failed at Strasbourg on the Article 8 privacy point.

I want the mapping run carefully in both directions. Which strands of the majority's reasoning are legal moralism in Devlin's sense, which are paternalism or a straightforward harm argument about the participants themselves, and does that distinction survive contact with the judgment? Does Devlin's own concession that the law has no business with private immorality, taken together with his reliance on the indignation of the ordinary person, actually condemn the outcome? Is the principal dissent a Hartian position or something else, given how much of it rests on the limits of judicial lawmaking, and on repugnance being no ground for a new crime, rather than on the harm principle? And what, if anything, does the case tell us about Devlin's disintegration thesis, which presents itself as an empirical claim about social cohesion but has never been tested?

Close with an honest account of what the debate cannot reach here — the consent question, the private/public line, and the Strasbourg margin-of-appreciation reasoning, which may be a Devlinian move in a rights vocabulary — and say whether the episode is a confirmation of either side, an anomaly for both, or a case the debate never anticipated.
