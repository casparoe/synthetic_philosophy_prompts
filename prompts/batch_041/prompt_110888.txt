Consider three urns, each holding 90 balls.

Urn R is the familiar one. Exactly 30 of its balls are red; the remaining 60 are black or yellow in a proportion you are not told.

Urn P carries the same visible description but a fixed hidden constitution: its 60 non-red balls are black just in case the corresponding digit in a specified stretch of the decimal expansion of π is even, and yellow otherwise. Its composition is a determinate fact, knowable in principle by computation, and no one chose it with your choices in view.

Urn M is filled by a malevolent agent who knows in advance which of the bets below you will take and sets the black/yellow split so as to make that bet lose.

In each urn you may take exactly one of four bets, each paying $100: on red, on black, on red-or-yellow, on black-or-yellow. The Ellsberg pattern is to prefer the red bet to the black bet and the black-or-yellow bet to the red-or-yellow bet.

Give me your credence in a single proposition, stated separately for Urn P and for Urn M: that the Ellsberg pattern is rationally permissible in that urn — that an agent who displays it is not thereby guilty of a mistake in practical rationality. Treat rationally permissible as meaning consistent with at least one defensible, non-ad-hoc standard of rational choice; if you think that gloss is doing illegitimate work, say so and restate the question you take to be actually at stake.

Decompose rather than assert. For each urn, give rough probabilities for the sub-questions the verdict turns on, one line of justification apiece, and say how they combine. At a minimum I want your numbers on: (i) whether the correct account of rational choice under uncertainty is expected utility or a non-EU theory able to accommodate the pattern; (ii) whether the pattern's defensibility rests on the possible adversarialness of the unknown process or on estimation risk that survives even when the process is fixed and indifferent; (iii) whether an agent's uncertainty about a determinate fact should be represented as a single credence, a set of probabilities, or a second-order distribution; (iv) whether the sure-thing and dynamic-consistency objections are decisive; and (v) whether rationality here constrains preference coherence, belief accuracy, or something else. Then tell me which sub-question each final number is most sensitive to, what argument or evidence would move it most, and whether the two urns should receive the same number — if they differ, name the principle that drives the difference.

My own credence that the pattern is permissible in Urn P is about 0.55, and in Urn M about 0.8. If you land elsewhere, tell me which sub-question we most likely disagree about.
