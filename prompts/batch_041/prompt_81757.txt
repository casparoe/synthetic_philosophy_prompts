I am a philosopher of physics preparing a proposal for a one-semester graduate seminar paper of roughly six thousand words, and I should like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a proper research plan. Please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I will add those myself, and I would rather the proposal refer to positions and arguments by description than appear to cite work it cannot check.

The subject is Gödel's rotating-dust solution to the Einstein field equations, published in 1949, and the philosophical use he made of it. The solution describes a homogeneous universe in rotation; it contains closed timelike curves, and it admits no global division of events into an objective past, present, and future of the kind a single advancing present would require. Gödel took this to show that the objective lapse of time cannot be a feature of the world, and concluded that time in any robust sense is something ideal, or at best dependent on the observer. The familiar replies are well known in outline: that the solution is physically unrealistic; that the argument equivocates among several things the unreality of time might mean; that it covertly imposes an observability or verificationist condition on what may count as real. Each seems to have a familiar rejoinder, and I do not want a survey of them. I want to know where, if anywhere, the argument breaks.

My half-formed idea is this. The argument appears to need a premise that is never stated cleanly: that a temporal structure counts as real only if it is physically significant, in the rough sense that it must make some difference detectable from within the universe and must not be an artefact of our choice of coordinates. Call this the significance constraint. Accept it and Gödel's conclusion may follow, but the argument threatens to prove too much, since the same constraint appears to tell against temporal structure in spacetimes that no one regards as time-less. Reject it and the premise that does the work disappears. Either way the real question migrates to the constraint itself: whether it can be given a formulation that is neither trivial nor a restatement of the conclusion.

I should like the proposal to make that question exact. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions leave it open, by locating which premise each familiar reply attacks and why the reply is not decisive; set out the approach and why it is the right one rather than the obvious alternatives; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give a realistic scope for a single semester.

For the approach I have some initial thoughts, which you may use or overrule. The proposal should distinguish at least three things the unreality of time might mean — the absence of a global simultaneity or foliation, the absence of objective becoming, and the reducibility or elimination of temporal relations — and decide which of them the argument targets. It should reconstruct the argument in a form in which every premise is explicit, isolate the significance constraint, and test whether that constraint is carrying the weight. It should then examine the modal status of the premise that the solution exists: does the argument require mere mathematical existence, or physical possibility, or something stronger? It should ask whether Gödel's own prior idealist commitments are doing work his physics cannot, and consider briefly whether the disappearance of time in canonical quantum gravity supports the same conclusion or a different one. A purely formal reconstruction would establish validity and leave everything interesting untouched, and treating the dispute as merely verbal would beg the very question I want answered; the proposal should say why neither is the way to go.

On success and failure: a successful outcome would state clearly which reading of the unreality of time the argument establishes, together with an account of physical significance that a defender of the reality of time can state without triviality or circularity — or, failing that, would identify the exact premise at which the argument fails and argue that no repair is available. Failure would be the analysis dissolving into a claim that it depends on what one means by time with no principled way to choose; the significance constraint turning out to be vacuous or a restatement of the conclusion; or the project requiring technical work in general relativity that a single semester cannot accommodate.

The scope section should say what to include and what to defer, offer a month-by-month plan, and be honest about the conditions under which the project is too large for the time available.
