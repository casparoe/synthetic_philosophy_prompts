I'm leading a discussion series that pairs science fiction with decision theory, and I want a critical reading of Liu Cixin's Remembrance of Earth's Past trilogy through the lens of evidential cooperation in large worlds. By that I mean the idea that agents who are causally isolated but logically similar — who share a decision procedure, or who know they are reasoning about the same abstract problem — may be justified in cooperating even when their values differ, because one's own choice to cooperate is evidence that the similarly constructed other cooperates too, and because trade between differently valuing agents can leave both better off. The literature models this as a bargaining problem among agents who cannot communicate, and it is usually framed as a reason for optimism about a large universe.

The trilogy is the obvious test case, and it appears to reach the opposite verdict: two civilizations with different values and no causal contact are supposed to be maximally dangerous to each other, and the dark-forest conclusion is that the only safe policy is to hide and, where possible, to strike first. I want a reading that takes both sides seriously rather than using the novel as a foil. Where exactly does Liu's argument depend on causal reasoning — on the fact that a strike is a threat that must be preempted — and would the conclusion survive if the parties were correlated in the way evidential cooperation requires? Do the novel's own devices (the wallfacer deception, the deterrence standoff, the escalating mutual suspicion between civilizations) presuppose something specific about the other side's decision procedure, and is that presupposition an empirical claim about aliens or a conceptual claim about rationality? Conversely, does the fiction dramatize a failure mode the evidential-cooperation literature underplays: that correlation is fragile, that a single agent reasoning causally can profit by defecting from a cooperative equilibrium resting on nothing but similarity, or that uncertainty about another's values is severe enough that the gains from trade disappear? And where the novel resists the reading, say so — it may be a story about survival rather than about compromise, and the honest critic has to concede that it is answering a different question.

Write this as criticism, not plot summary, and assume readers who know the trilogy and the decision theory. Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novels to an illustration of a theorem, and do not bend the theorem to save the novels. End by saying what the trilogy adds to the debate that the bargaining models do not — in particular, what it captures about what it is like to be one of the correlated agents who must decide, before any contact, whether the other is like her.
