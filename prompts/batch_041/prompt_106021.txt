I've been sitting in on a small, stubborn reading group that has spent the last several months working through contemporary material on distributive justice, and one member — a postdoc I'll call Dana, who describes herself, without hedging, as a luck egalitarian — has taken to issuing verdicts on cases as we raise them, which the rest of us have started logging in a shared document, partly so we can tell when she's sliding between the modest and the ambitious version of her view. My own formation is mostly the rationalist blogosphere rather than a philosophy department, so my instinct when someone offers a slogan is to go hunting for the rule underneath it, and lately Dana's slogans and Dana's verdicts have stopped lining up in my head. Before our next session I want to know which rule is actually doing the work in her judgments, and what it commits her to.

Here is the track record as the document has it. A hiker struck out of nowhere by lightning and left permanently disabled: the rest of us owe her full compensation, no questions asked. A wealthy person who knowingly stakes a large chunk of her portfolio on a speculative venture and loses: nothing is owed, because that was option luck rather than brute luck — Dana's shorthand for risks you deliberately run versus harms that simply befall you. A child born into severe deprivation who later falls ill from environmental exposure: full compensation. A person raised in comfortable, stable circumstances who freely takes a dangerous job, is injured, and lands just above what Dana calls a decent minimum: nothing beyond her own savings. And a person, again from a comfortable start, who waves off a cheap and effortless insurance policy and then suffers a loss that drops her below that same minimum: she gets bare emergency relief and no more, which Dana is careful to label a humanitarian exception rather than a claim of justice. The case Dana has not ruled on yet is this one: a woman raised in severe deprivation, whose risk tolerance and financial habits were formed by that upbringing in ways that were plainly not her doing, but who is now a competent adult — she understood the odds, and nobody coerced her — makes a foreseeably bad financial decision and ends up below the decent minimum.

I'd like three things from you. First, your best prediction of Dana's verdict on that new case, with your confidence and the reasoning behind it. Second, an explicit statement of the two or three candidate rules that fit all five earlier verdicts equally well, and what each of them predicts about the new case — I care far less about which label she would accept than about which rule survives contact with the cases. Third, if those rules come apart on the new case, name the single further case that would force her to choose between them: the case I should actually put to her next session if I want to move her rather than win. Please write in American English.
