I teach a mid-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of perception, and I have one seventy-five-minute session in which to introduce cognitive penetration: the thesis that a perceiver's beliefs, desires, expectations, or other cognitive states can make a difference to the content or phenomenal character of perceptual experience itself, and not merely to the judgments, guesses, or reports that follow it. My students will have had one session on modularity and one on attention, and no cognitive science at all. I do not want the thesis explained to me. I want a plan for how I should explain it.

Give me the sequence in which the components have to come, and for each step say what depends on what — in particular, which distinction has to be in place before the thesis can be stated at all, and which assumption about perception my students bring that has to be unsettled first.

Then tell me the one case that should carry the whole session. Specify what it must have and what it must lack: I want a case in which the difference shows up in experience rather than in what the subject says, that cannot be accounted for by where the subject looked, by what the subject expected to see, or by how the subject was asked to respond, and that is robust enough for me to state in a sentence and have it stick. Say explicitly why the duck-rabbit, the dress, and the fact that the Müller-Lyer lines still look unequal once you know they are equal are all the wrong choices for this role.

Then the misconceptions, in the order my students will actually form them, each with the exact moment in the sequence where I should head it off: that this is just top-down attention; that it is the old theory-ladenness-of-observation point from philosophy of science; that predictive processing has already settled it; that if perception is penetrated then perception cannot justify belief; and that it cannot be a genuine empirical question, since everyone already knows that what you believe changes what you see. Add any others you expect me to meet.

Finish with a single question I can put to the room in the last five minutes whose answers will separate students who have understood the thesis from students who have learned only the slogan, and a short list of what I should refuse to cover at this level and announce that I am refusing.

Keep the whole plan under 700 words, in American English.
