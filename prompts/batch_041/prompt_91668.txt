Ok so I'm a novelist, second draft of a book, and I've hit a snag that isn't really a plot snag. Setup: a small town gets an anonymous bequest, about four million dollars, and a committee has to decide which applicants get help. I thought those scenes would write themselves. Instead I keep discovering I have strong gut feelings about the applicants and they dont line up, and I can't write the arguments until I know what I actually think. Also the pot is fixed, so every yes is a no for someone else, which is making me squirrelly.

Can you be a political philosopher who actually works on distributive justice -- someone who knows the technical stuff but can talk like a person. Please don't give me citations or a reading list, I'm not going to look anything up, I just want the reasoning. And start by brainstorming: sort my cases into the ones a decent principle has to save and the ones it's allowed to sacrifice, before you commit to anything.

The applicants, with my gut reactions:

- Dana, 34, born with a bad heart valve, needs surgery. Never did a thing to cause it. Fund her all the way, not even a question.
- Ray, 58, two packs a day since he was fifteen, now lung cancer. Fund him all the way too. The smoking doesn't dock him in my gut, and that surprises me a little.
- A guy who took a job scrubbing chemical tanks for triple pay because his kid needed braces, now has nerve damage in both hands. Fund him, maybe all the way, maybe most of the way.
- A woman who put her savings on the horses and then on some crypto thing and lost the lot. Nothing. She eats it, she knew the odds.
- A man who can only really be happy restoring antique clocks, spent everything on tools and parts, now broke and miserable. His misery is real but it's his. Nothing.
- A kid hit by a drunk driver, paralyzed. Everything, obviously.
- A woman hurt backcountry skiing, a thing she loves and has done for years. This is the one I go back and forth on. Some days yes, some days she took the risk.
- A farmer wiped out by hail two seasons running. Everything.
- A man who sank his whole life into a restaurant that failed. Nothing. He rolled the dice.
- A woman who left school at sixteen to nurse her dying mother, never got qualifications, unemployable at fifty. Everything -- even though leaving was a choice, and she'd say so herself.

The principle I keep reaching for is: you get help for the bad stuff that happened to you, not for the bad stuff you did to yourself. It handles most of these and then faceplants on Ray, who I still want to help all the way.

What I want from you: the principle, or the small set of them, that gets the most of this, stated precisely enough that I can tell what it says about cases I haven't listed. For each clause, name the case that forced you to put it in. Then give me your verdicts on a couple of new applicants you invent. Then be honest about which of my gut reactions the thing can't hold, and which I should give up. If the only principle that saves every one of my verdicts is ugly and has five clauses, say so, and tell me exactly what the clean version costs me. And tell me whether the skier and the woman who nursed her mother are actually the same problem or two different ones.
