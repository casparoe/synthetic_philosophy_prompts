Borges's short story Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius begins with a chance mention of a country no atlas contains, proceeds through the discovery of a full encyclopedia volume describing an invented world — its languages, its geometry, its idealist metaphysics, its hrönir, the objects that come into being because someone expected to find them — and ends with that world quietly displacing our own. I teach a graduate seminar on pseudohistory and the demarcation problem and want to assign the story. Give me a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that treats the story as literature rather than as an illustration, and the epistemology of pseudohistory as philosophy rather than as a label to hang on the plot.

Bring the standard demarcation apparatus to bear and say honestly how far it reaches. Falsifiability, the obvious Popperian move, looks weak here: the Tlönian project makes no risky predictions; it builds a self-reinforcing, internally coherent universe. Consider whether a Lakatosian picture of research programmes, or the more recent family of accounts that diagnose pseudoscience by a cluster of epistemic vices rather than a single criterion — selective source criticism, credulity toward favored testimony paired with hypercriticism toward orthodox testimony, the postulation of a grand hidden design — fits the story better, and where each of these misfires.

I am especially interested in two distinctions the story seems to force. First, between pseudohistory and fiction: the Tlönian encyclopedia makes no claim to be true, so is it pseudohistory at all, or is the pseudohistorical act located elsewhere — in the society's insertion of the fiction into the real record, and in the readers who begin to inhabit it? Second, between content and method: is pseudohistory identified by its subject matter (lost civilizations, secret conspiracies) or by how it handles evidence, and does Borges dramatize one at the expense of the other?

Note where the story resists the philosophical reading: its frame is itself a scholarly hoax, dense with invented citations, so it may be performing the difficulty of demarcation rather than illustrating a criterion; and its idealism may quietly remove the realist background that any demarcation criterion presupposes. Point me to the specific scenes and devices that carry the argument — the aphorism about mirrors, the hrönir, the postscript in which the narrator concedes that he is being changed — and paraphrase rather than quote.

End by saying what the story adds to the debate that argument alone cannot: whether it shows the demarcation problem to be, at bottom, a matter of the social certification of testimony and the seductions of coherence, rather than a logical test that a theory either passes or fails.
