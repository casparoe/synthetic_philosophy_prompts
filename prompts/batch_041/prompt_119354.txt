I am a novelist; the book I am writing follows one family across three generations, and the argument I keep having with the sociologists and editors I know has not moved in two years. One side holds that generations are real historical formations — a cohort stamped by a shared formative experience carries the stamp into adulthood, and the label picks out something that genuinely constrains its members' beliefs, tastes and votes. The other side holds that Millennial and Gen Z are marketing categories retrofitted onto arbitrary birth-year cutoffs, and that generational explanation is a genre of pseudo-explanation that quietly displaces class and policy. The demographers I have asked add that because cohort is simply period minus age, a model containing all three as effects cannot separate them: some identifying constraint must be imposed, and different constraints yield different verdicts about how much of any difference is the cohort's doing. Everyone agrees about the survey data and about ordinary usage, yet nobody concedes anything. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a verbal dispute about the extension of the word generation, a metaphysical dispute about whether social kinds can be real without being natural, or an identification problem in statistical clothing — or are these three questions being answered under one heading? I suspect the third is doing most of the work and that the first two are proxies for it, but I would rather be corrected than confirmed. Say what each side is actually committed to, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a constraint on the model, a distinction between the questions, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
