I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and there's a pattern I keep running into at work and in the AI-adjacent stuff I read on the side, and I want to know whether it actually has a name or whether I'm just describing deterrence badly.

Here's the shape of it. Two parties, and one of them makes a threat they'd never carry out, because carrying it out would hurt them at least as much as the other guy. Fine, that's a bluff, everybody knows about bluffs. The weird version is when nobody treats it as a bluff. Both sides act like the threat is binding, the other side complies, and it works for years purely because the situation never comes up. If anyone ever called it, it'd turn out to be hot air — but calling it costs so much that nobody does, so it never gets tested and never has to be credible. Three examples. A small lab says something like 'if you ship a model that does X, we'll publish our weights and delete the entire moat this industry runs on'. They obviously wouldn't, it'd wreck them too. But the big lab doesn't ship X, and both sides talk about it like a real constraint. Second: my old startup had a we-never-do-custom-work-for-enterprise-clients rule that existed only so sales could say it out loud. Third, the one that really messes me up: a friend says the trick is to deliberately adopt a goal you don't care about at all, announce it loudly, and then when somebody tries to get leverage on you by threatening the thing you actually care about, they waste the threat on the decoy instead, which you're happy to throw away. But if you're happy to throw it away, why would anyone believe you cared? He insists that's not the bug, that's the whole point.

So does this have a name? I've been told it's 'acausal' something, and also 'surrogate' something, and I don't know if those are two names for the same thing, two different things I'm mashing together, or blog jargon that doesn't mean anything. What I actually want isn't the label though. I want to know which of the standard things this is and which it isn't. Is it just a non-credible threat, i.e. the textbook thing that's supposed to fail? Is it a commitment device even though nobody pays anything to commit? Is it the same move as acting unpredictable / madman, or different, since here both sides are completely sure about each other's payoffs? Does it only work if both sides reason the same way — same decision theory, same code — and does it evaporate if one side is a normal human who doesn't play that game? And is the decoy-goal thing the same phenomenon or a seperate one that just tends to show up in the same arguments?

If there's no name for the exact thing, coin one, define it, and tell me where the edges are — what the nearest thing is that isn't it, and what would have to change for it to stop working.
