I sit on the internal forecasting desk of a mid-sized public agency, where for the last three years we have run a small prediction market on questions that matter to us: whether a particular piece of legislation will clear committee before the end of the quarter, whether a named supplier will miss a delivery date, whether a pilot program will be renewed. Participation is open to staff across the agency, we seed each market with a small liquidity subsidy so that quotes always exist, and our guidance to senior leadership is that the current price is the best available estimate of the event's probability and should be cited as such in briefing documents. At the same time, our compliance handbook bars anyone with privileged access to the underlying facts -- the staffer who drafted the bill, the engineer who knows the supplier has fallen behind -- from taking positions on the corresponding question, on the ground that they would corrupt the signal. I have been reading around the economics of information markets and keep running into a result usually called the no-trade theorem, which as I understand it says something like this: if the participants are rational, share a common prior, and it is common knowledge among them that they are rational and that the market clears, then no trade can occur for the mere purpose of pooling private information -- any trade that does happen must be driven by something else, such as differing priors, hedging, or noise. If that is right, a market like ours should not be doing what it visibly does: printing prices that move, and move for reasons that look informational. My colleagues treat this as a technicality of no practical consequence. I suspect it tells us something about what we are actually committed to. Please act as a philosopher of economics who has thought hard about the epistemology of markets, and help me work out what our practice presupposes.

What I want is not a survey of the literature but a reconstruction of the implicit theory our practice commits us to. Take the practice as a whole -- the quoted prices, the instruction to treat them as best estimates, the liquidity subsidy, and the ban on informed trading -- and tell me what view of disagreement, of the common prior, of rationality, and of what a price is supposed to represent it only makes sense under. Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Is it internally consistent, or does the ban on informed trading pull against the informational justification for running the market in the first place? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the very same set of rules. Before you write, spend a moment brainstorming the candidate readings -- heterogeneous priors, non-common-knowledge of rationality, non-informational trading motives, price-as-forecast versus price-as-equilibrium-object -- and then choose among them rather than listing them.

I want a committed answer: take a definite position on whether the practice is coherent, on which single presupposition is load-bearing, and on whether the no-trade theorem actually threatens the practice or merely constrains how we should describe it, and give your reasons rather than laying out options for me to choose from. Structure the response under exactly these headings, in this order: "Summary", three to four sentences stating your verdict; "The practice and its apparent commitments", roughly 300 words; "What is strictly presupposed and what is optional", roughly 400 words; "Where the practice is underdetermined", roughly 250 words; "Verdict", roughly 200 words, saying plainly whether we should keep the market, keep it but change the compliance rule, or abandon the informational reading of our prices; and "What would have to change", roughly 250 words, on the concrete adjustments to our rules that your verdict implies. Do not hedge your way out of the verdict, and where you are genuinely uncertain, say which way you would bet and how strongly.
