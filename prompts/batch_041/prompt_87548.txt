I did 40 years in marine biology, mostly squid and cuttlefish, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy — slowly, and probably badly. Which may be why my daughter Rosa and her partner Ellis let me sit there while they went at each other over dinner last week. I didn't take a side, and honestly I couldn't, so I'm outsourcing it to you.

Here's how it went. Ellis had read somewhere that corvids and parrots have forebrain neuron counts in the same ballpark as monkeys — birds pack them two to four times as densely as mammals do — and that an octopus has something like half a billion, most of them sitting out in the arms rather than in the central brain. Ellis said: fine, so moral status should track neural hardware, roughly how much there is to suffer with. Brain *size* on its own is junk as a measure — it's confounded by body size and by how the tissue is packed, which is why nobody thinks the sperm whale is automatically a bigger deal than you or me. But the amount of cognitive machinery is the real thing, and it's continuous. So a crow and an octopus deserve more moral weight than a mouse, and a mouse more than a clam.

Rosa said that quietly turns ethics into a IQ test, and one that plenty of humans would fail. Suffering is what matters, not cleverness — if two creatures can both be hurt, the one that also does puzzles doesn't get extra credit for its pain. She said once you start grading moral worth by how much brain a creature has, you've rebuilt a ladder, and ladders have a bottom rung that somebody gets to stand on.

Ellis came back with: pain isn't a switch. To suffer at all you need machinery that can represent your own state over time, and richer machinery means richer suffering — a creature that can dread it's own future can be hurt in ways a creature living entirely in the present can't be. And if none of that counts for anything, what exactly is supposed to be wrong with stepping on a beetle? He said if capacity makes zero difference, we owe the beetle what we owe the dog.

Rosa said no, that's a threshold, not a scale. Somewhere a nervous system gets rich enough that there's someone home, and past that line we're equals, full stop. She said his more-neurons-more-standing line would also mean a person with an unusually large brain outranks one with a small one, and then said something about infants and dementia; Ellis said that was a cheap shot and that the continuity point still stands.

Then they both turned and looked at me. Can you take the four moves one at a time and say who's right about what — and what the pair of them are missing between them? I'd like the actual philosophy here, not just a scorecard.
