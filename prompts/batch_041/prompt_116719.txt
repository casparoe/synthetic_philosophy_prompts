I spent thirty-five years as a health physicist in the civil nuclear industry, and since retiring I have been reading moral and political philosophy. The effect has been to make the institutional world I worked in look strange to me, and I would like you to tell me what philosophical commitments the practice of radiation protection, and the wider policy that surrounds it, actually presupposes.

Here is that practice as an outsider would see it. Exposure of the public from licensed facilities is capped at a level well below the ordinary variation in natural background radiation from one district to the next. Every increment of dose, however small, is assumed to carry a proportionate cancer risk, with no threshold beneath which it is treated as harmless. Exposures already far below the legal limit are to be pushed lower still wherever that is reasonably achievable, a rule we knew in the trade as ALARA. At the same time the very small doses received by very large populations are summed into a collective dose and then spoken of as though it forecast a definite number of future deaths. And the safety case for a geological repository must be made for spans running from tens of thousands to a million years.

The surrounding policy looks stranger still. Germany shut its last reactors in 2023 while its lignite mines went on operating; France kept a fleet that supplies roughly two-thirds of its electricity; and both countries answer to much the same international advisory bodies and work to broadly similar dose limits.

What I want to know is what all of this presupposes. Does the practice commit us to a deontological prohibition on imposing risk on the unwilling, or to a consequentialism with an extreme aversion to catastrophic loss, and which rival accounts of the ethics of risk does it quietly exclude? Does treating natural background as the baseline against which artificial increments must be justified presuppose that the line between risk imposed and risk merely suffered is morally fundamental, and can that line bear the weight placed on it? Does a million-year safety demonstration presuppose that distant future persons have claims on us as strong as those of our contemporaries, and that we can know enough about their circumstances to specify what we owe them? And where is the practice internally inconsistent? Collective dose looks to me like an aggregation that the dose-limit and ALARA structure, which treats each imposed increment as a wrong needing justification, would not license.

Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, say where it is philosophically underdetermined so that several incompatible theories would license the same rules, and tell me what would have to change in regulation and in practice if the presupposed view were replaced by a plain cost-benefit approach, or by a rights-based one.
