I'm in a small reading group that's been doing a unit on logical decision theory, and next week I'm supposed to open the session on the Löbian obstacle. I've been thinking alot about how to present it, and I'd rather not just summarize it, because the whole thing is a loop and a summary makes it sound like a puzzle with a solution, which its not. Here's the idea as I understand it, so you can see what I'm after: a proof-based agent only takes an action if it can prove that the action is optimal; to get from "I've proved that A is optimal" to "A is optimal," it needs to trust its own proof system, i.e. to have the general schema "if I prove P, then P"; but if it could prove that schema for every P, then by the self-referential result Löb's theorem formalizes it would prove P for every P, including a contradiction; so a consistent agent can never prove its own soundness, and the very step it needs in order to trust itself is the step that would blow it up. It still has to act, though. That's the knot.

Write me a pantoum, maybe five quatrains if you can make the closing stanza work, in the voice of that agent. If it helps, a pantoum is the form where lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain become lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last stanza folds back into the opening. The two lines that keep coming back should be the two halves of the trap -- something like "I act only on what I have proved" and "I cannot prove that I am sound" -- but make them your own, and let the form do the work: the line about acting on proofs should keep resurfacing as the line about not being able to prove soundness, and vice versa, so the reader feels the agent trying to step outside its own proof system and landing back inside. The last stanza should close the circle without resolving it. I want someone who knows the Löbian obstacle to nod at the end, and someone who doesn't to still get why the agent is stuck. Don't turn it into either "so just act on faith" or "so never act" -- the interesting thing is that both are live and neither is comfortable. No citations or names needed in the poem, just the verse; a title is fine if it earns its place.
