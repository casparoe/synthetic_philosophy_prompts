I am the managing editor of a young, broad-scope, peer-reviewed journal that publishes across the natural sciences and deliberately keeps a door open to work that sits near the boundary — cosmology, the more speculative corners of theoretical biology, consciousness research, quantitative social science, and the occasional submission from a field whose standing is contested. Our referees have developed a habit that has begun to cost us: when they want a paper rejected, a good number of them write, in one form or another, that the manuscript is not science at all and should never have been sent out for review. This has now cut both ways. In one case we published something that, after a long and ugly correspondence, we had to reclassify as not a scientific claim in any sense, which did our credibility no good. In another, a reviewer's not-science objection led us to reject a manuscript that later appeared elsewhere, where it was tested and, as it happened, refuted — a scientific outcome reached by scientific means, which we had turned away on the grounds that it was not science to begin with. The board has therefore asked me to produce a screening procedure that the three of us on the editorial team can run on a new submission in about half an hour — we screen perhaps four a month before review — and that will sort a manuscript into one of three outcomes: send it out for review as a scientific claim; decline it as not a scientific claim, with a letter the author can read without feeling accused of fraud; or stop and refer the matter to the editor-in-chief, because what is really at stake is what kind of journal we want to be rather than any fact about the manuscript. Please write that procedure as a set of questions to be asked in order, saying for each what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates, and where it hands the decision to a person. It will be run by editors with a solid general science education and no specialist knowledge of the submission's field, it must not depend on familiarity with that field's literature, and it has to yield a short written justification we can put in the decision letter and in the board minutes. Any decline on demarcation grounds must be signed off by the board, so the procedure should make the reasoning easy to inspect and defend.

The procedure has to survive the cases that actually cause us trouble, so treat the following as constraints on it rather than as background. Some claims are unfalsifiable only in practice, because their defenders can adjust a surrounding assumption whenever the data threaten them: a cosmologist who tells us that had the observations come out differently they would have changed the mechanism that generates the ensemble of universes is making a claim of that kind, and I need the procedure to say something useful about it rather than simply shrugging. Others are probabilistic or statistical, and can never be contradicted by a single observation, only by an observation relative to a threshold and a decision rule that somebody chose; here the falsifiability question has to be answered as falsifiable under which rule, and set by whom. Still others are bare existence claims, of the form that there is at least one thing of a certain sort somewhere, which no observation can refute and which we nevertheless call science without hesitation. And some claims are perfectly testable, were tested, and came out one way or the other, in fields we happen to hold in low regard; the procedure must not let an editor's disbelief do work that a demarcation argument is supposed to do, since the entire point of the exercise is to keep the question of whether a claim is scientific separate from the question of whether we believe it. At the far end sit claims whose authors say outright that no possible observation could tell against them, which we should decline as not scientific claims while still refusing to call them pseudoscience or misconduct, because not being a scientific claim and being bad science are different verdicts and the letter has to keep them apart.

Then run the procedure on three submissions from last month's queue and show me the paperwork each one produces. The first argues that our universe is one member of an enormous ensemble produced by a stated mechanism, offers a statistical prediction that the authors say the existing data already satisfy, and, when pressed on what would refute it, says they would modify the mechanism. The second is a preregistered, multi-site, adequately powered study reporting a positive effect in a field most of my board regards as fringe; as far as I can tell it is falsifiable, it was tested, and the test was severe. The third argues that certain experiences have a property that no possible observation could disclose, and the author says as much and treats it as a point in the argument's favour. For at least one of these I expect an answer I will not enjoy, and I would rather you tell me plainly which step is doing the work when it does. Finally, tell me which of the distinctions the procedure rests on would be lost if I compressed it to a single page for a new associate editor, which of its steps are judgments in procedural clothing, and what the procedure simply cannot decide.
