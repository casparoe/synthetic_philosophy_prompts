I have no training in statistics or in philosophy, but I have been reading about the risk scores that some courts and lenders now use to decide who gets held before trial or approved for a loan, and I have gotten stuck on something in the way these tools are audited.

Here is the situation I keep circling. Suppose a county adopts a risk tool that outputs a number from 1 to 10, predicting how likely a defendant is to reoffend if released. The county's written policy contains three commitments, each of which sounds obviously right on its own. First, the tool may not use race, or any obvious stand-in for race, as an input. Second, the score must be calibrated: a 7 must mean the same thing for a Black defendant and a white defendant, namely the same real probability of reoffending. Third, the county audits the tool every year by comparing error rates across racial groups, and it treats it as a failure if the tool falsely flags one group more often than another, or falsely clears one group more often than another. The policy presents all three as requirements that a good tool satisfies at once.

Then someone points out that they cannot all hold at once. If the two groups actually reoffend at different rates, then a tool that is calibrated within each group and also has equal false-positive and false-negative rates in each group is impossible. You can have at most two of the three, and which two you choose decides who bears the cost. The county's response was to keep all three in the policy document and to keep auditing for all three, without ever saying which one wins when they conflict.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of what this practice is actually committed to. Treat the policy as a set of behaviors rather than a set of stated beliefs, and ask what view of fairness it only makes sense under. A few specific things confuse me:

- Is fairness, as this policy conceives it, a property of individuals or of groups? The tool refuses to look at race at the input end but sorts people by race at the audit end. Is that flatly inconsistent, or does it reveal that the policy is holding two different notions of fairness at the same time?
- The impossibility only bites when the underlying rates differ between groups. Does the policy therefore presuppose something about those rates, that they are equal, that they ought to be made equal, or that they are beside the point because the tool is measuring each individual's own risk? If the policy never takes a stand on the rates, is it simply underdetermined rather than contradictory?
- Does the policy presuppose that the score measures a fact about the defendant, a real propensity to reoffend, rather than a decision rule the county has chosen? The calibration requirement sounds like it assumes there is a truth to be calibrated against.
- Which parts of the practice strictly presuppose a particular theory, and which parts merely fit several theories at once? I suspect that banning race as an input is compatible with a great many incompatible views about why one would ban it.
- Finally, if we replaced the implicit view with a rival one, say that fairness is only about equalizing error rates and that calibration is negotiable, or the reverse, what concretely would have to change in the policy, in the audit, and in what the county tells the public?

Please take the role of someone who works at the intersection of statistics and moral philosophy, a person who can move between the mathematics and the ethics without confusing the two, and who is used to explaining this material to people outside both fields.
