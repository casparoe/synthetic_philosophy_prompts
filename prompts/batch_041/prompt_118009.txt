I've been reading this old Chinese text — like 2300 years old, the White Horse Dialogue from the Gongsun Longzi, part of whats called the School of Names — and theres a chunk of it I genuinely cant untangle. Its two unnamed guys going back and forth (this is a translation I found online):

"Can 'a white horse is not a horse' be asserted?" — "It can." — "How so?" — "'Horse' is what designates shape. 'White' is what designates color. What designates color is not what designates shape. Therefore I say: a white horse is not a horse." — "If there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse. If one cannot say there is no horse, does that not mean there is a horse? Since having a white horse counts as having a horse, how can the white [one] not be a horse?" — "If one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may all be delivered. If one seeks a white horse, yellow and black horses may not be delivered. Suppose a white horse were [just] a horse. Then what is sought [in the two cases] would be the same. If what is sought were the same, then 'white' would not differ from 'horse.' If what is sought does not differ, how is it that yellow and black horses are acceptable [in one case] and unacceptable [in the other]? ... This is a white horse's not being a horse: it is certain."

My problem: I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and the "seeking" argument is the one part I feel like I follow — it's exactly like how a function that wants a Horse will happily take a WhiteHorse, but a function that wants a WhiteHorse will choke on a BlackHorse. Nobody concludes from that that WhiteHorse isn't a subtype of Horse, so it reads to me like the argument establishes the opposite of its own conclusion. Someone told me the whole thing is just a pun on "is" — "is not" meaning "is not identical to" versus "is not a member of" — but that feels too quick, since the seeking argument seems to be about what a term picks out when you're looking for something, not about identity.

So two things. (1) Is the seeking argument actually valid on some reading, or does it break — and if it breaks, is it a false premise or a step that just doesn't follow? (2) Does the shape/color argument at the start do any real work, or is that the part that quietly assumes a name can only do one job at a time?
