I am a compiler engineer by trade, and for about a year now I have been moonlighting on a question in the semantics of conditionals that began as a nuisance in a configuration language I maintain and has since turned into something I have to defend in public. Next month I am giving a short paper at a graduate workshop that sits at the boundary between formal semantics and philosophy of language, and I am far less worried about delivering the talk than about the discussion that follows it. The room will be about a dozen people, and I can already picture them: three or four formal semanticists who handle modality in the Kratzer style and think in terms of ordering sources and conversational backgrounds; two or three philosophers of language who work on the Ramsey test and on the probabilities of conditionals and who will immediately want to know what my account does about the triviality results; one or two people who defend a broadly Stalnaker-and-Lewis possible-worlds treatment and treat counterfactuals as quantifying over worlds ranked by comparative similarity; and a few generalists who will ask the embarrassingly basic question I will not have a crisp answer for. None of them works on my exact topic, but every one of them can smell a naive unification claim from across the room.

Here is the thesis I intend to defend, stated plainly. I claim that indicative and counterfactual conditionals share a single semantic core — one two-place operator ranging over a set of possibilities — and that the whole felt difference between "if" and "would" is a pragmatic overlay carried by a condition I call epistemic liveness. An indicative antecedent is presented as live: it has not been excluded by the conversation's common ground. A counterfactual antecedent is presented as dead: the common ground already settles against it. On my story, the past-tense morphology in the counterfactual is a conventional marker of that deadness rather than a device for shifting the time of evaluation, and the apparent difference in modal scope — counterfactuals seeming to reach worlds unlike the actual one, indicatives seeming to stay within what we know — is derived from liveness together with the same ordering source, not stipulated as a separate mechanism. The example I keep leaning on, and that I want you to stress-test, is a coin already flipped but not yet revealed: I want to say that "If it came up heads, you win" and "If it had come up heads, you would have won" differ only in whether the antecedent is treated as live, and that a hearer who does not yet know the outcome sits awkwardly between the two — which is exactly where I suspect the account is softest.

I would like you to act as a professional semanticist who has refereed in this area for years, think carefully before writing, and begin by brainstorming the pressure points you would probe if you were in that room. Then draft me a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of my view: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this particular audience — not the ones I would get from a logic seminar or from a computer science audience — ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, each phrased the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it, with a two-minute spoken reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest and what I should concede up front. Flag any objection that turns on a commitment I may not realize my thesis carries, tell me the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices, and finish with the one question I am dreading and have no good answer to.
