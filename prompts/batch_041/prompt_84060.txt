I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, at present writing a chapter on the intellectual history of sortition and its recent revival in democratic innovation. My supervisor wants me to include a counterfactual section testing whether the current enthusiasm for randomly selected deliberative bodies can be given a genuinely early-modern pedigree, and I keep returning to Spinoza — partly because his democratic theory is usually compressed into a single claim about democracy being the most natural form of state, and partly because I suspect his actual commitments would sit awkwardly with the way sortition is now marketed. What I want from you is not a survey of the secondary literature but a disciplined reconstruction: treat Spinoza's own texts as the only evidence, and reason from his premises to a considered judgement about what he would have made of three developments that postdate him entirely.

First, mass suffrage: the extension of the vote to the whole adult population, women included, which his own discussion of democracy in the unfinished Political Treatise appears to foreclose, and which forces the question whether that exclusion is a load-bearing premise of his account or an unresolved inconsistency he never had the chance to revisit. Second, the emergence of organised parties and permanent faction as the ordinary machinery of representative government: does his insistence that a commonwealth's right is bounded by the power of the multitude, together with his hostility to sedition, give him resources to treat parties either as a stabilising channel for the passions or as a standing threat to them? Third, and most important for my chapter, the institutional design of the modern sortition body — the Irish Citizens' Assembly of 2016, with its ninety-nine randomly selected citizens stratified by gender, age, region and social class and its formal obligation on government to respond, and the permanently sitting citizens' council in Belgium's German-speaking Community, which as I understand it is chosen by lot, sets its own agenda and can commission further assemblies. I want you to say, with reasons drawn from the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus and the Tractatus Politicus, whether he would regard such bodies as a genuine improvement in the constitution of a democracy or as a dangerous displacement of sovereign authority, and in particular whether his account of the state's right over outward conduct but not over inward judgement implies anything about how far a sortition body's recommendations could legitimately bind a legislature.

Please write this as a continuous argument of roughly 800 words, addressed to a reader who knows the texts, and do not pad it with generalities about what Spinoza would have thought of modernity. Where the texts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so explicitly, explain which way the balance of his commitments tilts and why; where an apparent tension exists, resolve it rather than gesturing at it. The most important thing is that the conclusion be defeasible rather than triumphant — I want the strongest Spinozist case against my own enthusiasm for sortition, not merely the case for it. Finish with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, the most likely Spinozist stance on sortition-based deliberative bodies.
