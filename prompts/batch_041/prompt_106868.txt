You are a philosopher of probability who also writes literary fiction; take the following as a commission from a journal that publishes philosophy in narrative form. I am a doctoral candidate in formal epistemology, and I want a short story that does genuine philosophical work on imprecise probabilities — on the view that a rational agent's uncertainty need not be captured by a single real-valued credence, but may instead be represented by a set of them, an interval, or a family of distributions.

The story should be set somewhere concrete and turn on a decision that must actually be made under uncertainty; a coastal flood-defence authority deciding whether to raise a levee is the kind of thing I have in mind, but invent your own setting if it serves the ideas better. Give me at least three characters who embody genuinely defensible positions rather than strawmen:

- one who refuses to issue a single probability for a catastrophic flood, offering instead a wide interval, and who insists that the width is not a defect to be papered over but an honest report of how little the evidence constrains the answer;
- one who insists that a decision-maker must act, and that any interval can and should be collapsed into a point — by taking the worst case, the midpoint, or a maximin rule — and who argues that a forecast you cannot bet on is not a forecast at all;
- one who presses the sharpness advocate with a real technical puzzle: that under some conditions, conditioning on new evidence makes an imprecise agent's interval wider rather than narrower, so that learning can apparently make her less committal about the very event she has just learned about. Let this character state the puzzle correctly, and let the others try to answer it, without either side being obviously refuted.

Please also let the characters touch, in their own voices and without lecturing, on the sure-loss or Dutch-book objection — that an agent who declines to fix a single credence can be exploited by a book of bets she would accept — on the thought that ambiguity-aversion may be a rational response to evidence rather than a bias, and on the practical question of how a public body is supposed to act when its experts will only give ranges.

Constraints: 1,500–2,500 words; a plot and at least one decision actually made or refused; and no drifting into essay — no character should deliver a lecture longer than a few sentences, and the philosophy should be carried by the situation, the dialogue, and the consequences rather than by exposition. Invented institutions, place names, and characters are welcome. Do not cite or name real researchers, and do not quote from any existing text.

Before you write, think carefully and brainstorm privately: sketch the setting, decide what each character actually wants (not merely what they believe), and choose the concrete decision that will force the issue. Then write the story. After it, add a brief note of no more than 150 words identifying which positions the characters occupied and which question you deliberately left open.
