I'm workshopping a short paper for a seminar on probability and I'd like your comments on it before I send it round. It's about the old random-chord puzzle: pick a chord of a circle at random, what's the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? The three standard methods give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 (endpoints on the circumference; a random line, i.e. distance from the centre uniform; midpoint uniform in the disk). Here's my line of argument, roughly:

- The three answers aren't three answers to one question. Fine, everyone says that much.
- What I want to add is this: indifference only has content relative to a chance setup. If some physical process is actually randomising — a spinner, a dropped needle — then there's a fact about which variable is being randomised, and indifference over that variable is legitimate. The paradox only bites when the question is purely epistemic ('a random chord'), where 'random' names our ignorance rather than any mechanism.
- So the invariance fix — insist the answer be invariant under the symmetries of the plane, and you get 1/2 — is a stipulation dressed up as a constraint. Rotation symmetry alone doesn't even fix the answer: it acts transitively on the angular parameter and says nothing about how far out the chord sits, so you have to throw in translations, which is exactly where the extra assumption sneaks in.
- The generalisation I want: replace the principle of indifference with a 'process rule' — never spread credence equally over a partition unless you can point to a physical process that would make the outcomes exchangeable. Where there's no such process, the honest verdict isn't 'indeterminate probability', it's 'incomplete question'.

I know there are soft spots. What I'd like from you: (1) is the process rule actually doing anything different from the invariance approach, or is it a redescription of it? (2) does it survive the obvious objection that it makes probability depend on facts about the world we usually don't know — say I'm about to flip a coin I haven't inspected? (3) what's the strongest objection to the 'incomplete question' line? Bottom line first, please: one sentence on whether the thesis survives at all, then your comments. Under 500 words, and I'd definately rather hear the objections now than from a referee.
