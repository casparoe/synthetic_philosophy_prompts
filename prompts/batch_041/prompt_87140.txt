I'm a novelist, but the writing program where I teach has roped me into co-teaching this interdisciplinary seminar this term — nominally it's about narrative and belief, but in practice it's turned into a course about why people don't update, which is honestly great for me, because the novel I'm drafting is about a woman who gets pulled into a very tight online community and then, at the worst possible moment, starts reading what outsiders say about it. So I've been reading around the difference people draw between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber — the idea being that a bubble is a case where you're just missing things because of who you happen to know and what the feed shows you, so the cure is exposure, whereas an echo chamber is a case where the group has trained its members to treat outside disagreement itself as evidence of bad faith or capture, so exposure doesn't merely fail, it can make things worse. In the scene I'm writing, my character reads three fairly careful mainstream pieces about her group and comes away more committed, and I want to make sure I'm not just doing the cheap version of that where the community is obviously a cult and the reader is way ahead of her.

Anyway — the seminar has an in-class final, 90 minutes, closed book, third- and fourth-year students who've had a bit of epistemology but nothing on social epistemology specifically, and I've been handed the job of writing the essay question. I want one question, built around a short case, that a student can't answer by reciting filter bubbles are bad, read the other side — I want it to force them to say what the difference between the two structures actually amounts to, why the standard remedy only works if you've got the right diagnosis, and what a remedy for the other case would even have to look like. Closed book, so no citations, and don't build marks around a quote or a name — half of them would garble it anyway. One thing I'm chewing on and don't want to spoon-feed: the distinction can also be used as a dismissal, a way of saying they're not just misinformed, they're brainwashed, and that move is available to anybody about anybody, so a really sharp student ought to be able to notice it without me pointing at it.

Please give me the question and the standard together, laid out in these sections, in this order: "The exam question" (a short case, maybe 120 words, then the actual question or two the students answer, all in under 250 words total); "What this is really testing" (3-4 sentences on the crux, for my own benefit); "Model answer" (about 700 words, the kind of thing a strong student writes in the time, prose not bullet points); "Marking scheme" (five must-hit points, 20 marks total, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it); and "The A/B line" (two or three sentences on the specific move that separates an A from a B, which should be the thing I just said I don't want to spoon-feed).
