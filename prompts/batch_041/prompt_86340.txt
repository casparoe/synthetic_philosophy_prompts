ok so I've been keeping a note on my phone of my gut calls about 'better than' and I want to know what principle is behind them, if there even is one. Everytime I try to state it myself it either gets too complicated or it makes me give up a call I'm pretty sure about.

Here are the calls.

Two job offers that differ only in salary: the higher one is better, no question, and if I rank three of them the ranking never loops back on itself.

Two wines, one with a great nose and a dull finish, the other with a dull nose and a great finish. I don't think either is better than the other -- but I also don't think they're equally good. They're on a level, sort of, without being the same. And a third wine that's clearly worse than both is clearly worse than both. So the middle thing there, whatever it is, feels like it does the work that 'at least as good as' does, and I can't tell if it's transitive or not.

Three apartments, A, B, C. A beats B because of the commute, B beats C because of the light, C beats A because of the rent. Ask me pairwise and I'll pick a winner every time, by whatever I happen to be attending to. Ask me about all three at once and I'll say I'm being inconsistent and one of the pairwise calls has to be wrong. So there's a cycle in my head, but I don't believe betterness itself is cyclic.

Now the one that actually keeps me up. I can imagine a life that's 80 years of pretty intense good stuff. I can imagine one that's 100 years, slightly less intense -- I'd take the 100-year one. Then 130 years, a bit less intense still -- take that too. Keep going: each step trades a small drop in how good the average day is for a big chunk of extra years, and at every step I take the longer life. Eventually you get to ten million years of a life that's barely better than nothing, and I do not take that, I'd take the original 80 years over it every time. Every adjacent step I say the longer life is better; the endpoints I say the shorter one is better. That can't all be true if better-than is transitive.

And it isn't stable across who it's for. For my own life I'll walk a long way down that chain. For my kid's life I bail after about two steps. If it's about a whole population rather than one person I refuse the very first step. Same chain, different verdicts depending on whose life is on the line, and I don't think that's just sentimentality.

Something I notice: the chain only ever varies one dimension at a time -- intensity versus duration -- with a fixed exchange rate, and each step feels clean. When the two things differ on more than one dimension, like the wines, I get the neither-better-nor-equal feeling. Someone told me the chain thing is called a spectrum argument. Maybe that's the clue.

My rough guess is: better-than is transitive, full stop, and what goes wrong in the spectrum is that my step-by-step calls aren't really comparing the same two things, or aren't all equally confident. And the wines show that better-than has a sibling relation -- on a level, not equal -- which isn't transitive and isn't identity either. But I can't state any of that precisely and I don't know which of my verdicts it makes me give up.

What I want:

- the precise principle, or small set of candidates, that gets the most of my calls, and for each clause the case that forces it
- whether transitivity survives, and of exactly which relation -- better than, at least as good as, on a level, equally good
- what your principle says about two things I haven't decided: (i) a chain where the exchange rate itself slowly degrades, so each step is a smaller gain for the same loss; (ii) the same spectrum run over paintings, or over scientific theories, instead of lives -- does the shape of the argument depend on it being about welfare?
- and which of my calls has to go, said straight

If the honest answer is that my calls are just inconsistent and there's no principle, fine, but then tell me the single call to drop to make the rest consistent, and what to do about the wines.
