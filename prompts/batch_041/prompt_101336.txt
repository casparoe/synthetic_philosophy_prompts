ok so i've gotten wierdly into counterfactual history lately — it started as a christmas argument with my brother-in-law about whether WW1 would've happened anyway without the assassination, and now i'm reading everything i can get my hands on about it. he's a big "it was all inevitable" guy and he has this analogy he keeps coming back to and i can't tell if it's doing real work or just sounding deep.

he says history is like water running off a landscape. the water is human choices, the terrain is the material situation — geography, demography, technology, class structure — and the channels the water carves are institutions. on his picture the water always finds the low ground, the exact path it takes is a ripple, and ripples don't matter. so asking "what if franz ferdinand had missed" is like asking "what if this particular raindrop had fallen a foot to the left" — the watershed drains the same way regardless. he also likes to point out that lots of things got invented or discovered independently in different places at roughly the same time (writing, agriculture, the state, whatever) and says that's just the water finding the same outlet from different starting points.

i want to actually take this apart instead of rolling my eyes. so: spell out what maps onto what here, then where the mapping breaks. i'm already suspicious of a few spots — water doesn't deliberate and can't decide to dig a canal, whereas people can; a watershed has one lowest point basically by construction and i'm not at all sure "the modern state" is like that; and the convergence argument smells like a selection effect, where we only notice the things that happened to converge and never count the ones that didn't.

then the bottom line: does the inevitability conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? if it doesn't survive, is there a better analogy for what he's actually trying to say, or does the whole thing have to be argued case by case with no picture at all?

one more thing — he'll say "fine, forget the water, it's just a metaphor." so i'd also like to know what's left when you strip the picture away and keep only the bare structural claim: that individual events are small perturbations on large-scale causes. is that claim independently defensible, or does it only ever get its force from the river picture?
