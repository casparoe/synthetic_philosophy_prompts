Here's the post as I've summarised it, compressed but I think faithful:

Ordinary Bayesian conditionalisation is for the case where your evidence arrives as a certainty: you learn that A is true, you set P(A) = 1, and you renormalise. But most of the time, the post says, nothing arrives as a certainty — you look at a cloth in candlelight and your credence that it's blue goes from 0.5 to 0.7, say. Jeffrey's rule is what you use then. You take the proposition your evidence is about, A, and your new credence in it, q, and for every other proposition X you set P'(X) = q·P(X|A) + (1 − q)·P(X|¬A). The conditional probabilities P(X|A) and P(X|¬A) are held fixed; the post calls this the "rigidity" condition and says it isn't really an extra assumption — it's just what it means to take your evidence at face value, so it holds automatically whenever the evidence is genuine. It also says the only thing you need in order to apply the rule is some new credence to plug in: you run it on whatever propositions you happen to have evidence about, one at a time, and the rest of your credences sort themselves out.

Then the part I'm less sure about. The post says that since Jeffrey's rule is really just conditionalisation generalised, it inherits all of conditionalisation's good behaviour. Updating on your evidence in stages gives the same answer as updating on it all at once, so it doesn't matter what order you take things in or how you lump them together. And it's been proved that any other way of responding to uncertain evidence leaves you open to a Dutch book, so the rule is exactly as secure as Bayes' rule itself. It ends by saying Jeffrey's real point was that nothing is ever actually certain — evidence always comes in degrees — so plain old conditionalisation is basically a myth, and Jeffrey's rule is the one you're always using whether you know it or not; Bayes' rule is just the special case where q happens to be 1 or 0. It also says you can't squeeze Jeffrey's rule out of ordinary conditioning on some proposition, which is why he needed a new rule at all.
