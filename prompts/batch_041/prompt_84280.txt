There is a pattern of disagreement I keep encountering, and I would like to know whether it has an established name. I care less about the label than about the analysis, so let me describe the thing at some length.

The pattern appears whenever two people set out to establish which of two neighbouring countries is the more liberal — Sweden or Norway, Canada or the United States, Austria or Germany. Each can produce a ranking that is stable, reproducible, and defensible on its own terms. One ranks by the reach of state-provided welfare, the permissiveness of social legislation, the length of parental leave, the conditions of imprisonment; the other by the tax burden, the density of licensing and regulation, the ease of starting a business or choosing a school, the share of life left to private arrangement. The two rankings are not merely different. They tend to be inversely ordered, and each survives scrutiny: within its own dimension, any competent measurer would reproduce it, year after year. So the disagreement is not repaired when the evidence arrives; it changes shape. Each party ends up arguing that the other is using the word liberal in a debased or question-begging sense, and the dispute becomes a dispute about the word.

Two things prevent me from concluding that this is simply a matter of words. The first is that the choice of sense is consequential: whichever reading prevails determines which reforms get called advances of liberalism and which get called retreats, and both parties understand this and argue in full awareness of it. The second is that the dispute arrives dressed as an empirical question, and it is remarkably hard to persuade either party that no further fact about the two countries could decide it.

To isolate the structure, I constructed a case of the same shape with nothing at stake. Two people disagree about which of two cities is more walkable: one counts the destinations reachable within twenty minutes on foot, the other counts the fraction of the city that is pleasant to walk through. Both counts are uncontroversial, both are stable across seasons, and the cities come out in opposite orders. Is that the same phenomenon as the country case, or a different one? If it is the same, then perhaps the country dispute really is merely verbal and I have been over-impressed by the stakes. If it is different, then whatever the country case possesses and the city case lacks is the thing I want named, defined, and separated from the neighbouring ideas it is easily confused with — and I would also like to know what, if anything, would settle the country question rather than merely terminate it.
