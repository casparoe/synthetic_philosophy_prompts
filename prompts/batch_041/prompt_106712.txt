I cover behavioral economics and the science of decision-making for a general-interest magazine, and I've been asked to run a 75-minute session at a summer institute for about two dozen graduate students drawn from economics, psychology, and philosophy — a mixed room, some of them fluent in decision theory, some not. I want the session to do one thing well: make participants feel ambiguity aversion in their own choices, then feel the floor tilt when they see what those choices commit them to. I don't want the phenomenon named until the debrief. Please design the whole thing for me: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief, and a candid account of what will go wrong and what the activity cannot deliver.

Here is the structure I have in mind, which you should feel free to tighten or replace where it doesn't work. Use a single opaque urn holding ninety balls: thirty are red, and the remaining sixty are black or yellow in a proportion fixed in advance and hidden from the room. Write four bets on the board — win if the drawn ball is red; win if it is black; win if it is red or yellow; win if it is black or yellow. Before any draw, each participant privately writes down, in tokens, the most they would pay for each of the four bets (a fair price), with no talking and no comparing notes. Collect the sheets and show the room the modal pattern: people price red above black, and price black-or-yellow above red-or-yellow. Let a few volunteers defend their pricing out loud; they will say things like "at least I know the odds on red" and "I'd rather not bet into a color I can't pin down." That is the feeling you want on the table before anything is explained.

Then the turn. Have participants act as brokers against one another, or let me play broker against the room. Using each person's own stated prices, execute four trades: buy the red bet, sell the black bet, buy the black-or-yellow bet, sell the red-or-yellow bet. Each trade is fair by that person's own lights, and yet the resulting portfolio pays exactly zero in every possible state — red, black, or yellow — while the cash has already left their pocket. They hold a worthless position and are poorer for it. I want that to land physically, in tokens, before any theory. I'd also like the belief-inconsistency laid out plainly for the debrief: pricing red above black means they think black is less than a third of the urn; pricing black-or-yellow above red-or-yellow means they think black is more than a third. Both cannot be true, and I want the room to see that the contradiction was implicit in two innocuous-looking preferences.

Finally, a second round that complicates the moral. Re-run the elicitation, but this time announce that the hidden sixty will be chosen, after bets are placed, by a host who wants the room to lose. Now caution about the unknown looks less like a bias and more like good sense. I want the debrief to hold both faces at once: the incoherence on one side, the defensibility of caution on the other. Map each moment of the session onto the underlying distinction — risk versus ambiguity, the sure-thing principle, the difference between uncertainty about a chance device and uncertainty about a person's choice — and be specific about which moves belong to the activity and which I have to supply from the front of the room. Tell me honestly which feature of the idea this format cannot reproduce no matter how well I run it, and what I must therefore say myself.

Two practical requests. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — contamination between participants, order effects from the sequence in which the bets are elicited, the probabilistically sophisticated student who reports indifference and muddies the tally, the sense that the broker round is a trick — and how to handle each without draining the drama. Second, keep the materials cheap and the setup reproducible in an ordinary seminar room, and make sure the whole thing is legible to someone who has never seen the paradox before but still has teeth for the students who have.
