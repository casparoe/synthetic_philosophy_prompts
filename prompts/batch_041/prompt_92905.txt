A view I keep encountering — in popular neuroscience writing and in some philosophy of mind — holds that a split-brain patient is literally two people sharing one body: sever the corpus callosum and you have cut one mind into two, so it is a mistake to speak of the patient's consciousness in the singular. Treat this as an analogy and audit it rather than simply endorsing or rejecting it. Begin with the correspondence table: what is supposed to map onto the two persons, and what onto the single body, the shared environment, and the channel between them. Then locate where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that actually bear on the "two people" conclusion from those that are mere surface disanalogies. Two features I want tested against the analogy are the case in which one hemisphere offers a report or rationale for behavior that was in fact driven by the other, and the case in which one hand acts against the apparent intention of the other; say whether the analogy survives these, or whether it quietly presupposes the very conclusion it is being used to support. If it fails, propose a better comparison. Close by stating which further argument or piece of evidence would change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
