I am writing the conceptual chapter of a dissertation on signaling — animal and human — and I have decided not to compare the handicap principle with its rivals until I know what any of them would have to deliver. I am working with the handicap principle in its plain form: a signal is credible because it is expensive, and the expense is what stops the low-quality from sending it. Please write me the specification rather than the comparison: what must a theory of signal credibility be able to do before it counts as adequate? I want to use the finished list to sort traits into signals, cues, and noise, and to explain why some honest signals are cheap.

The theory has to cover at least the following, which are the cases I actually have to explain.

- The peacock's tail: costly to grow and to carry, correlated with the bearer's condition, preferred by receivers, with the offspring of preferred males inheriting the viability advantage. Here the cost is a handicap in the ordinary sense.
- The stag's roar: its pitch is fixed by the size of the animal's resonating chambers, so a small male cannot produce a deep one. Nothing was spent to make the signal honest; the physics did the work.
- The alarm call of a bird surrounded by relatives: cheap to produce, no reliable difference in what it costs different callers, honest because the callers' interests coincide.
- A courtship display that is difficult to perform: what excludes the weak and the young is a rate of failure, not an expenditure of resources. I want to know whether a failure rate is a cost in the relevant sense, or whether counting it as one empties the notion of cost of content.
- A signal that imposes the same fixed toll on every signaller, with no discount for poor quality. A colleague says this cannot be honest, since anyone can pay it. I suspect he is wrong: where the value of the receiver's response rises with the signaller's quality, the marginal benefit separates signallers even when the marginal cost does not. If I am right, the honesty condition cannot be stated in terms of cost at all, and I want the list of requirements to make that explicit.
- A deception that persists: a mimic is believed because receivers cannot afford to check, and the equilibrium is stable.
- A trait that carries information without being a signal: a warm body tells a predator where its owner is. The theory has to say what distinguishes a signal from a mere cue, and whether the difference lies in the signaller's design, in the receiver's use, or in the information itself.

Then the requirements. For each, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is a genuine desideratum or a commitment of one camp — that signals must be adaptations, that honesty must be expensive, that signallers must be able to assess their own quality, that receivers' preferences must be explained rather than assumed, and so on. Tell me which requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates. Flag any requirement that is really a demand for the theory to do something no theory of a natural phenomenon could do.

Keep the weight on the criteria; I will run the comparison of candidates myself once I have the list.
