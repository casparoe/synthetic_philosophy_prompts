I am drafting a position piece on consequentialist cluelessness and would like a consistency audit of my commitments before I circulate it. I am not asking whether they are true. I am asking which of them can be held together, which only appear to conflict, and what each real conflict would cost me. Here is where I stand.

(1) An act is right just in case no available alternative would in fact have produced more value. Rightness is fixed by how things actually go, not by what the agent could have known at the time.

(2) For nearly every act open to me, the value of its consequences is not merely unknown but unavailable in principle. This is not a limit on computing power. Causal chains run indefinitely, their later links depend on what other agents will freely do, and the expected value of an act is not something I could compute even with unlimited time, because there is no probability distribution over the relevant outcomes for me to take an expectation over.

(3) Because of (2), the sensible course in practice is to follow ordinary moral rules — keep promises, tell the truth, help when the cost is small — and not to attempt any consequentialist calculation at all. I make an exception for the small class of decisions with short causal chains and measurable outcomes, such as which charity to support; there I do try to calculate.

(4) Those ordinary rules are themselves justified by their consequences and by nothing else. If I became convinced that following one of them made things go worse on the whole, I would abandon it.

(5) I do not think (2) tells against (1). (1) is a claim about what makes acts right, not a recipe for deciding what to do, and a criterion of rightness need not be usable by an agent deliberating in real time.

(6) Where two options cannot be ranked by their expected value, both are permissible. Cluelessness widens the space of what one may do; it does not paralyze the agent.

(7) All the same, some acts are flatly wrong and not merely disfavored by a rule of thumb. Torturing a child for amusement is wrong, and it is not wrong because a rule forbids it or because I happen to be ignorant of its effects.

Now the audit. Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which pairs only look that way? For each genuine tension, say which claim is doing the damage and why, rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and give the cheapest revision together with what it costs — including what it would cost to weaken (2), which I suspect is load-bearing in more than one place. Where a tension turns on an ambiguity in one of the claims, name the ambiguity and say which disambiguation dissolves it. Where two of my claims look like the same move but are not, or look like different moves but are the same, say so and explain the difference. I do not need the standard menu of positions rehearsed; I need to know what my own package commits me to. And tell me which single revision would repair the most.
