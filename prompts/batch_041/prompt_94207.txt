So I'm taking this seminar on formal epistemology and we've just hit Jeffrey conditionalization, and I think I basically get the formula -- you've got a partition of possibilities, you come to have new degrees of belief in the cells of that partition without becoming certain of any single one of them, and you re-weight your old conditional probabilities by the new weights. Fine. What I can't get my head around is all the stuff that's supposed to be doing the work underneath it, and my professor keeps hand-waving when I ask. Concretely: my flatmate is a radiologist. She looks at a scan and comes away 80% confident the lesion is benign, where before she was 50/50. She insists she didn't come to fully belive any clean proposition -- nothing like 'the scan showed a smooth border' got settled in her head, she just has a new credence. So how do I even write that down? If I take the partition to be {benign, malignant} and fix P(benign) = 0.8, I get one answer for every other proposition in the space. But surely whatever happened to her partitioned the space more finely than that -- her visual experience could've been of a smooth-edged mass, a spiculated one, an ambiguous blur, and so on. If I use that finer partition instead and set new credences on its cells, do I get the same answer? I have a nagging feeling I don't in general, and I want to understand why that isn't a fatal problem for the rule, or whether it is one.

Are those the same operation or not? When do they agree, when do they come apart, and which one is actually the right thing to do when the evidence is uncertain? What I'd really like is an intuitive, honest explanation of what the rigidity assumption is actually buying us, where the choice of partition is quietly doing all the work, and whether the rule is a genuine generalisation of ordinary conditioning or really a rival to it.
