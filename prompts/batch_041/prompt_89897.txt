Retired geochemist here, forty years on ice cores and ash layers before I traded the lab for a pile of philosophy books, and I've ended up with a view about how nature gets into history that I'd like a conistency check on before I inflict it on anyone.

(1) Big eruptions and slow climate shifts are genuine causes of famines, migrations, state collapses. Not scenery, not context. Causes.
(2) But I'm no enviromental determinist: what people do with a bad harvest is doing real work, and you can't read a society's fate off its climate.
(3) I hold the difference-making account of causation: X causes Y if Y wouldn't have come out that way without X.
(4) In an overdetermined famine, failed harvest, war cutting the roads, a state sitting on its grain stores, I want to call every one of those a cause, including the ones whose removal would've left the famine happening anyway.
(5) A government that could have moved the grain and didn't did cause the deaths. Yet I've never been able to believe omissions are causes in the same sense the volcano is, since nothing is transferred from one to the other, no mark is made.

Which of these are actually pulling against each other, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest thing to give up in each case? Rank the real ones by how bad it is. I'd rather not surrender (1) or (2), those are the ones I'd defend at a dinner party.
