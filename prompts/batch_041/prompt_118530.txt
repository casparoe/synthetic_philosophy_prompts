You are a philosopher of action with a strong background both in ancient Greek epic and in the decision theory of intertemporal choice. I am preparing a session for an undergraduate seminar that reads Homer's Odyssey alongside the philosophical literature on weakness of will, and what I need from you is a critical reading, not a summary. Think carefully before you begin. Do not quote the poem: you are working from memory of the episodes, the students have the text in front of them, and paraphrase will serve us better. No bibliography or citations are needed.

Take the following framework as given, since I want the reading anchored in it rather than in general impressions. The philosophical tradition offers two quite different pictures of weakness of will. On the first, akrasia is a failure of motivation or of the will itself: the agent judges one course best, wants to act on that judgment, and is moved instead by a competing desire — on the hierarchical version of this picture, the agent's endorsement of the better desire is simply defeated by a desire she does not endorse. On the second picture, borrowed from the economics of intertemporal choice, there is no separate faculty of will to fail: the agent's preferences are time-inconsistent because the discount rate applied to future rewards falls with the delay, so the relative attractiveness of a smaller sooner reward and a larger later one reverses as the moment of choice approaches. A theory of this kind distinguishes three types: the naive agent, who does not foresee her own reversal and is repeatedly surprised by it; the sophisticated agent, who foresees it and arranges in advance to be constrained; and, on some accounts, a resolute agent, who simply refuses to reopen a plan she has already settled and holds to it with no external constraint at all. I want these three types to do real work in your reading, and I want you to be explicit about which type fits which character.

Read the Odyssey through this material, attending at least to the Sirens, the bag of winds that the crew opens while Odysseus sleeps, the year spent with Circe, the cattle of Helios at Thrinacia, and the lotus-eaters. Structure your response in the following sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

1. Two pictures of akrasia, and the three types (about 200 words). Set out the alternatives as sharply as you can, and say what each predicts about precommitment.

2. Odysseus at the mast (about 350 words). Treat the Sirens as the test case. Does the binding solve the problem of weakness of will, or does it relocate it? What is the philosophical status of a constraint designed by someone else, imposed on the agent by others, and in force only while he is unable to act on his own? Decide whether this is self-control, a change in the choice situation that makes self-control unnecessary, or something the poem leaves open.

3. The crew (about 400 words). The bag of winds and the cattle at Thrinacia are both failures of a group, and in both cases the leader is asleep; at Circe's island it is the crew who must remind Odysseus of the return. Ask whether the hunger at Thrinacia is better described as a shift in the discount rate than as a failure of will, whether the lotus changes what the agents value rather than producing an akratic lapse at all, and whether the poem locates responsibility in a collective rather than in an individual will.

4. Where the poem pushes back (about 350 words). This is the honest critic's part. In a world where folly can be sent by a god, and where the decisive error is a leader falling asleep, the concepts of will, choice, and responsibility may not line up with anything in the modern debate. Name the places where the reading distorts the poem, and say what the poem assumes about agency that the framework denies.

5. What the poem adds (about 250 words). End with what these episodes show about weakness of will that neither theoretical picture captures, and state whether the poem supports the motivational picture, the time-inconsistency picture, or neither.

Open with a short plan of no more than six lines: the episodes you will use and the claim you will make about each.
