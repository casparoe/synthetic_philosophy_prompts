Hey, I'm in a reading group on philosophy of mind and AI and we keep circling back to Dennett's intentional stance, and I want to finally pin down whether it's actually the right tool for talking about LLMs 'believing' things. Here's what's bugging me. There seem to be two readings of the stance floating around and people slide between them without noticing. On the instrumentalist reading, calling a chatbot a believer is just a predictive convenience — you get better predictions of its outputs if you model it as having goals and beliefs, full stop, no further claim about whether the beliefs are 'real'. On the realist-about-patterns reading (Dennett's own, I think), the patterns are genuinely there in the system, so the attribution is true or false in a mind-independent way, not merely useful.

A friend in the group floated a third idea I can't shake, and I want you to take it seriously even though it's half-baked. Call it the 'commitment stance': on this view, attributing a belief to a system isn't mainly about predicting it or detecting a pattern, it's about *holding it accountable* — you're asserting that it ought to update a certain way, that certain inputs make its output defective, that it can be criticized. So 'the model beleives P' comes closer to 'the model is committed to P and is wrong if it then outputs not-P'. I like this because it seems to match how we actually argue with chatbots, but I worry it's just smuggling a normative claim in under a descriptive word.

So here's what I want: compare Dennett's intentional stance (in whichever reading you think is strongest) with this commitment-stance idea, specifically for the case of belief attribution to LLMs. Where do they actually disagree, and where is the disagreement merely verbal? Does the commitment stance buy us anything the intentional stance can't already give — does it explain anything about how we *should* treat model outputs, or about the difference between a model that's confidently wrong and one that's 'lying'? And is there a case where the two come apart in a way that matters for how we build or regulate these systems? Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I've got the Dennett covered; I want the argument. End with one final line that starts with 'Verdict:' naming which of the two frameworks you'd actually use for belief-talk about LLMs, and in a single sentence why.
