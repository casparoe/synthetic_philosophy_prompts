I retired two years ago after forty years of protein crystallography, and since I'm not running a lab anymore I've been sitting in on the philosophy department's evening seminars, which is how I got into a week-long argument with a grad student about tipping. She gave me this and I've been chewing on it ever since. I've cleaned it up into steps because she insisted.

(1) A convention, in the standard philosophical sense, is just a regularity that a group follows, expects everyone else to follow, and prefers to follow given that the others do. Queuing in Britain, twenty percent in an American restaurant, standing on the right of the escalator, not walking into an empty street against the light.
(2) Nothing in that description says the convention has to be a good idea, or fair, or efficient. It's just what everyone does and what everyone expects.
(3) When I break one as a visitor — I don't tip, I cut the queue, I cross against the light on an empty street — I impose a foreseeable cost on the locals. The server is out money, the queue gets less predictable, people have to watch me to guess what I'll do next.
(4) Imposing a foreseeable cost on people who aren't doing anything to me, for no reason beyond my own convenience, is wrong.
(5) So when I'm in Rome I do as the Romans do, not because the Roman practice is better, but because the expectation is there and stepping on it costs somebody something.
(6) But nothing in (3) or (4) cares about the border. If expectations are what generate the obligation, they generate it at home too. The guy who doesn't tip at his own diner, my neighbor who jaywalks on his own street — same costs, same kinds of people.
(7) And it goes further. If the expectation does the work, then any convention enough people hold is binding, including ones I'd call rotten — a town where everyone expects to hand the building inspector an envelope, an office where everyone expects the women to make the coffee. And nobody could ever reform anything from the inside, since the reformer is by definition the one who breaks the expectation. Every reformer is a queue-jumper.
(8) So either conventions aren't obligations at all, or visitors are bound and locals aren't, or every convention binds everyone, bad ones included.

I can't accept (8). I'd most like to keep (4) — stiffing a waitress who's making two dollars an hour plus tips seems to me plainly wrong, and I don't want a theory that says it's fine as long as it's a Tuesday and I'm home. So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me?

What I actually want out of this: what does the visitor owe that the local doesn't, if anything? Does the answer come out different for the queue than for the tip? My econ colleague says the queue is a coordination problem and the tip is a price, and that they aren't the same animal at all — if that's the move, spell it out, because I don't see how coordination problem does any moral work by itself. A seminar-mate mumbled something about fair play, that you're bound once you've taken the benefits of a cooperative scheme, and then said the whole thing collapses because nobody asked for the benefits. Is that a real distinction or a restatement of the problem? And I keep feeling there's a word doing quiet work in (3) and (4) that I haven't pinned down — tell me if that's the flaw or just what obligation is.
