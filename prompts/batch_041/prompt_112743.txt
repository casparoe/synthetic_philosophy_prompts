I want a credence estimate on a fairly specific interpretive and evaluative claim about Kant on animals. The claim is this: the best reading of Kant's considered view is that our duties regarding animals are duties to oneself, grounded in the way cruelty corrupts the agent's own moral disposition rather than in any downstream effect on how the agent later treats human beings; and this self-regarding reading, once separated from the cruder habituation reading (be cruel to dogs, become cruel to people), both fits the late ethical writings better and is independently defensible as a position that gives animals robust protection while still denying them direct moral status. I'm at about 0.5 myself and want to know where that should move. Give me your number and decompose it: how much depends on which reading the texts actually support, how much on whether the self-regarding reading is internally coherent given that Kant grounds the duty in something about the animal — an analogy to our duties to rational beings — which looks like it smuggles in a direct status claim; how much on whether the reading yields determinate verdicts where the habituation reading is silent or contingent, namely private cruelty with no witnesses and no character spillover, factory farming, vivisection, and wild animal suffering, about which Kant appears to say nothing at all; and how much on the independent moral plausibility of protecting animals only for the sake of the agent who mistreats them. Assign each term a rough probability with a line of justification, combine them, then tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to and what single argument or piece of textual evidence would move it most.
