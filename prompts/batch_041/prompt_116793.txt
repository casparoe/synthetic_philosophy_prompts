Hey, so I'm putting together a panel for our department's grad student workshop series — the theme this term is 'the commons after Ostrom', deliberately loose — and the closing session is a conversation with a political economist who's spent her career arguing that the tragedy of the commons framework is basically a solved problem that keeps getting unsolved for rhetorical reasons. Her line is that once you stop modeling common-pool resources as one-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas between anonymous strangers, the 'tragedy' stops being a theoretical prediction and becomes an empirical question about institutions, which is definately a less exciting thing to put on a book cover.

I'm the moderator and I get maybe eight to ten questions plus follow-ups. I want to actually make her work for it, not just let her take a victory lap. Can you draft the sequence?

Stuff I want in there, roughly:

- Whether it's fair to say common-pool resources aren't really Prisoner's Dilemmas at all, and what actually turns on the game-theoretic classification. I have a vague memory that some of these situations are better modeled as chicken or as assurance games, but I don't trust myself on the details, so press her on what concretely changes — for prediction, for policy — if you swap the game out.

- The obvious objection that the design principles she's famous for are just a list of features that happen to be shared by the commons that didn't collapse, i.e. survivorship bias. Then the follow-up to her most likely reply: I'd bet she points to the lab experiments, so have the next question ready about what those experiments can and can't show, especially given that they mostly involve people who can talk to each other during the session and then go home to unrelated lives.

- Scale. Someone always asks whether any of this transfers to the atmosphere or to high-seas fisheries, and she always says 'it's a design problem.' I want to pin down what the actual design proposal is for something with no bounded community, no shared history, and nobody who can realistically be sanctioned. If she comes back with 'polycentric governance,' follow up on whether that's a theory or a slogan.

- One question that turns on a distinction I think gets blurred constantly: whether we're talking about non-excludability or non-rivalry, and whether it matters which one the thing actually has. I suspect the answer is 'yes, a lot,' and that a pile of bad policy follows from treating a depletable resource like a public good. Make her spell out why.

- End on what would actually make her give up the view. What would a real, non-fixable commons tragedy look like, and has she ever seen one she couldn't redescribe as an institutional failure?

Please make the follow-ups specific to what she'd plausibly say instead of generic 'and why do you think that?' — the whole point is to close off the escape routes. And nothing about how she got into the field, obviously.
