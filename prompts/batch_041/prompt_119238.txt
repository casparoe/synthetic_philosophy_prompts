I'm a media studies prof at a small liberal arts college and I've somehow been roped into giving the commencement address for our communications program next month -- a few hundred people, mostly the graduating seniors and their families, plus a row of my colleagues who will be grading me silently the whole time. Here's my problem. Last spring a group of our own seniors ran a fundraising campaign that went viral: they set up an anonymous account that read like it belonged to a broke nineteen-year-old who was donating her plasma money, and the story was 100% true -- it really was a student, the money really went to the food pantry -- but nobody knew the account was being run by the marketing club. It raised a ton, the administration quietly hated it, and a good chunk of the seniors still think it was the best thing they did in college. Since then I've been argueing with my colleagues about whether that was persuasion or manipulation, and I keep landing on "the facts being true isn't the whole story," which I can't seem to get past. I want the address to hand these kids something they can actually use in their first jobs at agencies and campaign shops, not a finger-wag, and I also don't want to pretend the plasma-girl campaign was simply shameful, because I don't believe that either.

Can you write me the speech, around seven or eight minutes spoken, with a shape I can actually deliver out loud without fumbling? It needs to draw a real line between legitimate persuasion and manipulation -- one that does structural work in the speech, so that some things about that campaign become sayable and others don't -- and it has to land for the parents in the audience who have never heard the phrase "dark patterns" without talking down to them. If the honest answer means conceding that the campaign was partly manipulative even though it was truthful and for a good cause, go ahead and say that; I'd much rather the speech be a little uncomfortable than evasive, and the students will smell a dodge instantly.

One more thing: at the end, add a short note addressed to me, clearly seperate from the speech itself, telling me which parts of the line you drew are genuinely settled among people who study persuasion and manipulation and which parts are live disagreements that I'd be presenting to a captive audience as though they were obvious. I don't want to stand up there and hand 300 people a controversial position dressed up as common sense.
