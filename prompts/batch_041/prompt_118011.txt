I have no training in philosophy, and what I know of the idea below comes from a popular book about medieval Jewish thought rather than from the sources themselves, so I may have parts of it slightly wrong. I have been turning it over for a long time, and what I would like from you is a short story that does something with it, not an explanation of it.

The idea, as I understand it, is Maimonides' account of the laws of sacrifice. He thought those laws were not given because sacrifice is what God wants, but because of where the people were. They had been raised among nations whose worship consisted of killing animals in temples, bowing to images, and burning incense before them, and he says it is not in human nature to drop everything at once — that a people cannot be moved from one extreme to the other in a single step, any more than an infant can be taken off milk and put straight onto bread. So the outward form of the worship was left standing and its object was changed: the altar, the animal, the smoke, the priest, the incense all continue, but addressed now to God alone and forbidden to anything else. The aim, in his telling, was to uproot idolatry and establish the unity of God without unsettling people by abolishing the only worship they knew. I believe he expects his reader to find this strange, and answers by pointing to the same gradualness in the way nature itself brings a young animal along.

What I cannot settle is not whether he is right about the ancient Israelites. It is the shape of the thought once you pull it out of that setting. It seems to say several things at once, and I cannot get them to sit still together:

- the form of a practice and its purpose can come apart, so that you keep the whole outward thing and change what it is for;
- the arrangement is a concession to weakness rather than an ideal, which means the one who ordains it knows it is not what he would have chosen had the people been different;
- a concession of that kind has a life of its own, since the generation that receives a ladder hands on a house;
- and telling people that their most serious rites are provisional may be either an act of care or an act of vandalism, and I cannot tell which, or when it is which.

So: a story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words in which those four thoughts are in conflict and none of them wins. Some things I would ask of it.

Invent the place. Keep it clear of the ancient Near East, of Israelite religion, and of any real faith, so that I can see the shape of the problem rather than the costume. Any world will do — a town, a guild, a school, a hospital, a family trade — so long as the practice at the center is one the people treat as sacred or as simply what is done, and so long as it involves the killing of animals. I want that killing to be ordinary to them: not exotic, not dwelt upon, done the way it has always been done.

Make no one a villain and no one a fool. The person who wants to keep the practice and quietly change what it means, the person who thinks a rite that must be reinterpreted for people is a lie told to them, and the person who wants the thing abolished outright should each have a case I can feel.

Let the story cross more than one lifetime, so that I can watch the accommodation become something else — the ladder become a wall, or a curiosity, or a thing people are willing to be killed for.

Do not let anyone explain the idea. No wise elder laying it out, no closing scene in which the protagonist says what it all meant. I want the reader to work it out from what people do, what they are willing to lose, and what they notice and fail to notice.

I would rather it end on a concrete detail — an object, a gesture, a smell, a bill of sale — than on a moral.

And please do not name Maimonides in the story or set it anywhere in his world. I want the thought, not the man.
