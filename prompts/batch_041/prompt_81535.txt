Act as a philosopher of science with genuine expertise in the reduction, emergence, and inter-theoretic relations literature, and as someone who can explain these matters to a working scientist without hiding behind jargon. Think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming the space of possible readings before you evaluate any of them.

I am a fourth-year PhD student in a computational biology group, and I keep having the same argument with my PI. Whenever anyone proposes to explain a higher-level phenomenon by the behaviour of the parts, she says: the whole is more than the sum of its parts, and the parts are different inside the whole than they are outside it. She has now deployed this in three settings. First, when I said that our model system's population-level behaviour, in which a population of cells commits or fails to commit to a fate, could in principle be predicted from the rules governing individual cells. Second, when a visiting speaker claimed that developmental biology will one day be derived from molecular biology plus chemistry. Third, when I asked whether the population's decision is a real feature of the world or merely a convenient shorthand. I suspect the slogan is doing different work each time, and that she would resist being pinned to any single reading.

I do not want a verdict on her; I want a menu. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, and for each one state what it commits you to, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and which of my three settings it would actually support. Make sure you separate at least the following axes, and add any you think are missing: first, the valuational reading, on which the more is about value or significance; second, the ontological reading, on which the whole contains entities, properties, or causal powers absent from the parts; third, the predictive reading, on which the whole is not derivable from the parts even given unlimited computation; fourth, the explanatory reading, on which higher-level explanations are legitimate even if reduction is possible in principle; fifth, the nomological reading, on which new laws or regularities genuinely appear at the higher level; and sixth, the second clause in particular, the claim that parts are altered by being parts, which I take to be a separate thesis about relational or context-dependent properties. Be careful to keep claims about what there is apart from claims about what we can know or compute, and to distinguish versions that concern a single time-slice from versions about how a system develops over time.

Then tell me which reading is most likely what someone who repeats this slogan in a lab meeting actually means, and, the part I care most about, which single reading, if any, does the most real argumentative work for the anti-reductionist, and under what further assumptions, whether about multiple realizability, the failure of bridge laws, or downward causation, that reading becomes plausible. Write it as a structured list I can take to my next meeting.
