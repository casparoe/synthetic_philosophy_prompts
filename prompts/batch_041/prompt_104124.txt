I direct a small chamber-music festival, and on the first night I have to give the opening welcome. The room will be roughly half professional musicians and half donors and their families. I want the welcome to rest on a conviction I have come to from reading Schopenhauer — that music represents nothing, that it is not a copy of this or that emotion or of the composer's state of mind but a direct objectification of the will itself, in a manner the other arts do not achieve — and I want that idea to govern what I can and cannot say, rather than being merely name-dropped. That is where the difficulty lies, since almost everything one normally says on such an occasion assumes the contrary: that the pieces are about something, that the slow movement is grief and the finale joy, that the players are sharing their feelings, that we have gathered in order to be moved. Write the welcome so that it keeps faith with the Schopenhauerian line and still says something real to a room that has come to hear music — including to the musicians, who will know the philosophy better than I do and will notice if I fudge it. Let it also face, rather than dodge, the question the view raises about the occasion itself: if music communicates nothing, what are we all doing in the same room? Four minutes or so, spoken aloud, in continuous prose, the way one would actually deliver it — no headings, no bullet points, no lists.
