You are an experienced designer of philosophy classroom simulations, and you know the set-theoretic literature well enough to be precise about the options available. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the foundations of mathematics — 18 students, one 90-minute session, partway through a unit on naive set theory — and I want an activity in which they collide with Russell's paradox using their hands rather than hearing it named. Think carefully before you answer: sketch at least two candidate rule-sets for the activity, satisfy yourself that the one you keep makes the collision unavoidable rather than merely likely, and keep that work out of the reply. Answer with exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no headings beyond the numbering, and no more than two sentences per item.

The surface must be administrative rather than mathematical, and it must not be the village barber. Concretely: a federation of clubs, each posting a membership list that may include its own club; a federation rulebook; and one club — call it the auditor's club — whose posted list is required to contain exactly those clubs that do not list themselves, with the auditor's club itself being a club in good standing and therefore subject to the same requirement. The words set, membership, comprehension, and Russell stay out of the room until the debrief. Participants need a scoring scheme, in points rather than money, that gives every club a reason to want its own list complete and accurate, plus a certifier role whose signature is required before the round can be closed; materials should be index cards and wall space.

The five items should cover, in order: (1) the setup — room, materials, roles, and the exact state of the board when play begins; (2) the rules and the scoring scheme in full, including the one clause that produces the collision and how the payoff makes that clause impossible to satisfy; (3) the run of play to the minute — when the collision lands, what the facilitator says and pointedly does not say while the group flails, and a contingency second surface in a different idiom, neither clubs nor sets, in case the group believes it has escaped in under fifteen minutes; (4) the debrief, which must map each specific move the group actually made, including the ones they took to be cheats, onto the real repair it resembles — restricting comprehension to separation from an existing collection, stratification or a theory of types, admitting proper classes that are not themselves members, or allowing a true contradiction — naming the price of each; and (5) what predictably goes wrong the first time the activity is run, how to handle each failure, and an honest statement of which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must therefore supply in words.

By the end they should be able to say, in their own terms, that the offending list can be neither posted nor withheld, that their own workarounds were the historical repairs in miniature, and that none of those repairs is free. Where you are not confident that a claim about a repair is correct, mark it as uncertain rather than asserting it.
