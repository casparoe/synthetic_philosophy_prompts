My flatmate and I have been going round in circles for about a year about Dutch book arguments, and I've started to suspect we arent even having the same disagreement. Her position: the theorems are decision theory dressed up as epistemology. All they establish is that an agent who puts a price on each bet separately, takes every bet that looks good to her in isolation, and then lets the bookie combine them will lose for sure -- which is a fact about her betting dispositions, not about whether her credences are correct. She says she's happy to be 'incoherent' in that sense, since her credences are just her degrees of confidence and she never promised to price anything; she also points out the bookie has to know her credences in the first place, and she doesn't have to tell him. My position: someone who is 0.6 confident in A and 0.6 confident in not-A is getting something wrong about the world; the sure-loss book is a symptom of a genuine epistemic defect, not a quirk of her preferences. We both know the standard arguments, so please dont just explain sure-loss back to me. What I want is a diagnosis: is this substantive, verbal (we mean different things by 'rational'), or two different questions -- norms of action vs norms of belief -- hiding under one label? Is the very concept of credence the thing in dispute? I have a half-formed idea that the combination step is doing all the work: she'd refuse the package, and I can't tell whether refusing the package is itself irrational or whether that only looks obvious from inside the betting picture. And what would move this forward -- some result showing incoherent credences are inaccurate by her own lights, or an argument that nothing like that can be had? Honest answer please, including if you think no progress is to be expected.
