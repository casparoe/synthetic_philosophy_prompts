So I've been circling Dennett's counterfeit-people essay in The Atlantic ever since it came out, and I keep hitting the the same wall. I get the broad shape — the counterfeit-money analogy, the point that unlike nukes these things can reproduce and evolve on their own, the worry that the intentional stance is basically irresistible so we're all going to be sitting ducks — but I can't figure out what the actual ask is supposed to be. As I read it, the whole problem is that you can't reliably tell a counterfeit person from a real one, so the remedy has to be a better test: Turing's imitation game is what landed us in this mess, so we need its inverse, some procedure you can run on whoever you're talking to, in the moment, to settle whether there's a person on the other end or a counterfeit. That's the part I want help building.

So: if you were the engineer Dennett is implicitly asking for, what would the test look like? What do you probe — consistency across time, some kind of embodied or situated know-how, the capacity to be held to account, a willingness to admit you're wrong? And how do you stop it from degrading, because the obvious move is that the counterfeits will just train on your test until it's worthless, and I don't see how that arms race ends well for us. There's a nagging thing underneath this too, which maybe I should just ask directly: when Dennett says counterfeit, is he saying the thing only seems conscious, or that it only seems responsible? Because if it's the former, I feel like what I really want is a test for real personhood or sentience and not just for provenance, and those seem like very different engineering problems — one of which I suspect isn't an engineering problem at all. Give me the design, but also tell me if I'm even asking the right question, because something about the framing feels off and I can't put my finger on it.
