Right, I'm rebuilding the mind & meaning half of my phil of mind course and I want a fiction piece that actually does some work instead of just being a fun break, so I'm commissioning you. Write me a short story — call it 1200 to 2000 words, first or third person, whichever the story wants — that dramatizes the symbol grounding problem. It's going in the reader, so it should stand on its own for someone who's never heard of the problem, without ever naming it or explaining it.

Now, the obvious version of this (a room, a rulebook, a guy who doesn't speak the language) is played out and my students will nod off, so I want the harder, weirder version, and I want the story built on two claims that I'll state badly here and you should feel free to sharpen.

First: you can't ground symbols one at a time. The tempting move is to bolt a sensor onto a single word — photodiode to "red", pressure plate to "pain" — and declare victory. But a symbol only means anything against a whole background of other symbols (looking red vs. being red, surface vs. illumination, the difference between pain and the noise a system makes when it's damaged), so the grounding is either all-or-nothing for the web or it isn't happening at all. Piecemeal anchoring is a cheat.

Second: a system can have meaningful symbols without doing any anchoring of its own, because the meaning gets in secondhand, from the agents that built it or read its output. I've seen this described as a machine stealing its meaning from the people who made it. It's genuinely meaning, but it's borrowed, and it fails the moment the lenders walk away.

So: a story where those two things are the actual stakes. Some concrete setting where somebody is trying to get a system's words anchored and the plot turns on the fact that partial grounding is a mirage and parasitic grounding is a loan, not a possession. I'd love the failure to be specific and mundane rather than apocalyptic — a certification that gets signed, a demonstration that goes fine until the humans leave the room, a word that keeps working right up until the one case where it matters.

Couple of constraints. No character delivers a lecture summing up the debate at the end, and no narrator winks at the reader about what it all means; I want the idea to be the engine of the plot, not the moral of it. Don't cite or name any real papers or researchers, don't put in an afterword, and don't lean on the standard thought experiments — invent your own apparatus. End on something concrete. I'll probably teach it alongside some pretty dry stuff, so if it's got a bit of a chill to it, all the better.
