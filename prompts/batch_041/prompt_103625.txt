Ok so I keep going back and forth on Hofstadter's idea that the 'I' isn't a little thing sitting in your head but a pattern — a self-referential loop — and that this pattern can get partly copied into other people's brains. So the more two people carry the same pattern the more they're the same self, and when someone dies the pattern just keeps running in everyone who knew them. A friend whose much smarter than me turned this into an arguement and now I can't sleep:

(1) A self is a pattern, not a lump of stuff — a certain kind of self-referential organization of information.
(2) Patterns are repeatable: the same pattern can be instantiated in two places at once. That's just what it is to be a pattern.
(3) So if my self is a pattern, and the pattern can be instantiated in your brain, then my self is at least partly running in your brain.
(4) Any two brains carrying enough of the same self-pattern are, to that degree, the same self.
(5) But 'enough' has no natural cutoff — similarity comes in degrees, and any threshold you pick is arbitrary.
(6) So for any two people there's some nonzero degree to which they're the same self, and no fact of the matter about where one self ends and the next begins.
(7) If selves have no boundaries then 'I' and 'you' are conveniences, not facts.
(8) And then there's no content left to 'I should care more about my own future pain than a stranger's,' since 'my own' picks out nothing.
(9) Which is absurd — it dissolves the very person the theory was meant to explain, and it can't tell me apart from a perfect copy.

I'd rather keep 1 and 2, they seem like the heart of the view. So which step do I give up, why did it look so reasonable, and what does giving it up cost me — what do I have to say about death, love, or duplication to keep the rest? Under 450 words please.
