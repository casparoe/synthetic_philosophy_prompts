Suppose David Hume were confronted with the contemporary debate over future contingents and the sea-battle argument. One position, open futurism, holds that propositions about future contingent events are neither true nor false, and that this indeterminacy is a feature of reality rather than merely of our knowledge; the future is genuinely open, while the past is fixed. A second position, tenseless truth, holds that every proposition has a determinate truth value at every time, because truth is tenseless and the future is as real as the present. A third position, which I will call retro-closure, holds that future contingents lack truth value until the event occurs, but once it occurs the original future-tensed proposition acquires a truth value retroactively, so that the past is not entirely independent of the future. How would Hume have positioned himself among these views? In particular, how would his distinction between relations of ideas and matters of fact, his skepticism about necessary connection, and his account of causation as constant conjunction shape his response to the claim that open futurism requires giving up bivalence for future contingents, and to the claim that retro-closure preserves bivalence only by making the past depend on the future? Which of the three would he have found most congenial, and which most antithetical to his own considered view? And how, if at all, would he have revised the sea-battle argument in light of these modern distinctions?
