I'm a novelist by trade and a very amateur philosopher by temperament, and I've landed myself in something. A group in my town has finally got permission to take down the bronze of a Victorian mill owner that's been glowering over the market square since about 1890, and instead of putting it in a warehouse they're going to have it melted down and recast — most of it as a bench, a bit of it as a drinking fountain for dogs, and a lump kept back for the museum's handling collection. They've commisioned me to write a poem to be printed on a card at the unveiling of the bench, and I said yes before I'd worked out that the whole thing is a philosophy problem in fancy dress.

A friend of mine teaches metaphysics and she's been no help at all, in the best way. Her line is that the recasting isn't a curiosity, it's the statue-and-the-clay problem with a council budget. The bronze and the man are in the same place at the same time, made of the same atoms in the same arrangement — she says there's a whole literature on what on earth could ground the difference between them if there's no material difference at all — and yet they come apart the second you ask what each of them can survive. Melt the thing down and the bronze is fine, only differently shaped; the mill owner is gone, and not because he died in 1894. And she insists philosophers don't even agree that there are two objects there to begin with: some think there's only the bronze, arranged mill-ownerwise, and that we should stop saying there are statues at all; some think there's one thing with two histories; some think there are genuinely two things sharing every atom and differing only in what's true of them. She says my poem is only worth anything if it doesn't quietly pick a side.

So: please act as a poet who actually knows this literature, brainstorm for a bit before you write a line, and give me a sestina for the unveiling. Here's what I need, and its the form that should do the work, not me. The six end-words should all be words that can be said truly of both the statue and the metal, so that at any line-end the reader can't tell from the word alone which of the two they're being told about; the sestina's fixed shuffling should make that ambiguity accumulate rather than clear up, the same six things returning in new arrangements, matter under a form it never chose. The envoi should be the melting — six words crammed into three lines, the form giving out, the man going and the bronze carrying on. The modal asymmetry has to be there plainly (the bronze can survive being a bench; he can't survive being anything), and the grounding question should stay audible as a question, unanswered. No name-dropping, no footnotes, no citing anybody at all; I want a specialist to nod and someone at the unveiling to get a poem out of it. British English throughout, and definately don't end on a tidy consolation — the bench is a bench, that's all, and the poem should leave the one-thing-or-two-things question exactly where it found it.
