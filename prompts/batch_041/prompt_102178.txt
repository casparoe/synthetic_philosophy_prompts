I recently joined the board of the twelve-unit housing cooperative where I live, and at my third meeting we deadlocked on a proposal to hire a part-time caretaker for the shared courtyard, stairwells, and bin store. The board had agreed to treat the decision as resting on three sub-questions, with hiring to follow if and only if all three came back yes: (1) can the co-op absorb the cost within the current maintenance budget; (2) does the work genuinely need doing beyond what residents already do; (3) would creating one paid post make it harder to turn down other paid requests later. We then voted on each sub-question and on the bottom line. The result was 7–5 yes on (1), 7–5 yes on (2), and 7–5 yes on (3), but 8–4 against hiring. No member had answered yes to all three sub-questions — the yes-coalitions on the three questions overlap in different places, so the board's majorities are jointly inconsistent even though each member's answers to the sub-questions were internally consistent. (Four members then voted for hiring anyway, which sits awkwardly beside the premises they had just affirmed.) I was chairing and, honestly, could not referee the argument that followed.

Rehana argued that we should simply take the sub-questions as the decision: "We voted that we can afford it, that the work needs doing, and that it wouldn't wreck our ability to say no later. Those three answers, each endorsed by a majority, entail hiring. If we refuse to hire, we have to report to the annual meeting that all our stated reasons point one way and we went the other."

Marek replied that this inverts the whole point of a vote: "The motion on the table was to hire a caretaker, and that motion lost 8–4. A board that hires when a clear majority voted not to hire has stopped being answerable to the members. You would be letting five people outvote seven on the only question anyone actually cares about."

Rehana came back that the 8–4 is an artifact: "The no-majority is not one objection, it is three different objections wearing a trench coat. Seven members each reject a different one of the three conditions. There is no reason a majority of us shares."

Marek said that made things worse rather than better: "If no majority shares a reason, then the group has no common rationale for hiring, and a decision that rests on premises no individual member accepts is a decision nobody can be answerable for. And your procedure is wide open to gaming. If I want no hire, I can vote against whichever sub-question I judge pivotal even when I believe it true, and I have changed the outcome without changing anyone's mind."

Rehana answered that insincere voting is a hazard of every procedure, not a special defect of hers, and that the remedy is transparency about reasons rather than suppressing them. She added that voting the bottom line first and the sub-questions afterwards would likely produce a different result, because people would answer the sub-questions in light of the position they had already staked out — so Marek's method is at least as order-sensitive as hers.

At that point our treasurer proposed a compromise: run both procedures, and hire only if the premise-based answer and the conclusion-based answer agree; otherwise the motion fails and the courtyard stays as it is. Both Rehana and Marek objected, though for opposite reasons — she said it hands a structural advantage to the status quo and rewards the board for being internally conflicted, he said it lets a procedural tie-break masquerade as a decision and would paralyze the board even when we agree on every reason and differ only on the bottom line. We adjourned without resolving anything, and the question is back on next month's agenda.

Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it: for each substantive claim Rehana, Marek, and the treasurer make, say whether it is right, wrong, or right only under qualifications, and why. I would also like to know what both main parties are missing that neither of them raised, and finally, given that this is a real decision the board has to make, what procedure we should actually adopt and what it would commit us to.
