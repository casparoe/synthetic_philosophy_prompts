You are a philosopher of economics with expertise in decision theory, welfare economics, and the foundations of revealed preference. I want you to reason carefully and explicitly, and to begin by brainstorming the space of candidate principles before you commit to any of them.

Background. A colleague of mine — a senior economist who works on the foundations of choice — has spent years issuing verdicts on whether particular patterns of behavior reveal a preference, and if so, for what. She is careful, internally consistent, and unafraid to say that no preference has been revealed. I have kept a running list of her rulings, and I want to predict how she will rule on a case she has not yet seen.

Her verdicts so far:

1. An agent whose choices cycle under identical presentation — A chosen over B, B over C, and C over A, all in one session with the same wording — has, in her view, revealed no preference. She treats the data as too unstable to support the attribution of any preference at all, rather than as three preferences held in turn.

2. An agent who repeatedly buys the stale of two identically priced cartons, where the staleness is visible only on close inspection, has not revealed a preference for stale goods. She calls it a mistake: the agent did not choose the object she took herself to be choosing.

3. An agent who is indifferent between two brands and reliably settles the matter by flipping a coin has still revealed a preference — a weak one — and she insists that indifference plus randomization is not the absence of preference.

4. An agent who, fully informed and unpressured, chooses to keep smoking has revealed a genuine preference to smoke. She rejects the suggestion that this is a mistake or a failure of will, and equally rejects the suggestion that we should look past the choice to what is really good for him.

The new case. A retirement-savings scheme automatically enrolls employees unless they actively opt out. An employee is auto-enrolled, never opts out, and contributes for years. When later surveyed, she reports that she would not have signed up had she been required to opt in, and that she has simply never gotten around to opting out. My colleague has not yet ruled on whether this employee has revealed a preference, and if so, for what.

What I want from you:

(a) Brainstorm the candidate rules or principles that would fit verdicts 1 through 4 — aim for at least three genuinely different ones, and state each as a principle she could be running, not merely as a label. Include at least one rule under which the earlier verdicts all come out the same but which nonetheless implies something distinctive about the new case.

(b) For each candidate rule, state exactly what it predicts about the new case: does the employee count as having revealed a preference, and if so, a preference for what — for the savings plan, against it, or for some more finely described object?

(c) Give your best guess at her actual verdict, with your reasons for preferring that rule to the others.

(d) Say where her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, and name the single further case — not the present one — that would best separate the surviving rules from one another.

(e) Flag any point at which you are extrapolating beyond what her verdicts support, and state the assumptions you are making.

Do not simply rehearse the literature on defaults and choice architecture; I am interested in the inference from her track record to this case. Keep the distinction between "what she prefers" and "what is good for her" firmly in view throughout, and be explicit about which of the two each candidate rule is really tracking.
