I'm a trustee of a small egg farm co-op, about thirty family farms, and we're going for a salmonella-free certification so the supermarkets will stock us. The certifier wants a statement from the board that no egg we have ever sold has carried salmonella, and we vote on it Thursday, they want it by Friday.

Our evidence is three years of lab testing, roughly 12,000 eggs, every one of them clean. Our lab man is honest that his test isn't perfect: he reckons if an egg did have it he'd catch it about nine times out of ten. And then one of our trustees pulled the county outbreak regester for the same three years, about forty confirmed salmonella cases, every one traced to other farms, mostly chicken meat, none of them ours, and he wants to stick that in the submission as extra evidence.

That's where we fell out. Trustee B says the outbreak data is rubbish evidence, they were other people's eggs, it tells us nothing about ours. Trustee C said something I can't shake: if a salmonella case from somebody else's farm supports our claim, then every egg in the world that isn't ours and doesn't have salmonella supports our claim as well, and that is obviously daft, so something must be broken in the way we're thinking about the whole thing.

I went down a rabbit hole online. There's this old puzzle, the raven paradox, I think it's Hempel, where the claim that all ravens are black is supposed to get support from a green apple, on the grounds that all ravens are black means the same as everything that isn't black isn't a raven, and a green apple is a thing that isn't black and isn't a raven. I dont know whether that's a real argument or a conjuring trick, and I don't know which of my trustees it's actually backing. One comment under the blog post I read said the whole thing hinges on how you went looking, that a green apple only counts if you were checking in a way that could have turned up a raven, and I couldn't tell if that was the answer or a dodge.

Can you write me the short memo I'll hand round at the meeting. What I need from it: a recommendation I can actually put to a vote, on whether the outbreak data goes into the submission and, if it does, how we word it; a standard for what counts as evidence that we can use for the next few years, because we'll be making claims like this again and I don't want to keep having this argument; something that does justice to Trustee C, because I think he's found something real and I can't say what; and the honest case for the other side, including if the right answer is that the outbreak data stays out, say so and say why, because two trustees will vote against me and I need to know what I'm up against. Plain English, no citations, nobody on our board reads academic papers, I just need to be able to defend it out loud on Thursday.
