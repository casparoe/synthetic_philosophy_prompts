I chair the board of a small education nonprofit. We run a six-week summer reading program for rising third and fourth graders in a mid-sized city: about 180 children, roughly $400,000 a year, funded almost entirely by a single family foundation whose renewal decision comes up in the spring. Children at our partner schools may apply; we admit by lottery. Our own evaluation last summer found gains of about a tenth of a standard deviation over a comparison group, with a confidence interval that includes zero.

At our last meeting, one of our trustees — a retired actuary who has been reading up on behavioral genetics — circulated a memo arguing that we should either change how we admit students or shut the program down. His reasoning, as fairly as I can restate it: (1) reading ability is highly heritable, with twin studies putting it around 70 percent; (2) if genes account for most of the variation, then a six-week program can only be pushing on the small environmental remainder, which is why our evaluation found nothing; (3) recent work makes it possible to compute a polygenic score from a saliva sample that accounts for roughly a tenth of the variation in how far people get in school; and (4) we should therefore collect samples with parental consent, admit the children with the highest scores, and get a much larger effect for the same money — and the same score could tell us which children are most likely to benefit from the program in the first place. He has asked for a vote in March.

A second trustee, a former elementary school principal, says this is all beside the point, because a heritability figure is just a ratio for one population at one time and tells you nothing about whether a program works. I think she is right in spirit, but when the actuary asked her what the heritability numbers would look like if our program did work, she had no answer, and the room drifted toward his side.

I have ten minutes at the next meeting and then I have to take questions. Please help me prepare. I want to know: whether the inference from the heritability figure to the conclusion that our program cannot work goes through, and if not, exactly where it breaks; what does follow about our program's prospects if we take the figure at face value; whether the score-based admission plan makes sense, and what would have to be true for it to deliver the improvement he promises; what to say to the second trustee, who I suspect is overstating her case as much as he is overstating his; and what we should actually do in March. Assume I am reasonably numerate but not a specialist, and give me the argument I can stand behind in the room rather than a reading list — including what we could learn over the next year that would genuinely move me off my position.
