I am a final-year high school student, and next month I have to give a talk to a joint meeting of my school's politics society and the politics society of the university nearby — an event our head of politics has arranged and which I am beginning to regret agreeing to. I speak for about twenty minutes and then take questions for as long as people want to ask them, and I have been told more than once that the questions are the part that actually matters.

The thesis I am defending is this: that the British monarch's surviving personal powers — the reserve powers, the power to refuse royal assent, the power to dismiss a prime minister or refuse a dissolution of Parliament — are not the harmless ceremonial relics that nearly everyone, including most of my teachers, takes them to be, and that constitutional monarchy works not in spite of these powers but because of them. My argument is that an unused power is not the same thing as a non-existent power, and that the monarch functions as a genuine check on the executive precisely by holding a weapon that remains credible only for as long as it is never fired. I want to push this further than is comfortable: I want to claim that a directly elected president cannot replicate this, because an elected president is a politician with a mandate, whereas the monarch's authority rests on not having one.

I am aware of the obvious counters and I do not yet have good answers to them. I know that royal assent has not actually been refused since the Scottish Militia Bill under Queen Anne in 1708. I know that the power to dismiss a government was last used in Britain in 1834, and that the one modern instance, in Australia in 1975, looks less like a safeguard and more like a coup. I have also been reading about a set of conditions, drawn up in 1950 by the King's private secretary and published in The Times under a pen name, setting out when a sovereign might be entitled to refuse a dissolution of Parliament — conditions about whether the existing Parliament is still viable and whether another prime minister could command a working majority — and I want to use them, though I am not certain I have understood them correctly. I have even less grip on how a law from 2011 that took the dissolution power away, and another from 2022 that gave it back, bears on any of this.

The audience is the difficulty. The undergraduates will have studied constitutional law and will know the conventions better than I do, and one of them is a committed republican who I am fairly sure will raise Australia within the first minute. My politics teacher, who is a republican and used to teach law, will attack the logic of my argument rather than its history. My history teacher, who is a monarchist, will probably agree with me for reasons I do not share, which will somehow make my case look worse.

Please take the role of a constitutional lawyer and political philosopher who has spent years preparing students for exactly this kind of interrogation. Before you write anything, work out for yourself how each of these people thinks — what each will concede, what each will pounce on, and what each will dismiss as a cheap point. Then give me a preparation sheet in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, written the way you would write a long email to me, one person talking to another. I want the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, and for each one: whether it has real force or is merely a debating move, what I should concede at once, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me, too, what I should concede up front before anyone has to ask, and which single reply I most need to land for the argument to survive. Do not soften any of it. Finally, tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks, and how I should answer it if it comes. I do not need sources or further reading; I need the substance.
