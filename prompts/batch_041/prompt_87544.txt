For a graduate seminar on the foundations of game theory, I am assembling a problem set on program equilibrium, and I want one question that forces students to see what the usual argument for cooperation actually presupposes. I would like you to write the model answer: an argument for a specific verdict, namely that the familiar program-equilibrium defence of cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma succeeds only by trading on an idealisation, and that the idealisation in question is not a harmless technicality but the substance of the argument. I got into an argument about exactly this over lunch with a colleague, who insisted the whole thing was obvious bookkeeping, so I want the answer to be pointed enough to settle the dispute.

Here is the setup I want you to work with. Two agents play a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma and each submits a program in advance; when the programs run, each is handed the full source text of the other program and returns a single action, Cooperate or Defect, with the ordinary payoffs — mutual cooperation is better for both than mutual defection, defecting on a cooperator is best of all, and being defected on while cooperating is worst. Call a pair of submitted programs an equilibrium just in case neither agent could have improved her payoff by submitting different code, holding the other agent's submission fixed. The usual cooperative construction is the program that outputs Cooperate if and only if the text it has been handed is the very same program as itself, and Defect otherwise. Now add the wrinkle I want the argument to exploit: before any program is handed to the opponent, an arbiter is entitled to replace it with any behaviourally equivalent rewrite — dead-code removal, variable renaming, loop unrolling, substituting one equally correct routine for another, and so on — and the agents know this will happen, though not which rewrites they will receive. Argue that under these conditions the usual construction no longer produces mutual cooperation, and, more importantly, that this failure exposes the usual defence as resting on an accident: the construction works only because a program can be textually identical to itself, so as soon as the identity of a program is treated as a matter of what it does rather than what it says, the cooperative equilibrium it advertises is no longer delivered in the way advertised. Be explicit about where the burden of proof falls — the defender of the construction owes us a program that survives the arbiter — and say what any such program would have to accomplish, and why the obvious repairs, such as having each program run the opponent and react to its output, either beg the question or run into trouble of their own.

You may close with a sentence or two on whether you think some more sophisticated mechanism — a proof-carrying program, a mediator, repetition, anything of that kind — could restore cooperation, and what that would show about the original claim. Keep the answer self-contained: do not assume the reader has any particular paper in front of them, and do not cite sources or supply a reading list.
