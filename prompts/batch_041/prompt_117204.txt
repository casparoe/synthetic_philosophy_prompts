I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for the past two years I have been reporting on the revival of anthropic reasoning — the family of arguments that treat facts about observers as evidence about the world those observers inhabit. I am writing a book on the subject, and in four weeks I will present my own argument at a small invitational workshop on probability and cosmology. The book depends on that argument surviving contact with people who know the material far better than I do, so I would rather spend the remaining time preparing for the discussion than for the talk. Please draft the Q&A prep sheet I will carry into the room.

The position I intend to defend, stated roughly as I plan to state it:

The literature has settled into two rival rules for reasoning about one's own place among observers. On the self-sampling rule, you should reason as if you were a random draw from the observers in your world — which is what generates the doomsday argument. On the self-indication rule, you should reason as if you were a random draw from observers across all candidate worlds, weighted by how many observers each world contains — which is what generates the figure known in the literature as the presumptuous philosopher, who concludes that we should expect the world to contain astronomically many observers, on the grounds that the more observers there are the more likely it is that one of them is you, and who is therefore content to let a minuscule prior probability of a vast universe be swamped.

My claim is that these are two answers to a question that should not be asked in that form, because both rules treat the fact that it is I who exist as a piece of evidence, and they differ only over how heavily to weight it. I separate what I call the existence datum — that there is an observer having these experiences, here, in a world of this kind — from the identity datum, that the observer in question is me. I argue that the first is evidence and the second is not, and I give a name to the extra likelihood both camps grant the bare fact of being the particular observer one is: the indexical surcharge. My rule is that the surcharge is zero, and I call the resulting view flat anthropics. On flat anthropics the doomsday argument fails and the presumptuous philosopher fails, and they fail for the same reason, which I present as the view's chief selling point rather than as a suspicious coincidence. I should be clear about what I am not claiming: I do not say that facts about observers are evidentially inert. I say that the fact that the universe is old enough for stars, or that we occupy a hospitable patch of it, still does real work; only the "me" part is idle.

The room will be small, and I will be the only non-specialist in it. Five people matter, and they do not agree with one another. The first is the organiser, who has spent her career defending the self-sampling rule and who regards the doomsday argument as basically sound; her view is that anyone who rejects self-sampling owes an account of why he is not simply refusing to reason. The second defends the self-indication rule and accepts the presumptuous philosopher's conclusion as a bullet he is willing to bite; he holds that the only honest options are enormous populations or no indexical reasoning at all. The third is a cosmologist who works on measures in eternal inflation and who uses anthropic reasoning as a tool; he has no patience for the philosophical preliminaries, will want to know what my rule delivers for the measure problem, and will treat "nothing" as an answer that disqualifies me. The fourth is a Bayesian statistician who holds that indexical credences are not probabilities at all and that both camps are committing a category error; he will agree with my conclusion for reasons I do not share, and I need to know what that agreement costs me. The fifth is a philosopher of language who works on indexicals and centred worlds; she will say that the identity datum I propose to discard simply is the centre, and that discarding it is not a bookkeeping choice but a rejection of centred-worlds semantics — a far larger commitment than I have so far acknowledged. There will also be a few observational cosmologists at the back who suspect the entire debate is a way of avoiding the actual physics.

Please give me the objections I will actually get in that room — not the ones I would get in an online forum — ordered by how much damage they would do to my position, each phrased the way the person who holds it would phrase it, each with a reply I can deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is soft or where I will be improvising. Tell me which objections are fatal to the argument and which are fatal only to the way I have framed it. Tell me what to concede in my opening remarks so that the worst of them lose their force before anyone raises them. And tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
