I'm a grad student in philosophy and I've basically swallowed David Lewis's story about convention whole: a convention is just a regularity that solves a recurrent coordination problem, it's arbitrary among several equally good alternatives, and it's kept alive because each of us expects the others to conform and prefers to conform given that expectation. I want to push it further than Lewis does and claim that's all there is — no shared agreement, no rules stored in anyone's head, no common knowledge of each other's rationality, and the regularity can be held up in a large population by nothing but imitation and reinforcement, with most people never forming any belief about what others expect. Please act as a philosopher of social science and game theorist, start by brainstorming a list of candidate defeaters before you rank them, and think carefully about each one.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd really be — which observations, experiments, formal results, or actual social phenomena tell against it, versus which are merely awkward. Say what a defender like me would have to concede counts against us, not just what critics would throw. And be honest about whether I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability: if any regularity that happens to stick around can be relabeled a convention, is the theory risking anything at all? Push hard on the arbitrariness condition too — are there stable norms everyone would rank the same way, where there's a unique best option, that we'd still want to call conventions? And can the picture even explain how conventions evolve or flip from one alternative to another, or does it just describe the equilibrium once its there? I'd be most moved by a case where the mutual-expectation machinery is missing yet the norm persists, or the reverse — expectations all present and the behavior still unstable.
