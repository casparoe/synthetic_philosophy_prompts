OK so I just lost about three hours of my life arguing with my friend Priya about money pumps and I need to see this written down properly. Her line is that if someones preferences go in a circle then a money pump follows automatically, and that that just IS what it means to be irrational, end of story, no further questions. My line was that the pump only ever runs if you accept every trade in the sequence and you never bind yourself ahead of time, so the whole thing is an argument about whether thats a fair assumption or just a description of a sucker. She said I was doing cope. So I want a satirical piece, sustained and deadpan, in the form of an official guidance note from a fictional regulator, something like the Office of Diachronic Consumer Protection, around 900 words, in the flat numbered-section voice of a real government guidance document, with a definitions section, worked examples, and a short appendix of forms. The premise of the world: the state has declared money-pumpability to be a consumer harm, so the office audits citizens preference orderings for cycles and then charges them the value of the arbitrage a pumper could have extracted from the cycle, minus a handling fee, on the stated grounds that otherwise private arbitrageurs would just get the money instead.

The jokes have to come from actually getting the philosophy right, so that someone who works on diachronic choice laughs rather than winces, and a reader who has never heard of any of this still follows it. Things I want in there, but shown through bureaucratic furniture rather than explained: that the pump needs the agent to take each pairwise trade at the time and to be unable to commit in advance; that the resolute chooser and the sophisticated chooser are the two obvious escapes, and that the office can only handle them by inventing licence classes and exemption schedules which, if you read them closely, quietly concede the disputed premise; that a sequence of choices each of which is fine on its own can be terrible as a whole, so synchronic and diachronic rationality come apart; that exploitability is a standing disposition rather than something that happens to you, which raises the awkward question of whether the office is charging people for cycles they would never actually walk; and that the office offering its own precommitment service to citizens is a bit rich, since the office is now the thing doing the pumping. I dont want the document to argue with itself or wink at the reader. It should stay completely straight, paper over every problem with a definition, a fee schedule or a form, and let the reader feel the objections without the document ever naming them.

No citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and dont name any real philosophers, economists, or living people, its a government document, it doesnt cite anyone and it shouldnt mention me or Priya. If you need a case study just invent a citizen with a cycle and a sad little income. And it should be legible to someone who has never heard the term money pump: the definitions section can explain it in one plain sentence and then never explain it again.
