In a seminar I reconstructed an argument that strikes me as proving too much, and I want you to diagnose it. It is meant to draw out a consequence of the holistic picture of belief associated with Quine's attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction — the picture on which our statements face the test of experience collectively rather than one by one, and none of them is exempt from being given up. The argument runs as follows.

(1) For every statement in our web of belief, we can preserve that statement as true by making suitable adjustments elsewhere in the web.
(2) So for each statement, there is a total system of belief that contains it.
(3) Therefore there is a total system of belief that contains all our statements at once.
(4) If some total system of belief contains all our statements, then no statement ever has to be abandoned.
(5) But sometimes a statement does have to be abandoned — a prediction fails, and we give up the hypothesis rather than patch something else.
(6) Therefore (1) is false, and no statement is revisable in the sense the holistic picture needs.

The conclusion is absurd to me, and yet each step reads like a fair statement of the picture. Tell me which step I should reject. Explain why that step looked acceptable, and then price the alternatives: if I give up the step you name, what does that commit me to regarding the difference between revising a belief and changing the meaning of a word, and can that difference be drawn without appealing to the analytic-synthetic distinction the picture was supposed to do without?
