I have no training in philosophy — everything I know about this I've assembled from arguments I've stumbled into online and a couple of popular books — so I've had to reconstruct the following debate myself, and I've reached the point where I can no longer tell what the two sides are actually fighting about.

One side holds that if I can conceive of something, that is at least evidence that it is possible, and on the stronger version the only evidence we could ever have about what is possible beyond the actual world. The case usually offered is a world physically identical to ours in which no one is conscious: I can apparently imagine it, I find no contradiction in it, and that is supposed to be a reason to believe such a world is possible. The other side says the instrument is broken. People once thought they could conceive of water that wasn't composed the way water in fact is, or of heat that wasn't the motion of particles, and those turned out to be impossibilities. Conceivability, on this view, tracks our habits of thought rather than the space of what could have been.

What frustrates me is that both sides accept the same cases. Nobody denies the imaginings occurred; nobody denies that they misled us before. The disagreement survives every example I throw at it, and I've begun to suspect the two sides are not answering the same question — one is describing what occurs in a mind when it imagines, the other is asking what that occurrence entitles us to believe — and that the word conceivable is quietly doing two jobs: on one reading it means that I can picture something without noticing a contradiction, on the other that no contradiction could be uncovered by an ideal reasoner however long they searched.

My working hypothesis is that this is the whole of the disagreement: a dispute about which of those two things the word names, dressed up as a substantive dispute about what is possible. But I can't make the hypothesis stick, for two reasons. First, I have tried to conceive of a counterexample to the link itself — a case conceivable in the strongest sense yet impossible — and I cannot, and I cannot tell whether that failure is evidence for the link or merely a report about the limits of my own imagination, which is precisely the point at issue. Second, it seems to matter which reading we take: if conceivability is only picturing, it cannot be a guide to possibility at all, so the dispute cannot be merely verbal if one side's position would collapse under the other's reading of the word.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal disagreement, a substantive one, two different questions wearing one name, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is some hidden factual question doing the work — about how imagination actually operates, or about whether there are necessary truths discoverable only through experience? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a case, a formal result, or nothing at all, with a reason either way.
