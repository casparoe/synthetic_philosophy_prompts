As I reconstruct it, Pascal's treatment of justice and force amounts to a thoroughgoing conventionalism: whatever a society's customs and established laws have settled on is just, with no rational standard available to us by which those customs could be judged, and since a justice that commands no force is helpless while a force that answers to no justice is tyranny, what passes for legitimate authority is force that has been made to look just — chiefly through custom, imagination, and our habit of mistaking the established for the natural. I read any gesture he makes toward a justice that is true independently of custom as inert, not as a standard he expects us to apply. I am inclined to accept this position, and I want to know what counts against it. Give me the objections, cases, and consequences that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, and keep separate those that attack the claim that there is no standard of justice from those that attack the psychological and political account of how force becomes authority. Say what a sympathetic reader would concede counts against it, and which objections such a reader would dismiss as misunderstanding the view. Finally, tell me whether the position has been insulated from refutation — by the doctrine of the Fall, by the appeal to an unknowable justice, or by sliding between a descriptive and a normative reading — and whether that insulation is a defect or simply the price of the view. What would move me most is a showing that the position cannot describe something it is committed to describing, rather than merely a showing that its implications are distasteful.
