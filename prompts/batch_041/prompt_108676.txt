I'm a science journalist — I write long features about ideas, mostly for a general-ish audience that's willing to sit through some abstraction — and for the last few months I've been working on a piece about Bayesian confirmation: the picture where belief comes in degrees, and evidence just means conditionalizing on what you learn. It's a beautiful picture and I basically believe it, which is exactly why the old evidence thing has me stuck. I've been interviewing philosophers and statisticians about it for weeks and gotten definately more confusion than clarity. Here's my version of the problem. A theory shows up and explains something everybody has known forever — the anomaly in Mercury's perihelion, say, or why some particular constant has the value it does — and everyone, including the people who are lukewarm on the theory, treats this as a serious point in its favour. But if evidence is conditionalizing, that can't happen: I was already certain of the fact, and conditionalizing on something you're already certain of doesn't move your credences at all. So the single most impressive thing a new theory can do — make sense of something we already knew — comes out as worth exactly zero. I gather the standard response is to say: fine, don't use your actual credence, use the credence you would have had before you found out, and conditionalize on that instead. And every time I hear it I have the same double reaction: it's obviously the right thing to say, and it's obviously a cheat, because I'm now running my update on a number nobody ever held, in a history that never happened, and the size of the boost seems to depend alot on how ignorant my counterfactual self is allowed to be.

What I want from you is a real explanation of this, not a survey of who said what. Concretely: what is the old evidence problem once you strip away the textbook framing, and is it about the evidence being old or about the certainty (someone told me it's the latter and I couldn't tell whether that was a dodge or the whole answer)? Why does the counterfactual move feel both necessary and illegitimate — is the illegitimacy a technical wart that somebody has patched, or a symptom of something deeper about the whole project? Does the fix turn confirmation into a fact about a person's biography — when exactly they happened to learn what — rather than a relation between a theory and the world, and if so is that a bug or just the honest position I should stop resisting? And here's the one that really nags me: if the fix works, a theory that was reverse-engineered to fit the known fact gets the same boost as one that called its shot blind, which seems to throw away everything that's supposed to be impressive about prediction. Please don't send me to the literature — I can't read a stack of papers on deadline, half of them are paywalled anyway, and I don't need the names. I want the concepts untangled in plain language, the moves laid out in the order you'd actually make them, and an honest note on where each move leaves you worse off than you started. Use Mercury if it helps, but don't spend your time on the history.
