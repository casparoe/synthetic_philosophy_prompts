I am grading the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate course on causal inference and quantitative reasoning, and I would like you to grade one student's answer for me. Below you will find the exam question, followed by the student's response. Please return a grade and the specific comments that justify it.

The exam question was:

A new drug is tested against a placebo. The investigators also recorded each patient's baseline severity, classified as mild or severe. Recovery rates were as follows:

- Mild patients: treatment 9 of 10 recovered (90.0%); placebo 80 of 90 recovered (88.9%).
- Severe patients: treatment 18 of 90 recovered (20.0%); placebo 1 of 10 recovered (10.0%).
- Pooled: treatment 27 of 100 recovered (27.0%); placebo 81 of 100 recovered (81.0%).

Answer all four parts.

(a) Report the recovery rate for each arm in the pooled sample and within each severity stratum.
(b) Name the phenomenon these data exhibit and explain, referring to the numbers, how the reversal comes about.
(c) State which analysis, the pooled or the severity-stratified one, should guide the decision about whether the drug is effective, and justify your choice.
(d) State a general principle for deciding, in any study, whether to report a covariate-stratified result or the pooled result.

The student's answer was:

Pooled, the treatment arm recovers at 27% (27 of 100) and the placebo arm at 81% (81 of 100), so on the surface the placebo looks substantially better. Disaggregated by severity, however, the treatment is superior in both strata: among mild patients, 90% (9 of 10) on treatment recover versus 88.9% (80 of 90) on placebo, and among severe patients, 20% (18 of 90) on treatment recover versus 10% (1 of 10) on placebo. The direction of the association therefore reverses when the data are pooled.

This is Simpson's paradox. The reversal is produced by baseline severity, which is a confounder: severity predicts recovery, since severe patients recover less often, and it also determines which arm a patient tends to fall into, since the drug was given mainly to the severe patients (90 of the 100 treated patients are severe, whereas 90 of the 100 placebo patients are mild). Because the treated arm is weighted toward the patients least likely to recover, its pooled rate is dragged below the placebo's even though the drug helps within each stratum.

Simpson's paradox is confounding in disguise: a reversal of this kind arises exactly when a third variable confounds the treatment-outcome relationship, and the cure is to condition on that variable. The general rule, then, is that whenever a third variable changes or reverses the association, the stratified analysis is the causally correct one and the pooled analysis should be set aside; this is the same logic by which department-level analyses overturned the university-wide figures in the Berkeley admissions case. Applying that rule here, the stratified result should guide the decision: the drug is beneficial in both severity groups, and the pooled table should not be read as evidence that the placebo is superior.
