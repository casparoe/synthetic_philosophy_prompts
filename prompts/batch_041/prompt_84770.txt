I am setting the take-home final for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy. The closing unit of the course is on truthfulness and deception, and the students have read Kant's short 1797 reply to Constant, including the example of the murderer at the door. I need one essay question for the final, and I have already settled the case it is to turn on. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme.

The case, which I will hand to the students verbatim, is this. You live alone in a ground-floor flat. Your neighbour has told you that she is being stalked by a former partner, that he has been violent towards her before, and that she does not want him to know where she is; she is in the next room. Nothing outside the flat suggests that anyone is at home. Late in the evening the former partner knocks, says he knows she has been staying with you, and asks, "Is she here now?" Four responses are available to you. (A) You say nothing and close the door. (B) You say, "I am not going to answer that," knowing that a refusal from you in these circumstances will be heard as a yes. (C) You say, "No, she is not here." This is false, and he leaves. (D) You say, "She was here last night." This is true, and you intend him to conclude that she has since moved on; he does conclude that, and he leaves.

The question must oblige students to do four things, and I want the model answer and the marking scheme organised around them.

1. Locate the duty Kant actually invokes and say to whom it is owed. In the essay the duty of veracity is put forward as a duty of right owed to humanity in general, and expressly not as a duty owed to, or forfeitable by, the particular person who asks. The question should make students say what follows from that, and must not let them settle the first question by appealing to the questioner's deserts.

2. Decide, on Kant's own terms, which of the four responses does and which does not violate the duty of veracity. This requires separating the prohibition on declaring what one holds to be false from any prohibition on producing, or trading on, a false belief in another. The case is constructed so that the two come apart, and a strong answer will say which of the two the essay's own argument supports.

3. Confront the fact that the essay's central argument about responsibility is a consequence argument: the liar answers for what follows from his declaration. Students must ask whether that argument can be confined to lies, given that a calculated refusal, or a true but misleading statement, can interpose the speaker between wrongdoer and victim just as effectively as a lie can. They must then say what follows if it cannot be confined: either the duty of veracity is broader than the essay states it to be, or the responsibility argument is carrying weight that the duty of veracity cannot supply.

4. Say what duty, if any, the speaker owes to the person in the next room, and whether it is of the same kind as the duty of veracity. The essay classifies veracity as a duty of right and the relief of another's distress as a duty of virtue; the question should make students say whether that classification settles the case or merely relocates the difficulty.

Constraints on the question itself: it should be answerable in about sixty minutes and run to no more than 250 words; it must not be answerable by reciting the Constant exchange, by the bare formula that Kant forbids all lies, or by a general essay on absolutism against consequentialism.

Constraints on the model answer: about 700 words; it must reach a definite verdict on each of the four responses and defend it; it should paraphrase Kant rather than quote him; and it must not cite editions, page numbers, or secondary literature, since the students have no library access and I do not want invented references.

Constraints on the marking scheme: 25 marks in total, distributed over five must-hit points; for each point, its mark value, a one-line statement of what a top answer does to earn it, and a one-line statement of the specific error that costs it; and a closing note naming which point or points separate an A from a B.
