The hook is a clinical condition called pain asymbolia. After certain kinds of brain damage -- typically to the insula and the cortex around it -- patients report that a stimulus hurts. They can tell you where it is and how intense it is. But it doesn't bother them. It's not that they're faking or being stoic; some of them seem almost cheerful about it, they'll smile while you're testing them. And yet they still flinch or pull their hand away. So here's a person who seems to have the sensation of pain without the awfulness of pain. Philosophers argue about what that shows. Maybe pain is just a bodily sensation and the badness is a separate motivational state that can get disconnected from it. Maybe they aren't really in pain at all. Maybe it shows that pain isn't intrinsically bad, which would be awkward for anyone who treats pain as the paradigm of something bad for you.

Now read Huxley's Brave New World through this. The World State has basically engineered suffering out of existence -- soma, conditioning, the works -- and the Savage's whole objection is that he wants the right to be miserable, to feel things, to have the pain along with the pleasure. The standard reading is utopia versus the value of suffering, and thats fine as far as it goes, but the asymbolia stuff makes the book weirder and more interesting. So: what has the World State actually removed? The pain, the awfulness of the pain, or both? Does the novel even see the difference? And does the Savage's defense of suffering really need pain to be awful, or could you keep the sensation -- the information, the warning, the intensity -- and just drop the awfulness, which would give the World State most of what it wants without the cost he's complaining about?
