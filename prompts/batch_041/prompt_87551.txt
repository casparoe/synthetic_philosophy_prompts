I am setting the final examination for an upper-level seminar on mind, mechanism, and evolution, and I need one essay question on competence without comprehension — the pair of inversions of reasoning standardly attributed to Darwin and to Turing. It must not be answerable by reciting that Darwin showed how design can arise without a designer, or that Turing traded the question whether machines think for a test of performance. The crux I want is the disanalogy between the two inversions: Darwin's concerns how a competence can come to exist with nobody understanding it, Turing's concerns how a competence can be realized with the realizing mechanism understanding nothing. The essay must say what each inversion licenses and what it leaves untouched.

Two elements should be built into the question. First, a case that neither inversion handles cleanly: a system that acquires a competence in a single episode by copying a competent model, with no selection history behind that competence and no comprehension in the mechanism — a child who reliably applies a rule she cannot state, or a network trained to imitate text; the question should ask what each inversion implies about such a case and where each falls silent. Second, a formulation put forward in seminar that students must adjudicate: that the two inversions are a single thesis in two domains, since wherever a competence is found it can be explained without appeal to comprehension at that level, comprehension being merely a competence at the level above.

Then supply a model answer of about 800 words and a marking scheme out of 25 marks, itemised so that an A answer and a B answer are separated by named moves rather than by fluency, and recording the specific errors that should cost marks: treating Darwin's inversion as turning natural selection into a purposeful agent, treating Turing's as behaviourism or as the claim that nothing is left to explain, and deploying the level-up formulation in a way that makes the inversions unfalsifiable. The model answer should not lean on quotation or citation; students write from memory.
