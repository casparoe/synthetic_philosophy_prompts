I have been trying to write down a settled position on vagueness and keep failing, so before I commit anything to paper I want a proper audit of whether the package even hangs together. Here is what I take myself to hold.

(1) Bivalence and classical logic are non-negotiable. Every meaningful declarative sentence is either determinately true or determinately false; there are no truth-value gaps and no true contradictions. I am therefore neither a supervaluationist nor a dialetheist, and I want a theory on which excluded middle is not merely a theorem to be preserved but a claim about truth itself.

(2) Vagueness is not a defect of language to be engineered away. A vague predicate has no boundary that could be drawn even in principle: there is no pair of items differing minutely such that one is the last to satisfy the predicate and the other the first to fail. Any theory that assigns the predicate a sharp cut-off has misdescribed the phenomenon.

(3) Borderline predications nonetheless have a determinate truth value; it is just that we cannot know which. The sorites is unsound, one of the conditional premises is false, but we can never identify the false premise, and no amount of empirical information or linguistic reflection would help us do so.

(4) What a speaker means by a predicate is fixed by use: by the dispositions of competent speakers to apply and withhold it. There is no meaning-constituting fact over and above those dispositions.

(5) A speaker has privileged, reflective access to what they mean. I know what I mean by 'tall' without having to investigate empirically how I or anyone else applies the word.

(6) Vagueness is a feature of the world, not merely of our language or of our knowledge: there are states of affairs that are neither determinately one way nor determinately the other, and this is so independently of us.

I am aware that (2) is the very intuition that is usually taken to motivate the supervaluationist picture I reject in (1), and that (6) is usually taken to motivate views that abandon bivalence outright, so I suspect I am pulling in more than one direction at once. But I do not trust my own sense of where the real fault lines are, and I would rather be told than guess.

Please give me an audit rather than a survey. First, separate the pairs that are genuinely in tension from those that merely look as though they are, and say what a defender of the package would have to say about the latter to dissolve the appearance. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity and, for each, state what exactly would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost — which commitment is weakened, and what is lost in weakening it. Third, tell me whether any single revision would clean up the most, and what the resulting package would predict about a case or commitment I have not listed. Do not assume I already know which commitment I am least attached to; I genuinely do not. Please write your answer in British English.
