I'm on a civic commision that keeps having to rule on statues and street names one at a time -- a bust here, a square there -- and we're doing it entirely ad hoc, case by case, whoever bothers to show up to the meeting. Before we look at any actual theory of commemoration I want the spec sheet first: what does a theory of when a community may keep, remove, relocate, or annotate a monument actually have to deliver? Treat it as the adequacy conditions, not the answer.

So: the cases it has to get right, with the case given each time. Use these, and add any you think the list is missing:

- A fountain put up by public subscription in 1902 for a man who funded the town's first schools and also led a militia that burned a neighbouring settlement. Both facts are in the local paper of the day.
- A statue of a poet erected twenty years ago by an immigrant association, in a language most of the city doesn't read. The community that put it up has since moved away, and it's now a gathering point for a group that reads the poet as theirs.
- A street named after a living donor who has since been convicted of fraud. Nobody much cares, but renaming means readdressing half the city.
- A monument whose plaque was stolen in the 70s. No one alive knows who it commemorates; the plinth is a well-known meeting spot.
- A monument to a figure who was, by the standards of his own time, on the progressive side of the main question of his day, and by ours is on the wrong side of it.

Then the distinctions I don't want collapsed, because I suspect half our fights are just these getting smeared together:

- honouring vs. recording (the museum-plaque answer -- is the objection to the statue, or to it standing in the square?)
- erecting vs. keeping. This is the one I care about most: is keeping a monument an endorsement, or just inertia plus the cost and hassle of removing it? If a theory can't say which, it can't say anything.
- the honoree's life vs. the monument itself -- can a monument be objectionable in a way the person wasn't, or the reverse?
- who has standing: current residents, the honoree's descendants, descendants of the people harmed, people who live elsewhere but use the square every day.
- remove / relocate / re-annotate / counter-monument / leave it up and fund something else -- is that a real menu of distinct options or one option in five costumes?
- public ownership vs. private, and public land vs. public money.

And the constraints it can't contradict: that a city is a temporally extended body whose present members never agreed to its past speech acts; that a state's speech is held to different standards than an individual's; that the same object can be public art and a political claim at once; that there may be no neutral option, since taking a statue down is also a statement; and that some value attaches to settlement -- we can't relitigate everything every ten years, but I don't know how to state that without it becoming a licence for whatever happens to already be up.

Two proposals from colleagues I'd like the list to judge. First, the one-way ratchet: anything can be removed by a two-thirds vote, but nothing new can be erected for fifty years, since we can't know how our own honours will age. Second, the marble rule: only the dead, and only a century dead, on the grounds that a monument is a wager on the future and we should bet small. Does an adequate theory vindicate either, and does it do so for the right reasons?

Most of all I want the pairs that can't both be satisfied. The one I keep bumping into: the theory has to be able to say the honoree's record was bad enough to matter, but it also has to avoid turning the council into a court of historical moral judgment, since we have neither the competence nor the legitimacy for that -- and yet every removal decision is exactly that judgment. If that's a genuin contradiction, say so, and tell me what has to give. And tell me which single requirement does the most work, i.e. rules out the most otherwise-plausible views (the history-not-heritage line, let-each-community-decide proceduralism, the contextualise-don't-remove compromise, the preservationist default). Skip the reading list, I don't need to be told what to read.
