I am a high school student, and for an independent research project I am writing up a disagreement between two older members of my school's philosophy of science club. One of them holds that the genetic code really is a code: that the cell stores information in DNA roughly the way a machine stores it in memory, with arbitrary assignments between symbols and what they stand for, and with machinery that reads those symbols off; the other holds that this is a metaphor borrowed from human communication, that nothing in a cell assigns a meaning to anything, and that the chemistry is doing all the work while the word code is doing none. They have been arguing since September, neither has given ground, and I suspect they are talking past each other more than either would admit.

Please draft the joint statement the two of them could both sign at the close of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I would like a numbered section of claims both must accept, covering what the standard genetic code does and does not determine, what each of them must mean by information for their disagreement to be a genuine one rather than a verbal one, and what the translation machinery does and does not add; then the precise points on which they still differ, each phrased in wording its own side would accept, which you may set out as a table; then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them; and, for each crux, a case or argument that both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, together with what each would count as evidence against its own position rather than the other's.

A few matters I would like considered, if they belong. Whether something can count as a code only if the mapping between symbol and effect is arbitrary, and what the variant codes show about that — the cases in which a codon that usually means one thing means another, or in which a codon that ordinarily ends translation instead inserts an amino acid. Whether a code requires a reader that understands, or whether a machine that reliably converts one thing into another is sufficient, and what the comparison with a computer's memory is worth here. Whether the notion of a misreading has to be defined independently of what actually happens in order for information to be doing any work, which I would like tested against a silent mutation and against a gene that is never expressed. And whether calling DNA a code has ever let anyone predict something the chemistry alone would not have, or has mainly produced errors of the kind found in the picture of DNA as a blueprint. If part of the argument turns out to be verbal, I would like that said plainly, with the verbal part identified.

I do not need citations or a reading list; I want the argument itself, written as the document the two of them would put their names to.
