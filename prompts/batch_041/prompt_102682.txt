I am a graduate instructor preparing a problem set for a seminar on conditionals and probability, and I want one exercise in the "here is an argument; find the misstep" genre — something a student cannot dispatch by reciting the standard menu of theories. The argument I intend to hand them is this.

Assume the Equation: for any propositions A and B and any probability function P, the probability of the indicative conditional "if A then B" is the conditional probability of B given A, that is, P(A→B) = P(B | A). Now reason as follows. (1) The Equation holds for every probability function whatsoever. (2) For any proposition C, the function P(· | C) obtained by conditionalizing P on C is itself a probability function. (3) So, applying the Equation to P(· | C), we have P(A→B | C) = P(B | A ∧ C) for all A, B, C. (4) Take any A and B with P(A ∧ B) > 0 and P(A ∧ ¬B) > 0, and let C be B. Then P(A→B | B) = P(B | A ∧ B) = 1, so P((A→B) ∧ B) = P(B): whenever B holds, the conditional holds. (5) Let C be ¬B. Then P(A→B | ¬B) = P(B | A ∧ ¬B) = 0, so P((A→B) ∧ ¬B) = 0: whenever B fails, the conditional fails. (6) Hence "if A then B" is true in exactly the cases in which B is true; the conditional is equivalent to its own consequent. (7) That is plainly false — "if the die came up odd then it came up low" is not equivalent to "the die came up low." (8) Therefore the Equation is false.

I find the conclusion genuinely unsettling and I cannot decide what to tell the students. I would most like to preserve the Equation itself, and I am unwilling to give up the claim in (2) that conditionalizing yields a probability function, but I also refuse to accept (6)–(7) as anything other than a reductio. (I recognize the argument has the shape of the triviality results sometimes cited against the thesis that a conditional's probability is a conditional probability, but I want the students to reconstruct the diagnosis rather than look it up.) Please do the following: identify the premise or tacit assumption that has to be given up, explain why it looked innocent in the first place — what makes it tempting, and where the argument quietly exploits it — and then price the alternatives: if a student rejects the step you name, what else must they abandon, and is there any coherent package of commitments that keeps most of the premises while blocking the slide to (6)? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the step you would reject and the single most important cost of rejecting it — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
