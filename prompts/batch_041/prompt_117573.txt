I'm applying to a small foundation that funds conceptual work in the social sciences, for a six-month half-time grant, and I need a proposal of roughly 1,500 words. The subject is whether "generations" — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and their kin — are real kinds or merely convenient labels. I want to avoid the standard exchange in which one side says generations are socially constructed and the other says they are real, since both sides are usually vague about what they mean by real. Here is the half-formed idea I would like you to turn into a proposal.

The question I actually care about is whether cohort labels earn their keep in the way that kinds are supposed to earn their keep: by supporting inductive generalization. I want to test that against a criterion rather than assert it. The criterion I have in mind is a robustness requirement. A cohort label names a real kind only to the extent that the generalizations it licenses survive arbitrary re-drawing of its boundaries. If shifting a cutoff by three years, or substituting one polling organization's convention for a rival one, destroys most of what we confidently say about a generation, then the label was doing descriptive work that the underlying phenomenon was not doing. I suspect this criterion is necessary but not sufficient, and I want the proposal to say what else is required. One candidate: a real cohort kind must be underwritten by a shared formative cause, in which case the kind is really an event-kind — a discrete historical shock that a birth interval happens to index — rather than an interval-kind, and the birth-year boundary is a proxy that can drift from its object. Another candidate: some notion of explanatory indispensability, which would have to do the work in cases where the causal story is diffuse and no discrete shock can be identified.

The proposal needs to do the following. State the question so that it has a determinate answer. Explain why the existing positions leave it open, without a literature review: refer to positions and arguments rather than to works, since I will supply the citations myself. Set out the method concretely enough that a reader can see what I would actually do in each of the six months, given that this is conceptual work rather than data collection. Say what a result would look like, including a worked verdict on two or three candidate cases chosen for their structural differences rather than their notoriety. Be explicit about what would count as the project failing; I would rather the proposal name the outcome that would reveal the criterion to be trivially satisfiable than pretend the risk away. And give an honest assessment of whether six months is enough, and if it is not, what should be cut.

The audience is a review panel of social scientists, several of whom regard the metaphysics of kinds as a distraction and will want to know why a conceptual project, rather than a data-driven one, makes progress on a question that looks empirical. Address that objection directly rather than in passing. Do not include a bibliography or reading list.
