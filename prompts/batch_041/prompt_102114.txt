I am a philosopher by training but not a probability theorist, and I have somehow agreed to lead the next meeting of my reading group — a mixed bag of two lawyers, a retired economist, a graduate student in literature, and a frighteningly sharp sixteen-year-old — through the St. Petersburg paradox. The occasion is a running argument with a colleague who insists the paradox proves that expected-value reasoning is simply broken, and I have been failing, so far, to say why I think that verdict is too quick. I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and an audience whose algebra is serviceable but whose probability is nil. What I need from you is not an explanation of the paradox but a plan for the session.

Please take on the role of an experienced teacher of decision theory — someone who has watched first-timers get this wrong many times — and, before you commit to anything, think carefully about the dependency structure of the ideas and about where a novice's intuitions will misfire. I want: first, the order in which the components must be introduced, with the reason each depends on the one before it, from the moment I put a number on a risky prospect to the moment the session's real question is on the table; second, the single carrying example, and why it should be that one — here I have a bias I would like you to argue with rather than obey, because I would rather not open with the textbook doubling game if some variant does more work, and I want you to say what the carrying example must have, and what it must conspicuously lack, for the paradox to bite rather than merely impress; third, the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface — my own guess is that the audience will slide from "the expectation is infinite" straight to "expected-value reasoning is refuted", that they will assume playing the game many times rescues the rational player, and that a "typical" outcome tells you what the game is worth — together with the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off and how; and fourth, the one question I should ask at the end that would show whether they have grasped the structure of the paradox rather than merely been dazzled by an infinity.

I would also like you to say plainly what I should refuse to open at this level, and to flag any step where the honest answer is more delicate than the tidy one — in particular, I am unsure whether to present the classical diminishing-utility move as the resolution, as one resolution among several, or as a change of subject. If it helps, I am inclined to build the session on the contrast between what the game pays on average and what it pays typically, and to end by pricing the game for a counterparty who genuinely cannot fund an unbounded payout; but tell me if that is the wrong spine to hang it on, and please keep the whole plan free of names, dates, and anything my audience would have to look up.
