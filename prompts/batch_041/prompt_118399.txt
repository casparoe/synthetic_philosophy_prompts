A colleague of mine, arguing that the current nuclear landscape is less fragile than it appears, offers the following analogy: the Cold War standoff was like two scorpions in a bottle. Each could sting the other; each knew it; so each stayed still. On his account, the very fact of mutual vulnerability produced a kind of paralysis that made war irrational, and the same logic should reassure us about a world with several nuclear powers. I want an audit of the analogy rather than a general essay on deterrence. First, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the scorpions, the bottle, the sting, the stillness, the glass, and the observer. Then identify where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from those that are merely decorative. Pay particular attention to features the scorpion picture quietly imports: symmetry between the parties, two-player simplicity, complete information, a single decisive sting, no command-and-control, no accidents, no third parties, no conventional or proxy conflict, and no possibility of surrender or alliance. Then bring in the brinkmanship paradox: if the threat that deters is not a deliberate sting but the risk that events slip out of control, does the analogy capture that, or does it model deterrence as too deliberate and too stable? Finally, say whether his reassurance survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, sketch a better analogy for the multipolar case, one that keeps what is genuinely useful in the scorpion picture while representing delegation, misperception, and the always/never problem of nuclear command, where a system must be reliable enough to launch when ordered and safe enough never to launch by accident. Answer in no more than 900 words, with the correspondence table first.
