I teach a seminar on probability and its philosophy, and I want to build a session around Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead. The play opens with the two of them flipping a coin that lands heads again and again, and Guildenstern's mounting unease is not really about a streak of bad luck; it is about what a fair coin could even mean, and about whether the world is being run by chance or by a script — which, given that they are minor characters in Hamlet whose deaths are already written, is not an idle question. I would like you to read the play through the lens of the principle of indifference and the paradoxes that show how fragile it is, above all Bertrand's paradox about the random chord of a circle.

Please do the technical side honestly before you do the literary side. Set out why the principle of indifference — the rule that says we should treat possibilities as equally likely when we have no grounds to distinguish them — needs some specification of how the possibilities are parametrized before it yields any number at all, and why the chord example, where different but seemingly equally natural ways of picking a chord give different answers, is the classic demonstration of this. Bring in the closely related problem of the reference class, where the same event can be assigned different probabilities depending on which class we place it in, and keep clear the distinction between physical chance and a rational agent's credence.

Then read the play. Where does Guildenstern tacitly invoke something like indifference, and does the coin's behaviour expose the principle's weakness or merely a biased coin? Does the fact that their fates are dictated by another play within the play push the reading toward determinism, toward a metaphysical chance that no credence can track, or toward something the probability theory has no name for? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the play to an illustration of a paradox, and do not bend the mathematics to make the play come out neatly. Where the reading strains — for instance, if the discrete coin toss is simply not Bertrand's continuous case, or if the play's real subject is dramatic irony rather than probability — say so plainly. End by saying what the play supplies that the technical literature does not: perhaps the phenomenology of living under a rule that keeps telling you the odds are even while the world keeps refusing to comply.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this — that indifference is underdetermined without a parametrization, that the three standard chord methods are each uniform in some respect — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether Bertrand's paradox is a real paradox or only the exposure of a bad principle, whether any parametrization is privileged, and how the reference class problem should be resolved. Do not present a contested position as the consensus.
