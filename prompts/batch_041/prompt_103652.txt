Below is a draft essay on Buridan's ass. I would like detailed comments on it, especially on whether its central distinction is drawn accurately and whether the argument is philosophically interesting or merely verbal.

Essay: Choice Without Preference: A Policy-First Reading of Buridan's Ass

The familiar parable of the ass equidistant between two identical bales is usually taken to show that an agent with no preference between options cannot act, or cannot act rationally. I argue that this inference is too quick. The parable conflates two questions: whether an agent prefers A to B, and whether the agent has a reason to choose A over B. An agent can lack the former while having the latter if she has adopted a higher-order policy for cases of indifference. The policy need not rank A above B; it can instruct her to choose either, to randomize, or to follow an arbitrary but fixed rule. What matters is that the policy is endorsed in advance and applied consistently.

Call this the policy-first account. On it, the ass is not irrational merely because it lacks a preference. It is irrational only if it has no tie-breaking policy and yet is required to choose. The ass's tragedy, if there is one, is not the absence of preference but the absence of a procedure. The distinction matters because it separates the metaphysics of preference from the normativity of choice. Preference is a comparative attitude; choice is an action. On the policy-first account, the rationality of action depends on the structure of the agent's intentions rather than on the existence of a ranking.

Objection: the policy itself seems arbitrary. If the agent has no reason to adopt one policy rather than another, the policy cannot supply the missing reason. Reply: the policy is not meant to supply a first-order reason to prefer A; it is meant to make the choice determinate and answerable to a standard the agent can own. Whether that suffices depends on whether rationality requires maximizing preference satisfaction or acting from a coherent plan.

Please comment on three things. First, is the distinction between preference and policy accurate, or does it collapse into the familiar distinction between first- and second-order preferences? Second, is the arbitrariness objection fatal? Third, does the account help with cases of incommensurability, or only with cases of indifference? In your comments, state what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict on the policy-first account, and where its reasoning is most likely to fail.
