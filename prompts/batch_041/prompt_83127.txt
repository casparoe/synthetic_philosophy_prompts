Public speech now travels mostly through a small number of recommendation systems, and the institutions that run them have settled into a three-way practice rather than the two-way choice between permitting and prohibiting that the free-speech tradition tends to assume. Some expression is removed outright: credible threats, sustained harassment, non-consensual intimate images, sexual images of children. Some expression stays up but is never recommended; it breaks no published rule and anyone who goes looking for it will find it, but it is withheld from feeds and suggestions, its author cannot earn from it, and it reaches almost nobody who was not already looking. And a great deal of expression, including material that many people consider more dangerous than what falls in the middle category, circulates with no restriction at all, on the familiar ground that the remedy for bad speech is better speech. The same utterance can move between these categories according to who said it, who was likely to see it, and how it arrived; a warning screen, a click-through, or a change of distribution channel can be the difference between a post that is suppressed and one that is promoted.

I would like to know what conception of harm, and what conception of the value of speech, this arrangement presupposes. The harm principle is usually read as a constraint on prohibition: the only warrant for coercing expression is that it causes harm to determinate people, and the showing must be empirical and prospective. But an institution that leaves an utterance available while shrinking its audience seems to be operating on a different picture, on which the harm of speech is a function of exposure and attention rather than of the utterance itself, on which the same proposition is innocuous in a pull regime and dangerous in a push regime, and on which distribution rather than permission is where the real work is done. Reconstruct that implicit theory as carefully as you can, then tell me how much of it the practice actually needs. Which parts are genuinely presupposed, which are merely a comfortable fit that other theories would license equally well, and where is the practice underdetermined between a Millian account on which only downstream causal harms count, an account on which certain utterances constitute harm rather than cause it, and an account on which what is protected is not the utterance but the audience's chance of encountering it?

I am also bothered by the ordering. Outright removal is reserved for a narrow class of acts whose damage falls mainly on individuals, while quiet deamplification is applied to a much wider class of claims whose alleged damage is to the public at large, claims about elections and vaccines among them, even though those are precisely the cases in which the tradition says more speech should be tried first. And the practice treats reach as a speech-relevant dimension while the law, as far as I can tell, does not: incitement turns on the likelihood of imminent lawless action, not on how many people heard. Does the practice require an audience-relative theory of harm, or can it be reconstructed so that audience size is merely evidence about the same harm the law already recognises? Is the reach of a message part of the message? Is a right to speak a right to be heard, or neither?

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a strict reading of the harm principle: whether deamplification would count as coercion at all, whether the warning screen could survive, and what would become of treating two identical utterances differently because of the channel that carried them.
