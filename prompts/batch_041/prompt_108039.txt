A tax scheme I'll call proportional allocation works like this: no church is established, but on her annual return a taxpayer may designate a small fixed fraction of her income tax to any registered worldview community, religious or secular; the fraction left undesignated is then allocated among those communities in proportion to the designations that were actually made; and the state separately funds the historic majority church's buildings, chaplaincies, and broadcast slots out of general revenue, as cultural heritage. European church-tax systems approximate pieces of this. A colleague's credence that a liberal state may permissibly operate such a scheme is about 0.15; mine is about 0.6.

Give me your own credence, decomposed rather than asserted. I want a probability for each leading conception of neutrality being the correct one — neutrality of aim or justification, neutrality of effect, non-entanglement, equal treatment — and, for each, a probability that the scheme satisfies it; then the terms that do not reduce to a conception of neutrality at all: whether allocating undesignated shares in proportion to designated ones treats non-choosers as consenting, whether heritage funding is establishment by another name, and whether the comparison class should be strict separation with the majority church's existing endowments left intact. Show how the terms combine, tell me which one my colleague and I most likely disagree about, and say what evidence would move your number most.
