I hold a chair in philosophy but came to it late, after several years as an analyst in a public-health epidemiology unit; I am at ease in the technical vocabulary of that field — case definitions, incidence and prevalence, person-time at risk, ascertainment and surveillance bias, age-standardisation, competing risks, measurement invariance, construct validity, the ecological fallacy — and I would rather think about this problem in those terms than in the terms of the philosophy journals. The debate I want translated is the one over whether violence has declined: the thesis associated with Pinker and the objections of its critics, including the objections that turn on what is to count as violence and on whether the relevant quantity is absolute or per-capita. Restate that debate, as faithfully as you can, in the epidemiological vocabulary, and then report what does not survive the translation. Be scrupulous about not smuggling in epidemiological assumptions — that a case definition, once fixed, is stable across contexts, that a measured rate is a rate of a natural kind — and presenting them as translation when they are in fact replacements. Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: first, the decline thesis itself stated as a claim about rates; second, the critics' objections restated as claims about case definitions, ascertainment, and comparability across populations and eras; third, the per-capita-versus-absolute dispute rendered as the difference between an incidence rate and a count, and what that difference does to the argument on each side; fourth, the residue — the parts of the original dispute that have no counterpart in the vocabulary of rates and cases, or that survive only in mutilated form; and fifth, your verdict on whether that residue marks a defect of the epidemiological framework or a sign that the philosophical dispute is less determinate than it appears.
