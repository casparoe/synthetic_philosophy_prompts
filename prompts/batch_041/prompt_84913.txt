Ok so I'm a novelist, I'm maybe 200 pages into a book that follows one family from the early 60s to now — three generations, one narrator each — and the whole architecture rests on the idea that each of them was formed by the era they came of age in. Which means I've spent two years half-convinced that generations are real. Not real like electrons, obviously, but real the way money is real: a constructed kind that nonetheless picks out something, a cohort that shares a formative situation and reacts to it in recognisable ways.

So here's what I want. Tell me what would count against that. And I mean if generations are real kinds in that socially-constructed-but-real sense, what would we have to observe for it to be false? Sort the answers by how decisive they'd be, fatal first, and be honest about which ones are only awkward.

Some things I'd like covered, though tell me if I'm wrong to want them:

- A demographer friend told me you can't separate age, period and cohort effects. Age plus birth year just is the date, so the three are perfectly colinear and you have to bolt on an extra assumption before you can get any of them out. Does that mean my three-generation structure is unfalsifiable in principle, or just expensive? And does the extra assumption ever get justified on independent grounds, or is it picked to taste — i.e. picked to give the answer the researcher already wanted?
- What would the data have to look like for generations to be real kinds? Is there an observation that distinguishes a genuine cohort effect from a bunch of people simply being young in the same few years? I want the shape of the evidence, not a reading list.
- The boundaries bug me — 1981-96, or 1980-94, or whatever the polling firm decided. But I notice I don't think arbitrary boundaries kill teenager or middle age, so maybe that's a red herring. Tell me whether it is.
- The one that keeps me up: the label might be doing the work. If calling someone a Millennial makes them act in the way we then point at as proof of Millennial traits, is that a defeat or a vindication? It feels like cheating but I suspect it's actually the honest picture of how social kinds work, and I want to know which it is.
- Is the whole thing just period effects wearing a costume — a recession or a pandemic hitting everyone alive at the time, with the age brackets making it look like a cohort? How would I tell the difference?
- Half the popular books on this stuff are really selling a cyclical theory of history: every 80-odd years the same four cohort-personalities come around in the same order. Is that separable from the modest claim that cohorts differ, or am I quietly signing up for the cycle when I sign up for the modest claim? And if I strip the cycle out, is there anything left that predicts something?

Then the part I actually need: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability? If someone says generations are real but fuzzy, overlapping, contested, and defined largely by self-identification, have they said anything at all? Where's the line between a view that's appropriately modest and one that's bought itself immunity? And what would the strongest defender of generational realism concede counts against them — I dont want the critic's list, I want the list they'd sign.

Last thing: tell me where your own reasoning is likeliest to fail here, and what would change your mind about whatever verdict you land on. If the honest answer is that it's underdetermined, say so and tell me what would settle it — but I want to know which way you'd bet, and what would flip the bet.
