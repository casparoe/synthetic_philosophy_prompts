Right, so I've been running van Fraassen's Reflection principle in my grad seminar for years and defending it fairly hard, and I'd like it actually stress-tested instead of just admired. The position: your credence in A, conditional on the proposition that your future self will have credence r in A, should be r. Or the blunt version I give students -- if you're certain that in an hour you'll be at 0.8 that P, you should be at 0.8 that P now. I treat that as a genuine requirement of rationality, not a bookkeeping convention, and as the thing that makes deference to our own future selves principled rather than sentimental. (Yes, I know the new-evidence objection is no objection; Reflection handles that fine, that's not what I'm after.)

What I want is a map of its exposure. Which cases and results actually bite? The ones I already no about: I'm certain that tomorrow, hungover and miserable, I'll be at 0.9 that I'm a fraud -- does Reflection really tell me to be at 0.9 now? Same shape with forgetting, where I know my future self will have lost evidence I currently have. Then there's the self-locating stuff, Sleeping Beauty and its cousins, where Reflection and conditionalization seem to pull in seperate directions, plus the worry that the restricted version -- defer only to future selves you regard as better informed -- either goes circular or collapses into a tautology. And here's a case of my own I'd like you to take seriously: I know that after teaching a book for a semester I'll be at 0.9 that its argument works, not because I'll have new evidence but because teaching it makes me sympathetic to it. Should I be at 0.9 now? I don't think so, but I can't say why without giving up something I like.

Two more things, since they're really why I'm asking. I'm a many-minds person about quantum mechanics -- if the mind branches at measurement, does talk of my future credence even have a unique referent, and does Reflection survive that, or does it need a metaphysics of the self that I don't actually hold? And the Jamesian will-to-believe picture: if I'm confident that the therapy will work and that I'll end up believing P, Reflection seems to hand me P now, which makes working on my own credences incoherent. Is that a reductio or a feature?

Rank the defeaters by how much they should move me, distinguish what a committed defender would concede from what only critics say, say whether anything supports the principle at all (do the Dutch book and accuracy arguments even reach a diachronic norm like this, or is Reflection just stipulated?), and if it turns out every counterexample gets absorbed by some rider, tell me straight that it's insulated and whether that's a vice here or just what a normative constraint of this kind looks like. Also: how confident are you in each thing you claim, and which single point are you least sure of?
