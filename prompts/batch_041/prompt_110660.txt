A colleague has handed me an argument that she says settles the supertask puzzles, and I cannot decide whether it is sound or whether it quietly proves too much. The setting is Thomson's lamp. At t = 0 the lamp is on. At t = 1/2 minute we flip the switch, at t = 3/4 minute we flip it again, and so on, flipping at 1 − 2^−n minutes for each positive integer n, each flip instantaneous. The last flip falls before t = 1 minute, so by t = 1 minute infinitely many flips have been performed. Her argument runs:

(1) A supertask is a sequence of acts indexed by the natural numbers, all completed within a finite time, with no act outside the sequence.
(2) A sequence of acts is completed only once its final act has been performed.
(3) Every act in such a sequence is followed by another; none is final.
(4) Therefore no supertask is ever completed.
(5) The flips of the lamp form such a sequence, so they are never all performed, and at t = 1 minute there is no fact about the lamp's state.
(6) The same holds of any infinite sequence of acts: the runner in Zeno's dichotomy never reaches the wall, a pen that inscribes 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, … on a tape never finishes, and a ball that repeatedly halves the distance to rest never stops.
(7) Hence completed infinities of acts are impossible.

I want to keep the setup and the observation that no flip is last, and I am unwilling to conclude that the runner never arrives; that seems to me a reductio. But I cannot say where the reasoning breaks.

Please tell me which step to reject, and why it looked compelling. If the flaw is an equivocation or a scope mistake, name it and set out the two readings the step trades on. Then price the repair: if I instead keep every step and accept (7), what do I owe on the physics of the runner, and does giving up (2) force me to say something about what "completing" an infinite sequence actually amounts to? I would also like your verdicts on three further cases the argument is meant to cover: a lamp whose brightness varies continuously through the sequence, so that a limit is plainly defined; a jar into which balls are added and removed at each stage in such a way that it ends empty although infinitely many balls were added; and the question whether the ordinary lamp, if it does have a determinate state at t = 1 minute, has that state because of the flips, because of the switch's mechanics, or because of something else. Where the argument is right and where it overreaches, say so plainly.
