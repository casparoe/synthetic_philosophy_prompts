Hi — I'm on the consumer protection board for our state. Small board, we mostly handle complaints about ads and sales claims, but lately it's been all AI stuff. I need to write up our decision on a complaint that's on the docket for the 14th and I'd like help drafting the memo.

Here's the situation. A woman called a company's customer service chatbot to ask about a contractor she was thinking of hiring. The bot told her the contractor had been "cited twice for unsafe work." Flat out wrong — the guy has a clean record, it was a hallucination. She passed on him and told people in her building. He wants the company penalized and forced to send corrections to everyone who got the same wrong answer (apparently a few hundred people).

Our statute covers "false statements of fact made to consumers." The company's lawyer says the statute doesn't reach this, because a chatbot doesn't make statements — it predicts text. Nothing it produces is true or false, so nothing it produces is false. He compared it to saying a coin flip "claimed" heads.

Our board's guidance was written years ago for a totally different world. It says an output counts as a statement if it's the kind of thing that can be true or false and the vendor means for consumers to rely on it. But there's a footnote the previous board added in the last round of revisions, and that footnote is what everyone's actually fighting about now. It says: "Aptness for truth or falsity is not enough where the production of the output is governed solely by what an audience will find plausible."

The company loves that footnote. They point out that the final training stage of their model is literally tuned to produce answers that human raters score highly — so by our own language, the outputs are governed by plausibility and not by anything in the world, and we should dismiss. The complainant's lawyer says the footnote doesn't save them, because the model was first trained to predict what text actually comes next in real documents, and that's tracking the world; the approval-tuning is just polish on top. And he adds that if we accept the company's reading, no chatbot can ever be held to anything, because every one of them gets tuned that way.

I don't think either side is being dumb, but I also don't think we can split the difference and call it a day. What I need from you: a short written opinion I can circulate before the 14th that

(a) recommends a concrete disposition — dismiss, find a violation, or find a violation with a limited remedy — and says exactly what the remedy or conditions should be and by when;
(b) explains what our footnote actually means, which is the hard part, and says whether the company's reading of it is right, wrong, or right in a way that still leaves them exposed; and
(c) deals honestly with the strongest thing the losing side has going for it, since whichever way we go, one of these lawyers is going to appeal us and I don't want to look like we just picked a side.

One more thing while you're in there: should we amend the footnote, and if so, what should it say instead? Whatever you propose has to be something four non-lawyers can apply to the next complaint without me having to convene a seminar.

Two constraints. Write it in American English — the board is in the US and I have to read it out loud at the meeting. And please, no reading lists or citations. I'm on a state board, not in a graduate program, and a memo full of references is a memo I can't vote on. Assume I'm reasonably smart and know the basics, just not the specialist stuff. Thanks — I've got to get this drafted by Wensday.
