I am a high school student preparing a poem for my philosophy club's unit on Descartes and the animal mind. I want to write a sestina from the point of view of a magpie that has been taught to mimic human speech, addressing Descartes directly. The six end-words should be: word, sign, organ, clock, beast, thought. The poem should be accurate to Descartes' position in Part V of the Discourse on the Method: he holds that animals are machines without thought, and he offers two tests to distinguish a machine that imitates a human from a real human — first, that no animal or machine can use words or other signs by composing them to declare its thoughts, and second, that even when such a being does some things as well as or better than we do, it fails in others, revealing that it acts not from knowledge but from the disposition of its organs. The magpie in my poem can repeat both tests perfectly but cannot pass either. The sestina's fixed set of end-words, permuted by rule, should do the structural work of showing that the bird's speech is a mechanical rearrangement of a fixed repertoire — the disposition of organs rather than the composition of thought. The envoi should be the bird's attempt to turn the tests back on Descartes, pointing out the asymmetry he himself notes: a machine made in the likeness of a monkey could not be distinguished from a monkey, while a machine made in the likeness of a man could be distinguished by the two tests, so the tests seem designed for creatures shaped like us. The poem should leave the reader unsure whether the magpie has declared a thought or only performed another permutation. After the poem, please add a short prose note explaining which line or turn corresponds to which of Descartes' two tests, and where the asymmetry enters, so that a specialist can check the poem's accuracy.
