I've been reading around the old argument about whether history can be objective — Ranke wanting the historian to get himself out of the way so the past can show itself as it essentially was, and Carr and Hayden White on the other side saying that the selection of what goes in and the shape of the story are the historian's own contribution, that there's no view from nowhere to do it from. I think the sceptics basically win, but with a qualification I keep failing to nail down, and I want you to try to break it. Here's my position: objectivity is real at the level of individual claims and vanishes at the level of narrative. 'The treaty was signed in that year', 'the harvest failed', 'the regiment broke' — those are true or false in a perfectly ordinary way, and no amount of theorising about standpoint changes that. But once you say a stretch of the past is a story of decline, or a tragedy, or the working out of a class conflict, you've stopped making claims and started imposing a shape, and I don't think anything in the archive can force one shape over another. The evidence underdetermines the story, and the story is where the historian's present-day concerns come in.

So what would count against this? I dont want the usual stuff about bias and present-mindedness, I already believe that part. I want the arguments, cases, or results that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be, and I want you to be honest about which of them a committed sceptic would concede counts against them versus which they'd wave away as not touching the real thesis. Two things nag at me and I'd like them addressed head-on rather than dodged. First, some descriptions seem to be true of an earlier event only because of what happened later — the war that broke out in 1618 turned out to last thirty years, and that's a true thing to say about 1618, but nobody in 1619 could have known it. If sentences like that are objectively true, does that mean the narrative layer has truth-values after all, or is it just a trick of grammar that the sceptic can absorb without giving anything up? Second, I can't work out what would even count as evidence against the narrative-level half of my view. If a cache of documents turned up that made some historian's grand story look ridiculous, would that be the archive forcing a shape, or would it just be more individual claims being added to the pile while the shape stays a free choice? If it's the latter, I worry my position has been quietly qualified into something nothing could touch — and I'd want to know whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just the price of it being about interpretation rather than fact.
