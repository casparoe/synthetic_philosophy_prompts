Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether the dispute between mereological nihilists and universalists is a real dispute at all, and I came away with the uneasy sense that she won on points. I want a rematch, and I want to practice against her actual position rather than against a caricature of it.

Play her. Her position, stated as fairly as I can manage: when one philosopher says there are no composite objects, only simples arranged tablewise, and another says there are tables over and above the simples, the two agree about every atom, every arrangement, every causal claim, every prediction, and every practical consequence; they differ only in the meaning they attach to words like "exist" and "object." So the question "are there really tables?" has no answer until a language is fixed, and once a language is fixed the answer is trivial. A choice of language can be more or less useful for this or that purpose, but it cannot be correct or incorrect, and there is no framework-neutral fact of the matter waiting to be discovered. Hence the dispute is verbal, and the right response to it is not to take a side but to notice what is going on.

Constraints on the role. Hold that position as strongly as it can honestly be held: concede a small point if I earn it, never the main one. Argue by drawing distinctions and constructing cases, not by appealing to authority — do not cite books, papers, or authors, and do not name any philosophers. If you need an example, invent one; if you need a term of art, define it yourself. If you think a question I put to you is malformed, say so and explain why instead of answering it. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, no lists, so that it feels like a conversation and I cannot hide behind bullet points. Stay in character until I say "step out." Write in American English.

My opening move. Here is a case I want you to classify. Two people share a language completely: same words, same meanings, same dispositions to assent, the same grasp of all the physics, and identical predictions about everything that will ever be observed. One of them says that the table is nothing over and above the atoms arranged tablewise; the other says that the table is something over and above the atoms arranged tablewise, though it is not an extra atom. By your test, they agree on all the facts and differ only in words. Now run your test on us. You and I agree on all the facts about what people mean by their words, all the facts about how this debate actually goes, and all the facts about which sentences get asserted — and we disagree about whether the debate is verbal. Is our disagreement verbal too? If it is, then your view is something I can adopt or decline, but never something you can be right about, and I want to know why adopting it beats adopting mine. If it is not, then your test has a counterexample, and I want to know what marks the difference. And do not tell me the difference is that my case turns on "metaphysical" words while yours turns on "semantic" ones; that only moves the question back a step. My suspicion is that what is really doing the work in your position is the assumption that two people who share all the facts cannot disagree about anything except words, and I want you to defend that assumption rather than restate it.
