Ok so a couple months back I did something that half my town still isn't speaking to me about, and I honestly dont know if I was in the wrong, so I'm asking. I volunteer on our village's little rescue boat. Bad storm one night, two calls come in at basically the same time: three people whose car had gone into the flooded river on one side of the estuary, and one guy clinging to a tree on the other. Our boat could realistically reach one spot and get back before the current made the other unreachable. So we could save one group, not both. And as far as I could tell the stakes were identical -- everyone there was going to drown otherwise, everyone was equally likely to still be alive by the time we got there, nobody was hurt worse than anyone else.

I couldn't find any reason to pick one group over the other, so I flipped a coin, and it came up for the one guy on the tree. We got him. By the time we could've gotten back across, the three were gone. Now people are saying I killed three people to save one, and that any idiot knows you go for the three. A friend tried to lay it out for me properly: he said if I go for the one, then three people lose their lives, but if I go for the three, only one person loses his, so obviously three beats one. But that feels like a sleight of hand to me -- each of those three loses exactly what the one guy would've lost, so I dont see where the extra weight is supposed to come from. Someone else told me numbers only really start to matter when the many each stand to lose less than the one would -- like if it was save one person's life vs save three people from losing a leg -- and that in my case, where everyone faced the same thing, its a different question entirely. Is that right? Was I wrong to save the one? And if I wasn't, was the coin flip a legitimate way to decide, or was I just dodging the choice?
