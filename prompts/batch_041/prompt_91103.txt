I volunteer as a chaplain at a medium-security prison, where I run a Wednesday-evening discussion group that has gradually turned into something closer to a seminar. One of the men in the group, a former paralegal who reads widely, has been pressing me on the doctrine that Christ bore the penalty for human sin in our place. He grants that forgiveness is available and that God is entitled to extend it; what he cannot accept is the mechanism. If the penalty is simply waived, he says, then the wrong was never taken seriously. If it is instead borne by a substitute, then the wrong was taken seriously at the expense of the wrong person, which he calls a sanctified version of scapegoating. I have found myself falling back on the argument that we already believe in penal substitution in ordinary life — that we routinely let one person absorb the penalty due to another, as when someone pays a friend's fine or assumes a relative's debt — but his reply is sharper than my response to it: paying a fine is not being punished, and the doctrine needs punishment, not mere payment. I would like to see the two positions developed properly, rather than traded as slogans, because I suspect that the real disagreement lies somewhere more specific than either of us has yet located.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors. The first defends the position that the doctrine is coherent and that its structure is already embedded in ordinary practices — third-party payment of penalties, voluntary assumption of another's liability, collective forfeiture accepted by a group on behalf of one member — and holds that the standard objection conflates guilt, which cannot be transferred, with penalty, which can. The second holds that this defence trades on an equivocation: that penalties, unlike debts, carry an expressive and retributive dimension addressed to the offender, so that transferring them either becomes unjust or ceases to be punishment at all, in which case the doctrine has quietly become a story about cancellation rather than substitution. Have them work through a concrete case I have been turning over: a high-school athletic league whose rules require an entire team to forfeit a season's wins when a single player is found to have used a banned substance, and a team that votes, after the violation is discovered, to accept the forfeiture rather than appeal it on the ground that only one member was at fault. Let the defender use the case and the critic show where it may be disanalogous, and let them also address the structural oddity that in the Christian doctrine the wronged party is himself the one who provides and bears the substitute — a point the critic may press as collapsing substitution into simple forgiveness, and to which the defender must offer something more than insistence.

The dialogue should run roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words. Neither character should be a strawman, and neither should merely restate a position: each should respond to the specific point the other has just made, conceding where a concession is warranted and pressing where it is not. Neither should quote or cite any text, and neither should appeal to what some author is said to have established; the arguments must stand on their own. Do not force a resolution, but do not leave the exchange hanging in mutual incomprehension either — let it end wherever the arguments actually leave the two of them.
