I'm running a session for a second-year PPE seminar on direct democracy, and I want to do a game rather than lecture at them. Twenty-four students, 90 minutes, the rooms just got movable chairs and a whiteboard, no tech.

What I want them to feel in their gut is something that usually only lands as a puzzle on paper: that majority rule over a set of connected decisions can produce a verdict that no single person in the room actually holds, and that how you count decides who wins. The cleanest way in, I reckon, is the set-up where everyone reasons perfectly consistently on their own — they answer a couple of linked sub-questions and then give an overall verdict that follows from their own answers — but when you tally the sub-questions separately from the tally on the verdict, the two results contradict each other. (Some people call this the discursive dilemma; in a court setting it gets called the doctrinal paradox.) I definately don't want to hand them the name or the trick up front — I want them to walk into it and only clock what's happened at the end.

You're an experienced seminar leader who also designs classroom games, so act as both. Before you design anything, take your time and brainstorm: sketch two or three candidate scenarios, think carefully about which one will actually produce the contradiction reliably with 24 people in a 90-minute slot, and flag the ones you reject and why. Then give me the plan.

The plan needs to include:

- A self-contained scenario — please invent a plausible referendum or citizens'-assembly case, don't use a real one, and don't assume anyone knows any real-world facts.
- The linked sub-questions and the overall question, plus a way of seeding participants' views (role cards, briefing sheets, a pre-set evidence pack — your call) so the contradiction is guaranteed to show up rather than just hoped for. I want the two tallies to genuinely diverge in the room.
- Rules, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, including what I, the facilitator, do and say at each stage.
- The reveal: a point where the group has to decide which tally counts as the result, and discovers that the choice flips the outcome and that neither counting rule is neutral. Ideally let them argue themselves into picking one, then spring the fact that the other would have won.
- A second, shorter beat where changing the order of the votes (deciding the overall verdict first, say) flips it again, so they see the procedure itself is doing the work.
- A debrief that maps what just happened onto the idea: premise-based versus conclusion-based counting, the fact that every individual was rational and it's the aggregation that breaks, and the bigger point that the will of the people can be an artefact of procedure rather than something sitting there waiting to be read off.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each thing. I'm expecting at least one student to say the case is contrived, one to insist you should just vote on the whole thing at once, and one to spot the paradox early and spoil the surprise — tell me how to deal with those.
- An honest note on what the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.

Keep it practical, and don't send me off to read anything — no reading lists, no citations, no names of papers. I want to be able to run this next week.
