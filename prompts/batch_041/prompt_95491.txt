A colleague in a reading group has reconstructed what she calls the austere form of the Buddhist analysis of the person, and I cannot decide whether it is the spine of the doctrine or a reductio of it. Here is her reconstruction.

(1) For anything to be a self it would have to be permanent — not itself a process of arising and passing away — and it would have to be such that it could not be turned against its own wishes. The tradition sets up the question in just this way: if any aggregate were the self, it would not lead to affliction, and one could make it be as one wishes. Whatever fails this test cannot be a self.

(2) Each of the five aggregates that exhaust a person's makeup — bodily form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, consciousness — is impermanent and liable to affliction, and none of them is under anyone's control.

(3) So none of the aggregates is a self; each is to be disowned, neither owned as mine nor taken as what I am nor taken as my self.

(4) A person is either one of these aggregates or the collection of them. There is no third thing for it to be.

(5) So there is no self.

(6) If there is no self, there is no person: no one who acts, no one to whom anything happens, no one who is the same individual tomorrow as today.

(7) If there is no person, there is no one who bears the fruit of their actions, no one for whom a next life could be theirs or a matter of concern, and no one at whom compassion could be aimed.

(8) But the tradition that supplies (1) and (2) insists that actions bear fruit for the one who does them, treats the question of rebirth as urgent, and makes compassion for beings the point of the whole exercise.

So (1), (2), or one of the inferences has to give, and each of them, on its own, looks close to undeniable.

Two things make me trust the argument less than I trust its steps. First, it does not seem to stop at persons. Run the same form of reasoning on a chariot, a river, or a flame, or on the aggregates themselves, and it dissolves them too; yet the tradition seems to want ordinary things to be real enough to live among, and it warns explicitly against the nihilistic reading of its own teaching. Second, one member of the group said the person is neither one of the aggregates nor something separate from them, but a further entity that is real in its own right even though no part of it can be picked out as the person — and that some early Buddhist schools defended exactly this rather than treating it as an outsider's confusion.

Tell me where the argument goes wrong, or, if it does not go wrong, which premise is least costly to surrender. Name the impostor step, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives. In particular, say what is left of a not-self teaching once the price is paid, and whether an account that keeps persons, agency, responsibility, and compassion can still deserve that name.
