I have been trying to settle, for myself, whether and when a person's death is bad for the person who dies, and my verdicts on particular cases do not obviously add up to a principle. I would like a principle I can test against the cases, not a survey of what others have said; assume I have no texts to hand. Here is what I think, case by case.

A woman of ninety, after a full and happy life, dies painlessly in her sleep, never knowing it was coming. Her death was bad for her, but only a little.

A man of twenty, healthy, with everything ahead of him, dies painlessly in his sleep. His death was very bad for him.

A man in the final stage of a painful illness that will certainly kill him, who has said he wants to die, dies in his sleep. His death was not bad for him; if anything it was good for him.

A woman of forty dies of a condition that, unknown to her, had been cured a month earlier; had she lived she would have had thirty more good years. Her death was bad for her, and roughly as bad as the twenty-year-old's.

A man of sixty dies. By his own account his life was complete and there was nothing left he wanted to do or to have. I am inclined to say his death was barely bad for him at all.

A man dies, and afterwards a rival deliberately destroys his life's work and ruins his reputation, so that everything he cared about is undone. I think this is bad for him — he is harmed by it — even though he never learns of it.

That same man, had he lived, would have been forgotten within a generation, as nearly everyone is. I do not think being forgotten is bad for the dead.

It was not bad for me that I did not exist for the aeons before I was born, but I do think it is bad for me that I shall not exist for the aeons after I die.

The rough principle I have been using is this: a death is bad for you to the extent that it deprives you of goods you would otherwise have had. That seems to handle the first five cases tolerably well. It is hopeless on the sixth and seventh: the man whose work is destroyed loses nothing he would otherwise have had, since he is dead either way, and yet I cannot shake the verdict that he is harmed; while being forgotten, which is just as posthumous, does not seem to harm anyone. The eighth verdict is also doing something the deprivation principle does not explain, though I am less sure what.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that delivers all eight verdicts? If there is not, say precisely which verdict I should give up, and why that one rather than its partner. Then use whatever you settle on to predict a case I have not listed, so that I can see it working: a man dies, and after his death the rival is exposed, the work is restored and is at last celebrated as he always hoped it would be. Is that posthumous vindication good for him? If ruin is bad for him, vindication should be good for him, and I want to know whether your principle says so and whether that verdict sits with the seventh case or against it. Please write in British English.
