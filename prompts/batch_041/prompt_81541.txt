I retired a few years back after a long career in marine biology -- fisheries ecology mostly, so I spent alot of time with population models -- and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, which has been humbling. I'm in a small reading group that meets at a bar once a week. Last time, two of the regulars got into it over Aumann's agreement theorem, and I've been chewing on it since, because I honestly could not tell who had the better of it. Hoping you can referee. I don't need a reading list or names and dates -- just tell me what's actually right and wrong in the arguments.

The two of them: Dana, who did a physics PhD before going into industry and is now a working statistician, and Ray, who came out of history of science and is now a philosopher-of-science type. Roughly how it went:

Dana: the theorem says two agents who share a prior, are rational, and have common knowledge of each other's opinions cannot agree to disagree -- their posteriors for any event have to come out equal. So the idea that reasonable people can just disagree is never the last word. If she and Ray really share priors, and she knows he's rational, and she knows where he stands, then his posterior should just equal hers. Which means that when she and a peer she respects end up stuck in a real disagreement, that's a signal: something has to give. One of them isn't being rational, or their priors were never really the same.

Ray: the theorem is nearly a tautology and does no work here. It's a consistency property you build into a probability space -- you give the agents a common prior and make each other's posteriors common knowledge, and lo, the posteriors match. That's just what you put in. It says nothing about real disagreement, because that common-knowledge condition is basically never satisfied outside a model, and the theorem is silent about the case where the two agents disagree about the prior itself. So Dana can't lean on it the way she wants.

Dana: fine, it's an idealization, but idealizations still constrain. If she thinks Ray is about as reliable as she is, the theorem at least tells her the disagreement is unstable -- she should move toward him, not dig in.

Ray: that last step doesn't follow. The theorem is about an equilibrium, not about how you should revise when you discover someone disagrees. There's a whole seperate fight about whether you should be conciliatory about disagreement, and the theorem doesn't settle it -- it assumes the disagreement has already been ironed out inside the model. And the move is circular anyway: the theorem only bites because it assumes a common prior, and Dana wants to use it to conclude that disagreement means someone has gone wrong, which only follows if you've already granted the common prior.

Dana: the common prior isn't just assumed for free -- differences in belief should trace to differences in information, not to people starting from different priors; two rational people with the same information ought to end up in the same place. And she said the theorem obviously isn't empty, because there's a whole family of economics results about how rational agents with common priors shouldn't want to bet against each other or trade purely on disagreement. A tautology wouldn't be doing that kind of work.

Ray: those results are in exactly the same boat -- same idealizations, same caveats.

Then the part that stumped me. Apparently they'd actually tried it once. We'd all read a paper about a fish stock collapse, and they disagreed about the main driver -- pathogen outbreak vs. a shift in the prey base. Dana wrote 0.8 for the pathogen on a napkin, Ray wrote 0.3, they showed each other, and then... nothing. Neither budged. Dana said that was exactly her point: the theorem predicts they converge, they didn't converge, so either their priors aren't shared or one of them isn't being rational. Ray said no, it just shows the common-knowledge premise fails -- seeing his number once doesn't make his posterior common knowledge, doesn't make her certainty about his rationality common knowledge, and so on.

Can you go through this move by move -- Dana's opening, Ray's tautology charge, the idealizations-still-constrain move, Ray's equilibrium-not-dynamics reply, the common-prior exchange, the betting-and-trade point, and finally the napkin episode -- and say who's right about what, where each of them is overreaching, and what both of them might be missing? Move by move, not a summary. And then on its own final line, give me your overall call in this exact form: Verdict: <Dana / Ray / even> -- <one short clause saying why>.
